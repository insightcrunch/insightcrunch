Any plan for how to stop Robert Lewandowski has to begin with a concession that most defensive briefings quietly refuse to make: you are not going to keep him out of your penalty area, and a plan built on that hope collapses within twenty minutes of kick-off. He will get in there. He will get in there against a low block, against a high line, against a back three, and against a side that has spent the week rehearsing nothing else. The question a coach actually has to answer is much narrower and much more useful, which is what happens in the one second after he arrives.

![How to stop Robert Lewandowski, a penalty-area defending plan and marking scheme - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-43.webp)

That narrowing is the whole article. Against most forwards you are building a marking plan, a set of instructions about a man and where he goes. Against a box occupier you are building a penalty-area defending plan, which is a different document with different contents: starting distances, body orientations, contact rules, second-ball responsibilities, and a goalkeeper protocol that has been agreed rather than assumed. The decisive events happen inside a twelve-yard square in less time than it takes a defender to look up, and nothing you can shout during those events will change them. Everything that matters was decided before the delivery left the crosser's foot.

## How to Stop Robert Lewandowski: the plan in one sentence, and the price of it

Defend the penalty area rather than the man, which means you accept that he receives inside the box, and you spend your entire preparation making sure that when he receives he is one half-step further from goal, one degree more square, and one body-part more encumbered than he wants to be, with a designated defender already moving toward the rebound.

The price is real and you should quote it to your players out loud, because a plan that hides its cost gets abandoned the first time it looks uncomfortable. You will concede crosses. You will concede corners, because a defensive block that stays compact and low around its own area clears the ball out for corners rather than out for throw-ins. You will concede possession in wide areas that a braver side would contest. You will look, from the stand, like a team being camped in on. Coaches who cannot tolerate that appearance change the plan at half time, push the line up to relieve the psychological pressure of the picture, and lose to the exact pattern they had already survived twice.

What you get in exchange is a duel you can actually win. A forward whose game is concentrated inside the area is, paradoxically, one of the few elite attackers whose decisive moments are entirely predictable in location. You cannot predict where a dribbler will beat his man. You can predict, to within about three yards, where a box occupier will make contact with a cutback. That predictability is the seam, and everything below is the method for using it.

## What box occupation actually is

Start by discarding the word poacher, or at least by refusing to let it do any analytical work. Poacher is a description of outcomes, and outcomes are what you are trying to prevent, so they are useless as a planning input. What you need is the mechanism, which is a chain of three very small physical wins that occur in sequence, each of which is defeated by a different defensive behaviour, and each of which has to be defended in its own order.

The first win is detachment. Before the delivery arrives, the forward has to create separation from his marker, and against a good centre-back the separation available is not two yards or even one. It is a half-step, which is roughly the distance between a defender who can extend an arm into the contact and a defender who cannot. Detachment is bought with a preparatory movement that has nothing to do with the ball: a two-pace drift toward the near post that pulls the defender's weight onto his front foot, followed by a check back into the space the defender's own momentum has just vacated. It can also be bought by moving across the defender's blind side while the defender's eyes are following the crosser, which is the version that gets defenders blamed for concentration when the real error was orientation.

The second win is occupation, meaning the placing of the body between the defender and the arriving ball. This is not strength in the gym sense. It is the acquisition of the inside line, half a stride before the defender realises the inside line is what is being contested, and then the maintenance of that line through contact using a low base, a wide stance, and an arm that establishes distance without becoming a foul. A forward who has won detachment but not occupation gets a shot that the defender can still block. A forward who has won both gets a shot the defender can only watch.

The third win is immediacy. The ball is struck first time, or after a single controlling touch that is really part of the strike, and the reason this matters is not aesthetics. It is that the goalkeeper's set position is a function of time. A goalkeeper who gets three quarters of a second sets his feet, drops his hands, and narrows the angle he actually has to cover. A goalkeeper who gets a quarter of a second is still moving when the shot is struck, and a moving goalkeeper covers a fraction of the goal that a set goalkeeper covers. First-time finishing is not a flourish. It is a mechanism for removing the goalkeeper's preparation, and it is the reason a shot from ten yards with three defenders nearby beats a shot from eighteen yards with none.

### How does Robert Lewandowski score from positions defenders think are covered?

Because coverage is measured in distance and the duel is decided in time. A defender standing two yards away with his weight on the wrong foot and his eyes on the crosser has coverage on paper and none in practice. The half-step of separation bought before delivery, plus a first-time contact, removes the goalkeeper's set time entirely.

Those three wins are sequential and dependent. Occupation without detachment gets you nothing, because the defender is already on the inside line. Immediacy without occupation gets you a blocked shot. That dependency is the good news for a defensive coach, because it means you do not have to defeat all three. You have to defeat the earliest one you can reach, and the earliest one is always detachment, which is decided before the ball is even delivered and therefore falls entirely within the part of the match you can rehearse.

The bad news, and the reason so many well-drilled sides still concede, is that a defender who wins the first contest can still lose the goal. He can win the detachment battle, arrive first, get a head or a boot on the delivery, and then stand admiring the clearance while the same forward has already turned and is arriving at the rebound with a two-yard head start. Box occupation is not one duel. It is a duel followed immediately by a second duel that most teams have not assigned to anybody.

## The half-step economy

Here is the claim this entire plan rests on, and it is worth naming because a named idea survives the walk from the video room to the training pitch in a way that a loose principle does not. Call it the half-step economy: against a box occupier, the duel is decided by whether the defender concedes a single half-step of separation before the delivery arrives, which makes the defender's timing relative to the crosser, not relative to the striker, the coachable variable.

Read that second clause again, because it is where most coaching goes wrong. The instinctive instruction to a centre-back marking a forward inside the area is to watch the forward. Stay tight. Feel him. Do not lose him. Every one of those instructions makes the defender's reference point the man, and a defender whose reference point is the man is always reacting, always a beat late, and always vulnerable to the check-and-go, because the check is designed precisely to make a man-referenced defender commit.

The correction is to make the crosser the reference point. The defender's job is to have completed his positional adjustment by the moment the crosser's head goes down, because that is the last instant at which the delivery can still be redirected, and everything after it is fixed flight. If the defender is goal-side, contact-established, and half-turned at that instant, the forward's preparatory movement has nothing left to exploit, because the picture the movement was designed to disturb is already set. If the defender is still shuffling at that instant, the half-step is gone and no amount of strength recovers it.

This reframing has three practical consequences that a coach can install in a single session. First, it gives the defender a clock he can actually see. Watching a forward gives you no information about when the duel starts. Watching the crosser's plant foot and head position tells you exactly when it starts, every time, without exception. Second, it converts a reactive job into a proactive one, and defenders are far better at proactive jobs. Third, it makes the coaching point objectively reviewable on video, because you can freeze the clip at the crosser's contact and ask a simple binary question: was the defender set or was he moving? No judgment, no debate, no argument about effort.

The economy in the name is deliberate. Half-steps are the currency of this duel and they are spent and earned in tiny increments that add up over ninety minutes. A defender who concedes half a step on eight deliveries has effectively conceded four full yards of cumulative advantage across a match, and one of those eight will be the one that matters. A defender who concedes it on none will look, in the post-match summary, like he had an easy afternoon against a forward who was quiet, and the truth will be that the afternoon was decided in eight moments nobody outside the coaching staff noticed.

There is a corollary that is worth stating plainly because it protects your defenders from unfair criticism, which in turn protects the plan. When a goal is conceded this way, the visible error is almost never the actual error. The visible error is a defender apparently ball-watching at the moment of the finish. The actual error occurred one and a half seconds earlier, twelve yards further out, when he took his eyes off the crosser to check the forward's position and lost the half-step in the process. If you review this the way most teams review it, you will coach the wrong moment, your defender will start watching the forward even harder, and the problem will get worse.

## The shape and line height that frame the plan

Before any of the individual craft matters, the block has to be built so that the individual craft is possible. Three structural decisions do most of the work.

The first is line height, and the answer here is more specific than high or low. Against a forward whose threat lives inside the area and whose game does not depend on running the length of the pitch behind you, the line should sit at a height that keeps your centre-backs out of two situations at once. Too high and you invite the diagonal in behind, which forces your centre-backs to turn and defend the box facing their own goal, which is the single worst body orientation for the duel you are trying to win. Too deep and you compress everybody into the twelve-yard square voluntarily, which hands the forward the environment he prefers and removes the space your defenders need in order to attack the flight of the ball. The functional answer is a mid-block that defends the edge of its own area rather than the halfway line, with the line dropping to protect depth only in response to a genuine threat behind, and recovering forward the instant the danger passes so that you are not living inside your own box by default.

The second decision is horizontal compactness. The threat you are defending is fed by wide deliveries, and the distance between your full-back and your near-side centre-back is the corridor through which the best of those deliveries arrive. Cutbacks are the delivery type this forward profits from most, because a cutback arrives with the goalkeeper's momentum going the wrong way and the defenders' bodies square to the ball. The distance you actually care about is not the width of your back four across the pitch, which is what most coaches measure. It is the depth of your near-side full-back relative to the byline, because a full-back who defends the cross by getting tight to the touchline while retreating is a full-back who will be beaten to the byline and will produce exactly the cutback you cannot defend. Force the delivery earlier and from a worse angle, and you have already halved the problem.

The third decision is the number of bodies in the box at the moment of delivery, and this is a genuine trade you have to make rather than a free lunch. Every additional defender inside the area adds a rebound defender and a potential blocker, and simultaneously adds a body for the forward to use as a screen, a body that can obstruct your own goalkeeper, and a body that will be somewhere else when you win the ball and want to counter. The workable balance for most sides is a back four plus the near-side holding midfielder as a designated second-ball defender, with the far-side midfielder holding the top of the area rather than dropping in. That gives you five in and around, which is enough to handle the sequence without turning your box into a scrum in which your goalkeeper cannot see the flight of the ball.

Those three decisions interact, and the interaction matters more than any single one of them. A mid-block with poor horizontal compactness is worse than a low block with good compactness. A low block with five in the area but no assigned rebound defender is worse than a mid-block with four and a clear assignment. If you have limited training time, spend it on the assignment rather than on the shape, because the shape is what your side already knows and the assignment is what it almost certainly does not.

### Where should the defensive line sit against a penalty-box finisher?

At the edge of its own penalty area rather than near halfway, dropping for genuine depth threats and recovering immediately afterwards. Too high invites the diagonal in behind and forces your centre-backs to defend facing their own goal. Too deep hands him the crowded environment he prefers.

## The individual assignment and the defender profile that fits

The general guidance handed out about marking elite forwards is that you want a big, quick, aggressive centre-back, which is close to useless as a selection input because it describes a player who does not exist in most squads and would be the best defender in the side regardless of the opponent. The useful version asks which specific attributes are load-bearing in this particular duel and which are decorative, and the answer is unusual enough to change team selection.

Recovery pace is the least load-bearing attribute here. It matters enormously against a depth runner, which is why the plan for that type of forward, covered in the cluster hub on [stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), is built around it. Against a forward whose decisive actions occur within a twelve-yard square, a defender's top speed over forty yards is close to irrelevant, and selecting for it costs you the attributes that are load-bearing.

Height is only partially load-bearing, and the part that matters is not the part coaches assume. Standing height helps on a set piece delivered into a static duel. It helps far less on a moving aerial contest in open play, where the ball is won by whoever arrives with forward momentum, and a shorter defender attacking the flight of the ball beats a taller defender waiting under it almost every time. Select for the willingness to attack the ball rather than for the tape measure.

The genuinely load-bearing attributes are three. The first is short-area strength through contact, meaning the ability to hold a position while being leaned on, from a low base, with the feet moving. This is not the same as being physically imposing. Some very solid-looking defenders lose this duel constantly because they defend upright, and some slighter defenders win it because they defend from a low centre of gravity with active feet.

The second is orientation discipline, which is the acquired habit of keeping the ball and the man in the same field of view by half-turning rather than by swivelling the head. A defender who turns his head loses one of the two, and the moment he does, the half-step goes. This is the single most trainable attribute in the list and the one most teams never explicitly coach.

The third is what you might call second-event willingness: the mental habit of treating a won first contest as the start of the duel rather than the end of it. Defenders who have this look busy in a way that reads on video as nervousness. They win a header and immediately spin to face the drop zone. They block a shot and immediately move toward the rebound rather than toward the ball. It is a coachable behaviour and it is worth more than four inches of height.

Put those together and the profile that fits is a compact, low-centred, aggressive-into-contact defender with good peripheral discipline and a habit of playing the second ball. In many squads that is the smaller of your two centre-backs rather than the bigger one, which is a genuinely counterintuitive selection call, and it is the reason this analysis is worth doing rather than defaulting to the obvious.

The pairing question then follows. Because you are asking one defender to be aggressive into contact, you need the other to be the covering, calmer, spatially disciplined one, and you need the pairing to be explicit rather than assumed. Two aggressive defenders both attacking the delivery leave the drop zone empty. Two cautious defenders both holding position concede the half-step by default. State which one goes and which one covers, and state it by delivery type rather than by side, because the forward will move between them deliberately in order to make the answer ambiguous.

### What is the difference between marking the man and defending the area?

Marking the man makes your shape a function of his movement, which hands him control of where your defenders stand. Defending the area fixes your structure and attaches responsibility inside it, so his movement stretches the contact rather than relocating your defence. The second is what elite blocks actually do.

## The box-occupation defence grid

Everything above becomes usable only when it is reduced to something a defender can hold in his head at speed. This is that reduction. Six reception situations recur inside the penalty area against this forward profile, and each one has a different correct starting distance, a different correct body orientation, a different contact rule, a different goalkeeper start position, and a different recovery behaviour if the first contest is lost. Learn the grid and you have the duel; learn the general principle without the grid and you will apply the wrong rule under pressure, which is worse than having no rule.

| Reception situation | Defender starting distance | Body orientation | Contact rule | Goalkeeper start | If first contest lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deep cross to the far post | Goal-side, touching distance, on the inside shoulder | Half-turned, chest toward the flight, hips open to goal | Establish forearm contact before the flight arrives, then attack the ball | Central, off the line, ready to attack the six-yard box | Defender spins goal-side immediately; far full-back covers the back post line |
| Cutback from the byline | Two yards off, between him and the near post, never level | Square to the ball, feet active, weight forward | No contact until his first touch; then close the shooting line, do not slide | Near post pinned, weight forward, hands low | Nearest midfielder blocks the shot line; goalkeeper stays big rather than diving early |
| Near-post flick or first-time contact | Tight, front-foot side, denying the run in front | Facing the crosser, shoulder on his shoulder | Contact early and continuously, physically follow the run across | Two yards off the near post, set before delivery | Second centre-back covers the flick zone behind; goalkeeper does not commit |
| Low delivery into the six-yard box | Goal-side, ball-side foot forward, arm across | Half-turned, eyes on the flight, body between man and target | Contact maintained through the delivery, no reaching around | On the line, feet set, hands ready for the deflection | Designated rebound defender steps in front; nobody chases the original ball |
| Rebound or blocked shot in the area | Off him by a yard, facing the drop zone | Square, weight on the balls of the feet, low | Body between him and the loose ball, arms wide, no lunging | Recovering to the line, calling for the clearance direction | Clear the area rather than the ball, accept the corner |
| Back-to-goal reception on the edge | Two yards off, not touching | Side-on, showing him toward the touchline | Delay only, no contact from behind | Set on the line, ready for the turn and shot | Midfield screen collapses on the turn; centre-back holds position |

Three things in that grid look wrong to most coaches until you work through why they are there.

The cutback row instructs the defender not to make contact until the first touch, which contradicts every other row. The reason is that a cutback arrives at a defender who is facing the ball with his back to his own goal, and any contact made in that orientation is either a foul in a lethal area or a shove that spins the defender past the forward and out of the picture entirely. Against the cutback, the defender's job is not to win a physical contest. It is to be closer to the near post than the forward is, so that the only available finish is across the goalkeeper into the far corner, which is the harder finish and the one your goalkeeper is set for.

The rebound row instructs the defender to clear the area rather than the ball, which sounds like a distinction without a difference and is the most important line in the grid. A defender in a scramble who tries to clear cleanly makes contact with a moving ball, off balance, with an opponent leaning on him, and the outcome is close to random. A defender who commits to getting the ball out of the twelve-yard square by any legal means and accepts the corner has converted a random outcome into a certain one at a small cost. Teams that concede repeatedly from second phases almost always have defenders trying to be tidy at the exact moment tidiness is worthless.

The back-to-goal row says delay only and no contact from behind, and it is the one row where the entire logic of the plan inverts. That inversion is important enough to have its own section below.

## The collective triggers and the cover behaviour

An individual assignment without collective triggers is a defender left alone with the best penalty-box forward on the pitch, which is not a plan, it is a sacrifice. Four triggers convert the individual job into a team job.

The first trigger is the crosser's plant foot, which starts the clock for every defender in the area simultaneously. When the winger plants, everybody in the box has to be set. Not moving toward being set. Set. This is a shared reference point rather than an individual one, and its value is that it synchronises five players without anybody having to communicate, which matters because the box is loud and nobody hears anything.

The second trigger is the forward's first movement, and the instruction attached to it is counterintuitive: do not follow it. The first movement of a double move is bait, and a defender who follows it has already lost, because the check back arrives while his weight is committed. The rule is that the defender responds to the second movement only, and holds his ground through the first while maintaining contact. This is genuinely difficult and it takes repeated exposure to install, because the instinct to follow is strong and the first movement is often toward the ball, which makes following feel like commitment rather than error.

The third trigger is the moment the ball enters the final third on the ball-side flank, which is when the near-side holding midfielder drops into his rebound position at the top of the six-yard box rather than tracking a runner into the area. Most sides lose this because the midfielder is tracking somebody and never gets there. Take him off the tracking job explicitly. His job is the second ball, and you should tell him that a runner arriving unmarked at the penalty spot is somebody else's problem, because you have decided it is.

The fourth trigger is the loss of the first contest, at which point the entire block collapses toward the goal line for one second and then re-expands. Sides that defend the second phase well have this reflex; sides that do not have players still recovering to their original positions while the rebound is being converted. This is drillable in ten minutes a week and almost nobody drills it.

Cover behaviour follows from the same logic. The far-side centre-back covers the space behind the near-side one and does not step across to contest the delivery, because two defenders attacking the same ball leaves the rebound zone empty and gives the forward exactly the environment he wants. The far-side full-back tucks to the back-post line and stays there, since the back-post arrival is the delivery type that produces the simplest finishes in football. The goalkeeper's zone is his own and defenders do not enter it, which requires the goalkeeper to claim it verbally with one word used consistently rather than with a shout that varies match to match.

## The plan phase by phase

Written out in sequence, from the moment the opponent begins to build, the plan looks like this.

In the build-up phase, when the ball is in the opposition half and there is no immediate threat, your defensive line holds its mid-block height and your centre-backs are not thinking about the forward at all. This sounds obvious and is routinely violated. A centre-back who spends the whole build-up phase watching his man arrives at the moment of danger already mentally fatigued and, worse, has developed the habit of man-reference that will cost him the half-step later. The instruction in this phase is spatial: hold the line, hold the distance to your partner, and let the midfield screen manage the forward's movements between the lines.

In the progression phase, when the ball reaches the flank inside your half, the block shifts and the specific assignments switch on. The near-side full-back's job is to prevent the byline, which he does by defending on an angle that closes the outside rather than by backing off toward his own goal. The near-side centre-back picks up contact responsibility. The far-side centre-back drops half a yard deeper than his partner and takes the covering role. The near-side holding midfielder begins his drop to the rebound position. The far-side full-back narrows to the back post. This is the phase in which the match is actually decided, and it is the phase that gets the least training attention in almost every side, because it looks like transition rather than defending.

In the delivery phase, the clock has started and nothing new is decided. Everybody is set at the plant foot. The contact rule applies according to the delivery type in the grid. The goalkeeper's start position has been taken. If your side has done the progression phase properly, this phase requires no decisions at all, which is the point of it.

In the contest phase, which lasts under a second, the near-side centre-back attacks the flight of the ball if the delivery is aerial and holds the inside line if it is low. The covering centre-back does not join. The rebound defender does not join. The goalkeeper either claims or stays, according to the protocol below, and does not change his mind halfway, because a goalkeeper who commits and then retreats is the single most reliable source of a tap-in in football.

In the second phase, which begins the instant the first contest resolves either way, the rebound defender attacks the loose ball, the near-side centre-back spins goal-side rather than admiring his clearance, the block collapses for one second, and somebody clears the area rather than the ball. This phase is where the goals actually come from against a well-drilled side, and it is where you should spend a disproportionate share of your preparation, because everybody else spends none.

In the recovery phase, the block re-expands to its mid-block height rather than staying deep. A side that concedes territory permanently after every cross has converted one dangerous moment into a permanent siege, and the forward's threat multiplies with the number of deliveries he receives. Getting back out is a defensive action, not an attacking one, and it should be coached as such.

## Hold-up play, where the correct answer inverts

Everything above tells your defender to be tight, to establish contact early, and to be aggressive into the duel. There is one situation in which every one of those instructions is wrong, and getting it wrong is how sides that have defended the box competently for an hour concede a goal that starts thirty yards out.

When the forward receives with his back to goal outside the area, usually to feet, usually from a midfielder or a goalkeeper's distribution, the aggressive defender's instinct is to arrive with him, get tight, and press from behind. Against a forward with genuine hold-up ability, pressure from behind is not defending, it is a gift. He feels the contact, which tells him exactly where the defender is without needing to look. He uses the defender's momentum as a fixed point to rotate around. He either wins a foul in a dangerous area, rolls the defender and turns into space, or lays the ball off with the defender committed and out of the picture, which produces a three against two behind the ball.

The correct answer is delay and support. The defender takes a position roughly two yards off, side-on, showing the forward toward the touchline rather than toward the centre, and does not touch him. Two yards is far enough that the forward cannot feel where the defender is and has to look, and looking costs him time. Side-on means the defender is not committed and can move either way. Showing toward the touchline means that if the forward does turn, he turns into the least dangerous available space. And the absence of contact removes the fixed point he wants to rotate against.

The support half of the instruction is the part that gets forgotten. Delay is only useful if somebody arrives. The near-side midfielder's job is to collapse onto the forward the instant he turns, not before, which means the midfielder holds a position slightly behind and to the ball side rather than pressing early. Two defenders, one delaying and one arriving on the turn, beat one defender pressing from behind every time, and cost nothing structurally because the second defender was already there.

The reason this inversion exists is worth understanding rather than just memorising, because it generalises. Inside the box, time is the forward's weapon and space is irrelevant, so you take away time by being tight. Outside the box, space is the forward's weapon and time is on your side, because every second he holds the ball is a second your block spends getting organised. Tight defending inside the area and loose defending outside it are not inconsistent. They are the same principle, which is to deny whichever resource the situation makes valuable.

A side that gets this wrong in the other direction is also common. Defenders told to stand off will stand off inside the area too, and you will concede uncontested finishes from eight yards. State the boundary explicitly, and state it as a line on the pitch rather than as a judgment: inside the area, contact. Outside the area, no contact. The rule has to be that crude to survive contact with a real match.

## The aerial contest and the first-contact problem

Aerial defending against a forward who heads well while moving is a different discipline from aerial defending against a tall target man, and conflating them is a common preparation error.

Against a static target man in a set-piece duel, height and standing leap do most of the work, and the contest is largely decided by physical attributes you either have or do not. Against a forward attacking a moving delivery in open play, the contest is decided by momentum at the point of contact. The player moving forward into the flight of the ball wins it against a player who is stationary or backpedalling, almost regardless of the height difference, because the moving player generates upward force from a running approach while the stationary player is jumping from a standing start with no run-up and often with his body already turned.

That single fact reorganises the whole defensive job. The objective is not to out-jump him. It is to be the player moving forward at the moment of contact, and that is a positional decision made a second and a half earlier, not an athletic contest resolved in the air. A defender who starts two yards behind the point where the ball will arrive, and moves forward into it, wins. A defender who starts on the arrival point and has to hold it wins nothing, because the forward arrives with momentum and he does not.

The practical instruction, then, is to defend from behind the flight rather than under it. This feels wrong to defenders, who have been told their whole careers to get goal-side, and it requires a distinction: goal-side of the man, yes, but behind the ball's arrival point rather than on it. A defender who gets that right looks like he is giving up ground in the two seconds before contact and then arrives with force at the exact moment it matters.

The second half of the aerial problem is what happens to the header once it is won. A defensive header that goes straight up, or that drops inside the area, has not resolved anything, because the forward is already positioned for the drop and the defender is not. The instruction is direction over distance: head it wide toward the touchline rather than long down the middle, because a header out for a throw-in is a completed defensive action and a header dropping twenty yards out on the central axis is the start of the next attack with your block still recovering.

### Why does momentum beat height in an open-play aerial duel?

Because a player moving forward into the flight generates upward force from a running approach, while a stationary player jumps from a standing start with his body often already turned. The height difference between the two is usually smaller than the momentum difference, so arrival matters more than reach.

## The goalkeeper's half of the plan

A penalty-area defending plan that does not include the goalkeeper is half a plan, and it is the half most coaches skip, usually because the goalkeeper trains separately and the coordination never gets rehearsed with the back four present.

Start position is the first item, and it varies by delivery type rather than being a fixed habit. Against a deep cross toward the far post, the goalkeeper starts centrally and off his line, prepared to attack the six-yard box, because the deep cross gives him flight time and the far-post header is the delivery type a goalkeeper can genuinely intercept. Against a cutback, the opposite: he pins the near post, weight forward, hands low, because the cutback finish is struck low and hard and the goalkeeper's only chance is to have already narrowed the near-post option so the forward has to go across him. Against a low delivery into the six-yard box, he is on his line with his feet set, because there is nothing to claim and everything to react to, and a goalkeeper who steps out into that situation removes his own ability to react.

Claiming discipline is the second item and it needs a rule rather than a preference. The rule that works is that the goalkeeper claims only what he can take at full extension in front of his own defenders, and everything else belongs to the defenders. The failure mode you are protecting against is not the goalkeeper who does not come. It is the goalkeeper who comes halfway, because a halfway commitment removes the defender's ability to attack the ball and does not replace it with anything. Whatever your goalkeeper's preference, the decision must be binary and it must be made before the flight, not during it.

Communication is the third item, and it should be one word, used consistently, and audible. The specific word matters less than its consistency: a goalkeeper who uses three different calls across a match has effectively no calls, because his defenders are processing rather than reacting. The corresponding defender rule is that the absence of a call means the defender plays the ball, which puts the burden of communication on the goalkeeper where it belongs and removes the hesitation that causes most of the damage.

The fourth item is the second ball, and this is where the goalkeeper's job connects to the rest of the plan. After a save or a parry, the goalkeeper's next action determines whether the second phase is defensible. Parrying wide, toward the touchline, converts a rebound into a corner or a throw-in. Parrying centrally, back into the twelve-yard square, hands the forward the easiest finish available in football. This is a rehearsable technical habit and it should be rehearsed against this specific delivery pattern rather than in generic shot-stopping work.

The broader craft of beating and being beaten by elite goalkeepers belongs to the [guide on beating elite goalkeepers](/2024/08/01/beating-elite-goalkeepers-guide/), which owns that territory; what matters here is only the coordination between the goalkeeper and the four defenders in front of him.

## Set pieces and transitions: the two addenda

The plan above governs open play. Two adjacent situations need their own treatment because the rules change.

At attacking set pieces conceded by your side, the forward is a threat by a different mechanism. Corners and wide free kicks produce static duels in a crowded area, which removes the momentum advantage you were relying on in open play and returns some of the value to height and standing leap. The adjustment is that your aggressive contact defender is no longer the right man for this job, and the taller of your centre-backs takes him, with the smaller one taking a zonal position at the near post. Trying to keep the same man-marking assignment across open play and set pieces because it is simpler produces a mismatch in one of the two, and the set-piece mismatch is the one that costs goals in bunches.

The second set-piece adjustment is the short-corner and second-phase problem. A side that has drilled its corner defending will still concede if it treats the delivery as the end of the situation, and this forward's value on set pieces is often in the second and third contests rather than the first. Keep your rebound defender assignment alive on set pieces, and accept that this means one fewer body on the delivery.

At transitions, the picture inverts again. When you win the ball and are counter-attacking, the forward is not your problem; when you lose the ball while attacking, he is, and the danger is different from anything above. A counter-attack that finds him running onto a through ball with your defence stretched is a version of the duel you have not prepared for, because your entire plan assumes a set defensive block and a delivery from wide.

The protection is rest defence, which means the shape you hold while you are in possession. Against a side built around a penalty-box finisher, the rest-defence requirement is one centre-back holding the central axis at all times, never both stepping into midfield, and the near-side holding midfielder positioned to cover the first pass out rather than to support the attack. This costs you an attacking body and you should accept the cost consciously rather than discovering it after conceding.

The transition rule for the defender himself is the reverse of the box rule: when the forward is running onto a ball with space in front of him, the defender does not engage, does not commit, and does not dive in. He shepherds, delays, and waits for support, because a one-against-one against a finisher of this quality in open space is a duel with a bad expected outcome, and the alternative, which is a delayed attack with your block recovered, is a duel with a good one.

## When the first plan fails: the fallbacks

Plans get beaten. A plan without a stated fallback gets beaten and then abandoned, which is much worse, because a side improvising in the last half hour against an elite penalty-box forward is a side conceding two more.

The first fallback is the double-up inside the area, and it is the most commonly reached for and the most commonly mishandled. Putting a second defender on him inside the box works only if the second defender's job is explicitly the rebound rather than the same ball, and only if you have simultaneously accepted that one opposition player elsewhere in the area is now unmarked. Nominate who that unmarked player will be, rather than letting it be whoever happens to be furthest from the ball, and make it the opposition's least dangerous box arrival rather than their second striker.

The second fallback is upstream rather than downstream, and it is usually the better one. If the deliveries are arriving in a form you cannot defend, the answer is frequently not to add defenders in the box but to change what is happening on the flank. A more aggressive full-back position, a midfielder dropping to double the winger, or a switch from a back four to a back five for the specific purpose of preventing byline access, all reduce the supply of the one delivery type you are losing to. Attacking the supply is almost always cheaper than adding bodies to the destination.

The third fallback is the personnel change, and the trigger for it should be defined before kick-off. The failure signal is not a goal conceded, which is a lagging indicator. It is the repeated loss of the half-step, visible as your defender arriving late to three consecutive deliveries. When you see that three times, the defender is not going to recover it through effort, because the loss is usually a mismatch of movement speed in tight areas rather than a lapse in concentration, and telling him to concentrate harder will not add reaction time.

The fourth fallback is the structural retreat, which is dropping the line deeper and adding a body. This is the last resort rather than the first because it feeds the problem: a deeper block means more of the match is played inside your area, which is the environment where he is strongest. Use it only to protect a lead in the closing stages, and use it with the rebound assignment intact, because a deep block without a rebound defender is a queue of bodies waiting to watch a second-phase finish.

## What you concede on purpose

Every plan concedes something, and the plans that work concede on purpose rather than by accident. Against this forward profile, name these four concessions in the meeting so that nobody spends the match trying to prevent them.

You concede crosses from deep and from wide positions that are not the byline. Deep crosses are the delivery type your goalkeeper can attack and your defenders can meet with momentum. Do not send a player out to prevent them; you need him inside.

You concede the ball in front of the block. A side that is compact around its own area will let the opponent circulate at will thirty yards out, and this will look passive to anybody watching from the side. Circulation in front of a compact block produces very little, and every player you send out to contest it opens a lane into the area.

You concede corners. Clearing the area rather than the ball, parrying wide rather than centrally, and heading toward the touchline rather than down the middle all produce corners by design. A corner is a defensible situation with a rehearsed scheme; a second phase in your own six-yard box is not.

You concede the shot from distance, mostly. A defender stepping out of the block to close a shot from twenty-five yards is a defender who has just vacated the space the cutback is aimed at. There are opponents against whom this trade is wrong, and this profile of forward is not one of them, because his threat is concentrated close to goal rather than from range.

What you do not concede, ever, is the near-post space in front of him, the uncontested second ball, and the byline. Those three are the plan. Everything else is negotiable.

## How the plan degrades with a weaker squad

Almost everything written about defending elite forwards assumes a back four of professionals who have trained together for years. Most of the people who need this plan are coaching a school side, a Sunday league team, or an academy age group, and they are facing the local version of this forward: the one who scores twenty goals a year from six yards and whom nobody in the league has worked out. The plan degrades in a specific order, and knowing that order tells you what to keep when you cannot keep everything.

The first thing to drop is the delivery-specific goalkeeper start positions. They are correct and they are unteachable in two sessions a week to a goalkeeper who is fifteen. Replace all six rows with one instruction: stay on your line unless you are certain, and parry wide. That single simplification retains most of the value and removes almost all of the risk.

The second thing to drop is the aggressive contact rule. Contact discipline inside the penalty area is difficult, and a young or inexperienced defender told to establish forearm contact will either foul or wrestle, and both are worse than not touching him at all. Replace it with position only: be goal-side, be on the inside shoulder, be closer to the near post than he is. Position without contact beats contact without position at every level, and it beats a penalty at all of them.

The third thing to drop is the pairing distinction between the aggressive defender and the covering defender, which requires the two of them to read each other. Replace it with a fixed rule: the nearer centre-back goes, the further one drops. It is cruder and it will occasionally be wrong, and it will be right often enough to be worth more than a subtle rule nobody executes.

What you keep, in every version, is the rebound defender. If you take one idea from this entire plan into a team that trains once a week, take that one. Name a player, tell him his only job when the ball comes into the box is to stand at the top of the six-yard box and attack anything loose, and tell him you do not care about anything else he does. It costs nothing, it requires no technical ability, and it removes the largest single source of goals against sides that defend the first contest adequately.

What you also keep is the crosser reference point, because it is free. Telling a defender to be set when the winger's head goes down costs no fitness, no technique, and no tactical sophistication. It is simply a better instruction than the one he is currently getting, which is to watch his man.

For the youth coach specifically, there are two situations worth teaching at each age and only two. For younger age groups, teach goal-side positioning on the inside shoulder and the habit of turning to face the drop zone after a clearance. For older ones, add the crosser reference point and the rebound assignment. Everything else in this article belongs to a training environment that a youth coach does not have, and attempting it produces confusion rather than defending.

The session work that installs any of this belongs properly to [training a team to contain a superstar](/2020/10/15/training-to-contain-a-superstar/), which owns the drill design, the constraints, and the progressions. What matters here is the order of degradation rather than the exercises.

## The argument that he was carried by his service

The most persistent counter-reading of this forward profile is that the goals are a function of the teams he played in, that a box occupier is a beneficiary rather than a creator, and that placed in a poorer side he would be ordinary. The argument deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed, both because it contains something true and because dismissing it makes the analysis weaker.

What is true is that a forward whose value is concentrated inside the penalty area is more dependent on delivery volume than a forward who creates his own chances from wide or from deep. That is not a slight; it is a structural fact about the role. A dribbler generates shots from nothing. A box occupier converts entries into shots at an exceptional rate but does not manufacture the entries himself. Put him in a side that cannot get the ball into the final third and his output falls further than a creator's would. This is simply what specialisation means, and it applies to every player who has ever specialised.

What is false is the inference. The service argument treats the delivery as the cause and the finish as the consequence, and inside the penalty area the causal arrow frequently runs the other way. A cross is only a chance if somebody has already created separation to meet it, and the separation is created by the forward before the crosser has decided where to put it. Watch the sequence rather than the still image and you see it repeatedly: the crosser's decision about where to deliver is made in response to the movement, not the other way round. The forward is not receiving service; he is generating a target and instructing the delivery by where he puts himself.

There is a defensive test that settles this more cleanly than any argument about quality of teammates, and it is the test this whole article implies. If the service were doing the work, then defending the service would be sufficient, and the plan against this forward would be a plan against wingers. Every coach who has actually prepared for this knows it is not, because sides that successfully reduce the volume of deliveries still concede from the two or three that get through, at a rate that sides facing an ordinary forward do not. The finishing conversion inside the box is not a passenger on the delivery quality; it is a separate variable, and it is the one that decides matches.

The honest verdict is a split one. Yes, the role is service-dependent in the sense that output scales with entries, and a version of this forward in a side with poor final-third supply produces fewer goals. No, the role is not passive, and the claim that the movement is incidental to the goals does not survive contact with the actual sequences. The correct conclusion for a defensive coach is the practically useful one: reducing the service is a necessary part of the plan and it is not a sufficient one, which is exactly why this article spends most of its length on what happens after the delivery arrives rather than on how to prevent it.

The mirror-image question, which is what a forward should be doing to attack a well-organised centre-back pairing, belongs to the [guide on attacking elite centre-backs](/2023/10/01/attacking-elite-centre-backs-guide/), and it is worth reading alongside this one, because the two articles describe the same duel from opposite ends and the disagreements between them are where the interesting coaching lives.

## Which version of the forward is on the pitch

A long career is an analytical fact rather than a biographical one, and it matters to the plan. A forward who plays at the top level across many years does not present the same threat profile throughout, and preparing for the wrong version is a real and common error, because the version most coaches carry in their heads is the one from the highlight compilations rather than the one they are about to face.

The earlier version of a box occupier of this type typically carries more mobility across the full width of the attacking third, more running into the channels, more pressing from the front, and a greater proportion of goals that begin with a run rather than with a position. Against that version, the plan needs more emphasis on the defensive line's depth management and on the full-backs' inside shoulders, because the threat is partly a running threat and you cannot defend a running threat with a set block alone.

The later version compresses. The running reduces, the positional intelligence increases, and the goals concentrate further inside the area and closer to goal. Against that version, the depth management matters less and the twelve-yard square matters more, which means the plan in this article applies more purely and the line can sit slightly higher without much risk. The trade is that the box duel gets harder rather than easier, because everything the forward has stopped spending on running is now being spent on the two-yard movements that buy the half-step, and the accumulated pattern recognition of many years of doing it makes those movements earlier and cleaner.

State this neutrally in your preparation, because there is a lazy version of this observation that will lose you the match. The lazy version says the threat has diminished and can be handled with less attention. The accurate version says the threat has relocated, from a wider area of the pitch into a smaller one, and that a plan calibrated for the earlier version leaves your centre-backs prepared for runs that are not coming and unprepared for the duel that is. Athletic change is a tactical input, not a verdict on a player, and treating it as the latter produces both bad manners and bad defending.

The practical instruction for the analyst is to establish which version you are facing from the footage rather than from reputation, and the marker is simple: count the receptions outside the penalty area across two matches. A high count means the earlier profile and a depth-management emphasis. A low count means the later profile and a box-duel emphasis. It takes twenty minutes and it will change your line height by five yards, which is worth more than most of the rest of the analysis.

## The contrast that clarifies the plan

The fastest way to understand why this plan looks the way it does is to set it against the plan for a forward whose threat sits in the opposite place. The counter-plan for [stopping Harry Kane](/2020/03/15/how-to-stop-harry-kane/) is built around a question that barely arises here, which is whether the centre-back follows the forward out of the defensive line when he drops toward midfield. That is a midfield problem disguised as a striker problem, and its resolution involves screening midfielders, vacancy management, and a hand-off protocol between lines.

Against a box occupier, that question mostly does not exist, and the absence is the point. He is not going to drag your centre-back thirty yards up the pitch, which means your line stays intact, which means your structural problems are smaller and your duel problems are larger. Where the drop-and-link forward turns your defence into a coordination puzzle across three lines, the box occupier turns it into a series of tiny physical contests in a confined space, most of which are decided before anybody touches the ball.

This is why generic advice about defending elite strikers is close to useless. The two forwards are the same nominal position, similar reputations, and comparable output, and the correct plans against them share almost nothing. One asks your midfield a question. The other asks your centre-backs a question. A side that prepares for the wrong one will look organised right up until the moment it concedes, and the coaching staff will spend the following week reviewing the wrong sixty yards of pitch.

The generalised version of that distinction, covering the full range of forward types and which structural answer each requires, sits in the cluster hub rather than here, and a coach preparing for an opponent he has not yet identified should start there rather than with any single counter-plan.

## The double move, and the only way to defend it

One movement pattern deserves separate treatment because it beats good defenders more often than anything else in the box, and because the standard coaching response to it makes it worse.

The pattern is a committed movement in one direction followed by an immediate check back into the space the defender's response has created. It works because it exploits a defender doing exactly what he has been told to do, which is stay tight. The tighter the defender, the more completely he is beaten by it, because tightness requires him to mirror the movement and mirroring requires him to shift his weight. Once the weight has shifted, the check back is unanswerable, and the half-step is bought.

The response most coaches reach for is to tell the defender not to be beaten by it, which is not an instruction. The response some reach for is to tell him to stand off, which converts a half-step of separation into two yards and makes things worse.

The only answer that works is to change what the defender is mirroring. Instead of mirroring the movement, he holds his position relative to the ball's arrival point and maintains contact with the man, which means the forward's movement stretches the contact rather than moving the defender. He gives ground only when the movement takes the forward away from goal, and never when it takes him across. In practice this looks like a defender who is being pulled around slightly, who appears to be losing his man for a moment, and who is in fact in exactly the right place when the delivery arrives, with the forward having spent his preparation on a defender who never went anywhere.

The instruction that installs this is short enough to survive a match: hold your ground on the first movement, travel on the second, and never let him cross your body. Three clauses, no judgment calls, and it is the closest thing this article has to a single sentence that would improve most defences immediately.

## The failure signals to watch for in the match

A plan you cannot evaluate during the match is a plan you can only evaluate afterwards, which is too late. Four signals tell you the plan is failing before the scoreline does.

The first is repeated byline access. If the opposition is reaching the byline on the same flank more than twice in a quarter of an hour, the cutback is coming and no amount of box organisation will hold indefinitely. This is a full-back and midfield problem and it is fixed upstream, not in the area.

The second is your defender arriving after the delivery. Watch three consecutive crosses and ask whether he is set at the crosser's plant foot. If he is not, the half-step is being conceded systematically and the goal is a matter of time. This is the signal that triggers the personnel change, and it should trigger it early rather than after evidence has accumulated in the form of a goal.

The third is the uncontested second ball. If a loose ball in your area is being reached by an opponent first, your rebound assignment is not being executed, usually because the assigned player is tracking a runner. Restate the assignment from the touchline in four words, because it is fixable within a minute and it is the highest-value correction available to you during a match.

The fourth is your block failing to re-expand. If your defensive line is sitting on the edge of its own area for long stretches rather than recovering to mid-block height between attacks, you have accidentally converted a mid-block plan into a siege, and the number of deliveries you face will now rise steadily until one of them lands correctly. This is a fitness and habit problem as much as a tactical one, and the fix during a match is a substitution rather than an instruction.

Tracking those four signals through a match, and recording which of them appeared and when, is precisely the kind of preparation that is worth keeping between fixtures rather than rebuilding from scratch. ## The movement patterns an analyst should tag

Preparing this plan properly requires footage, and the amount of footage most coaches actually have time to watch is roughly two matches. That constraint is worth accepting rather than resenting, because two matches watched with a specific list of things to find is worth more than six watched generally.

Tag the preparatory movement rather than the finish. The finishes are what the highlight package contains and they tell you nothing you can use, because the finish is the outcome of a duel already lost. What you want is the two seconds before each finish, and specifically the direction of the first movement. Most box occupiers have a dominant preparatory direction, usually toward the near post, and a secondary one they use when the marker over-anticipates. Knowing which is dominant against which type of delivery tells your centre-back where to hold his ground.

Tag the receptions outside the area, as described above, because the count establishes which version of the threat profile you are facing and therefore your line height.

Tag the second-phase involvements separately from the first-phase ones, and count them. This is the metric almost nobody produces and it is the one that most directly justifies the assignment you are about to make. If a meaningful share of the forward's box involvements are rebounds, blocked shots, and scrambles rather than clean first contacts, your rebound defender is not an optional refinement, it is the primary intervention, and you can tell your players so with evidence rather than assertion.

Tag the body orientation at the moment of contact for aerial deliveries, because it tells you whether the aerial threat you are facing is the momentum version or the standing version, and those need different defenders.

Do not tag anything to do with pressing, work rate, or defensive contribution. It is interesting and it is irrelevant to the duel you are preparing for, and analyst time spent on it is analyst time not spent on the two seconds that decide the fixture.

The dossier that comes out of this should fit on one page. Six rows of grid, four tagged patterns, two assignments, one goalkeeper protocol. If it runs longer than that, it will not reach the pitch, and analysis that does not reach the pitch is a hobby rather than preparation.

## Defending him in a front two, and as a lone striker

The same forward presents a materially different problem depending on how many attackers he shares the penalty area with, and the plan needs a version for each.

As a lone striker, he is the only permanent occupant of your box, which means your two centre-backs face one attacker for most of the match and you have a spare defender by default. This sounds comfortable and produces a specific failure: the spare centre-back has no assignment, drifts, and ends up either doubling the contest or ball-watching. Give him the rebound job explicitly, which frees your holding midfielder to stay higher and gives you a better rest-defence position when you win the ball. Against a lone striker, the plan is at its most economical and your block can sit slightly higher, because there is nobody making the second run that punishes a higher line.

In a front two, or with an arriving midfielder from deep, the arithmetic changes and the temptation is to man-mark both. Resist it. The moment both centre-backs are attached to a man, nobody owns the space, and a forward of this quality will use the second attacker as a screen deliberately, arranging contact between his partner and your defender so that his own marker cannot follow him across. The correct adjustment is to keep your centre-back pairing zonal with man responsibility, and to give the second attacker to the near-side holding midfielder while pulling the far-side midfielder into the rebound job. You have effectively swapped which midfielder does which task rather than adding anybody.

The variant that causes the most damage is the one where a wide forward occupies the far post while the box occupier attacks the near. That combination is designed to make your far-side full-back choose, and the answer is that he does not choose: he holds the back-post line and lets the far-side centre-back deal with the near-post overload. Full-backs who step in to help on the near post are the most common source of the completely unmarked far-post header, and it happens because helping feels active and holding a post feels passive. Tell them it is not passive, it is the assignment, and hold them to it in review.

## Contact, fouls, and the penalty risk you are taking on

A plan that tells defenders to establish physical contact inside their own penalty area has to be honest about what it is exposing them to, because a coach who issues the instruction and then criticises the defender for the resulting penalty has made his side worse in two ways at once.

The distinction that keeps contact legal is between contact that establishes distance and contact that alters the opponent. An arm bent at the elbow, held against the opponent's back or shoulder, maintaining a fixed gap while both players move, is a positional tool and is officiated as such almost everywhere. A straight arm that extends, a hand that grips shirt fabric, a shoulder driven into a player who is off the ground, and any contact initiated after the ball has passed are alterations, and they are the ones that get punished. The difference is not subtle to a defender who has been shown it, and it is invisible to one who has only been told to be aggressive.

The timing rule matters as much as the technique. Contact established before the delivery arrives is rarely penalised, because the officiating frame of reference is the moment of the ball's arrival and a contest already in progress reads as a duel. Contact initiated at the moment of the ball's arrival reads as an intervention and gets looked at. Contact initiated after the forward has begun his movement toward the ball reads as a foul and usually is one. Early is legal, late is not, which conveniently matches everything else in this plan: the defender who is set at the crosser's plant foot is also the defender least likely to concede a penalty, because he had no reason to reach.

The place where the risk is genuinely high is the scramble, and the grid handles it by telling the defender to clear the area rather than the ball. A defender who commits to a legal clearance out of the twelve-yard square has an action he can complete. A defender trying to be precise in a crowd, off balance, with a forward leaning on him, ends up reaching, and reaching in your own penalty area in a crowd is the single most common source of the penalties nobody understands afterwards.

The last piece is the review, and it is a coaching-culture point rather than a technical one. If you punish your defenders for the fouls that come from executing the plan while saying nothing about the goals that come from not executing it, you will get a back four that stops making contact within three matches, and you will have taken away the mechanism the plan depends on. Review the timing, not the outcome. A defender who established contact early and was punished did the right thing badly. A defender who reached late and got away with it did the wrong thing luckily, and he is the one who needs the conversation.

## The near-post geometry, in detail

One piece of the grid is worth expanding because it recurs in four of the six rows and because most defenders execute it by feel rather than by rule.

The instruction "be closer to the near post than he is" sounds like a positional preference. It is actually a geometric constraint that determines which finishes remain available to the forward, and once you see it that way the whole duel simplifies. A forward receiving inside the area has, in principle, the near-post finish, the far-post finish, and the finish straight through the goalkeeper. The near-post finish is the easiest of the three, because it requires the least accuracy, travels the shortest distance, and arrives before the goalkeeper can transfer his weight. It is also the one that is eliminated entirely by a single positional decision made before the delivery.

If the defender occupies the line between the forward and the near post, the near-post finish now has to pass through a body, which means it is not a finish, it is a block. What remains is a shot across the goalkeeper toward the far post, which is a longer, more accurate, slower-arriving shot that the goalkeeper has been set for since the delivery type was identified. You have not stopped him shooting. You have reduced three options to one, and you have chosen the one your goalkeeper is best placed to handle.

This is why the instruction is never "get between him and the goal", which is vague and which produces a defender standing square in front of the forward, blocking nothing in particular and unable to move either way. It is always "get between him and the near post", which is specific, which produces a defender on an angle, and which leaves the defender able to close the far-post line as the shot is struck.

The corollary matters for your goalkeeper. If your defenders are executing the near-post rule consistently, your goalkeeper can commit further toward the near post in his start position than he otherwise would, which shortens his distance to the one shot that remains. If your defenders are inconsistent, he cannot, and he ends up covering both options badly. Goalkeeper positioning and defender positioning are one system rather than two, and rehearsing them separately is how you end up with a goalkeeper who is technically excellent and still beaten at his near post every other week.

## When you have to chase the match

Everything above assumes you are able to hold a mid-block and defend as designed. Sometimes you are a goal down with twenty minutes left, and the plan has to survive the fact that you now need the ball.

The trade you are making is explicit: pushing your line up and committing bodies forward increases your chance of scoring and increases the number of situations in which your centre-backs face this forward in transition rather than in a set block. That second condition is the worst version of the duel for you, because everything in this plan depends on being organised before the delivery arrives, and a transition by definition means you are not.

The mitigation is not to abandon the plan but to change which part of it is load-bearing. In a set block, the load-bearing element is the box assignment. In a chasing phase, it becomes the rest defence, which means one centre-back holds the central axis permanently and does not step, the holding midfielder positions himself to cover the first pass out rather than to support the attack, and the far-side full-back stays home while the near-side one goes. You are accepting a narrower attack in exchange for keeping a defensive spine that can delay a counter long enough for the rest of the block to recover.

The individual instruction changes too. In transition, with space in front of him, your centre-back does not engage this forward, does not commit, and does not dive in. He shepherds toward the touchline, delays, and waits for the second defender who is arriving because you kept the spine intact. A one-against-one against a finisher of this quality in open space has a bad expected outcome and a delayed attack against a recovered block has a good one, and the difference between those two outcomes is entirely a decision your defender makes in the first second.

Say this in the meeting before the fixture rather than shouting it from the touchline in the eighty-first minute, because a defender who has been told all week to be aggressive into contact will be aggressive into contact in exactly the moment you need him not to be.

## The arguments you will hear, and what each is worth

Four positions circulate whenever this forward profile comes up, in coaching conversations, on forums, and in match threads. Each contains something, and each leads a coach astray if accepted whole, so each gets a verdict.

The first is that a poacher is easier to defend than a complete forward, on the grounds that his threat is narrower and a narrower threat is simpler to prepare for. The premise is correct and the conclusion does not follow. A narrow threat is easier to locate and harder to prevent, because all of the opponent's specialisation is concentrated in the one situation you cannot avoid conceding. A complete forward gives you more problems and each of them is more dilutable; you can push him wide, deny him the turn, or make him beat a man. A box occupier gives you one problem you must solve, in a space you cannot deny him, in under a second, against a player who has spent a career specialising in exactly that instant. Easier to plan for, harder to beat. The wider version of this decision, and which archetype a coach should actually want in his own side, belongs to the head-to-head on the [poacher and the complete forward](/2021/03/15/poacher-vs-complete-forward/) rather than to this counter-plan.

The second is that the aerial threat is overstated, usually argued from the observation that the forward in question is not among the tallest in the position. This one is simply a category error, and the section on first contact above explains why: open-play aerial contests on moving deliveries are won by momentum, and standing height is a much weaker predictor than the coaching folklore assumes. The correct version of the observation is narrower and worth keeping: on static set-piece duels, where the momentum advantage disappears, the aerial threat genuinely is smaller than the open-play record suggests, which is why the set-piece assignment in this plan differs from the open-play one.

The third is that his age has removed the threat, in whichever form the person making the argument means it. The section above gives the accurate version, which is that the threat relocates rather than disappearing, and that the relocation makes the box duel harder rather than easier while making the running threat smaller. A coach who plans on the basis of diminishment prepares a high line and a set of instructions about depth, and then loses to a forward standing eight yards from goal doing the thing he has always done. Plan for the relocation, not for the decline.

The fourth is that man-marking inside the box is outdated, which comes from the broadly correct observation that zonal schemes handle set pieces better than pure man-marking does. Extended into open play, it becomes wrong. Pure man-marking gives the forward control of your defensive shape, because he can drag his marker into positions that suit him and open the spaces his teammates want. Pure zonal marking concedes the half-step by design, because in a zonal scheme nobody owns the contact and the forward can position himself in the seam between two zones with nobody attached to him. The working answer is neither: zonal structure with man responsibility inside the zone, plus a named hand-off word when he crosses a boundary. That is not a compromise between two schemes, it is a third scheme, and it is what nearly every well-organised defence actually plays whatever it calls itself.

A fifth position deserves a mention because it is the one that does the most damage inside a coaching staff: that a forward of this quality cannot be planned for at all, and that the only realistic approach is to score more than him. This is defeatism dressed as realism, and it is contradicted by the evidence available to anyone who watches enough matches. Sides do keep elite penalty-box forwards quiet, regularly, and when you examine what they did it is never mysterious. They prevented byline access, they were set at the plant foot, they had a rebound defender, they kept the goalkeeper's decisions binary, and they cleared the area rather than the ball. None of that requires better players than you have. It requires the assignments to exist.

## Delivering this to your players

A plan that stays in the analyst's document is worth nothing, and the conversion from analysis to instruction is where most preparation dies. Three rules make this particular plan survive the journey.

Give each player only his own row. The grid above is a coaching tool and an analyst's tool, and handing all six rows to a centre-back three days before a fixture guarantees that he executes none of them. He needs two: the delivery type this opponent produces most, and the one he is personally worst against. The goalkeeper needs his four start positions and nothing else. The rebound defender needs one sentence.

Phrase every instruction as a trigger and an action rather than as a description. "He is very good at creating separation before the cross" is a description and changes nothing. "When the winger's head goes down, you are already set on his inside shoulder" is a trigger and an action, and it changes behaviour. Every line of a briefing should have the shape of when this happens, you do this.

Rehearse the largest change and only the largest change. If your side has never had a designated rebound defender, that is the change, and it deserves the session time. Attempting to install contact rules, orientation discipline, goalkeeper start positions, and second-ball assignments in the same week produces a side that half-knows five things, which defends worse than a side that fully knows one.

Then check that the plan arrived, which is a separate step from checking that it was correct. Ask a defender, on the training pitch, what he does when the winger's head goes down. If the answer takes more than three seconds, the instruction has not arrived and no amount of correctness in the analysis will save it.

## The verdict

The plan against a forward whose game is built inside the penalty area is not a marking plan and it is not a matter of finding a big enough centre-back. It is a penalty-area defending plan, and it is decided in four places, only one of which is inside the twelve-yard square.

It is decided on the flank, where preventing byline access removes the delivery type you cannot defend. It is decided in the second before the cross, where a defender who is set at the crosser's plant foot has already denied the half-step that the entire threat depends on. It is decided in the assignment of the second ball, which most sides never make and which accounts for a large share of the goals conceded by sides that defend the first contest competently. And it is decided in the goalkeeper's protocol, where a binary decision taken before the flight beats a good decision taken during it.

The half-step economy is the idea to keep. Everything the forward does inside the area is designed to buy a half-step of separation before the delivery arrives, and every defensive instruction that is worth giving is one that makes that half-step more expensive. Instructions that are about effort, concentration, or desire do not make it more expensive and should be cut from your briefing, not because they are unkind but because they are inert.

What this plan will not do is keep him quiet for ninety minutes every time. He is one of the most accomplished penalty-area finishers the position has produced, and a plan that promises to eliminate him is lying to your players in a way they will notice the first time he scores. What it will do is reduce the number of situations in which he receives with a half-step and a set goalkeeper from perhaps six in a match to perhaps two, and convert most of the remaining ones into contested finishes rather than clean ones. That is the honest offer, and across a season it is the difference between a defence that concedes to this profile of forward routinely and one that does not.

The last thing to say is the thing a coach should say to his centre-backs on the way out of the meeting. The duel you are about to have will be decided in moments nobody in the stand will see, and if you win it, nobody will notice. The forward will be described afterwards as quiet. That is the whole objective, and it is a strange kind of job, because the evidence of doing it well is an absence. Do it anyway, and do it on the eight deliveries that arrive rather than on the one that gets replayed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Robert Lewandowski?

By defending the penalty area rather than the man. Prevent byline access on the flanks so the cutback, the delivery you cannot defend, arrives rarely. Have your near-side centre-back set and goal-side at the instant the crosser's head goes down, on the inside shoulder, closer to the near post than the forward is. Name a rebound defender who stands at the top of the six-yard box and attacks anything loose, and do not give him any tracking responsibility. Agree a binary goalkeeper protocol so he either claims or stays and never commits halfway. Then accept what the plan concedes, which is deep crosses, circulation in front of your block, corners, and shots from distance. The plan does not eliminate him. It reduces the number of clean receptions he gets in a match and converts most of the rest into contested finishes, which is the realistic objective.

### Q: What is Lewandowski's biggest weakness?

The honest framing is that his game is specialised rather than flawed, and specialisation carries a structural cost. His decisive actions are concentrated inside the penalty area, which means his output scales with the volume of entries his side generates. A team that cannot reach the final third reliably will get less from him than it would from a forward who manufactures chances from deeper or wider positions. The second limitation follows from the first: in a match where the deliveries are prevented at source, through a full-back who denies the byline and a midfield that blocks the entry passes, there are fewer situations in which his strongest quality applies at all. That is a limitation relative to elite peers under specific conditions rather than a deficiency, and it is exactly the condition a counter-plan is designed to create.

### Q: How do you defend Lewandowski inside the penalty area?

With a rule per situation rather than one general instruction, because the correct behaviour changes completely with the delivery type. Against a deep cross, be goal-side at touching distance, half-turned with hips open, and attack the flight of the ball. Against a cutback, do the opposite: sit two yards off, stay closer to the near post than he is, make no contact until his first touch, and close the shooting line rather than sliding. Against a low delivery into the six-yard box, keep contact through the delivery and never reach around him. In every case, be set at the moment the crosser plants rather than tracking the forward's movement, and after the first contest resolves, turn to face the drop zone rather than watching your own clearance.

### Q: Does Lewandowski need good service to be dangerous?

Partly, and the part that is true is less useful than it sounds. A forward whose value is concentrated in the box converts entries at an exceptional rate but does not manufacture the entries himself, so output does scale with supply. What the argument gets wrong is the direction of causation inside the area. The separation is created before the crosser decides where to deliver, and the delivery is frequently a response to the movement rather than the cause of the chance. The defensive test settles it: if service were doing the work, reducing the service would be sufficient, and every coach who has prepared for this knows that sides which cut the volume still concede from the two or three deliveries that get through. Reduce the supply, certainly. Do not mistake that for the whole plan.

### Q: How do you stop Lewandowski's first time finishing?

Understand what first-time finishing actually removes, which is the goalkeeper's set time. A goalkeeper given three quarters of a second sets his feet and covers a large share of the goal; a goalkeeper still moving at the moment of contact covers a fraction of it. That means the defensive objective is not to block the shot, which is usually impossible at that range, but to add time and remove the angle before the ball arrives. You add time by making him take a controlling touch, which happens when a defender's body is close enough to disturb the reception. You remove the angle by being closer to the near post than he is, so the only available finish is the harder one across the goalkeeper, which is the one your goalkeeper has been positioned for.

### Q: Should you mark Lewandowski man to man?

Not purely, in either direction. Pure man-marking gives him control of your defensive shape, because he can relocate your centre-back wherever he wants and open the space his teammates are running into. Pure zonal marking concedes separation by design, since nobody owns the physical contact and he can stand in the seam between two zones untouched. The scheme that works is zonal structure with man responsibility inside the zone, plus a named hand-off word used consistently when he crosses a boundary between two defenders. The hand-off is the part that fails in practice, usually because the two defenders use different words or one of them assumes the other has taken responsibility. Agree the word, rehearse the boundary, and review the hand-offs rather than the goals.

### Q: How do you defend Lewandowski in the air?

By treating it as a momentum contest instead of a height contest. On a moving delivery in open play, the player arriving forward into the flight wins the ball against a player who is stationary or backpedalling, largely independent of the height difference between them, because one is generating force from a running approach and the other is jumping from a standing start. So start two yards behind the arrival point rather than on it, and move into the contact. Then manage the outcome: a defensive header should go wide toward the touchline rather than long down the central axis, because a header that drops twenty yards out with your block still recovering has resolved nothing. On static set-piece duels the calculation shifts back toward height, which is why the set-piece assignment should differ from the open-play one.

### Q: Does Lewandowski's age change how you defend him?

It changes where the threat lives rather than how large it is, and planning for diminishment is the error that loses matches. The earlier profile carries more running into the channels and more pressing from the front, which means the plan needs emphasis on line depth and on the full-backs' inside shoulders. The later profile concentrates further inside the area, with the two-yard movements becoming earlier and cleaner because pattern recognition has accumulated. Against that version the depth management matters less and the box duel matters more, so the line can sit slightly higher while the penalty-area work gets harder. Establish which version you are facing from footage rather than reputation by counting receptions outside the area across two matches, and set your line height from that count.

### Q: What shape limits Lewandowski's movement in the box?

A mid-block that defends the edge of its own penalty area, with horizontal compactness prioritised over width across the pitch, and the near-side full-back positioned to prevent byline access rather than to retreat toward his own goal. Five bodies in and around the area works better than seven: a back four plus the near-side holding midfielder as rebound defender, with the far-side midfielder holding the top of the area. More bodies than that gives him screens to use, obstructs your own goalkeeper's sight of the flight, and leaves you with nobody to counter with. The shape matters considerably less than the assignments inside it, and a coach with limited training time should spend it on the assignments, because the shape is already familiar to his players and the assignments almost certainly are not.

### Q: How do you stop Lewandowski when he holds the ball up?

By inverting every instruction that applies inside the box. Against a back-to-goal reception outside the area, pressure from behind is counterproductive: it tells him exactly where you are without him having to look, gives him a fixed point to rotate against, and invites either a foul in a dangerous area or a lay-off with you committed and out of the picture. Instead, take a position around two yards off, side-on, showing him toward the touchline rather than the centre, and do not make contact. Then support it, because delay is only useful if somebody arrives. The near-side midfielder collapses onto him at the moment he turns rather than before. State the boundary crudely so it survives a real match: inside the area, contact; outside it, none.

### Q: Which centre-back type troubles Lewandowski most?

Not the one most coaches would pick. Recovery pace is close to irrelevant in a duel decided inside a twelve-yard square, and standing height matters much less than the folklore suggests on moving deliveries. The attributes that carry the load are short-area strength through contact from a low base with active feet, orientation discipline meaning the habit of keeping the ball and the man in one field of view by half-turning rather than swivelling the head, and second-event willingness, which is the reflex of treating a won first contest as the start of the duel. In many squads that describes the smaller of the two centre-backs rather than the bigger one. Pair him with a calmer, spatially disciplined partner who covers rather than joins, and state which of them goes on each delivery type.

### Q: Is Lewandowski still a threat from outside the box?

Less than inside it, and the plan should be built on that asymmetry rather than ignoring it. A defender who steps out of the block to close a shot from twenty-five yards has just vacated the space a cutback is aimed at, and against this profile of forward that trade is a bad one. There are opponents against whom stepping out is correct, and a forward whose finishing volume is concentrated close to goal is not among them. The genuine outside-the-box threat is different in kind: it is the back-to-goal reception that becomes a lay-off, a turn, or a foul won in a dangerous area, and it is defended by delay and support rather than by closing the shot. Hold your shape, concede the distance shot, and defend the entries.

### Q: How does a goalkeeper prepare for Lewandowski's finishing?

By fixing his start position to the delivery type instead of defaulting to one habit, and by making his decisions binary. Against a deep far-post cross he starts centrally and off his line, ready to attack the six-yard box, since he has flight time and that is the one delivery he can genuinely intercept. Against a cutback he pins the near post with weight forward and hands low, forcing the finish across him. Against a low ball into the six-yard box he stays on his line with feet set, because there is nothing to claim and everything to react to. The claiming rule is that he takes only what he can reach at full extension in front of his defenders. The most damaging failure is not staying, it is committing halfway. Then parry wide, never centrally.

### Q: What do sides that kept Lewandowski quiet actually do?

Nothing mysterious, which is the encouraging part. Look at the matches where an elite penalty-box finisher had a quiet afternoon and the same five things recur. The opposition rarely reached the byline, so the cutback almost never arrived. The marking centre-back was set at the moment the crosser planted rather than tracking movement. Somebody was standing at the top of the six-yard box with no job other than the second ball. The goalkeeper made early binary decisions and parried wide rather than back into the danger area. And defenders cleared the area rather than trying to clear the ball cleanly in scrambles, accepting corners as the price. None of that requires better players than most sides have. It requires the assignments to exist and to be rehearsed, which is a coaching decision rather than a recruitment one.

### Q: How should an amateur side defend a poacher like Lewandowski?

Keep two things and drop the rest, in a specific order. Drop the delivery-specific goalkeeper start positions and replace them with one instruction: stay on your line unless certain, and parry wide. Drop the aggressive contact rules, because an inexperienced defender told to establish forearm contact will foul or wrestle, and replace them with position only, meaning goal-side, inside shoulder, closer to the near post than he is. Drop the subtle pairing logic and replace it with the crude version: nearer centre-back goes, further one drops. What you keep is the rebound defender, which costs nothing and removes the largest single source of goals against sides that defend the first contest adequately, and the crosser reference point, which is simply a better instruction than telling a defender to watch his man.

### Q: What do scouts look for when analysing Lewandowski's movement?

The two seconds before the finish rather than the finish itself, because the finish is the outcome of a duel already decided. Tag the direction of the preparatory movement and identify which direction is dominant against which delivery type, since that tells the centre-back where to hold his ground. Count receptions outside the penalty area across two matches to establish which version of the threat profile is in front of you, which sets your line height. Count second-phase involvements separately from clean first contacts, because a high proportion of rebounds and scrambles makes the rebound assignment your primary intervention rather than a refinement. Note body orientation at the moment of aerial contact to distinguish the momentum threat from the standing one. Ignore pressing and defensive contribution entirely; it is interesting and it does not affect this duel.
