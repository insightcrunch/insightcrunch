Preventive detention occupies a strange and uneasy place in Indian law because it authorises the State to take a person into custody without charge, without trial and without conviction, on the strength of what that person might do rather than on proof of what that person has done. The National Security Act 1980 is the standing central statute through which that power is exercised for reasons of national security, public order and the maintenance of essential supplies and services, and its everyday operation raises a question that is more practical than abstract. When a detention order under this Act is challenged before a court, what decides whether the order stands or falls? The answer, worked out across decades of litigation under this Act and under the detention statutes that came before it, is rarely the strength of the suspicion that prompted the order. Orders survive or collapse on matters that look, at first glance, like administration. A ground communicated late, a document relied upon but never supplied, a representation left undecided while weeks pass, an approval that arrives after its deadline, a report that reaches the government after the Board was required to send it. The machinery of dates and papers is not decoration around the real law of the Act. In practice, the machinery is the real law.

That is the central claim of this article, and it repays stating plainly at the outset. Detention orders under the National Security Act fail on the clock, not on the merits, because courts almost never second-guess the subjective satisfaction of the detaining authority, so the entire practical law of the statute is the law of timelines, communication and documents. A detaining authority that is satisfied, on material placed before it, that a person must be detained to prevent prejudicial conduct has made a judgment that a court will approach with considerable restraint. The court does not sit in appeal over that satisfaction, does not weigh the material as a trial court would weigh evidence, and does not substitute its own view of whether detention was wise or necessary. What the court does examine, closely and without deference, is whether the satisfaction rests on relevant material, whether a live and proximate link connects the grounds to the object of detention, whether the procedural commands of the statute and of Article 22 were obeyed, and whether the representation of the detenu was considered and decided with reasonable expedition. Each of those inquiries is, in substance, an inquiry about process. Was the material relevant and was it actually considered? Was the incident relied upon close enough in time and character to support a prediction? Were the grounds communicated within the period the statute allows? Were the documents behind the grounds handed over so that a representation could be made at all? Was that representation decided promptly by the government itself? An order that is impeccable on suspicion and careless on any one of these points is an order at risk, and an order that is thin on suspicion but disciplined on every procedural point is remarkably hard to dislodge.

Understanding the Act in this way changes how the statute should be read. A reader who approaches the National Security Act as a list of grounds for detention will miss the structure that gives the grounds their meaning, because the grounds occupy only a small part of the eighteen sections and because almost every other section exists to discipline what happens after the power is invoked. The sequence matters more than any single provision viewed in isolation. An order is made, it is executed, the detenu is held in a place and under conditions regulated by the government, the grounds must be communicated, a representation may be made, the papers must travel to an Advisory Board within a fixed period, the Board must report within a further fixed period, the government must then confirm or revoke, and a confirmed detention cannot exceed a stated maximum. Break the sequence at any link and the detention that follows is exposed. Keep the sequence intact and the detention acquires the only kind of legitimacy the scheme offers, which is the legitimacy of a power exercised inside its own stated limits. This article therefore reads the Act as a timetable with a power attached, rather than as a power with a timetable attached, and it treats every deadline, every communication duty and every documentary duty as a substantive protection rather than as a formality.

This article is concerned with one operative central statute, taken on its own terms and traced through its text, its making and its working life. The wider field is larger than any single Act, because preventive detention in India rests on a constitutional foundation in Article 22 and has been carried, at different times and for different purposes, by a family of central and state laws that overlap, replace one another and borrow language from one another. That wider field, its constitutional root and its full family of laws, is mapped by the series pillar in [the complete guide to preventive detention law in India](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/), and the reader who wants the whole landscape should begin there. The task here is narrower and deeper. It is to follow a single detention order from the moment of satisfaction to the moment of release or expiry, to show where the Act places its safeguards, to explain why those safeguards so often decide cases, and to account for the odd history by which a statute repealed in one political season returns in another under a fresh name and with its essential machinery intact.

## What Existed Before the National Security Act 1980

No detention statute arrives without ancestors, and the National Security Act arrived with a particularly crowded ancestry. To understand why its provisions take the form they do, why its safeguards are expressed as deadlines rather than as hearings, and why governments that denounce preventive detention in opposition so often preserve it in office, the reader has to follow the line of central detention laws that Parliament created, extended, hardened, repealed and recreated across the first three decades after the Constitution came into force. The pattern is not one of steady development toward a settled scheme. It is a pattern of enactment followed by disillusion, repeal followed by regret, and recreation in a form close enough to its predecessor that the resemblance cannot be accidental. The National Security Act is best understood as the third major expression of a single legislative instinct, adapted each time to the politics of its moment but recognisable each time in its structure, its language and its allocation of trust to the executive.

The first expression was the Preventive Detention Act 1950, which holds the distinction of being Parliament's first post-Constitution detention statute. Its significance lies partly in that priority. The Constitution itself, in Article 22, had contemplated preventive detention and had surrounded it with particular safeguards, including communication of grounds, an opportunity to make a representation, and Advisory Board review for longer detentions. A Constitution that had just guaranteed personal liberty in Article 21 and freedoms in Article 19 nevertheless left room for detention without trial, and the Act of 1950 was the instrument by which Parliament first occupied that room. The measure was repeatedly extended rather than allowed to settle into permanence, a legislative habit that itself tells a story. Extension after extension kept the power alive while preserving the formal position that the power was exceptional and temporary, a response to conditions that might pass. In the event, the conditions were always found to persist, and the extensions continued until the Act was at last allowed to lapse at the end of 1969. The lapse matters because it demonstrates that the central detention power was not treated, even by the Parliaments that renewed it, as an ordinary and permanent part of the criminal law. It lived on borrowed time, renewed in instalments, and when the renewals stopped the statute died without repeal, simply by the expiry of its own term.

The interval after the lapse of the Act of 1950 did not last. The Maintenance of Internal Security Act 1971, known universally by its initials as MISA, followed and became the second major expression of the same instinct. MISA carried the preventive detention power into a new decade and, in time, into the most controversial chapter in the history of Indian detention law. During the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, the Act was hardened, and the hardening is essential to any honest account of the lineage, because the reputation that attaches to preventive detention in Indian public life was formed in large measure in those years. A statute that on its face contains deadlines and Board review can be transformed in practice by amendments that loosen those protections, by executive practice that treats the protections as obstacles, and by a political atmosphere in which challenge itself becomes difficult. The detailed story of that transformation, of the persons detained and of the legal changes that made mass detention easier to sustain, belongs to the companion account of [MISA 1971 and Emergency detention](/2010/07/01/misa-1971-emergency-detention/), which traces the Emergency chapter in full. For the purposes of lineage, the point is narrower. MISA showed how elastic a detention statute can be in the hands of a government determined to stretch it, and that demonstration shaped everything that followed, including the determination of the government that repealed it and the caution of the courts that later reviewed detention under its successor.

Repeal came with a change of government. MISA was repealed by the Maintenance of Internal Security (Repeal) Act 1978 under the Janata government, and the repeal was a deliberate political act rather than a quiet expiry. Where the Act of 1950 had been allowed to lapse when its extensions ran out, MISA was taken off the statute book by a government that had defined itself in opposition to the Emergency and to the detention regime that the Emergency had produced. The distinction between lapse and repeal carries meaning. A lapse suggests that a power has become unnecessary. A repeal of this kind suggests that a power has been judged wrong, or at least that its continued existence is an embarrassment to the order that replaces it. For roughly two years after that repeal, India had no standing central preventive detention statute of general application. The qualification matters and should be kept precise. Specialised detention legislation survived, because the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act of 1974, known as COFEPOSA and aimed at smuggling and foreign exchange offences, remained in force and continued to authorise detention in its narrower field. What the country lacked in those two years was a general central instrument, available across the broad grounds of security, public order and essential supplies, of the kind that the Acts of 1950 and 1971 had supplied and of the kind that the Act of 1980 would supply again.

The two year gap is the most instructive part of the lineage, because it tests the claim that a general detention statute is indispensable to governance. For that period the ordinary criminal law, the surviving specialised statute and the detention laws of the states carried the whole burden, and the absence of a general central Act did not remove preventive detention from Indian law altogether. Article 22 continued to permit it, state legislation continued to provide for it in particular contexts, and COFEPOSA continued to operate in its field. What was absent was convenience of a particular kind, the convenience of a single central text under which the Centre and the states could detain on the classic general grounds without turning to narrower instruments. The return of such a text in 1980 therefore cannot be explained solely by legal necessity, if necessity means that detention was otherwise impossible. It is better explained by a combination of executive preference for a general instrument, administrative familiarity with the older model, and a political judgment that the conditions of the time called for the power to be visibly available. Legislatures recreate this instrument after repealing it for reasons that recur with enough regularity to be stated as a pattern. A government formed in reaction against detention inherits the ordinary pressures of office, including disorders, shortages, security anxieties and demands from its own administrative machinery for a power that acts before an offence rather than after it. Opposition to detention in the abstract coexists, in office, with reluctance to be the government that lacks the power when a crisis arrives. Repeal is also easier to defend as a repudiation of a predecessor's abuses than as a permanent renunciation of the tool itself, and so the tool returns with assurances that it will be used sparingly and lawfully, assurances that the text alone cannot enforce.

One correction belongs in this history, and it should be made in its honest form rather than in either of the exaggerated forms in which the point is often put. The National Security Act is not a colonial statute. It dates from 1980, it was enacted by the Parliament of independent India three decades after the Constitution, and it was closely modelled on the Preventive Detention Act 1950, itself a post-Constitution measure. To call the Act colonial is to misdate it and to misplace responsibility for it. At the same time, its lineage does run back, through MISA and through the Act of 1950, to colonial era detention instruments, and that longer lineage is a matter of record rather than of rhetoric. The instruments named in that lineage include Bengal Regulation III of 1818, an early executive detention measure of the colonial period, and the Rowlatt legislation of 1919, which occupied a notorious place in the nationalist critique of colonial rule. The honest position holds both truths together. The legal DNA of executive detention without trial is old, and parts of it were inherited rather than invented after independence. The decision to keep using that DNA, to re-enact it in 1950, to harden it in the Emergency, to repeal it in 1978 and to recreate it in 1980, was in each instance a decision of Indian legislatures and Indian governments, answerable to Indian politics and reviewable, within its limits, by Indian courts. Antiquity explains the availability of the model. It does not explain, and it does not excuse, the repeated choice of the model.

The modelling point deserves emphasis because it governs how the Act of 1980 should be interpreted. A statute closely modelled on the Act of 1950 inherits more than language. It inherits a theory of how detention should be controlled, which is a theory of executive satisfaction checked by communication, representation and Board review rather than by judicial trial of the underlying facts. It inherits a vocabulary of grounds, expressed at a high level of generality, whose meaning had already been contested under the earlier statutes. It inherits, too, a body of judicial doctrine about the narrowness of review, because courts that had learned to review detention under the earlier Acts carried that learning into review under the later one. When the Act of 1980 speaks of a person acting in a manner prejudicial to the security of India or to public order, it uses words with a past, and that past includes both legislative repetition and judicial attempts to confine wide words within workable limits. The lineage therefore does practical work for the reader. It explains why the Act trusts the detaining authority so far, why it distrusts that authority enough to surround the order with deadlines, and why litigation under the Act so often returns to questions that would have been familiar to a lawyer practising under the Act of 1950. The names and dates change. The argument, about how far executive prediction should be free from judicial second guessing and how strictly the procedural price of that freedom should be exacted, remains recognisably the same argument.


## How the National Security Act 1980 Was Made

The making of the National Security Act belongs to the politics of a return. Indira Gandhi returned to power in January 1980 after the Janata experiment collapsed, and the National Security Act was enacted within the year. That sequence, a government restored after an interlude defined by reaction against Emergency detention, choosing within months to restore a general central detention statute, is the central fact of the Act's birth and the reason the manner of its birth matters as much as its text. A government with a different history might have proceeded slowly, might have tested whether the gap left by the repeal of MISA could be managed with narrower instruments, or might have felt obliged to justify a general detention law at length before asking Parliament to enact one. The government of 1980 did none of those things in the order that a deliberative account would predict. It legislated first by ordinance, put the detention regime into operation, and brought the Act to Parliament afterwards, so that by the time the legislature debated the measure in its statutory form the power it conferred was already being exercised. The Act's biography therefore begins with executive action rather than with parliamentary deliberation, and that beginning is not a technical footnote. It is part of the explanation for the Act's character, for the litigation that immediately surrounded it, and for one of the most remarkable drafting episodes in modern Indian statute law.

The instrument of that beginning was the National Security Ordinance, 1980, Ordinance 11 of 1980, which came into force on 23 September 1980. The Act that replaced it, the National Security Act, 1980, Act 65 of 1980, received the assent of the President on 27 December 1980 and, by Section 18(2), is deemed to have come into force on 23 September 1980. The deeming provision is worth pausing over, because it closes the gap between ordinance and Act and gives the statutory regime a continuous life from the date of the ordinance. Detentions begun under the Ordinance were not left stranded when the Ordinance was repealed and replaced. Section 18 performs the repeal of the Ordinance and provides the savings that a replacement of this kind requires, and the retrospective commencement ensures that the Act speaks from the same date as the executive instrument it succeeded. In formal terms the sequence is tidy. An ordinance is promulgated, an Act follows within the same year, the Act is deemed effective from the ordinance date, and the ordinance disappears into its statutory successor. In political and constitutional terms the sequence is less tidy, because for the period between 23 September 1980 and the passage of the Act, persons could be detained under a law that Parliament had not enacted, made by the executive under a constitutional power designed for situations in which immediate action is said to be necessary while Parliament is not in session.

That is why the ordinance route is itself part of the story, and why no account of the Act is complete that treats the Ordinance as a mere preliminary. Legislation by ordinance concentrates, in a single episode, the very question that preventive detention poses in permanent form, which is how much lawmaking and liberty restricting power should be exercisable without the ordinary discipline of prior legislative debate. The parallel should not be overstated, because an ordinance is a recognised constitutional instrument with its own limits, its own requirement of later parliamentary engagement and its own exposure to judicial review, and the Supreme Court would in due course hold, in the litigation over this very statute, that an ordinance is law for the purposes of Article 21 and that the ordinance making power is legislative in character, an ordinance having the same force as an Act subject to the same limits under Part III of the Constitution. The parallel nevertheless illuminates the temper of the measure. A detention statute is a law that trusts executive judgment about future conduct. A detention ordinance is that trust doubled, because the executive has first judged that the power itself cannot wait for Parliament and has then acquired the power to judge individual cases under it. Readers who want the constitutional mechanics of that power, its conditions, its duration and its history of use and abuse, will find them traced in [the ordinance making power in India](/2022/03/01/ordinance-making-power-india/), and that account supplies the general framework within which this particular ordinance should be placed. In this particular case the framework had immediate human consequences, because detention orders were made under the Ordinance itself, including the order against A K Roy, a Marxist Member of Parliament detained by an order of the District Magistrate, Dhanbad, on a public order ground, whose challenge under Article 32 became the vehicle for the Constitution Bench judgment that tested the Ordinance, parts of the Act and a still larger constitutional question about an amendment that had never been brought into force.

The drafting episode at the heart of that larger question is remarkable enough to deserve careful statement, even though its full development belongs to a later part of this article. The Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 had been enacted in the aftermath of the Emergency with the object, among others, of strengthening the safeguards in Article 22 against preventive detention. Section 3 of that Amendment Act would have substituted a new Article 22(4), under which no detention could continue beyond two months unless an Advisory Board of a prescribed and more judicial character had reported sufficient cause before the two months expired, the Board to be constituted in accordance with the recommendations of the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court, with a serving judge of that High Court as chairman and serving or retired judges of any High Court as the other members. The Amendment Act received assent on 30 April 1979. Every other provision was brought into force, but Section 3 was left unnotified by the Central Government, because Section 1(2) of the Amendment Act left commencement to be appointed by notification, and the notification for Section 3 was never issued. The consequence was a constitutional oddity with direct statutory effects. The amended and stronger Article 22 existed as enacted law awaiting commencement, while the older Article 22(4), with its three month threshold and its weaker Board composition of persons who are, have been or are qualified to be judges of a High Court, remained the operative text under which detention statutes had to be framed.

Clause 9 of the National Security Ordinance was framed on the amended model. The Ordinance constituted its Advisory Boards in conformity with the Article 22 that Section 3 of the 44th Amendment would have produced, as though the stronger safeguard had already taken effect. Section 9 of the Act discarded that conformity. When the executive's own ordinance became Parliament's Act, the Board provision reverted to the unamended Article 22(4), with its three person Boards drawn from persons who are, have been or are qualified to be appointed as judges of a High Court and a chairman who is or has been a judge of a High Court. The movement is the opposite of the movement a reader might expect. The temporary executive instrument contained the stronger judicial safeguard. The permanent parliamentary statute removed it. No elaborate theory is needed to see why the episode attracted judicial attention, and the Constitution Bench in A K Roy against Union of India, decided on 28 December 1981, recorded the contrast in pointed terms, the majority noting that the Ordinance had modelled its Advisory Board clause on Section 3 of the 44th Amendment while the Act had discarded that model. The Court nevertheless declined to issue a mandamus compelling the Central Government to bring Section 3 into force, holding that commencement was for the executive and that the Court would not substitute its judgment where Parliament had prescribed no objective standard, although the majority also recorded its difficulty in seeing what practical obstacle could persist two and a half years after assent. The notification gap itself, its reasoning and its consequences for detention law, are developed in full in a later segment of this article, and the detail is held there deliberately. What belongs at this stage is the bare sequence and its strangeness. An amendment designed to strengthen detention safeguards was enacted and then left dormant. An ordinance briefly conformed to the dormant amendment. An Act then abandoned the conformity, and the dormant amendment remained dormant under the government that enacted the Act, as it has remained under every government since, Section 3 of the 44th Amendment never having been brought into force at all.

A further feature of the Act as enacted completes the picture of its making, and it concerns territory rather than time. As enacted, Section 1(2) extended the Act to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir. The exclusion was not an oversight and it was not a denial that preventive detention operated in that State. Jammu and Kashmir had its own Public Safety Act of 1978, and the central statute left that field to the state instrument. The territorial extent of the Act was later altered by the reorganisation legislation of 2019, a change that belongs to the later history of the statute rather than to its original design. For an account of the making, the exclusion matters for two reasons. First, it shows that the Act was never, even in ambition, a complete code of preventive detention for the whole country, because a significant state regime stood outside it from the start. Second, it underlines the federal character of detention law in India, in which central and state statutes coexist, overlap in purpose and differ in detail, and in which the survival of a detention in a given case may depend on identifying correctly which statute, which government and which set of deadlines governs it. The exclusion also became a point of challenge in the A K Roy litigation, where Section 1(2) of the 44th Amendment Act, the commencement provision, should not be confused with the similarly numbered provisions of other statutes that featured in the same arguments. Precision about numbering is not pedantry in this field. It is the habit on which the whole law of the Act depends, because detention cases turn on the right section, the right government, the right date and the right document, and confusion at any of those points is the beginning of a failed order or of a failed challenge.

Taken together, the circumstances of the making explain the Act's enduring tension. It was a restoration measure enacted by a returned government, carried into force by ordinance before Parliament had debated it, deemed effective retrospectively from the ordinance date, framed on an older statutory model, and stripped at the final stage of a stronger Advisory Board design that its own ordinance had briefly adopted. None of those facts determines, by itself, whether any individual detention under the Act is lawful. Each individual detention stands or falls on its grounds, its material and its compliance with the timetable. The circumstances of the making nevertheless set the terms on which individual cases are argued, because a statute born in this way invites a particular kind of judicial response, generous toward the existence of the power and strict toward the conditions of its exercise. That response, restraint on the merits joined to rigour on procedure, is the doctrine that the opening of this article identified as the practical law of the Act, and its roots lie as much in 1980 itself as in any later judgment.

## The Architecture of the National Security Act 1980

The National Security Act contains eighteen sections, and the most useful way to learn them is to stop treating them as a list and start treating them as a journey. A single detention under the Act passes through distinct stages, each governed by its own group of provisions, and a failure at any stage has consequences for everything that follows. The power must first exist and be invoked by the right authority on a permitted ground. The order must then be executed and the detenu held somewhere, under some conditions, with special provision for the person who absconds and for acts done in good faith. The grounds must be communicated and a representation made possible. The papers must go before an Advisory Board and the Board must report. The government must decide, within the shadow of that report, whether detention continues and for how long, and the statute must finally provide exits, by expiry, by revocation and by temporary release, together with the closing provisions that protect earlier action and preserve orders under state laws. Read in that order, the eighteen sections form a sequence with a logic that a bare recital of section numbers conceals. This section offers a guided map of that sequence, group by group, explaining what work each group does in the life of a single order, so that the detailed treatment of grounds and of the timetable in the remainder of the article can be followed against a clear picture of the whole.

The power itself is housed in Sections 1 to 3, and the grouping is instructive because it joins extent and commencement to the operative grant. Section 1 gives the Act its name and, as enacted, its territorial extent, the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, later altered by the reorganisation legislation of 2019. Section 2 contains the definitions on which the rest of the Act depends, including the identification of the appropriate Government for different classes of order, a definition of more than formal importance because the duties of communication, consideration, reference and confirmation fall on different governments depending on who made the order and in connection with which affairs the order was made. Section 3 is the engine of the Act. It confers the detention power in two tiers, distinguishes the grounds available in each tier, permits a State Government to delegate the second tier power to District Magistrates and Commissioners of Police for limited periods, and surrounds delegated and undelegated orders alike with reporting and approval duties that begin to run at once. To treat Sections 1 to 3 as a single group is to recognise that the question of power under this Act is never only a question of whether a ground exists in the abstract. It is also a question of which government or which officer claims the power, over which territory, in connection with which affairs and subject to which immediate reporting obligation. An order that misidentifies its own source of power, or that is made by an officer whose delegation has expired, or that is never reported as the section requires, is defective at the first stage of the journey, before execution or communication is even reached.

Execution and custody occupy the next stage, governed principally by Sections 4 to 7, with two related protections that the Act places later in its numbering but that belong to this stage by function. Section 4 provides that a detention order is executed in the manner provided for the execution of a warrant of arrest under the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, a provision that assimilates the taking into custody under a detention order to a familiar coercive process while leaving the detention itself governed by the Act rather than by the ordinary law of arrest and remand. Section 5 empowers the appropriate Government to regulate the place and the conditions of detention, including conditions as to maintenance, discipline and punishment for breaches of discipline, and it is under this power that the daily reality of detention is determined, the place of confinement, the separation of detenus from convicts that the Supreme Court directed in the A K Roy judgment, and the facilities for an existence consistent with human dignity that the same judgment described. Section 6 protects the order against a purely territorial objection, providing that an order is not invalid merely because the person to be detained or the place of detention lies outside the territorial jurisdiction of the authority that made it, a saving that prevents geography alone from defeating an otherwise lawful order while leaving every substantive and procedural objection untouched. Section 7 deals with the person who evades the order altogether, empowering the government or the officer to report the absconding to a Magistrate and to set in motion the proclamation machinery of the criminal procedure code, with failure to appear as directed punishable with imprisonment for a term that may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both. To this group belongs also, by function, the protection in Section 16 for action taken in good faith under the Act, which shields the machinery of execution and custody from suit, prosecution and other legal proceedings for acts done or intended to be done in good faith, a protection that concerns the officers who carry orders into effect rather than the validity of the orders themselves. The work of the group as a whole is to convert a paper satisfaction into a physical custody that the law can recognise, to locate that custody, to regulate its conditions, to pursue the person who frustrates it and to protect the persons who operate it when they act in good faith.

Communication and representation form the next stage, and in the working life of an order this stage is often decisive. Section 8 requires the authority making the order, when a person is detained in pursuance of it, to communicate to that person the grounds on which the order has been made as soon as may be and ordinarily not later than five days from the date of detention, and in exceptional circumstances and for reasons to be recorded in writing, not later than ten days from that date. The same provision requires that the detenu be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government. Section 8(2) qualifies the duty of disclosure by providing that nothing in the section requires the authority to disclose facts whose disclosure it considers to be against the public interest. The balance struck here defines the character of the whole Act. The detenu is not given a trial, is not given the evidence in the sense a criminal defendant receives it, and may be refused particular facts on public interest grounds, but the detenu must be given the grounds, must be given them quickly, and must be given a real opportunity to answer them by representation. Without the grounds there is nothing to answer, without the material relied upon there is no means of answering effectively, and without prompt consideration of the answer the representation is an empty ritual. It is for that reason that the case law of detention gathers so thickly around this single section and around the corresponding guarantee in Article 22(5) of the Constitution. Section 8 is short. Its consequences are not, because every later stage presupposes that communication has happened properly and on time, and a defect here infects the representation, the Board reference that must include the representation if any has been made, and the legitimacy of any confirmation that follows.

The Board machinery occupies Sections 9 to 12 and supplies the only institutional check inside the executive process itself. Section 9 obliges the Central Government and each State Government to constitute Advisory Boards, each Board consisting of three persons who are, have been or are qualified to be appointed as judges of a High Court, with a chairman who is or has been a judge of a High Court. Section 10 requires the appropriate Government, in every case where a detention order has been made under the Act, to place before the Advisory Board, within three weeks from the date of detention, the grounds on which the order has been made, the representation if any made by the detenu, and, where the order was made by a delegated officer, the report of that officer. Section 11 prescribes the procedure of the Board and its reporting duty. The Board considers the material placed before it, may call for further information from the government or from any person, hears the detenu in person if the Board considers the hearing essential or if the detenu desires to be heard, and submits its report to the appropriate Government within seven weeks from the date of detention, stating in a separate part its opinion whether or not there is sufficient cause for the detention. Section 11(4) provides that nothing in the section entitles a detenu to appear by a legal practitioner in any matter connected with the reference and that the proceedings and the report of the Board, except the part recording its opinion, are confidential. Section 12 translates the opinion into consequences. Where the Board has reported sufficient cause, the appropriate Government may confirm the detention order and continue the detention for such period as it thinks fit. Where the Board has reported that there is no sufficient cause, the appropriate Government shall revoke the order and cause the person to be released forthwith. The asymmetry in that language, may confirm against shall revoke, captures the function of the Board precisely. A favourable opinion permits continuation but does not compel it, because the government retains a discretion to release in any event. An adverse opinion compels release, because the one check the scheme provides would be worthless if the government could disregard the only answer that favours the detenu. The Board is not a court, its proceedings are confidential, the detenu has no right to legal representation before it and no right of cross examination, and its opinion binds only in the detenu's favour. Within those limits it remains the hinge of the Act, because detention beyond the short initial period depends on its report and because the deadlines for reference and report are among the firmest dates in the entire timetable.

Duration and exits are governed by Sections 13 to 15, which answer the question every detenu asks first and every adviser must answer exactly, namely how long the detention can last and by what routes it can end early. Section 13 fixes the maximum period for which a person may be detained in pursuance of an order that has been confirmed, at twelve months from the date of detention, while preserving the power of the appropriate Government to revoke or modify the order at any earlier time. The twelve month figure is a ceiling and not an entitlement, a distinction that matters in argument because an order confirmed without a specified shorter period does not authorise the government to treat the ceiling as the default term in every case, just as the existence of the ceiling does not excuse any failure in the stages that precede confirmation. Section 14 governs revocation and its aftermath. An order may be revoked by the government that made or approved it and, notably, an order made by a State Government may be revoked by the Central Government as well as by the State Government, a central override that reflects the constitutional distribution of the detention power. Revocation or expiry does not bar a fresh order against the same person, but where no fresh facts have arisen after the expiry or revocation of the earlier order, the total period of detention under the later order cannot extend beyond twelve months from the date of detention under the earlier order, a proviso that prevents the ceiling in Section 13 from being defeated by the simple device of serial orders on identical material. Section 15 provides the most flexible exit of all, temporary release. The appropriate Government may release a detenu for a specified period, with or without conditions including a bond, and may cancel the release at any time, with failure to surrender in accordance with the directions on release punishable with imprisonment for a term that may extend to two years, or with fine, or with both. Together the three sections show that the Act contemplates detention as a state that must be capable of ending in several distinct ways, by the efflux of the maximum period, by a considered act of revocation, by a Board opinion that compels revocation, and by a temporary release that suspends custody without ending the order, and that each route has its own conditions and its own penal consequences for misuse.

The closing provisions, Sections 16 to 18, complete the journey and should not be skipped as boilerplate, because each performs work that an earlier stage presupposes. Section 16, already noticed for its functional connection to execution and custody, protects action taken in good faith under the Act from suit, prosecution and other legal proceedings, and its placement among the closing provisions reflects a drafting convention rather than its practical operation, which runs alongside every act of enforcement. Section 17 provides that the Act does not disturb detention orders made under state laws in force at its commencement, a savings provision that preserves the parallel world of state detention legislation with which the Act has always coexisted and that prevents the commencement of the central Act from being read as an implied destruction of orders validly made under another competent law. Section 18 repeals the National Security Ordinance, 1980 and provides the savings appropriate to that repeal, while Section 18(2) supplies the deemed commencement date of 23 September 1980 on which the continuity between ordinance and Act depends. A reader who reaches Section 18 has travelled the full circle of the statute, from a power created by executive ordinance, through its exercise against an individual, its communication, its review and its confirmation, to its expiry or revocation and the tidying provisions that protect what was done under it and preserve what was done under other laws. The circle is closed in form. Whether it is closed in substance in any given case depends entirely on whether each stage was performed as the Act directs and within the time the Act allows.

That dependence is the point at which the map hands over to the argument, and the remainder of this article takes up, in turn, the two questions that the architecture leaves open and that every detention case must answer. The first question is one of substance, within the narrow limits in which substance can be tested at all. On what grounds may the power be used, by which government and by which officer, and how do the courts distinguish a ground that the Act permits from a ground that merely borrows the language of the Act for an object the Act does not authorise, above all in the contested territory between law and order and public order? The second question is one of sequence and proof. What must happen, by when, at the hands of which authority, and with which documents, for an order that begins as executive satisfaction to survive approval, communication, representation, Board review and confirmation, and at which of those points do orders in practice most often break? The architecture gives the reader the stations. The grounds give the journey its permitted destinations. The clock decides whether the journey was ever lawfully completed at all.

## The Grounds: What Section 3 Allows the State to Prevent

Section 3 is the operative heart of the National Security Act, 1980, and everything else in the statute exists to serve, to channel or to restrain the power that Section 3 creates. The section does not punish conduct that has already occurred, and it does not authorise detention as a penalty for an offence that a court has found proved. Its language is directed instead at prevention, at stopping a person from acting in a manner that the detaining authority considers prejudicial to one of a closed list of public interests, and that orientation towards the future shapes every question that later arises about the validity of an order passed under it. A reader who begins with the grounds therefore begins in the right place, because the grounds define both the reach of the power and the boundaries within which a court will later test whether the power was exercised for the purpose that Parliament conferred.

The section is divided into two principal sub-sections, and the division is deliberate rather than decorative. Section 3(1) gathers the grounds that face outward and upward towards the Union and its place in the world, while Section 3(2) gathers the grounds that face inward towards the ordinary life of a State and the community that lives within it. The separation matters because it organises the statute around two different kinds of threatened harm, the harm that touches the defence of the country, its external relations and its security as a whole, and the harm that touches the security of a particular State, the maintenance of public order within that State, and the maintenance of supplies and services on which a community depends for its daily existence. The lists are short, and their brevity is itself a form of discipline, since a detaining authority that cannot bring the conduct it fears within one of the named heads has no power under the Act at all, however troubling that conduct may appear to the authority on other grounds.

Section 3(1) empowers the Central Government or a State Government to make an order directing that a person be detained with a view to preventing him from acting in any manner prejudicial to the defence of India, to the relations of India with foreign powers, or to the security of India. Each of those three expressions carries a distinct field of concern, even though the fields overlap at their edges. The defence of India concerns the protection of the country against armed threat and the safeguarding of the means by which that protection is carried on. The relations of India with foreign powers concern the conduct of the Union in its dealings beyond its borders, a field in which a single act by an individual, if it is capable of damaging those relations, may carry consequences far beyond the place where the act itself occurs. The security of India is the broadest of the three expressions, and it reaches threats to the safety and stability of the country as a whole, threats that may arise from within its territory as readily as from outside it. Taken together, the three grounds in Section 3(1) describe harms whose scale is national, whose subject matter belongs principally to the responsibilities of the Union, and whose prevention Parliament considered serious enough to justify the exceptional step of detention without trial.

Section 3(2) turns from the nation as a whole to the State and the community. Under that sub-section, the Central Government or a State Government may make an order directing that a person be detained with a view to preventing him from acting in any manner prejudicial to the security of the State, to the maintenance of public order, or to the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. The security of the State in this setting is a narrower conception than the security of India in Section 3(1), directed at the safety of the particular State rather than the country entire, and the distinction preserves a federal sense of scale within the section, so that a threat confined in its reach and its effect to one State is described in the language of that State rather than inflated into a threat to India itself. The maintenance of public order concerns the peace and tranquillity of community life, the condition in which people may go about their ordinary affairs without disturbance that spreads beyond the individuals immediately involved. The maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community concerns the material foundations of that ordinary life, the food, the fuel, the transport and the other provisions and services without which a community cannot sustain itself, and it recognises that a deliberate interruption of those necessities can injure a population as gravely as a disturbance in its streets.

The division between the two sub-sections should not be mistaken for a division of governments. The fact sheet of the statute is plain on the point that both sub-sections are available to both levels of government, since Section 3(1) speaks of the Central Government or a State Government, and Section 3(2) speaks in the same terms. A State Government is therefore not confined to the state-facing grounds, and the Central Government is not confined to the Centre-facing grounds. The organisation of the section is an organisation of subjects rather than an allocation of exclusive fields, and the practical consequence is that the choice between the sub-sections turns on the character of the harm to be prevented rather than on the identity of the government that acts. That arrangement reflects the reality that a threat to the security of India may first present itself within the territory and the knowledge of a State Government, and that a threat to public order in a State may be of sufficient concern for the Central Government to act upon it. The statute trusts each government with the full range of grounds, and then disciplines that trust through the later requirements of reporting, approval and review that govern how an order is made and how long it may endure.

Within Section 3(1) there is a further limb that stands apart from the three preventive grounds, and it concerns the foreigner. For a person who is a foreigner, an order may be made with a view to regulating his continued presence in India, or with a view to making arrangements for his expulsion from India. The language differs in a telling way from the language of prevention that governs the rest of the section, because the object here is not described as preventing prejudicial conduct of a named kind, but as controlling presence itself, either by subjecting the continued stay of the foreigner to regulation or by preparing the ground for his removal from the country. The limb therefore connects the detention power to the control that a sovereign state exercises over the entry and residence of persons who are not its citizens, and detention under it serves an administrative purpose linked to presence and expulsion rather than the narrower purpose of forestalling a specified prejudicial act. Its presence inside Section 3(1) also explains the placement of the sub-section as a whole, since the foreigner limb belongs naturally alongside the grounds concerning foreign powers and the security of India, in the part of the section that looks outward beyond the borders of the country.

Against that breadth, the Explanation to Section 3(2) performs a small but significant act of exclusion. The Explanation provides that the sub-section does not extend to grounds on which an order could be made under the Prevention of Blackmarketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980, so that the two statutes do not overlap on that ground. The carve-out matters for two reasons, one of legislative housekeeping and one of substance. As housekeeping, it prevents two detention statutes from covering the same ground at the same time, with the confusion about procedure, safeguards and authority that such duplication would create for the detaining authority, for the detenu and for any court later asked to examine the order. In substance, it directs conduct connected with blackmarketing and with the supplies of essential commodities towards the statute that Parliament enacted specifically for that subject, and it withdraws that subject from the general power in Section 3(2), even though the supplies and services ground in the National Security Act might otherwise have appeared wide enough to contain it. A detaining authority that wishes to act on a ground falling within the other statute must therefore proceed under that statute, and an order under the National Security Act that rests on a ground carved out by the Explanation is an order that reaches beyond the power the section confers.

The supplies and services ground carries a further discipline that the Supreme Court imposed when the Act was challenged in A K Roy v Union of India. The Court upheld the ground in Section 3(2) only as read down, holding that detention on that ground is valid only where the supplies or services said to be essential to the community have been specified publicly in advance, since without prior public specification the discretion would be too open. The reasoning repays attention because it shows how a court can preserve a ground while narrowing the manner of its use. A person cannot fairly be detained for threatening a supply or a service whose essential character has never been announced to the public, and an authority cannot be left to declare a supply essential only at the moment when it wishes to detain. Prior public specification converts an open discretion into a bounded one, because the community knows in advance which supplies and services carry that protected status, and the detaining authority must bring its case within a specification that already exists rather than inventing one for the occasion. The Explanation and the reading down operate in the same direction, from opposite sides. The Explanation removes one defined field from the ground altogether, and the reading down confines what remains of the ground to supplies and services whose essential character has been made public beforehand.

Behind the lists of grounds lies the structure of the decision that the detaining authority must take, and that structure is as important as the lists themselves. The authority must be satisfied that detention is necessary with a view to preventing the person from acting in a prejudicial manner, and that satisfaction is, in the language the courts have used, subjective. It is the satisfaction of the authority named in the statute, formed on the material placed before that authority, rather than a finding that a court itself must reach on the evidence as if the court were deciding the matter afresh. The subjective character of the satisfaction does not place it beyond review, as the later discussion of the compliance clock will show, but it does define the kind of review that is possible, because a court examining the order asks whether the authority had relevant material on which such satisfaction could rest, whether the authority applied its mind to that material, and whether the connection between the material and the object of detention is real, rather than asking whether the court would itself have been satisfied on the same material.

The satisfaction is also preventive in its direction, and prevention has a particular logic that distinguishes the National Security Act from the ordinary criminal law at every point. The criminal law looks backwards to an act that is alleged to have been committed, charges that act as an offence, and requires the prosecution to prove it at a trial before punishment may follow. Preventive detention looks forwards from material about past conduct to a prediction about future conduct, and it authorises detention because of what the authority apprehends the person may do if left at liberty. Past conduct is therefore relevant under Section 3 not for its own sake, and not as a basis for punishing the person for what he has already done, but as the material from which an inference may be drawn about his propensity and his likely future course. A single grave incident may support such an inference in one setting, and a course of repeated conduct may be required to support it in another, but in every setting the inference is the bridge between the record and the order, and the order stands or falls on whether that bridge holds. Where the past conduct relied upon does not rationally support an apprehension about the future, the preventive purpose is missing, and the detention becomes in substance a punishment imposed without the trial that punishment requires.

That is why the courts insist that the grounds must show a live and proximate link between the conduct relied upon and the object of the detention. Live means that the connection must still subsist at the time the order is made, so that material which has grown stale, incidents which lie too far in the past to say anything reliable about the person's present propensity, cannot carry the weight of an order. Proximate means that the connection must be close and direct rather than remote and speculative, so that the chain between what the person has done and what he is apprehended to do must not depend on a succession of conjectures, each weakening the next. Staleness and remoteness are distinct defects that often appear together, and both are tested the same way, by asking whether the material, read fairly and as a whole, shows a propensity that subsists at the date of the order rather than a history that has spent itself.

The link requirement also protects the boundary between the detention power and the ordinary criminal process, a boundary that the Supreme Court guarded in Rekha v State of Tamil Nadu in 2011 when it warned that preventive detention is an exceptional measure that cannot be used as a substitute for the ordinary criminal process or to bypass bail. The warning belongs in a discussion of the grounds because it goes to purpose. If the conduct relied upon can be met by prosecution, and the only difficulty facing the State is that a court might grant bail in the ordinary course, then the apprehension professed under Section 3 is suspect, since the object of the order appears to be the avoidance of the criminal process rather than the prevention of a prejudicial act that the criminal process cannot address. The grounds in Section 3 are framed around prejudice to large public interests, to defence, to security, to public order and to essential supplies, and an authority that recites those words while pursuing the simpler object of keeping a person in custody for an ordinary offence has mistaken the purpose of the section. The recital of a ground is not enough, and the material must show prejudice of the kind the ground actually names.

No boundary within Section 3 has generated more litigation, or more orders brought down on examination, than the boundary between a problem of law and order and a problem of public order. The two expressions sound close, they share the word order, and in ordinary speech they are used interchangeably, yet in the law of preventive detention they mark different thresholds of harm, and an order that confuses them rests on a ground that the material does not support. The confusion of categories is listed among the recurring grounds on which detention orders fail, and above all the treating of a law and order incident as a public order disturbance. The distinction therefore deserves to be stated plainly before it is developed at length.

### What is the difference between a law and order problem and a public order problem under the NSA?

In Ram Manohar Lohia, law and order forms the largest concentric circle, public order a smaller circle within it, and security of the State the innermost circle, while under Arun Ghosh an act disturbs public order only when it disrupts the even tempo of community life rather than affecting particular individuals alone.

The image of concentric circles in Ram Manohar Lohia v State of Bihar, decided in 1966, has endured because it converts an abstract distinction into a question of reach that a detaining authority can be made to answer. Law and order is the largest circle because it embraces every breach of the peace and every infringement of the law that calls for a response from the authorities, from the isolated quarrel to the gravest disturbance, and its concern is with order in the most general sense across the whole field of conduct that the law regulates. Public order is a smaller circle drawn within that larger one, because not every breach of the law disturbs the public, and many offences, even serious offences, injure the individuals against whom they are directed without unsettling the community around them. The security of the State is the smallest circle at the centre, because it is concerned with threats of the most serious kind directed at the State itself, threats that lie within the field of public order disturbances yet exceed them in gravity and in object. An act must travel inward through those circles before it can support the more serious characterisation, and the authority must be able to show, from the material, why the act belongs in the smaller circle rather than the larger one that contains it.

The circles are not a mechanical test, and Lohia does not supply a list of offences that fall on one side of the line or the other. The same kind of act may remain a law and order matter in one setting and become a public order matter in another, depending on its scale, its setting, its audience, its repetition and the reaction it produces or is calculated to produce in the community. A fight between two persons in a private place, however violent, ordinarily injures those persons and breaches the law, yet leaves the life of the surrounding community untouched, and it therefore sits in the outer circle. Violence offered openly in a crowded public place, in circumstances that spread fear among people who are not parties to the quarrel and that interrupt the ordinary movement and business of the locality, may cross into the inner circle, because the harm is no longer confined to the victim. The difference lies not in the label attached to the offence in a penal statute, but in the effect of the conduct on the community, assessed on the material as a whole. That is why an order that merely recites the seriousness of an offence, without material showing its effect beyond the individuals involved, has not crossed the threshold that Section 3(2) sets when it speaks of the maintenance of public order.

Arun Ghosh v State of West Bengal, decided in 1970, supplied the language in which that effect is tested, and the test is the even tempo of the life of the community. An act affects public order when it disturbs that even tempo, when it interrupts the smooth and regular course in which community life ordinarily proceeds, so that people who are strangers to the act itself find their daily existence unsettled by it. The metaphor of tempo is carefully chosen, because a community, like a piece of music, has a rhythm of work, travel, trade, worship and rest that continues beneath the incidents of individual conflict, and a disturbance of public order is a disturbance of that rhythm itself rather than a discordant note within it. An act directed against a particular individual, even a cruel act, may leave the tempo undisturbed if the community absorbs the shock and continues in its ordinary course, while an act of lesser violence against an individual may disturb the tempo if it is committed in a manner, a place or a context that tells the wider community that its ordinary safety can no longer be assumed. The question is always one of degree and of reach, and it must be answered from the material relied upon, not from assertion in the grounds.

The two decisions work together rather than in competition, and a detaining authority that understands both will frame its grounds differently from one that understands neither. Lohia supplies the geography, the sense that public order is a narrower field than law and order and that the security of the State is narrower still, so that an authority must justify moving inward from the general to the particular. Arun Ghosh supplies the measure to be applied within that geography, the disturbance of the even tempo of community life, so that the justification must be given in terms of effect on the community rather than in terms of the gravity of the charge alone. Together they impose a discipline of description on the authority, which must set out material showing who was affected beyond the immediate parties, how the ordinary life of the locality was interrupted or threatened with interruption, and why the ordinary criminal law, which is fully capable of dealing with the individuals concerned, is insufficient to meet a disturbance of that character. Grounds that contain that material present a public order case. Grounds that contain only a narrative of offences against identified individuals present a law and order case, whatever label the order places upon them, and a court that finds the second in place of the first will treat the order as resting on a confusion of categories.

The discipline has a further consequence for the drafting of grounds under Section 3(2) as a whole. Because the three grounds in the sub-section sit beside one another, an authority may be tempted to recite them together, alleging prejudice to the security of the State, to public order and to essential supplies in a single formula, in the hope that one of them will survive examination. The concentric circles reasoning exposes the weakness of that approach, since the circles are arranged by gravity and reach, and material that supports the outer circle does not without more support the inner ones. Each ground that is recited must be supported by material appropriate to that ground, and the later insertion of Section 5A, which makes the grounds severable so that an order does not fail merely because one ground is vague, non-existent or irrelevant, does not relieve the authority of that obligation in relation to the grounds that remain. Severability preserves an order on its surviving grounds. It does not convert inadequate material into adequate material, and it does not cure a failure to distinguish between categories that the case law requires the authority itself to distinguish.

A K Roy adds a complementary instruction on the interpretation of the grounds, which is that expressions such as defence of India and security of India cannot be defined with precision, yet Sections 3(1) and 3(2) are not void for vagueness, and courts must strive to construe such words narrowly and to restrict the application of the Act to as few situations as possible. The instruction matters because it fixes the direction of interpretation whenever a ground is capable of a wider and a narrower reading. The narrower reading is the one the court should prefer, because the power being construed is a power to detain without trial, and a construction that enlarges that power enlarges a departure from the ordinary protections of the criminal process. For the detaining authority, the practical lesson is that the grounds are not elastic expressions to be stretched over any conduct that causes official concern. They are terms with a settled core of meaning, elaborated by decisions such as Lohia and Arun Ghosh, and an order that treats them as elastic invites the court to bring the order back within the narrower construction and to find that the material does not reach it.

The grounds in Section 3 therefore perform three functions at once. They confer the power, by naming the governments that may act and the prejudices that may be prevented. They limit the power, by closing the list, by carving out the blackmarketing field, by requiring prior public specification for essential supplies and services, and by demanding a live and proximate link between past conduct and apprehended future conduct. They also supply the vocabulary in which an order will later be judged, because every later stage of the statutory scheme, the communication of grounds, the representation, the reference to the Advisory Board and the consideration by a court, returns to the same question of whether the material answers to the ground that Section 3 names. An authority that begins with that discipline has done the largest part of the work that the Act requires. An authority that begins with the conclusion that a person should be detained, and searches afterwards for a ground to fit that conclusion, has reversed the order that the section prescribes, and the reversal is the defect that the courts most reliably detect.

## Who May Pass the Order: The Tiered Authority

The power conferred by Section 3 is in the first instance a power of governments, of the Central Government and of the State Governments, and the statute might have left it there. It did not, because preventive detention is a power that often has to be exercised where the apprehended conduct is occurring and at the speed at which that conduct develops, and a government sitting at the capital of a State cannot always deliberate and act within the time that a local situation allows. Section 3(3) therefore creates the first tier below the governments, by permitting a State Government to delegate the state-facing power to the senior officers of a district. The design of that delegation, its conditions, its duration and the controls that follow it, reveal the balance that the Act strikes throughout between speed at the point of action and responsibility at the point of review.

Section 3(3) provides that a State Government may, by order in writing, direct that a District Magistrate or a Commissioner of Police may also, if satisfied as provided in Section 3(2), exercise the power under that sub-section within the local limits of his jurisdiction. Three features of the provision deserve emphasis, because each is a condition on the delegation rather than a formality surrounding it. The empowerment must be made by an order in writing, so that the existence and the terms of the delegation can be proved from a document rather than asserted from memory or practice, and a detenu who wishes to challenge the authority of the officer who detained him can call for that document and test it. The empowerment is confined to the local limits of the officer's jurisdiction, so that the District Magistrate of one district acquires no power in the next, and the Commissioner of Police exercises the power within the area of his commission rather than at large across the State. The empowerment extends only to the power under Section 3(2), the state-facing grounds of the security of the State, the maintenance of public order and the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community, so that the delegated power is narrower than the power of the governments themselves, which extends to Section 3(1) as well. The officer at the district level is trusted with the grounds that arise from the life of his own district, and not with the grounds that concern the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers or the security of India as a whole.

The second condition in Section 3(3) is a condition of circumstance. The State Government may empower the officer where it is satisfied that the circumstances prevailing, or likely to prevail, in an area within the local limits of that officer's jurisdiction make it necessary to do so. The delegation is therefore not a standing transfer of power that continues regardless of conditions, but a response to a state of affairs that the government has considered and found sufficient, whether that state of affairs already exists at the time of the empowerment or is apprehended as likely to arise. The reference to circumstances likely to prevail mirrors the preventive character of the Act as a whole, since the government is entitled to prepare for a disturbance that has not yet fully developed, yet the requirement that the government be satisfied about those circumstances means that the empowerment itself rests on an application of mind at the State level before any individual detention is contemplated. A delegation made as a matter of routine, without regard to the circumstances of the area, departs from the scheme of the sub-section, because the sub-section makes the circumstances the reason for the delegation and not merely the occasion for it.

The third condition is a condition of time, and it is the most concrete of the three. An empowerment under Section 3(3) may be made for a period not exceeding three months in the first instance, and where the State Government considers it necessary to extend the empowerment beyond that period, it may do so for further periods not exceeding three months at a time. The officer's authority to pass detention orders is therefore never open-ended. It expires unless the government renews it, and each renewal requires the government to turn its mind again to the question whether the circumstances of the area continue to make delegation necessary. The three-month unit is short enough to ensure that the question is revisited regularly, and the requirement of a fresh order for each extension means that continuation is a decision rather than a default. In a scheme where the gravest defect is often the mechanical exercise of power, a provision that forces a periodic decision at a higher level is a safeguard in its own right, even though it operates before any individual case arises and even though the detenu in a particular case may never see the empowerment orders that stand behind the order against him.

An officer who has been empowered and who passes an order does not thereby conclude the matter. Section 3(4) subjects every such order to an immediate reporting duty and to a short approval window, and these two requirements together form the second tier of control. The officer must forthwith report the fact that the order has been made to the State Government, together with the grounds on which the order has been made and such other particulars as, in his opinion, have a bearing on the matter. Forthwith is a demanding word, chosen in place of a fixed number of days, and it requires the report to be made without delay as soon as the order is passed, so that the government learns of the detention from its own officer at once and not through the detenu, the detenu's family or the proceedings that follow. The report carries the grounds and the particulars with it, because a bare intimation that an order has been passed would tell the government nothing on which it could exercise the judgment that the sub-section next requires of it.

That judgment is the approval of the State Government, and the time allowed for it is twelve days. Section 3(4) provides that no order made by an empowered officer shall remain in force for more than twelve days after the making thereof unless, in the meantime, it has been approved by the State Government. The consequence of a failure to approve is therefore automatic and severe. The order does not continue in a provisional or doubtful state, and it does not await a later decision to give it effect. It ceases to have force at the expiry of the period, and detention continued beyond that point rests on an order that the statute no longer sustains. The twelve-day window is the point at which the tiered design shows its logic most clearly. The officer is trusted to act at once, on his own satisfaction, because the circumstances in his district may not wait for a file to travel to the State capital and return. He is not trusted to determine how long the detention should continue, because duration is a graver question than initiation, engaging the liberty of the person for a period that extends beyond the immediate emergency, and that question is reserved to the government that empowered him. Speed belongs to the district. Continuation belongs to the State.

A proviso adjusts the window in one defined situation. Where the grounds of detention are communicated to the detenu after five days but within ten days, under the exceptional route permitted by Section 8, the period of twelve days in Section 3(4) is to be read as fifteen days. The adjustment is a recognition that the steps in the early life of a detention order are connected, and that an order whose grounds have lawfully taken longer to communicate, for reasons recorded in writing under Section 8, should not be defeated by an approval timetable framed for the ordinary case. The extension is modest, it is tied to a specific and documented departure from the ordinary timetable, and it does not alter the character of the approval requirement itself. The government must still approve, the approval must still come within the window, and the order still lapses if the window closes without approval. What changes is only the length of the window, and it changes because another part of the statute has already authorised a longer time for a connected step in the same sequence.

Section 3(5) adds the third tier of information, directed not downwards to the district or upwards within the State, but outwards to the Union. When a detention order has been made by a State Government, or when an order made by an empowered officer has been approved by a State Government, the State Government must report the fact to the Central Government within seven days, together with the grounds on which the order has been made and such other particulars as, in its opinion, have a bearing on the necessity for the order. The duty arises in both situations, whether the State Government is the original author of the order or the approving authority for an officer's order, and the material that must accompany the report is the material that explains the necessity for the detention, so that the Central Government receives not a bare statistic but the substance of the case as the State Government understands it.

The function of that report must be understood precisely, because it is easily overstated and easily understated. The report to the Centre informs. It does not approve, and the Central Government's receipt of the report does not validate an order that was otherwise defective, just as a delay in reporting does not by itself supply the approval that Section 3(4) reserves to the State Government. Approval and information are different functions in the scheme, assigned to different governments for different reasons. Approval under Section 3(4) is a decision on which the continued force of an officer's order depends, and it belongs to the State Government because that government empowered the officer and bears responsibility for the exercise of the delegated power. Information under Section 3(5) serves the position of the Central Government as the government responsible for the security of India as a whole, for the defence of India and for the relations of India with foreign powers, subjects on which detentions ordered within a State may bear directly, and it ensures that the Centre has a complete picture of the use being made of a central statute across the country. A Centre that is informed can act where action is open to it. A Centre that is not informed cannot, and the seven-day limit is the measure of how promptly Parliament expected that picture to be assembled.

What the Centre can do with the information is completed by Section 14, which confers the power of revocation. Section 14 provides that a detention order may at any time be revoked or modified by the State Government where the order was made by an empowered officer or by the State Government itself, and by the Central Government in any case, whether the order was made by the Central Government or by a State Government. The Centre may therefore revoke even a State order, and that power is the point at which information under Section 3(5) acquires its practical edge, because a government that must be told of every State order within seven days, with its grounds and particulars, is a government placed in a position to consider whether the order should be allowed to stand. The tiered picture is then complete. The district officer initiates, under a written and time-limited empowerment tied to the circumstances of his area. The State Government controls continuation, by approving or withholding approval within twelve days, or fifteen days in the exceptional communication case, and it may revoke or modify an order at any time. The Central Government oversees, receiving reports within seven days and holding a power of revocation that extends across the orders of both tiers below it. Each tier is given the function that matches its position, the officer the function of immediacy, the State the function of approval, and the Centre the function of oversight backed by revocation, and no tier is given the function of another.

The tiering also distributes responsibility in a way that matters when an order is later challenged. Because the officer's order cannot survive without State approval, a challenge to a continued detention will examine both the officer's satisfaction and the government's approval, and a defect in the approval stage cannot be answered by pointing to the regularity of the officer's action at the earlier stage. Because the empowerment itself is limited in duration and in territory and in subject matter, a challenge may reach behind the order to the empowerment, asking whether the officer who passed the order held a subsisting empowerment for that area, for that period and for the ground on which he acted. Because the reporting duties are expressed in mandatory terms with fixed periods, a failure to report forthwith, or to report to the Centre within seven days, is a departure from the statute that the record will either disclose or conceal, and the state of the record on those points is frequently as telling as the grounds themselves. The tiers are therefore not administrative decoration around a single decision. They are a series of decisions, each with its own maker, its own material and its own time limit, and the validity of a detention that continues over weeks and months depends on each of them having been taken as the statute directs.


## The NSA Compliance Clock

A detention under the National Security Act is governed from its first hour by a sequence of dated obligations, and the sequence is tight enough, and the consequences of missing a step serious enough, that the scheme is best understood as a clock. Each obligation has its own starting point, its own period and its own actor, and the periods overlap and interlock, so that a delay at one stage compresses the time available at the next and may defeat a stage that would otherwise have been completed comfortably. The table that follows, The NSA compliance clock, sets out the principal steps in the order in which they fall due, the requirement that the statute imposes at each step, the time limit that governs it, and the consequence that the courts and the statute attach to a failure to meet it.

| Step | Statutory requirement | Time limit | What goes wrong when it is missed |

The clock begins with the order itself, and although Section 3 fixes no period within which an order must be made after the conduct relied upon, the absence of a stated period is not an absence of discipline. The discipline at this step is supplied by the requirement of a live and proximate link, which has already been examined in connection with the grounds, and which operates in practice as a limit on the age of the material that an order may use. An authority that reaches back to incidents long past, without material showing a continuing propensity in the interval, has allowed the clock to run against it before the order is even signed, because the staleness of the grounds is measured from the dates of the incidents to the date of the order. The measurement is unforgiving in practice, because the interval between the last incident and the order is itself a fact on the face of the record, visible to any court that reads the grounds against their dates. The lesson of the step is that the dates in the grounds are part of the grounds, and an authority must be able to explain the interval between the last incident relied upon and the date on which it decided that detention had become necessary.

The second step is the approval of an officer's order, and it has been described in the discussion of the tiered authority because Section 3(4) is where the requirement lives. Its place on the clock is within twelve days of the making of the order, or fifteen days where the exceptional communication route under Section 8 has been used, and its character is that of a condition of continued force rather than a ratification that can be supplied at leisure. What the step protects is the responsibility of the State Government for a detention it did not itself order. The officer acted on a delegated power, for reasons of local speed, and the approval stage is the mechanism by which the delegating government examines the grounds and the particulars and decides whether the detention should continue at all. A government that approves without reading the material, or that allows the period to pass in the belief that approval can be recorded afterwards with retrospective effect, misunderstands the step, because the statute makes the force of the order itself depend on approval within the period. When the step is missed, the challenge does not need to engage with the merits of the grounds at all. The dates of the order and of the approval, or the absence of an approval, decide the question.

The third step, the report to the Central Government within seven days under Section 3(5), protects a different interest, that of oversight across a statute that operates throughout the country through many governments and many officers. The step is sometimes treated in argument as a formality because the Centre does not approve the order, and that treatment understates its place in the scheme. The report carries the grounds and the particulars bearing on necessity, and it is the material foundation for the Centre's power under Section 14 to revoke an order made by a State Government or by an officer within a State. Oversight without information is an empty power, and information that arrives long after the detention has run its course is of little use to a detenu whose liberty depends on a decision taken while the detention continues. The seven-day period should therefore be read as part of the protective sequence rather than as correspondence between governments, even though the consequence of a breach at this step is argued about in a way that a breach of the approval period is not, since the statute attaches an express lapse to the approval step and does not attach the same express words to the reporting step.

The fourth step is the communication of the grounds to the detenu, and it is the step on which more of the protective scheme depends than on any other. Section 8(1) requires the authority making the order to communicate the grounds to the detenu as soon as may be, ordinarily not later than five days from the date of detention, and in exceptional circumstances, for reasons recorded in writing, not later than ten days from that date. The same provision requires that the detenu be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government. The two duties are connected by design, because a representation made without knowledge of the grounds is a form without substance, and grounds communicated too late to be answered before the Advisory Board considers the case are of diminished value to the person they are meant to protect. Communication is therefore not the service of a document for the completeness of a file. It is the act that sets the detenu's own part in the scheme in motion, and the five-day ordinary limit, with a documented extension to ten days and no further, is Parliament's measure of how quickly that act must occur in a system that dispenses with a charge, a trial and a legal practitioner before the Board.

Section 8(2) qualifies the duty in one respect, by providing that facts which the authority considers it to be against the public interest to disclose need not be disclosed. The qualification is a qualification about facts, and it does not displace the duty to communicate the grounds themselves, which must be stated with sufficient particularity for the detenu to understand the case he has to answer and to direct his representation to it. Grounds that are vague, that recite the language of Section 3 without connecting it to conduct attributed to the detenu, or that withhold the material particulars while disclosing a conclusion, fail the purpose of the step even if they are delivered within five days. The courts have built on this step a body of doctrine about the supply of documents, holding that the non-supply of the documents and material relied on in the grounds disables the representation right, because a detenu who has been told that an incident founds his detention, but who has not been given the statements, reports or records that describe that incident, cannot make an effective representation about it. Delay in communication and non-supply of material are accordingly listed together among the recurring successful grounds of challenge, and they share a common logic, that a right to represent is only as real as the information supplied to support it.

The fifth step is the representation itself, and it is the step at which the clock behaves differently, because the statute fixes the opportunity rather than a number of days. The detenu must be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation, and the corresponding duty on the government is to consider that representation and to decide it with reasonable expedition, independently of the view taken by the detaining authority. The duty of expeditious and independent consideration is established across the detention statutes, and it was stated, for example, in Kamarunnissa v Union of India, decided in 1991 in a matter arising under the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act, in terms that have been applied to detention generally. The absence of a fixed number of days at this step does not make the step unmeasurable. Delay is measured against the circumstances of the case, the length of the representation, the material that had to be examined and the explanation offered for the time taken, and an unexplained delay of a kind that suggests the representation was left unattended will vitiate the detention even though every earlier step was completed within its period. The reason is that the representation is the detenu's principal remedy inside the scheme before the Advisory Board reports, and a government that holds a person in detention while leaving his representation undecided is detaining him in disregard of the one avenue the statute gives him to persuade the detaining power itself.

### Why do NSA cases turn on dates rather than on whether the person is guilty?

Courts do not re-try the detaining authority's subjective satisfaction, so NSA litigation is won and lost on timelines, communication and documents, on whether grounds were supplied in time, papers were furnished, representations were decided promptly, and references and reports met their statutory dates.

The claim in that answer is the organising claim of detention litigation, and it deserves to be understood rather than merely repeated. Guilt, in the sense in which a criminal court uses the word, is not in issue in proceedings that challenge a detention order, because no charge has been framed, no evidence has been led at a trial, and no conviction is under appeal. The detenu may have committed the acts described in the grounds, or he may dispute them, or the acts may be admitted and their characterisation disputed, yet none of those questions is the question before the court in the form in which a trial court would decide it. The detaining authority's satisfaction is subjective, formed on material that the authority was entitled to consider, and the court does not substitute its own satisfaction for that of the authority by weighing the material as if it were hearing the case for the first time. A challenge framed as an invitation to find that the detenu is innocent therefore asks the court to perform a function that the scheme does not assign to it, and it will usually fail for that reason alone, however sympathetic the material may appear.

What the court does examine, with a rigour that compensates for the narrowness of its review of satisfaction, is whether the authority observed the conditions on which the power was conferred. Those conditions are expressed in the statute largely as dated obligations and as duties of communication, and that is why dates dominate the argument. The court asks whether the satisfaction rested on relevant material, whether there is a live and proximate link between the grounds and the object of detention, whether the procedural commands of the statute and of Article 22(5) were obeyed, and whether the representation was considered and decided with reasonable expedition. Each of those questions can be answered from the record by reference to documents and dates, the date of the incidents, the date of the order, the date of service of the grounds, the dates of the documents supplied or withheld, the date of the representation, the date of its disposal, the date of the reference and the date of the report. A record that is complete and consistent on those points will usually sustain an order. A record that is silent, contradictory or late on any one of them gives the court a ground for intervention that does not require it to trespass on the authority's satisfaction at all.

The consequences of that allocation of functions are practical, and they fall on both sides of the litigation. For the detenu and those who act for him, the productive line of enquiry is documentary. The papers that found the order should be called for and compared with the grounds, the dates on each document should be assembled into a chronology, and the chronology should be tested against every period in the statute, because a detention that is vulnerable will usually disclose its vulnerability in the chronology before it discloses it anywhere else. Even the challenges that look closest to the merits, a selective reading of the material relied on or an absence of material covering the interval before the order, turn in the end on what the record contains and what it omits. For the detaining authority, the corresponding lesson is that care in the preparation of the record is not bureaucratic fastidiousness but the substance of legality under this Act, since an order that was justified in the mind of the officer may still be lost because the grounds were served late, a relied-upon document was not supplied, a representation waited too long for a decision, or an approval was recorded after the period had closed. Non-application of mind, another recurring ground of challenge, is proved in the same documentary way, where an order copies material without engaging with it or is passed mechanically on a police proposal, so that the document itself shows that the satisfaction it recites was never genuinely formed.

The sixth step is the reference to the Advisory Board. Section 10 requires the appropriate Government to place before the Board, within three weeks from the date of detention, the grounds on which the order was made, the representation made by the detenu if any, and, where the order was made by an empowered officer, the report made by that officer under Section 3(4). The composition of the reference is significant. The Board does not receive only the case for detention, but also the detenu's answer to it and the officer's own report, so that the material before the Board represents the case as it developed through the earlier steps of the clock rather than the case as it stood on the day the order was signed. The three-week period is long enough to allow a representation to be made and included, and short enough to ensure that independent examination begins while the detention is still at an early stage. A reference delayed beyond three weeks postpones that examination and compresses the period within which the Board must complete its own work, and it leaves the detenu in custody for longer without the scrutiny that the Constitution and the Act make the substitute for the scrutiny of a court at a trial.

The seventh step is the report of the Advisory Board, which Section 11(1) requires to be submitted to the appropriate Government within seven weeks from the date of detention. The Board, constituted under Section 9 of three persons who are, or have been, or are qualified to be appointed as Judges of a High Court, with a chairman who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, considers the material placed before it, may call for further information from the government or from the detenu, and hears the detenu in person if the Board considers it essential to do so or if the detenu desires to be heard. Its report states its opinion in a separate part on whether there is sufficient cause for the detention, and the majority opinion governs where the members differ. The seven-week period marks the outer limit of the first phase of a detention, the phase in which the order operates on the authority of the detaining power and its approval alone. Beyond that point, continuation depends on the opinion of a body that is independent of the detaining authority and composed at a judicial level, and the report is the document that either authorises that continuation or requires the detention to end. Section 11(4) provides that a detenu is not entitled to appear by a legal practitioner before the Board and that the proceedings and the report, other than the part recording the opinion, are confidential, provisions that A K Roy qualified by holding that if the detaining authority or the government takes the aid of a legal practitioner or legal adviser before the Board, the detenu must be allowed the same facility, and that the detenu may be assisted by a friend who is not in truth a legal practitioner.

The eighth step follows from the report, and Section 12 states it as a fork with one discretionary branch and one mandatory branch. If the Board reports that there is sufficient cause for the detention, the government may confirm the order and continue the detention for such period as it thinks fit. If the Board reports that there is no sufficient cause, the government shall revoke the order and release the person forthwith. The difference between may and shall in the two branches is the difference between a power and a duty, and it allocates the final word in a way that reflects the purpose of the Board. A favourable report does not compel the government to continue the detention, because the government retains its own judgment and its power under Section 13 to revoke or modify an order at any time, and it may release the detenu even where the Board has found sufficient cause. An unfavourable report does compel release, because the independent body that the scheme interposes between the detaining authority and a long detention has found the case for detention insufficient, and a government that could override that finding and continue the detention regardless would reduce the reference to an advisory exercise in the weakest sense. Confirmation is therefore a fresh decision, to be taken after the report and on the basis of it, and a detention that continues without confirmation, or that continues after a report of no sufficient cause, has passed beyond the authority that the statute gives.

The ninth and final step is the maximum period, fixed by Section 13 at twelve months from the date of detention. The period runs from the date of detention rather than from the date of the order or the date of confirmation, a choice that prevents the earlier steps of the clock from consuming time that is then added to the total, and that gives the detenu a date, calculable from the beginning, on which the detention must end at the outermost limit. Within that period the government may revoke or modify the order at any earlier time, and Section 14 permits revocation by the State Government or by the Central Government, the Centre being able to revoke even a State order. Section 13 is thus both a ceiling and a reminder that the ceiling is not a target. An order confirmed for a period should be confirmed for the period that the case requires, and the power to revoke earlier exists because the preventive justification for a detention may dissolve long before twelve months have passed, as the apprehended conduct recedes, the circumstances of the area change, or the material on which the satisfaction rested loses its force. A fresh order after expiry or revocation is not barred by Section 14, but where no fresh facts have arisen, the total detention under the later order cannot extend beyond twelve months from the date of detention under the earlier order, a provision that prevents the ceiling from being evaded by the simple device of signing a second order as the first expires.

Read as a whole, the clock explains the character of the National Security Act more faithfully than any general description of it as a strong or a weak law. Its strength lies at the first step, where a government or an empowered officer may detain on a subjective satisfaction, without a charge and without a trial, on grounds that the Supreme Court in A K Roy held must be construed narrowly but declined to strike down for vagueness. Its restraint lies in every step that follows, in the five days for the grounds, the seven days for the report to the Centre, the twelve days for approval, the three weeks for the reference, the seven weeks for the report of a Board composed at a judicial level, the mandatory release that follows an unfavourable report, and the twelve months beyond which no detention under the Act may run. Each period is short in relation to the liberty it governs, and each is enforceable by a court that will quash an order for a failure at a single step without needing to decide whether the detenu would have harmed the interests that Section 3 protects. That is the bargain the statute strikes, and the clock is where the bargain is kept or broken, in dates that can be proved and in documents that either exist or do not.

## The Advisory Board: Review Without a Court

The Advisory Board occupies a strange and deliberate place in the scheme of the National Security Act, 1980. It is the only body outside the executive that must examine a detention before that detention can run its full course, and yet it is not a court, it does not conduct a trial, and its conclusions take the form of an opinion rather than a judgment. That strangeness is not accidental. Preventive detention under the Act rests on the satisfaction of the detaining authority, a satisfaction that is predictive and precautionary in character, formed on material that may include intelligence, police reports and assessments of propensity that would not be tested in the manner of evidence in a prosecution. Article 22 of the Constitution permits such detention, but it surrounds the permission with safeguards, and the central safeguard for any detention that is to last beyond the initial period is reference to an Advisory Board. The Act gives that constitutional design its working form in Sections 9, 10, 11 and 12, and the character of the review those sections create explains both the protection the Board can offer and the limits within which that protection operates. A reader who expects an appeal will misunderstand the Board from the outset, because nothing in the reference invites the Board to substitute its own view of what ought to have been done for the view of the authority that made the order. A reader who expects a rubber stamp will misunderstand it just as badly, because the statute arms the Board with material, with power to seek more material, with a hearing in defined circumstances, and with a report that in one direction binds the government completely.

Section 9 governs composition, and composition is where the design first declares its intentions. The Central Government and each State Government constitute Advisory Boards for the purposes of the Act. Each Board consists of three persons who are, or have been, or are qualified to be appointed as Judges of a High Court. The chairman must be a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court. The language deserves close attention because it sets a floor of legal standing without requiring that the Board be staffed by serving judges. A person qualified to be appointed as a Judge of a High Court may sit, and a person who has held that office may sit, and the chairman may be a serving or a former holder of the office. The choice of that formula ties the Board to the judiciary in qualification and in experience, while keeping it administratively separate from the courts. Its members are not assigned by a Chief Justice under the Act as enacted, they are brought into existence by the government whose detention orders they will examine, and that method of constitution is itself part of the constitutional history taken up in the discussion of Section 3 of the 44th Amendment below. For the working scheme, the point is simpler. The legislature wanted reviewers whose training fitted them to weigh material, to detect irrelevance and to understand the difference between suspicion and ground, and it expressed that want through the language of High Court qualification rather than through any requirement of judicial office at the moment of service.

The route by which a case reaches the Board is fixed by Section 10, and the timetable matters as much as the destination. The appropriate Government must place before the Advisory Board the grounds on which the detention order has been made, the representation made by the detenu if any such representation exists, and, where the order was made by an empowered District Magistrate or Commissioner of Police, the report of that officer. The placement must occur within three weeks from the date of detention. Three consequences follow from the terms of that duty. The first is that the reference is compulsory and not discretionary. The government cannot choose to keep a detention away from the Board and still rely on the longer detention the Act permits, because the reference is the statutory bridge between the initial order and any confirmation that follows. The second is that the file placed before the Board is a defined file. It begins with the grounds, which are the same grounds that Section 8 requires to be communicated to the detenu, so that the Board sees the case in the form in which the detaining authority expressed it, and it extends to the detenu representation, so that the answer to the grounds travels with the grounds themselves. The third is that the officer report, in cases where a subordinate officer made the order, lets the Board see the particulars that were reported upward for approval, and not merely the polished conclusion that survived that process. Time, completeness and identity of material are therefore built into the reference itself.

Section 11 then prescribes how the Board proceeds and how quickly it must finish. The Board considers the material placed before it. It may call for such further information as it considers necessary, whether from the appropriate Government or from any person connected with the matter or from the detenu through the appropriate Government, because the section contemplates that the initial file may be thin, ambiguous or incomplete on a point that bears on sufficient cause. It must submit its report to the appropriate Government within seven weeks from the date of detention. The seven week limit has a practical significance that is easy to miss. It compresses the whole review into the opening phase of a detention whose maximum confirmed length under Section 13 is twelve months from the date of detention, so that the Board speaks early, while the question is still whether detention should continue at all, and not late, when release would be little more than an acknowledgement that time had already done the executive work. The report must state the opinion of the Board in a separate part, and where the members differ, the opinion of the majority governs. That separate part device separates conclusion from reasoning process, and it connects directly with the confidentiality rule that follows in the same section.

The hearing occupies a carefully qualified position inside that procedure. The Board hears the detenu in person if it considers a personal hearing essential for the purpose of its inquiry, or if the detenu desires to be heard in person. Either trigger suffices. The Board may reach the view on its own assessment that the papers leave a doubt that only the person detained can address, or the detenu may express the desire and thereby activate the hearing. What the detenu receives in that setting is a personal appearance, an opportunity to speak to the material and to explain the conduct, the associations or the incidents on which the grounds rest, in a room where the decision will be formed on the papers and on that explanation together. What the detenu does not receive is the apparatus of a court hearing. There is no charge to be pleaded to, no witness to be confronted by right, no verdict of guilt or innocence to be pronounced. The hearing is an aid to an opinion on sufficient cause, and its form follows that function throughout.

The confidentiality rule completes the procedural picture and cuts in two directions at once. The proceedings of the Advisory Board and its report, except the part in which the Board records its opinion, are confidential. The exception is narrow and deliberate. The opinion part is the operative part, the part that triggers the consequences in Section 12, and it is separated from the rest of the report precisely so that the operative conclusion can be acted upon and communicated as the statute requires while the discussion that led to it remains protected. Confidentiality serves an institutional purpose that the scheme plainly values. Members who must assess sensitive material concerning the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, the security of India, the security of the State, public order or essential supplies and services are expected to speak freely to one another and to record their assessment without turning the report into a public brief for either side. The same confidentiality also imposes a cost that should be stated plainly in any honest account. The detenu who is told only the outcome cannot test the path by which the Board reached that outcome, cannot identify a mistaken premise inside the discussion and cannot frame a further challenge aimed at the reasoning rather than at the result. The Act accepts that cost as part of the price of the Board model, and any criticism of the Board that ignores the trade the section makes will miss why reform proposals have so often concentrated on composition and on assistance rather than on publication of reports.
To understand what kind of review this is, the surest method is to say what it is not and then to locate its actual question with precision. It is not an appeal. An appeal ordinarily reopens the merits, invites the appellate body to correct errors of fact and of law, and ends in an order that affirms, reverses or modifies the decision under challenge. The Board does none of those things in that form. It does not quash the detention order, it does not modify its terms, and it does not pronounce on guilt. It is not a trial either. A trial determines a past allegation on evidence led and tested in open proceedings, under a burden and standard of proof, with a consequence in punishment. The Board determines no allegation and imposes no punishment, and the detention it examines is justified, if it is justified at all, by a forecast of prejudicial conduct rather than by proof of a completed offence. The question the Board answers is whether there is sufficient cause for the detention. That question is addressed to the material as it stood and to the satisfaction formed on it, and the report speaks at the point in time fixed by the reference and the inquiry. Sufficient cause in this setting means that the grounds and the material supporting them provide a basis on which detention for one of the purposes in Section 3 can properly continue, judged as a matter of cause rather than as a matter of proof beyond reasonable doubt or even proof on a balance of probabilities in the civil sense. The Board weighs relevance, connection and sufficiency, and it does so as a reviewing body formed for preventive detention and for nothing else.

The consequences of the report reveal the sharpest feature of the entire design, which is its asymmetry under Section 12. If the Board reports that there is in its opinion no sufficient cause for the detention, the appropriate Government shall revoke the detention order and cause the person to be released forthwith. The language is mandatory. The government has no discretion to disagree, no power to confirm in the teeth of an adverse opinion and no authority to keep the person detained while it reconsiders the matter under the same order. In that direction the opinion binds completely, and the Board functions as a genuine veto on continued detention. If the Board reports that there is sufficient cause, the position reverses. The government may confirm the detention order and continue the detention for such period as it thinks fit, subject to the twelve month maximum in Section 13 and to its continuing power to revoke or modify the order at an earlier stage. A favourable report therefore permits confirmation but does not compel it. The government remains free to release a person even after a report in favour of detention, whether because its own assessment changes, because circumstances alter or because it judges continued detention unnecessary. Detention beyond the Board stage thus always requires two keys turned in sequence, a report of sufficient cause from the Board and a decision to confirm from the government, while release after an adverse report requires no second key at all. The asymmetry is the safeguard, and it is also the limit, because a Board that finds sufficient cause has not ordered detention and a government that confirms has acted on its own responsibility within a permission the Board created.

Section 11(4) introduces the restriction that has attracted the most sustained criticism of the Board procedure. Nothing in Section 11 entitles a detenu to appear by a legal practitioner in any matter connected with the reference made to the Advisory Board. The exclusion is framed as an absence of entitlement rather than as a prohibition addressed to the Board in every circumstance, but its practical effect for a detenu who wishes to engage counsel is direct. The person most affected by the inquiry, held in custody and facing a file prepared by the state, must present the answer in person or rely on the papers already submitted in the representation, without a trained advocate to organise the material, to isolate the irrelevant ground or to press the point of staleness or disconnection in the language of detention law. The reasoning offered for such an exclusion within schemes of this kind is usually stated in terms of informality, speed and the nature of the material. A Board inquiry is meant to be swift and inquisitorial rather than adversarial, its members are themselves legally qualified, and the introduction of counsel is said to risk converting a focused assessment of sufficient cause into contested litigation that the seven week timetable cannot carry. There is also the recurring official concern that sensitive material cannot be handled in a fully adversarial setting without defeating the purpose for which confidentiality was imposed in the first place.

The criticism answers each strand of that reasoning on its own ground, and it deserves to be set out without softening. Informality benefits the party that holds the file, because the government side arrives with the grounds drafted, the particulars assembled and the institutional experience of many references behind it, while the detenu arrives from custody with whatever understanding a single reading of the grounds can provide. Speed is a value for the detained person as well as for the state, yet speed purchased by denying assistance may produce a hearing at which the decisive point is never articulated at all. The legal qualification of Board members can test the government case from the bench, as it were, but a member who asks a searching question is still not the detenu advocate, and the functions of neutral inquiry and partisan presentation are different functions that a well designed procedure ordinarily keeps apart. Equality of arms in this context does not require the full ceremony of a court, it requires only that the person whose liberty is at stake should not be the sole unassisted participant in a legal assessment of legal grounds. That is why the exclusion in Section 11(4), read alone, states the harshest version of the Board model, and why the judgment in A K Roy against Union of India matters so much to the lived operation of the section. The Supreme Court left the text of the exclusion standing, but it softened its edges in two ways that the next section examines in full. A detenu may be assisted by a friend who is not in truth a legal practitioner, and if the detaining authority or the government takes the aid of a legal practitioner or legal adviser before the Board, parity requires that the detenu be allowed the same facility. Those two qualifications do not repeal Section 11(4), and they should not be described as if they did. They discipline its application, and the discipline comes from constitutional principle rather than from any amendment of the section itself.


## A K Roy v Union of India: The Judgment That Set the Terms

No account of the National Security Act can treat A K Roy against Union of India as a case that merely commented on the statute from outside. The judgment was delivered at the very threshold of the Act life, while the detention that gave rise to it had been made under the Ordinance that preceded the Act, and it settled the constitutional terms on which every later detention, every Advisory Board reference and every challenge would be argued. A K Roy was a Marxist Member of Parliament. He was detained under the National Security Ordinance, 1980 by an order of the District Magistrate, Dhanbad, on a public order ground. From that detention came petitions under Article 32 of the Constitution, and the petitions reached far beyond the facts of a single order. They challenged the Ordinance itself, they challenged parts of the Act that replaced it, and they challenged Section 1(2) of the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, the clause that left commencement of that amendment provisions to dates appointed by the Central Government. The case therefore placed three different kinds of question before the Court at the same time. There was a question about the source of detention law, whether an ordinance could supply the law that Article 21 requires. There was a question about the statute on its merits, whether its grounds were too vague to survive and whether its Board procedure denied fairness. And there was a question about an unfinished constitutional amendment, whether a court could compel the executive to bring into force a provision that Parliament had enacted and the President had assented to, but that the government had left unnotified.

The bench matched the scale of those questions. The case was decided on 28 December 1981 by a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court presided over by Chandrachud CJ and sitting with Bhagwati J, Gupta J, Tulzapurkar J and Desai J. Chandrachud CJ wrote the principal judgment for himself, Bhagwati J and Desai J. Tulzapurkar J concurred with the majority on most questions but joined Gupta J on one. Gupta J dissented in part, and his partial dissent, on the ordinance question and on the mandamus question, forms an essential part of the judgment legacy rather than a footnote to it, because it identifies precisely the two points at which the majority settlement was most contestable. The structure of the decision matters for a reader of the Act. On the existence and the validity of the detention power, the majority held the field and the statute survived. On the manner of its exercise, the majority read safeguards into the procedure and into the conditions of custody that the bare text had not spelled out with the same clarity. On the unfinished amendment, the majority refused the remedy the petitioners sought while recording reasoning that has kept the question alive in constitutional discussion ever since, and that refusal is examined in its own terms in the section that follows this one.

### What did A K Roy change about how the National Security Act works?

A K Roy settled the validity of the National Security Act, narrowed the supplies and services ground to publicly specified essentials, created assistance by a friend and parity of legal aid before the Advisory Board, and disciplined detention conditions with enforceable requirements of dignity, segregation from convicts and notice to the household.

The first holding concerned the ordinance itself, and it was foundational in the strict sense that every other holding depended on it. Article 21 provides that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law, and the petitioners' argument required the Court to decide whether an ordinance promulgated by the President counted as law for that purpose at all. Gupta J held that it did not. The majority held that it did. An ordinance, the majority reasoned, is made in the exercise of legislative power, the ordinance making power is legislative in character, and an ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament. It follows that an ordinance stands subject to the same limitations that Part III of the Constitution places on legislation, neither greater nor smaller. It can be tested against the fundamental rights, it can be struck down if it offends them, and it cannot claim any immunity from that test by reason of its executive origin in form. But if it survives that test, it is law within the meaning of Article 21, and a detention ordered under a valid ordinance is a detention under a procedure established by law so far as the source of the procedure is concerned. Roy's detention, made by the District Magistrate, Dhanbad under the Ordinance of 23 September 1980, therefore could not be defeated by the simple proposition that only an Act could authorise preventive detention. The inquiry had to move onward, to the content of the Ordinance and of the Act and to the fairness of what they prescribed.

The second holding upheld Section 1(2) of the 44th Amendment Act. That clause empowered the Central Government to appoint different dates for the commencement of different provisions of the amendment, and the challenge treated that power as if it transferred a constituent function to the executive. The majority rejected that characterisation. The power to notify commencement, the Court held, is not constituent power. Parliament, acting in its constituent capacity, had made the amendment and had itself chosen to leave the timing of commencement to notification, and an executive act that fixes a date under a power the amendment itself confers does not remake the Constitution or alter its text. The distinction is fine but consequential. Constituent power creates the provision. Commencement power activates a provision already created, under a discretion the creating instrument granted. Section 1(2) was therefore valid. That holding, however, answered only the validity of the clause. It did not answer what a court should do when the discretion to activate is exercised by indefinite silence, and on that separate mandamus question the majority took the position recorded in the next section of this article, refusing the direction while expressing a difficulty that no reader of the judgment can honestly overlook.

The third holding addressed vagueness, and it is the holding that most directly governs the daily interpretation of Section 3. Sections 3(1) and 3(2) authorise detention to prevent conduct prejudicial to the defence of India, to the relations of India with foreign powers, to the security of India, to the security of the State, to the maintenance of public order and to the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. The petitioners contended that expressions of that breadth left the citizen without fair notice of what conduct would attract detention and left the detaining authority with a discretion too wide to be lawful. The majority declined to strike the provisions down for vagueness. Expressions such as defence of India and security of India, the Court accepted, cannot be defined with precision, because the realities they name do not admit of an exhaustive verbal formula fixed in advance. A statute that attempted a complete catalogue of threats would fail by omission, and the Constitution itself uses language of comparable generality in this field. Yet the refusal to invalidate came with an instruction that is as much a part of the holding as the refusal itself. Courts, the majority directed, must strive to construe such words narrowly and to restrict the application of the Act to as few situations as possible. The breadth of the text was therefore to be contained by the discipline of construction in case after case. A detaining authority that treats public order as if it covered every breach of the peace, or security of the State as if it covered every serious crime, acts against the spirit of that instruction, and a court reviewing such an order has in A K Roy itself the warrant for a narrow reading.
The fourth holding took one ground out of that general treatment and subjected it to a specific condition, because the majority plainly regarded it as the ground most exposed to abuse through openness of language. Section 3(2) permits detention to prevent a person acting in a manner prejudicial to the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. Without more, the phrase leaves two questions in executive hands at the same time, the question whether a supply or service is essential and the question whether the conduct of the person detained is prejudicial to its maintenance. The Court upheld the ground only as read down. Detention on the supplies and services ground is valid only where the supplies or services said to be essential to the community have been specified publicly in advance. Prior public specification is the condition that makes the discretion workable in law. It gives the citizen notice of the field within which the extraordinary power may be used, it gives the detaining authority a defined list against which the grounds must be framed, and it gives a reviewing court an objective reference point that does not depend on accepting the authority's assertion made after the event. If no such specification was made publicly before the detention, the ground cannot carry the order. Reading down of that kind preserves the provision by narrowing it, and it should be distinguished from a mere exhortation to use a power carefully. An exhortation can be ignored without legal consequence. The A K Roy condition is a condition of validity, and an order that disregards it is vulnerable on that ground alone.

The fifth group of holdings concerned representation before the Advisory Board, and it built, step by step, the compromise that Section 11(4) still operates under. The starting point was unqualified. A detenu has no right to be represented by a legal practitioner before the Advisory Board. The text of Section 11(4) denied the entitlement, and the majority declined to read a contrary entitlement into the Constitution for proceedings of this character. But the judgment refused to leave the matter at that starting point, because two situations exposed the unfairness of an absolute bar. The first was the situation of the detenu alone. A person taken from a cell to the room where the Board sits may be tongue-tied or confused, may fail to follow the sequence of the grounds and may be unable to present an explanation that the person could have given with modest help. The bar on legal practitioners, the Court held, does not prevent the detenu from being assisted by a friend, provided the friend is not in truth a legal practitioner. The qualification matters. The friend route cannot be used as a device to smuggle counsel into the room under another name, and a Board is entitled to satisfy itself that the person assisting is genuinely a friend. Within that limit, the entitlement is real. The detenu who asks for the help of a friend is entitled to that help, and a refusal that leaves the detenu to flounder unaided would contradict the judgment even though it followed the literal words of the section. The second situation was the situation of unequal assistance. If the detaining authority or the government takes the aid of a legal practitioner or a legal adviser before the Board, the detenu must be allowed the same facility. The source of that rule is Article 14 and its guarantee of equality before the law. The state cannot arm its own presentation with professional skill while invoking Section 11(4) to keep the other side unassisted. Parity does not create a general right to counsel where the section has denied one. It creates a conditional right that springs into existence the moment the government side departs from the unassisted model on which the denial rests.

The sixth holding completed the procedural balance by defining what participation means short of cross examination. The detenu has no right to cross examine witnesses or the persons whose statements or reports supply the material against the detention. That denial follows from the character of the Board inquiry as the majority understood it, an inquiry on material placed before the Board rather than a trial on oral testimony. Yet the detenu is not confined to the representation already filed and to silent attendance at a personal hearing. The detenu may place evidence before the Board, oral and documentary, in support of the case against continued detention. The Board, for its part, regulates its own procedure within the frame of the statute, receiving the material, calling for further information where it considers that necessary and forming its opinion on the whole of what is before it. The resulting procedure is deliberately intermediate. It gives the detenu a voice, assistance by a friend, conditional parity of legal aid and a right to put material forward, while withholding the confrontational instruments of a criminal trial. Whether that intermediate position is generous or inadequate depends on the standard against which it is measured, and A K Roy itself supplies both standards at once, the statutory text that the majority upheld and the constitutional qualifications that the majority attached to its operation.

The final holdings moved from the hearing room to the place of detention, and they are often treated as secondary to the great questions of validity when in truth they govern the daily experience of every person held under the Act. The Court declined to frame a code of detention conditions. It nevertheless held that detenus must be afforded all reasonable facilities for an existence consistent with human dignity. That general holding was given concrete content. Detenus should be permitted to wear their own clothes rather than being reduced to the uniform of the convicted population. They should be permitted to eat their own food, within the arrangements that a place of detention can reasonably sustain. They should have interviews with family members at least once a week, so that custody does not become isolation from the household whose support and information the detenu may need for the representation and for the Board reference itself. They should have reading and writing material according to reasonable requirement, a facility that bears directly on the ability to prepare a case as well as on the bare decency of confinement. Preventive detention, on the logic of these holdings, punishes no offence and convicts of no crime, and its conditions must reflect that character. A regime that treats the detenu in every particular as a convict contradicts the legal basis on which the person is held.

Within those condition holdings, two directions were specific, immediate and unqualified, and the judgment is best closed on them because they leave no room for administrative dilution. Persons detained under the National Security Act must be segregated from convicts. The separation is not a matter of convenience or of available space to be arranged when circumstances allow. It is a direction, founded on the distinction between preventive custody and punitive imprisonment that runs through the whole scheme. A detenu has been tried by no court and sentenced to no term, and confinement among convicts erases in practice a distinction that the law insists upon in theory. Immediately after a person is taken into custody, the members of that person's household must be informed of the detention and of the place where the person is held. That direction also answers a practical reality that the papers in a case can easily conceal. A household that does not know where its member has been taken cannot visit, cannot supply clothes or food, cannot instruct the friend who may assist before the Board and cannot even frame a representation with confidence about the custody to which it is addressed. Information to the household is therefore not a courtesy added to a lawful detention. In the A K Roy settlement it is part of what makes the detention regime lawful in its operation, and a failure to give that information promptly stands as a breach of a direction of the Constitution Bench that decided the validity of the Act itself.

## The Notification Gap: Section 3 of the 44th Amendment

Behind the Advisory Board described in the first section of this segment stands an amendment that was enacted, assented to and then left unactivated, and the distance between the Board that Section 3 of the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 designed and the Board that Section 9 of the National Security Act constitutes is the distance between a reform Parliament completed on paper and a reform no government has chosen to commence. Section 3 would have substituted a new Article 22(4) in the Constitution. Under the substituted clause, no law providing for preventive detention would authorise the detention of a person for a longer period than two months unless an Advisory Board had reported, before the expiration of that period of two months, that there was in its opinion sufficient cause for the detention. The threshold would therefore have fallen from three months to two months, a change of one month in words and a change of real substance in practice, because every week of detention before independent review is a week in which the executive satisfaction operates without the check the Constitution itself regards as the condition of longer custody. The composition of the Board would have changed with the timetable. The Board would have to be constituted in accordance with the recommendations of the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court. Its chairman would have to be a serving judge of the appropriate High Court, and the other members would have to be serving or retired judges of any High Court. Judicial office, present or past, would have replaced mere qualification for judicial office as the standard for every seat, and the voice of the Chief Justice would have entered the constitution of the body that reviews the executive detention.

Each element of that design answers a weakness in the unamended text that a reader can identify from the operative scheme alone. The older Article 22(4), which remains the governing text, permits the longer period to run to three months before the Board opinion becomes the condition of continuance, and it describes Board membership in the weaker language of persons who are, have been, or are qualified to be appointed as Judges of a High Court. The National Security Act follows that weaker language in Section 9, as the first section of this segment recorded. A person who has never held judicial office at all may therefore sit on an NSA Board, provided that person is qualified for appointment, and the chairman may be a former judge rather than a serving judge. Section 3 of the 44th Amendment would have closed both openings. It would have required serving or retired judges throughout, a serving judge in the chair, a constitution of the Board shaped by the recommendations of the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court, and a report of sufficient cause obtained within two months rather than three. The reform was not a marginal adjustment of procedure. It redistributed authority over the reviewing body away from the government whose orders were under review and toward the head of the High Court judiciary in the relevant jurisdiction, and it shortened the period during which detention could rest on executive satisfaction alone.

The chronology of non commencement is short to state and difficult to justify in the terms the majority itself used in A K Roy. The Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 received the assent of the President on 30 April 1979. Section 1(2) of that Act left the commencement of its provisions to dates appointed by the Central Government by notification, and different dates could be appointed for different provisions. Every other provision of the Amendment was in due course brought into force. Section 3 alone was left unnotified. No government has brought Section 3 into force at any point after that assent. The result is a constitutional provision that exists as enacted law in the amendment Act and yet has no operation in the Constitution as it is applied, while the older Article 22(4) that Section 3 was meant to replace continues to govern every preventive detention reference in the country. The NSA itself bears the mark of the choice in its own drafting history. Clause 9 of the National Security Ordinance, 1980 had constituted Advisory Boards in conformity with the amended Article 22 as Section 3 would have made it, following the stricter model before that model had any operative force. Section 9 of the Act then dropped that conformity and reverted to the unamended Article 22(4). The Ordinance treated the reform as the proper pattern for a new detention statute. The Act, passed by Parliament within months of the Ordinance, discarded the pattern and returned to the weaker text that the unnotified amendment had been designed to supersede.

It was against that background that the petitioners in A K Roy asked the Supreme Court to issue a mandamus directing the Central Government to bring Section 3 into force, and the majority declined to issue a mandamus compelling the Central Government to bring Section 3 into force, the refusal that [the 44th Amendment Act 1978 explained](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/) places at the centre of the amendment's unfinished history. The reasoning of the refusal proceeded from the nature of the commencement power itself. Commencement, the majority held, is a matter for the executive under the scheme that Section 1(2) created, a clause whose validity the Court had upheld. Parliament had prescribed no objective standard by which a court could determine the date on which Section 3 ought to commence, and in the absence of such a standard the Court would not substitute its own judgment for the judgment of the government on a question of timing entrusted to the government. A mandamus ordinarily commands the performance of a duty that the law has imposed in terms a court can enforce. The majority treated the notification of Section 3 as a discretion awaiting exercise rather than as a duty already owing on a date the law had fixed, and on that characterisation the remedy failed. The statute book was left with a valid commencement clause, an uncommenced amendment and a judicial refusal that followed, on its own premises, from the distinction between the two.

The refusal, however, was not recorded in language of approval, and any account that stops at the formal holding misrepresents the judgment. The majority recorded its difficulty in seeing what practical obstacle could persist two and a half years after assent to stand in the way of bringing Section 3 into force. That observation was not a stray expression of impatience. Two and a half years is the measure the Court itself chose, and it connects the legal question of discretion with the factual question whether a discretion of that duration, exercised by silence, could still be described as a discretion about timing at all. The majority also addressed the drafting choice inside the detention statute directly. The Ordinance, the Court observed, had rightly modelled its Advisory Board clause on Section 3 of the 44th Amendment, while the Act had wrongly discarded that model. Rightly and wrongly are words of evaluation that sit uneasily beside a refusal to direct, and the unease is the point. The majority would not order the executive to commence the amendment, it doubted that any practical obstacle explained the delay, and it held that the government's own first draft of the detention law had chosen the better Board and that the enacted text had then abandoned that choice. The constitutional position after A K Roy therefore contained a reform that the Court regarded as the right model, a delay the Court could not explain, and a remedy the Court withheld.

Gupta J would have crossed the line that the majority declined to cross, and his dissent must be given in full because it joins the two questions on which he parted from the majority into a single view of executive power over personal liberty. On the ordinance question, Gupta J held that an ordinance is not law within the meaning of Article 21. Where the majority treated the ordinance making power as legislative in character and an ordinance as having the same force as an Act, subject to the same limitations in Part III, Gupta J denied the equivalence at the point that matters most for detention, the source of a procedure by which a person may be deprived of personal liberty. On the commencement question, he would have issued a mandamus directing the Central Government to notify Section 3 of the 44th Amendment into force within two months. The period he fixed is itself an answer to the majority objection that Parliament had supplied no standard and no date. A court that can see an assented amendment held in suspense without explanation can supply a reasonable period for performance without drafting the amendment anew, and two months was the period Gupta J considered reasonable for the performance of an act that required nothing beyond a notification. His reasoning rested on the character of the discretion that indefinite non commencement creates. The executive, in his view, could not be left with unfettered discretion to keep an assented constitutional amendment in suspense indefinitely. A power to appoint a date is a power to choose among dates. It is not, without far clearer language, a power to decide that a provision enacted through the constituent process shall never operate at all. To read Section 1(2) as conferring that larger power would let the executive defeat by omission a change that Parliament had made and the President had assented to, and it would invert the relationship between the constituent authority and the authority entrusted with a notification.

The practical meaning of the gap Gupta J identified is visible in every NSA reference that has been made under the unamended Article 22(4) in place of the substituted clause that Section 3 promised. A Board constituted under the Act may include persons who are merely qualified to be Judges of a High Court and who have never held judicial office. Its chairman may be a person who has been a Judge of a High Court rather than a serving judge of the appropriate High Court. No recommendation of the Chief Justice of the appropriate High Court shapes its constitution. The government that makes or approves the detention order also constitutes the body that will report on sufficient cause, under a text that the 44th Amendment had already judged inadequate and replaced in everything except operative force. The detention may run for the longer period that the older Article 22(4) allows before the Board opinion becomes decisive, measured against the three month threshold of the unamended clause rather than the two month threshold of the clause that was never commenced. None of those features is a drafting accident discovered long after enactment. Each was addressed by Section 3, each was left untouched when Section 3 was left unnotified, and each was preserved for the NSA when Section 9 of the Act discarded the conformity that Clause 9 of the Ordinance had adopted. The notification gap is therefore not a curiosity of constitutional history that floats above the statute. It is the reason the Advisory Board that reviews an NSA detention has the composition it has, sits on the timetable it sits on and derives its authority from the constitutional text it derives it from, and it remains, on the facts recorded in A K Roy itself, a reform completed by Parliament and withheld by notification.

## How NSA Orders Fail: The Quashing Record

A detention order under the National Security Act does not fall because a court disagrees with the detaining authority about danger. Courts reviewing detention orders do not re-try the merits, and they do not substitute their own assessment of a person or an incident for the assessment recorded by the authority empowered to make the order. The review is narrower, stricter in its own way, and directed at a different question altogether. The authority claims a subjective satisfaction, formed on material placed before it, that detention is necessary to prevent a person acting in a manner prejudicial to one of the grounds in Section 3. The court asks whether that satisfaction rests on relevant material at all, whether there is a live and proximate link between the grounds relied on and the object the detention is meant to serve, whether the procedural commands of the statute and of Article 22(5) were obeyed, and whether the detenu representation was considered and decided with reasonable expedition. If the answer on any of those questions fails in a material way, the order fails with it, however grave the underlying allegations may sound. If the answers hold, the order may survive even though another authority, reading the same papers, might have reached a different view. That division of labour explains both the strength and the limit of judicial review in this field. The court polices honesty, relevance, connection and procedure. It does not become the detaining authority.

The narrowness of that review is often misunderstood as weakness, and the misunderstanding matters because it distorts what a successful challenge looks like. A habeas corpus petition against preventive detention is not an appeal against a conviction and it is not a trial of the incidents described in the grounds. There is no prosecution case to prove beyond reasonable doubt and there is no defence case in the criminal sense to answer it. What there is, instead, is a paper record that must bear its own weight. The grounds must show what material was considered. The material must be capable of supporting the apprehension recorded. The apprehension must connect to a statutory object, whether the defence of India, the security of India, the security of the State, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. The sequence of dates must show that the commands built around communication, representation, reference to the Advisory Board and confirmation were observed within the time the statute allows. A challenger who treats the case as a general invitation to argue innocence will usually miss the points on which orders actually fall. A challenger who reads the record closely, tests each date, compares the grounds with the documents supplied, and asks whether the satisfaction recorded is the satisfaction of the authority named in the order, works with the grain of the doctrine. Most quashing judgments, read carefully, are judgments about discipline rather than about sympathy.

Delay in communicating the grounds is the most mechanical of the recurring failures and, for that reason, one of the least forgiving. Section 8(1) requires the authority to communicate the grounds of detention to the detenu as soon as may be, ordinarily not later than five days from the date of detention, and in exceptional circumstances, for reasons recorded in writing, not later than ten days. The same provision requires that the detenu be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government. Communication is therefore not a formality that follows detention at leisure. It is the act that makes the constitutional and statutory right of representation usable, and the clock that governs it is written into the Act itself. Where the grounds reach the detenu beyond the ordinary limit without the exceptional route being properly invoked and recorded, or beyond the outer limit altogether, the detention carries a defect at its foundation. The authority cannot answer such a defect by pointing to the seriousness of the grounds themselves, because seriousness was never the question the time limit was designed to test. The limit tests whether a person deprived of liberty without charge was told promptly why, in a form that allowed an answer to be made while an answer could still matter. An explanation offered long afterwards, however detailed, does not restore the days during which the detenu held an order without its grounds, any more than a later hearing restores an opportunity that was denied when it fell due.

Non-supply of the documents and material relied on in the grounds produces the same kind of failure through a less visible route. The grounds may be communicated on time and may be written in clear language, yet the representation right is disabled if the detenu is not given the documents on which the grounds rest. A ground that summarises a report, a statement or an incident record asks the detenu to answer a conclusion rather than the material behind it. Without the relied upon documents, the detenu cannot show that a passage was read out of context, that a date does not bear the meaning assigned to it, that a person named did not say what the summary attributes, or that an incident relied on had already been examined and closed in another proceeding whose record matters. The defect is therefore not a complaint about courtesy or completeness in the abstract. It goes to whether the earliest opportunity of representation promised by Section 8 was a real opportunity or only a printed one. Courts treat the point with corresponding seriousness because the Advisory Board and the Government, when they consider the representation, work from the same body of material. If that material was withheld from the one person with the strongest reason to test it, the later consideration proceeds on a record that has never been answered in full. Section 8(2) does allow facts considered against the public interest to be withheld from disclosure, but that protection covers the limited class it describes. It does not convert every relied upon document into a confidential one, and it does not excuse silence about material that formed the ordinary basis of the satisfaction recorded.

Delay in considering the representation attacks the detention from the opposite side of the same right. Communication and supply of documents open the door to a representation; consideration is what the Government owes once the representation walks through it. The duty is one of expeditious and independent consideration, established across the detention statutes and stated, for example, in Kamarunnissa v Union of India, decided in 1991 in a matter under the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act. The detenu writes to the appropriate Government, the very authority that made or approved the order, and asks it to reconsider. That structure already places a heavy burden of good faith on the deciding authority, because the reviewer and the author are connected. The least the doctrine can demand, and what it does demand, is that the representation be taken up promptly, considered on its own contents rather than merely forwarded through a chain of noting, and decided without unexplained delay. A representation that lies unattended while the detention continues day by day teaches a damaging lesson about the value the system assigns to its own safeguard. The harm is not cured by an eventual rejection that recites that the representation was carefully considered, if the dates show that careful consideration began only after a long and unexplained interval. Nor is the harm answered by treating the Advisory Board reference as a substitute, because the Board and the Government perform different functions under Sections 10, 11 and 12. The Board reports whether there is sufficient cause. The Government must still decide the representation addressed to it, and must do so with reasonable expedition, as a separate obligation owed directly to the detenu.

Non-application of mind is broader than any single procedural lapse and, in practice, it is the ground that gathers several smaller defects into one finding. An order shows non-application of mind where it copies material without engaging with it, where it is passed mechanically on a police proposal, where the satisfaction recorded in the order does not match the material said to produce it, or where the authority ignores a part of the record that plainly qualifies the part it relied on. The point is not that the authority must write a judgment. A detention order is an executive act and its reasoning is expected to be concise. The point is that the satisfaction must be recognisably the authority's own satisfaction, formed after looking at the material as a whole. A proposal may be placed before a District Magistrate or a Commissioner of Police empowered under Section 3(3), and the supporting papers may be assembled by subordinate officers, but the decision to detain must be taken by the empowered authority after application of mind to those papers. Where the language of the order follows the proposal without discrimination, where incidents are reproduced with their errors intact, where a conclusion is asserted that the enclosed material does not support, or where an obvious countervailing fact in the same file finds no mention at all, a court is entitled to conclude that the form of satisfaction was observed while its substance was not. That conclusion, once reached, is difficult to repair by affidavit, because the affidavit itself becomes another later document asking the court to accept a process that the contemporaneous record does not show.

Staleness raises a question of connection in time. Preventive detention looks forward. It detains not for what a person has done in the sense that punishment does, but because the authority apprehends what the person may do if left at liberty, judged from conduct and circumstances said to reveal a propensity. Past incidents can ground such an apprehension only where they remain live enough to support it. Where the incidents relied on are too remote, where a long interval separates them from the order, and where nothing in the intervening period bridges that interval with continuing conduct or a credible explanation for the delay in acting, the link between ground and object weakens until it breaks. The State then faces an uncomfortable choice of explanations. If the conduct was urgent enough to require detention without trial, the delay in making the order requires an account. If the delay was reasonable, the claim of urgent propensity requires a stronger account. Staleness challenges force that choice into the open. They also protect against a particular misuse to which preventive powers are prone, namely the assembly of an order from an old file when a fresh difficulty arises, so that detention answers a present administrative problem with past material gathered for another purpose. A live and proximate link is the doctrinal answer to that temptation. It insists that the past must still speak to the future the order claims to prevent, in language the record can support at the date the order was actually made.

Confusion of categories, above all the treatment of a law and order incident as a public order disturbance, goes to the statutory ground itself. In Ram Manohar Lohia v State of Bihar, decided in 1966, the concepts were arranged in concentric circles, with law and order as the largest circle, public order within it, and the security of the State as the innermost circle. Every public order disturbance affects law and order in a general sense, but not every breach of law and order disturbs public order. In Arun Ghosh v State of West Bengal, decided in 1970, the test was developed in terms of whether an act disturbs the even tempo of the life of the community. A serious offence against an individual, even a violent one, may remain a law and order matter for investigation, charge and trial. It becomes a public order matter, for detention purposes, only where its reach and effect disturb the community tempo in the sense the doctrine describes, so that ordinary prosecution, however successful, is not the whole answer the law allows the authority to consider. Where the grounds describe private quarrels, isolated assaults or offences with no shown effect beyond their immediate parties, and then assert a public order conclusion without material that carries the incident outward into community life, the order rests on a category the record has not earned. The error is not semantic. Section 3(2) separates the security of the State, the maintenance of public order and the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community as distinct heads, and the Explanation to that provision even excludes a ground that belongs under another named statute. Precision about heads matters because the head chosen defines what material is relevant, what apprehension must be shown, and what the Advisory Board is later asked to find sufficient cause for.

Standing behind all of these failure modes is the warning given in Rekha v State of Tamil Nadu, decided in 2011, where the Supreme Court warned that preventive detention is an exceptional measure that cannot be used as a substitute for the ordinary criminal process or to bypass bail. The warning identifies the temptation that gives many defective orders their shape. Criminal process is demanding by design. It requires investigation, charge, evidence, disclosure, trial and proof, and it allows the accused to seek bail and to test the prosecution case in open court. Where that process seems slow, where bail seems likely, or where a trial seems uncertain, detention without trial can appear to an administrator as the shorter road to the same incapacitation. Rekha denies that the shorter road is a lawful one. If the ordinary process can answer the conduct, detention is not justified merely because it is more convenient or more certain in its immediate effect. If bail has been granted or is likely, that fact does not by itself authorise an executive order achieving continued custody by another name. The doctrine does not deny that some conduct may properly ground both prosecution and a detention order, a question taken up separately in this article. It denies substitution. The detaining authority must be able to show a preventive object that prosecution does not exhaust, supported by relevant material and a live link, rather than a punitive or custodial object dressed in preventive language.

Parliament gave the State one statutory answer to single ground failure, and its limits deserve careful reading. Section 5A, inserted by the National Security (Second Amendment) Act, 1984, makes the grounds severable. An order resting on two or more grounds does not fail merely because one ground is vague, non-existent or irrelevant, and the order is deemed to have been made on the surviving grounds alone. Severability changes the arithmetic of challenge. A detenu can no longer expect an order supported by several independent grounds to fall in full because the weakest ground among them cannot stand. Each ground must be examined, and an order will survive on those that remain relevant, live and properly supported. Severability does not, however, repair the other defects described in this section. It does not cure delay in communicating the grounds as a whole, it does not supply documents that were withheld, it does not excuse delay in considering a representation, and it does not convert a law and order incident into a public order disturbance by placing it beside a stronger ground. It also does not answer a finding that the satisfaction itself was mechanical, because a mechanical satisfaction infects the order rather than merely one compartment within it. Properly understood, Section 5A rewards an authority that pleads several genuinely independent and properly supported grounds and it penalises a challenge that attacks only the margins. It offers no protection to an authority that pleads volume in place of care.

The case of Dr Kafeel Khan shows how several of these doctrines meet in a single record, and why a paper based challenge can succeed without the court trying the underlying speech as a criminal charge. Dr Khan was detained under the Act by an order of the District Magistrate, Aligarh, dated 13 February 2020, founded on a speech at Aligarh Muslim University in December 2019. On 1 September 2020 the Allahabad High Court, in a Bench of Govind Mathur CJ and Saumitra Dayal Singh J, quashed the order and both extensions. The Court held that a complete reading of the speech disclosed no call to hatred or violence and that the District Magistrate had made a selective reading of a few phrases, ignoring the speech's true intent. The Court also found no material showing prejudicial conduct between the speech and the order. Each element of that holding maps onto the doctrine already described. Selective reading is a form of non-application of mind, because the material considered was the speech as a whole and the satisfaction rested on fragments detached from the whole that alone could discipline their meaning. The absence of material between the speech in December 2019 and the order on 13 February 2020 raised the question of a live and proximate link, because the authority had to show why a speech already delivered required preventive detention at the date the order was made. The quashing of the extensions followed the fate of the order itself, since an extension continues a detention whose foundation has been found defective and cannot supply a foundation of its own.

The sequel gives the case its full weight. The Supreme Court declined to interfere on 17 December 2020, leaving the Allahabad High Court judgment undisturbed. By the date the order was quashed on 1 September 2020, the detention had lasted over six months. Those six months are the part of the record that no remedy restored. Quashing released the detenu and vindicated the challenge, and refusal to interfere closed the litigation in his favour, but neither result returned the period spent in custody under an order that the High Court found rested on a selective reading and on no intervening material of the kind required. That asymmetry runs through preventive detention law and should be stated without embellishment. The remedies are real. Habeas corpus can end a detention, a Board can report no sufficient cause and require revocation under Section 12, and a Government can revoke at any earlier time under Sections 13 and 14. None of those remedies operates retrospectively to make an unlawful or unsustainable detention unhappen. The consequence for practice is severe and practical. The challenge must be built early, from the first documents, because the value of a correct judgment declines with every week the detention it corrects has already run. That is also why the assembling and keeping of the paper record matters far beyond the courtroom, since journalists and lawyers who gather the grounds, the relied upon documents, the representation and the dates on which each step occurred are preserving the only material from which a timely challenge can be constructed, a task in which careful habits for protecting sources and sensitive material carry their own discipline, as in [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) where the protection of a source and the integrity of the record are treated as part of the same responsibility. A missing date, a lost copy of the grounds or a representation whose filing cannot be proved can each weaken a challenge as surely as a weak legal argument, because the doctrine turns so heavily on sequence and supply. The quashing record, taken as a whole, therefore teaches a single lesson from several directions. Orders fail where the record is late, incomplete, unconsidered, mechanical, stale or misclassified, and they survive where the authority treated the safeguards not as obstacles around the power but as the conditions on which the power was granted in the first place.

## The Punjab Chapter: Section 14A and the Amending Acts

The Punjab chapter of the Act is a study in how an emergency exception enters a general statute, expands inside it for a defined purpose, and then leaves without rewriting the scheme that surrounded it. The National Security (Amendment) Act, 1984, Act 24 of 1984, inserted Section 14A, creating a special regime for persons detained in the disturbed areas in Punjab and Chandigarh in connection with terrorist and disruptive activities. The special regime did not merely add a new ground to Section 3. It altered the procedural balance of the Act for the cases it covered, and it did so at precisely the points where the general scheme places its discipline, namely the Advisory Board, the time allowed before the Board opinion must be obtained, and the maximum period of detention itself.

Under the general scheme, the appropriate Government must place the case before the Advisory Board within three weeks from the date of detention under Section 10, and the Board must report within seven weeks from that date under Section 11. Under the modified Sections 10 and 11 as they applied through Section 14A, the Board reference and report timelines were stretched to a reference within four months and two weeks and a report within five months and three weeks. The change was not a modest extension for administrative convenience. It multiplied the period during which a detenu could remain in custody before the independent Board safeguard completed its work. Section 14A also permitted detention for up to six months without obtaining the Board opinion at all, against the general design in which Board scrutiny belongs to the earlier part of a detention and governs whether confirmation may follow. The maximum period rose from twelve months to two years, doubling the outer limit that Section 13 sets for a confirmed order under the general scheme. Taken together, the three changes moved in one direction. Executive custody lengthened, external scrutiny arrived later, and the point at which the State had to justify continued detention to a Board receded by months rather than by days.

The provision did not remain in its first form. The National Security (Amendment) Act, 1987, Act 27 of 1987, replaced Section 14A in a revised form, and the National Security (Amendment) Act, 1988 adjusted its terminal date. The provision applied only to orders made before a date in June 1989 and has long been spent, leaving the general scheme untouched. That ending is as instructive as the insertion. Parliament legislated for a disturbed area regime through a section marked by territory, by subject matter and by a terminal date, and successive amendments revised and then closed the window during which orders could be made under it. When the window closed, the Act did not require reconstruction. Sections 10, 11 and 13 resumed for later orders the shape they had always retained outside the special regime, and Section 14A remained in the history of the statute as an spent exception rather than as a permanent enlargement of the ordinary power.

The lesson is double sided and should be stated in both its parts. Emergency exceptions enter a general statute most easily when they are framed as limited answers to a named disturbance, confined to a place, a class of activity and a period, because limitation makes the exception easier to defend at the moment of enactment. Once inside, however, such an exception normalises for its class of cases a lower level of procedural protection than the general statute was designed to give, and detenus within the class experience the exception not as an abstraction but as months of additional custody with later Board scrutiny and a doubled maximum. The Punjab chapter shows that an exception can be contained and can end, since Section 14A was revised, given a terminal date, confined to orders made before a date in June 1989 and left spent by the end of the decade. It also shows that containment depends on the terminal machinery being drafted, maintained and honoured, rather than on an assumption that extraordinary powers will fade on their own once the disturbance that justified them recedes. A reader who compares the general scheme with the Section 14A variant learns to ask, whenever a special detention regime is proposed in any form, which of the ordinary safeguards is being moved, by how much, for whom, and until when. Those four questions, applied to the Punjab amendments, disclose the whole character of the change more reliably than the label of emergency alone ever could.

## Detention Alongside Prosecution: No Bail, No Charge, No Trial

The vocabulary of criminal process does not transfer to preventive detention, and much confusion about the Act begins with an attempt to make it transfer. A criminal case begins with an allegation of an offence already committed, proceeds through investigation and charge, and ends, unless it is withdrawn or compromised in a manner the law permits, in acquittal or conviction after trial. Bail belongs inside that structure. It is the mechanism by which a person accused of an offence, whose guilt has not been proved, is released from custody while the proceeding continues, on conditions designed to secure attendance and protect the process. Preventive detention under the National Security Act has no corresponding structure into which bail could be fitted. There is no charge framed by a court, no prosecution case awaiting proof, no trial date toward which custody is merely interim, and no verdict that will retrospectively classify the detention as justified or unjustified in the way a conviction or acquittal classifies an arrest and remand. The detention is itself the operative act, ordered by the executive on subjective satisfaction, limited by the Act's own clock, reviewed by an Advisory Board, supervised by the Government on a representation, and tested by the courts on the narrow grounds described in the quashing doctrine. To ask where bail fits inside that design is therefore to ask the wrong question in a revealing way. The release mechanisms that exist are revocation, a Board report of no sufficient cause followed by mandatory release, temporary release under Section 15, expiry of the confirmed period, and quashing by a court. None of them is bail, and treating any of them as bail obscures the conditions on which each actually depends.

### Is there any bail under the National Security Act?

There is no bail under the National Security Act because there is no charge to answer and no criminal proceeding in which bail could be granted. The order is preventive, executive in origin, and challenged through representation and habeas corpus, not through the bail provisions of criminal procedure.

The absence of bail is best understood through [preventive detention compared with ordinary arrest](/2010/10/01/preventive-detention-vs-arrest-india/), because arrest in a criminal case leads into a process whose later stages can correct custody, while detention leads into a separate process whose safeguards must be invoked on their own terms. A detenu cannot file a bail application that answers the detention order, cannot offer sureties to a criminal court as a substitute for the detaining authority's satisfaction, and cannot obtain release merely by showing that a court, in a parallel criminal case, would have released an accused on bail. That last point is frequently the hardest for families to accept, because bail in the criminal case feels like a judicial finding that custody is unnecessary. In detention law, it is material that the detaining authority must engage with, and in some records it may weaken the claimed necessity, but it does not operate as an order of release under the Act. Release must still come through a route the Act or the Constitution provides. The practical consequence is that advice in a detention case must begin by separating the files. The criminal file, if one exists, has its own court, its own applications and its own timetable. The detention file has the grounds, the documents, the representation, the Board reference and the confirmation order, each with its own date. Progress in one file does not automatically move the other, and a lawyer who allows the client to believe that a bail hearing will decide the detention has misdescribed both proceedings.

### Can an NSA order run alongside a criminal prosecution for the same acts?

Yes. An order under the National Security Act may lawfully accompany a prosecution founded on the same facts, because prevention and punishment pursue different objects. Acquittal or bail in the criminal case neither validates nor invalidates the detention order automatically, though both records must be engaged with honestly.

Parallel operation follows from the difference in objects. Prosecution punishes a past offence after proof. Detention, where it is lawful, prevents apprehended future conduct that falls within a Section 3 ground. The same incident can therefore figure in both proceedings without logical contradiction, appearing in the criminal case as evidence of an offence charged and in the detention grounds as part of the material from which a propensity and a preventive necessity are inferred. That compatibility has limits, and Rekha marks the most important of them. Parallel operation is lawful where the detention serves a genuine preventive object that the prosecution does not exhaust. It is not lawful as substitution, where the order merely continues custody that the criminal process has refused, or avoids a trial that the prosecution is unwilling or unready to face. The distinction turns on the grounds and the material, not on the bare fact that two proceedings exist. An authority that records why prosecution alone does not answer the apprehended harm, identifies the future facing element in the material, and shows a live link to a statutory head, pleads a preventive case. An authority that reproduces the criminal allegations, adds a conclusion about public order, and offers no preventive element beyond a desire to keep the person in custody, pleads the substitution that Rekha warns against.

The independence of the two proceedings also means that an outcome in one does not mechanically decide the other. An acquittal does not by itself validate a detention order, because acquittal means the prosecution failed to prove the offence charged to the criminal standard, and it says nothing directly about whether the detaining authority held relevant material and formed a lawful satisfaction at the earlier date. An acquittal also does not by itself invalidate the order in every case, for the same reason in reverse, though an acquittal that destroys the factual basis on which the grounds rested, or that rests on a finding that the central incident did not occur as alleged, may leave the detention record gravely weakened and may require the authority to confront that weakness directly. Bail stands in a similar position. The grant of bail is neither a command to the detaining authority nor an irrelevance it may ignore. Where the authority relies on the criminal record, it must engage with that record as it stands in full, including bail orders, acquittals, discharges or the absence of a charge where any of those form part of the material picture, and a satisfaction that proceeds as though a qualifying part of the record did not exist invites a finding of non-application of mind. Engagement, honestly done, does not guarantee that the detention will survive. It guarantees only that the satisfaction challenged in court is the satisfaction the full record produced, which is the minimum the doctrine has always required.

## Custody, Release and the Exits the Act Itself Provides

Detention under the Act is custody under executive direction, and Section 5 gives the appropriate Government the power to regulate the place and conditions of detention. That allocation matters because the detenu is not a convict serving a sentence and is not an undertrial held under a court remand order in the ordinary sense. The place of detention and the conditions imposed there derive from the detention framework itself, supervised by the Government responsible for the order, within the limits the Constitution and the courts impose on the treatment of a person deprived of liberty without trial. A change in the place of detention does not change the identity of the order or restart its clock. The maximum period under Section 13 still runs from the date of detention, the Board timetable still runs from the same date, and the representation still answers the same grounds. Understanding Section 5 in that modest way protects against a common error, namely treating transfer or a change in custody arrangements as though it were a fresh detention requiring a fresh satisfaction. It is administration of an existing order, important to the detenu's daily life and open to challenge where conditions fall below what dignity requires, but distinct from the making, confirmation or revocation of the order itself.

Section 15 provides the Act most flexible exit short of revocation, namely temporary release. The Government may release a detenu temporarily, on conditions and on bond, and may cancel the release. Temporary release recognises that a detention judged necessary as a general matter may still admit exceptions for defined periods or purposes without abandoning the order, and it gives the Government a graduated instrument between continued custody without interruption and outright revocation. The instrument is conditional in its nature. The detenu released under Section 15 remains a person subject to a subsisting detention order, released on the terms imposed, and liable to be returned to custody when the release ends or is cancelled. Failure to surrender as directed is punishable with imprisonment up to two years, or fine, or both, a penalty that converts disregard of the release conditions into a separate offence with its own consequences. The provision should therefore never be described to a detenu as freedom. It is a conditional interruption of custody, valuable precisely because it is real relief from confinement, and hazardous if its terms, dates and surrender obligation are not understood and observed exactly. A Government that uses temporary release carefully, with clear conditions and a clear return date, also creates a record that may inform later decisions about revocation, because compliance with conditions during release is conduct evidence of a practical kind.

Section 14 governs revocation and the making of a fresh order, and it draws a line that protects against serial detention on an unchanged record. Revocation may be by the State Government or the Central Government, and the Centre may revoke even a State order, placing a superior corrective power inside the executive scheme alongside the corrective powers of the Board and the courts. A Government may revoke or modify an order at any earlier time than the maximum period, as Section 13 also records, and revocation ends the detention founded on that order. A fresh order after expiry or revocation is not barred, because circumstances may change and new material may arise that an earlier order could not have considered. Where no fresh facts have arisen, however, total detention under the later order cannot extend beyond twelve months from the date of detention under the earlier order. That cap is the statute answer to the evasion that would otherwise be available, in which an authority allowed one order to lapse or revoked it and immediately made another on the same facts, thereby obtaining by succession a detention longer than the maximum Parliament set for a single course of detention. Fresh facts break the continuity that the cap polices. An unchanged record preserves it. The distinction returns the inquiry, once again, to honesty about the record. An authority asserting a fresh order must be able to identify what is fresh in fact, not merely fresh in drafting, and a challenger examining a second order should compare its grounds with the first order grounds line by line to test whether novelty is claimed where only repetition exists.

Two further provisions complete the picture of the Act's own machinery, one protective and one coercive. Section 16 gives protection for action taken in good faith, shielding officers and Governments from proceedings for acts done honestly under the statute. Good faith protection is not a validation of the detention order itself. An order may be quashed because its satisfaction was defective while an officer who acted under it in good faith remains protected from personal liability for that action, and the two questions should be kept apart. Section 7 supplies the machinery for a person who absconds. Where a person against whom an order has been made absconds or conceals himself so that the order cannot be executed, the Government or the empowered officer may report to a Magistrate and have the proclamation machinery of the criminal procedure code applied. Failure to appear as directed is punishable with imprisonment up to one year, or fine, or both. The provision closes an obvious gap, since a detention power that depended entirely on voluntary surrender would fail at its first step, while also showing the boundary of the Act method. Execution borrows the coercive machinery of criminal procedure under Section 4 and Section 7, even though the detention that follows execution is not criminal custody. This general account of the statute is legal information and not advice on any individual detention, and a person facing an order needs advice on that order's own dates and documents. That distinction is not a formality in this field, because two orders that look alike in their printed grounds may differ decisively in a single date, a single missing document or a single line in a representation reply, and only the actual papers of the case can show which difference matters.

## The Honest Assessment: Retention, Repeal and the Space Between

The case for retention, in its strongest form as its makers put it, begins from a claim about the limits of prosecution. There are harms, the defenders of the Act argue, that prosecution after the event cannot answer in time or cannot answer at all. Espionage compromises the defence of India while it continues and may be directed from outside the reach of an ordinary trial. Organised subversion builds capacity over time and presents its danger in preparation and association before a completed offence can be charged and proved. Communal mobilisation can move a locality from tension to violence faster than investigation and charge can intervene, and sabotage of supplies and services essential to the community can harm large numbers of people through a single disruption that a later conviction does not undo. On that account, a State that possesses only retrospective punishment must wait for damage it could have prevented, and the preventive power exists to close that gap in narrowly defined fields. The statute defenders, and the governments of every party complexion that have used the Act since 1980, also point to the discipline inside the scheme as a real answer to the fear of unchecked power. The grounds in Section 3 are enumerated rather than open ended. Communication is required within days under Section 8. A representation must be considered. An Advisory Board of persons who are, or have been, or are qualified to be Judges of a High Court must report within seven weeks under Sections 9 and 11, and a report of no sufficient cause obliges the Government to revoke and release forthwith under Section 12. Confirmation cannot carry detention beyond twelve months under Section 13. Courts enforce the procedural commands and quash orders that disobey them. Retention, presented in this form, is not a claim that executive satisfaction should be trusted without check. It is a claim that the checks exist, that they bite often enough to be seen in the quashing record, and that the residual power they leave is justified by harms that no purely prosecutorial system has shown it can prevent.

The case for repeal, in its strongest form as its makers put it, begins from the same record and reads it in the opposite direction. A power to imprison without trial for a year on executive satisfaction will predictably be used, those who argue for repeal say, beyond the grave fields named in defence of the Act, and will reach protesters, journalists and political opponents whose conduct belongs in public argument or, at most, in ordinary criminal process. Predictability is the core of the argument. It does not depend on attributing a single motive to every detaining authority. It rests on the structure of the power itself, which rewards speed, secrecy about material withheld in the public interest and custody without the embarrassment of a bail hearing or a failed trial, and which therefore invites use in the very cases where the ordinary process feels inconvenient. The quashing record, on this reading, shows routine procedural failure rather than occasional error. Grounds communicated late, documents withheld, representations left unconsidered, orders copied from proposals, stale incidents revived and law and order matters dressed as public order disturbances recur across decades and across Governments, suggesting that the discipline praised by defenders fails in ordinary administration often enough to be a feature of the system in practice. The repeal argument then presses its hardest point, namely the absence of a remedy that restores what unlawful detention takes. Dr Kafeel Khan spent over six months in detention before the Allahabad High Court quashed the order that held him, and release after six months does not return six months. A statute whose characteristic failure cost is measured in unreturnable liberty, the repeal case concludes, should not be retained on the assurance that its safeguards usually work, when its own case law documents how often they do not.

The evidence supports a verdict that lies in neither slogan, and stating it plainly is the obligation this article owes its readers. The statute text is not the main problem and it is not the main protection either. The text of the National Security Act is comparatively clear about its grounds, its clocks, its Board, its maximum and its exits, and most of the failures documented in the quashing record are not failures of drafting that a better printed section would have prevented. They are failures of administration, in which an authority communicated late, supplied incompletely, considered slowly, read selectively or classified carelessly, and successes of enforcement, in which a court insisted that the printed commands meant what they said. The operative law of preventive detention in India, as the record shows it, is therefore administrative discipline joined to judicial enforcement of the clock. Where the paperwork is honest, where the material is relevant and complete, where the representation is answered promptly, where the Board receives the case on time and where the category chosen matches the disturbance shown, an order can survive the narrow but real scrutiny the courts apply. Where the paperwork is dishonest in the broader sense of being careless, derivative, selective or evasive about dates and documents, the same courts quash, and they quash without needing to deny that the State possesses a preventive power at all. An order that survives is usually one whose paperwork was honest. That sentence is not praise for paperwork as bureaucracy. It is a recognition that, in a system without charge or trial, the paperwork is the safeguard, and honesty in it is the closest substitute the design offers for the open testing of evidence that detention dispenses with.

The three readers this article has kept in view leave with different work, and each kind of work follows from the verdict just given. The advocate is pointed to the clock, because dates decide more detention cases than rhetoric does, and the first task in any challenge remains the patient reconstruction of when the order was made, when the grounds were communicated, what documents were supplied, when the representation was filed and decided, and when the Board and the Government acted on it. The aspirant is pointed to A K Roy and the notification gap, because the distance between Section 9 as enacted and the stronger Advisory Board that Section 3 of the Constitution (44th Amendment) Act, 1978 would have required, left unnotified by every Government since assent on 30 April 1979, is the clearest single illustration in the whole subject of the difference between a safeguard promised in constitutional text and a safeguard operative in law. The journalist is pointed to the grounds and the dates in any reported order, because a report that repeats that a person was detained under the National Security Act without asking which Section 3 head was invoked, what material was relied on, whether the documents were supplied and whether the representation and Board timetable were honoured, reproduces the authority's conclusion while omitting the only facts by which readers can judge it. Detention without trial will be argued about for as long as the Act remains on the statute book. The argument is best conducted, by all three readers, on the record each order creates, in the belief that the record is where the power either justifies itself or fails.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the National Security Act 1980 and what does it allow?

The National Security Act, 1980 is India's standing central statute of preventive detention, enacted as Act 65 of 1980 after beginning life as an ordinance in September of that year. It allows the Central Government, a State Government, and, where a State Government has empowered them, a District Magistrate or a Commissioner of Police to order the detention of a person without charge or trial in order to prevent that person acting in a manner prejudicial to the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, the security of India, the security of the State, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. A confirmed order can detain for up to twelve months, subject to communication of grounds, representation, and Advisory Board review.

### Q: On what grounds can a person be detained under the NSA?

The grounds are closed by Section 3 and fall into two groups. Under Section 3(1), detention may be ordered to prevent a person acting in any manner prejudicial to the defence of India, the relations of India with foreign powers, or the security of India, and, in the case of a foreigner, to regulate his continued presence in India or to arrange his expulsion. Under Section 3(2), detention may be ordered to prevent a person acting prejudicially to the security of the State, the maintenance of public order, or the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. An Explanation to Section 3(2) removes grounds that belong to the Prevention of Blackmarketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980, and the Supreme Court has read the supplies and services ground down to supplies and services specified publicly in advance.

### Q: Does the National Security Act apply to foreigners?

Yes. The Act's definition of a person expressly includes a foreigner, and Section 3(1) contains a limb addressed to foreigners alone: the Central Government or a State Government may order the detention of a foreigner where it is satisfied that detention is necessary with a view to regulating his continued presence in India or with a view to making arrangements for his expulsion from India. A foreigner may also be detained on the ordinary grounds in Section 3, such as the defence of India or the security of India. The procedural scheme applies in the same way, so the grounds must be communicated, a representation may be made, and the Advisory Board reference follows, even though the object of the detention is the regulation of presence or removal rather than the prevention of prejudicial conduct by a citizen.

### Q: Who can pass a detention order under the NSA?

Three tiers of authority may pass an order. The Central Government and each State Government hold the power directly under Section 3. Below them, Section 3(3) allows a State Government, by an order in writing, to empower a District Magistrate or a Commissioner of Police to exercise the Section 3(2) power within the local limits of that officer's jurisdiction, where the circumstances prevailing or likely to prevail make it necessary. An empowerment lasts for not more than three months in the first instance and may be extended by not more than three months at a time. An officer who passes an order must report it forthwith to the State Government, and the order ceases to have force after twelve days unless the State Government approves it within that period, extended to fifteen days where the grounds are communicated through the exceptional route in Section 8.

### Q: For how long does a District Magistrate's NSA order survive without State Government approval?

Twelve days, and no longer. Section 3(4) requires the District Magistrate or Commissioner of Police who has passed an order under an empowerment to report the fact forthwith to the State Government, together with the grounds on which the order was made and the particulars bearing on the matter, and it provides that no such order shall remain in force for more than twelve days after the making of the order unless it has been approved by the State Government in the meantime. Where the grounds of detention are communicated after five days but not later than ten days under the exceptional circumstances route in Section 8, the proviso to Section 3(4) substitutes fifteen days for twelve. Approval is therefore a condition of the order's continued force, not a ratification that can be supplied late, and detention continued after the window closes without approval rests on an order the statute no longer sustains.

### Q: How soon must the grounds of detention be communicated under the NSA?

Section 8(1) requires the authority that made the order to communicate the grounds to the detenu as soon as may be, but ordinarily not later than five days from the date of detention. In exceptional circumstances, and for reasons recorded in writing, the outer limit is ten days from the date of detention, and there is no third extension. The same provision requires that the detenu be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government, because the grounds are the material on which that representation depends. Section 8(2) qualifies the duty in one respect only: the authority is not required to disclose facts which it considers to be against the public interest to disclose. The grounds themselves must still be communicated, with enough particularity for the detenu to understand and answer the case recorded against him.

### Q: What happens if the grounds of detention are served late under the NSA?

Late service is one of the classic defects on which courts quash detention orders. The five-day ordinary limit and the ten-day outer limit in Section 8(1) are mandatory in character: communication beyond ten days, or beyond five days without exceptional circumstances and reasons recorded in writing, breaches the section and the guarantee in Article 22(5) that the grounds be communicated and the earliest opportunity of representation afforded. The consequence is not a direction to serve the grounds again properly. Because the representation right is timed to the detention and to the Advisory Board reference that follows within three weeks, grounds that arrive late have already damaged the opportunity they exist to create, and courts treat unexplained delay at this step as vitiating the detention, however serious the allegations in the grounds may be.

### Q: What must the detenu be given along with the grounds of detention?

The detenu must be given the material on which the grounds rest, meaning the documents and records relied on by the detaining authority in reaching its satisfaction, so far as they are not withheld under the public interest qualification in Section 8(2). The grounds themselves state conclusions drawn from incidents, reports and statements, and a detenu who receives only the conclusions cannot make the effective representation that Section 8 promises. Courts have accordingly held across the preventive detention statutes that non-supply of relied-upon documents disables the representation right and vitiates the detention, because the detenu cannot show that a passage was read out of context, that a date was misstated, or that an incident had a history the summary omitted. The duty covers legible and complete copies of the relied material, and delay in supplying documents that were demanded or obviously necessary is judged by the same standard of expedition as the grounds themselves.

### Q: Must the grounds of detention be explained in a language the detenu understands?

Yes, in substance. Communication under Section 8 is not the physical delivery of a document; it is the conveying of the grounds in a form the detenu can understand and answer, because the purpose of the duty is to make the representation right real. Courts applying Article 22(5) and the detention statutes have treated grounds served in a language the detenu does not understand, without an explanation of their contents in a language he does understand, as no effective communication at all, and the detention has been held vitiated on that ground. The same principle covers the documents supplied with the grounds, where the material is in a script or language foreign to the detenu. An authority that serves papers it knows the detenu cannot read has performed the form of Section 8 while defeating its object, and the defect is measured by the detenu's resulting inability to represent, not by the date stamped on the service.

### Q: What is the detenu's right of representation under the NSA, and who must decide it?

Section 8(1) requires that the detenu be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order to the appropriate Government, which is the Central Government for a central order and the State Government for a state or officer-made order. The right is a working remedy, not a formality. The government must consider the representation itself, independently of the detaining authority's view, and must decide it with reasonable expedition, and unexplained delay in consideration vitiates the detention even where every other step was punctual, a duty the Supreme Court stated in Kamarunnissa v Union of India and has applied across the detention statutes. The representation also travels with the grounds into the Advisory Board reference under Section 10, so the Board sees the detenu's answer alongside the authority's case. A detenu may also address the Board in person in the circumstances Section 11 allows.

### Q: Is a detenu entitled to a lawyer before the NSA Advisory Board?

No. The Act gives no such entitlement. Section 11(4) provides that nothing in that section entitles a person against whom a detention order has been made to appear by any legal practitioner in any matter connected with the reference to the Advisory Board, and in A K Roy v Union of India the Supreme Court upheld that position. The judgment softened the bar in two ways. First, the exclusion of legal practitioners does not prevent the detenu from being assisted by a friend who is not in truth and substance a legal practitioner, and Boards must allow that help when it is asked for. Second, if the detaining authority or the government takes the aid of a legal practitioner or a legal adviser before the Board, the detenu must be allowed the same facility, because to permit it on one side and deny it on the other would breach Article 14. The proceedings and the Board's report, apart from the part recording its opinion, are confidential.

### Q: How long does the Advisory Board process take under the NSA?

The process is bounded by two periods that run from the date of detention, not from each other. Under Section 10, the appropriate Government must place the grounds, the detenu's representation if any, and, for an order made by an empowered officer, that officer's report before the Advisory Board within three weeks from the date of detention. Under Section 11(1), the Board must then submit its report to the appropriate Government within seven weeks from the date of detention, after considering the material, calling for further information if it wishes, and hearing the detenu in person where it considers a hearing essential or the detenu desires one. The report states the Board's opinion on whether there is sufficient cause for the detention, with the majority view prevailing. After the report, Section 12 governs: confirmation if the opinion is favourable, mandatory revocation and release forthwith if it is not.

### Q: What is the maximum period of detention under the National Security Act?

Twelve months from the date of detention. Section 13 provides that the maximum period for which any person may be detained in pursuance of a detention order that has been confirmed under Section 12 shall be twelve months from the date of detention, and no approval, confirmation or Board report can extend that period. The figure is a ceiling, not an entitlement to hold for a year. The appropriate Government may revoke or modify the order at any earlier time under the proviso to Section 13 and under Section 14, and detention for the whole period requires the full sequence to have been completed: timely grounds, a proper reference, a Board report finding sufficient cause, and a confirmation. One historical exception existed under Section 14A, which raised the maximum to two years for a defined class of disturbed-area detentions in Punjab and Chandigarh under amendments made in the nineteen eighties; that provision was spent by the end of that decade.

### Q: Can a person detained under the NSA be released temporarily?

Yes. Section 15 empowers the appropriate Government, at any time, to direct that a detenu be released for a specified period, either without conditions or on conditions stated in the direction which the detenu accepts, and the government may require a bond, with or without sureties, for the observance of those conditions. The government may cancel the release at any time, and the detenu must then surrender at the time and place and to the authority specified. Failure without sufficient cause to surrender in that manner is an offence punishable with imprisonment for a term that may extend to two years, or with fine, or with both, and breach of the conditions or of the bond leads to the bond being declared forfeited. Temporary release does not end the detention order or stop the twelve month maximum in Section 13 from running; it is a supervised interruption of custody within the order's own life.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court uphold the National Security Act?

Yes. In A K Roy v Union of India, decided on 28 December 1981 by a five-judge Constitution Bench presided over by Chandrachud CJ, the Supreme Court upheld the Act and the Ordinance that preceded it against challenges to the ordinance making power, to the vagueness of Section 3, and to the Advisory Board scheme. The Court held that an ordinance is law for the purposes of Article 21, refused to strike Section 3 down for uncertainty while directing that its words be construed narrowly, read the supplies and services ground down to supplies and services specified publicly in advance, and declined to compel the Central Government to bring Section 3 of the 44th Amendment into force. Gupta J dissented in part, holding that an ordinance is not law within Article 21 and that a mandamus should issue directing that notification within two months. The Act has stood on the terms A K Roy set ever since.

### Q: Why was the National Security Act first issued as an ordinance?

Because the government of Indira Gandhi, returned to office in January 1980 after the collapse of the Janata experiment, wanted a standing central detention statute in force without waiting for a parliamentary session to debate and pass one, and Article 123 allowed the President to legislate at once by ordinance on the advice of that government. The National Security Ordinance, 1980 came into force on 23 September 1980, and the Act that replaced it received assent on 27 December 1980 with retrospective effect from the ordinance's date. The route mattered to the Act's later history. A K Roy, the petitioner whose case tested the statute, had himself been detained under the Ordinance, and his first argument was that an ordinance is not law within Article 21 at all. The Supreme Court rejected that argument, holding the ordinance making power legislative in character, but the fact that a detention law began as executive legislation remained central to the criticism of its birth.

### Q: Is the National Security Act a colonial era law?

No. The Act was enacted in 1980 by the Parliament of independent India, thirty-three years after independence, and describing it as a colonial law is a factual error. The honest version of the criticism is about lineage rather than date. Preventive detention in India does descend from colonial instruments: Bengal Regulation III of 1818 allowed detention on executive satisfaction, and the Rowlatt legislation of 1919 carried a similar logic into the twentieth century. Independent India then chose, through its Constituent Assembly and its first Parliament, to retain the power in Article 22 and in the Preventive Detention Act 1950, continued it in the Maintenance of Internal Security Act 1971, and recreated it in the National Security Act 1980 after MISA's repeal. Each of those steps was a decision of an Indian legislature, which is precisely what makes the lineage argument stronger, not weaker, when it is stated accurately.

### Q: What was Section 14A of the NSA and why did Punjab have different rules?

Section 14A was a special regime inserted by the National Security (Amendment) Act, 1984 for persons detained in the disturbed areas of Punjab and the Union territory of Chandigarh in connection with terrorist and disruptive activities, at the height of the Punjab militancy. For that defined class it rewrote the general scheme: detention could continue for up to six months without obtaining the Advisory Board's opinion, the reference period in Section 10 stretched from three weeks to four months and two weeks, the Board's report period in Section 11 stretched from seven weeks to five months and three weeks, and the maximum period in Section 13 rose from twelve months to two years. The National Security (Amendment) Act, 1987 replaced Section 14A in a revised form and a 1988 amendment adjusted its terminal date. The provision applied only to orders made before a date in June 1989 and has long been spent, so the general scheme governs everywhere again.

### Q: Can an NSA order be passed against someone already in custody?

Yes. Custody on another basis, whether imprisonment after conviction, undertrial detention in a criminal case, or detention under another statute, is no bar to a detention order under Section 3, and the courts have accepted orders passed against persons already in custody. The order is not, however, a formality in that setting. Because the detenu is already confined, the detaining authority must show that it was aware of the existing custody and that it nevertheless had a genuine apprehension, founded on material, that the person would act prejudicially if released or at the end of that custody, for example because release on bail was imminent or a sentence was about to expire. An order that ignores the custody, or that is passed mechanically to defeat an expected bail order without engaging with the person's situation, invites quashing for non-application of mind. Where the authority does engage with the position and records a real and proximate apprehension, the order can stand and take effect when the earlier custody ends or alongside it as the court directs.

### Q: What happens when an NSA detention order is quashed?

The detention ends. A quashing by a High Court under Article 226 or by the Supreme Court under Article 32 sets the order aside as unlawful, and the detenu must be released, since no confirmation or approval can survive the destruction of the order they rest on; extensions founded on the quashed order fall with it, as the Allahabad High Court's judgment in the Kafeel Khan matter shows, where the order of 13 February 2020 and both extensions were set aside together. Release is the whole of the immediate remedy. Quashing does not itself produce compensation for the months spent in custody, does not bar a prosecution on the same facts, and does not automatically prevent a fresh detention order, although Section 14 requires that a later order rest on the statutory conditions and, where no fresh facts have arisen, the total detention cannot extend beyond twelve months from the date of detention under the earlier order. The practical finality of quashing is therefore liberty restored, on a record that also exposes why the order failed.
