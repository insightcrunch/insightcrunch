Samuel Johnson delivered the most consequential short judgement on Romeo and Juliet that the eighteenth century produced, and he delivered it in a handful of sentences at the end of his 1765 edition. The play, he wrote, is one of the most pleasing of its author's performances; its scenes are busy and various, its incidents numerous and important, its catastrophe irresistibly affecting. Then, in the same breath, he withdrew part of the gift: the comic scenes are happily wrought, but the pathetic strains are always polluted with some unexpected depravations, and the distressed persons of the tragedy keep a conceit in their misery, a miserable conceit. Praise and reservation sit side by side in Johnson's verdict, and the verdict has outlasted every rival from his century because both halves of it are earned. The praise rests on an argued theory of drama, worked out in the Preface to his edition, that acquits this play of the charges neoclassical criticism had pending against it. The reservation rests on a real defect, which Johnson located more exactly than any critic before him, even where his own framework prevented him from seeing what the defect was for.

The argument of the pages that follow is that Johnson's assessment remains the best short account of the play written before the Romantics, and that its balance is not the bland midpoint between enthusiasm and disapproval but a set of distinct findings, each testable against the text. His defence of mingled drama explains why the Nurse and Mercutio belong in the same play as the tomb. His demolition of the unities of time and place explains why the play's frantic four days feel probable rather than absurd. His sentence on the Nurse identifies, in eight paired words, the doubleness that makes her the play's most durable comic creation. His charge against the conceit fails as a general law and succeeds as a local diagnosis, and the places where it succeeds are places he marked himself, in notes on single lines that most accounts of his criticism pass over. Where he is weakest, he is weakest in a characteristic direction: a moralist's framework, announced in the Preface as the first of Shakespeare's faults, keeps reaching for this play and finding almost nothing to seize, and the two occasions on which it does seize something are the least convincing notes he wrote on the tragedy. Johnson judging Romeo and Juliet whole is therefore a double exhibit: a great critic at his most accurate, and a great system at the edge of its competence.

## What Johnson Wrote on the Play: The Edition, the Preface, and the Notes

Johnson's Shakespeare appeared in 1765, in eight volumes, after proposals issued nine years earlier and two decades after he had first announced himself as a prospective editor with a pamphlet on Macbeth. The edition's place in the century's editorial succession, the line that runs from Rowe through Pope, Theobald, Hanmer, and Warburton toward Steevens and Malone, belongs to the history of editing: the full account of [Editing Romeo and Juliet: Rowe to Malone](/2026/06/05/editing-rowe-to-malone-history/) treats the institution at length. What matters here is the critic, not the institution, and Johnson the critic of this play speaks in three distinct registers. The first is the Preface, a general essay on Shakespeare that constructs the framework within which every individual play is silently tried. The second is the body of notes attached to single lines of Romeo and Juliet, where Johnson glosses, defends, emends, objects, and occasionally moralizes, always at the scale of the passage in front of him. The third is the short stricture he appended to the end of the play, the general censure of faults or praise of excellence that he told his readers he had added to most of the plays, and which contains the famous verdict entire.

### Did Johnson write a separate essay on this play?

No separate essay exists, and the verdict's authority depends on understanding that. Johnson's judgement on Romeo and Juliet is distributed across the Preface's general theory of Shakespeare, a dozen notes on individual passages, and one closing stricture of four paragraphs that he added, by his own account, to most plays as a general censure of faults or praise of excellence.

The three registers do not always agree, and the disagreements are instructive. The Preface charges Shakespeare in general with sacrificing virtue to convenience and writing without a moral purpose; the notes on this play apply that charge exactly twice, in passing, and the closing stricture does not apply it at all. The Preface complains in general that Shakespeare's comic contests of wit are gross and licentious; the stricture on this play pronounces its comic scenes happily wrought. Johnson reading a whole oeuvre is a lawgiver; Johnson reading a single play is a judge deciding the case in front of him, and the case keeps escaping the law. Arthur Sherbo, who established the text of the notes for the Yale Edition of Johnson's works, demonstrated that the notes record a mind responding passage by passage, often against the grain of its own generalizations: Johnson the annotator corrects Johnson the theorist at the level of the line. That is why any honest account of his verdict on this play must work through the notes and not rest on the Preface alone, and it is why the paraphrase offered here follows his words closely while keeping quotation short, since his meaning lives in the sequence of small decisions as much as in the large declarations.

The edition's textual basis, Johnson admitted in the same Preface, was thinner than its reputation suggests. He collated such copies as he could procure and wished for more, but found the collectors of these rarities not very communicative; his text of the play descends, with silent rectifications, from the regulated text of his predecessors rather than from a fresh recensio of the quartos. The admission matters for weighing the verdict. Johnson's authority on Romeo and Juliet is judicial, not documentary: he did not know the play's early texts as the textual scholars of a later age would know them, and nothing in his stricture depends on pretending otherwise. What he brought to the play was not a better text but a better court, a framework for judging that had been argued in public and a willingness to let the case in front of him amend the law. Critics who dismiss the notes for their textual thinness and critics who revere the Preface for its theory both miss the stricture's actual foundation, which is neither: it is the record of a supreme reader reading the received play whole, and finding its account, on balance, pleasing.

Johnson himself supplied the taxonomy for sorting these remarks, in a passage of the Preface that accounts of his criticism rarely apply to this play. The notes he borrowed or wrote, he explained, are either illustrative, by which difficulties are explained, or judicial, by which faults and beauties are remarked, or emendatory, by which depravations are corrected. The notes on Romeo and Juliet distribute themselves across the three kinds with a telling unevenness. The illustrative notes are few and brief: the gloss identifying the golden story of Lady Capulet's book conceit (1.3) with the Golden Legend, and the traveller's observation appended to the day is hot (3.1). The emendatory notes are two, and they pull in opposite directions, as the discussion of his close reading below will show: a conjecture of his own offered at 1.2, and an emendation of Warburton's refused at the same speech's doorstep. The judicial notes are the mass of the whole: the oxymorons audited, the Chorus dismissed, the equivocations censured, the hypocrisy suspected, Benvolio acquitted of simplicity, the Friar's narrative regretted, and the closing stricture above all. On most plays Johnson explained more than he judged, since obscurity was the commoner obstacle; on this play, whose text is comparatively lucid, the proportions reverse, and the edition becomes a courtroom. That reversal is the precondition of the famous verdict. Romeo and Juliet is the play on which Johnson the annotator was forced, by the absence of difficulties to explain, to spend his labour on judgement, and the judgement he spent has never been displaced.

One further piece of candour in the Preface deserves prominence, because it disciplines everything claimed for the closing stricture. Johnson confessed that his judicial observations, the end-of-play verdicts, were distributed unevenly among the plays, not in proportion to their difference of merit but because he gave that part of his design to chance and to caprice. The confession is usually read as modesty. It is better read as a warning label printed by the manufacturer: the stricture on Romeo and Juliet is not the output of a system applied uniformly across the canon, and its excellence is the excellence of an occasion seized, not of a machine that could be trusted to grade the other plays as well. When Jean H. Hagstrum argued, in his study of Johnson's literary criticism, that the Preface articulates a coherent critical philosophy grounded in nature, reason, and moral use, he was describing the framework accurately; when the stricture on this play is weighed, the framework and the occasion have to be weighed together. The verdict is strong because, on this play, the two happened to coincide: Johnson's theory had specific things to say about mixed genre, compressed time, and probable action, and Romeo and Juliet is the play in the canon where those questions are most sharply posed.

## The Preface's Framework: Nature, Variety, and the Mingled Drama

Johnson's Preface begins its positive case with a test and a definition. The test is duration: works that appeal wholly to observation and experience can be estimated only by length of duration and continuance of esteem, and Shakespeare, having outlived his century, may begin to assume the dignity of an ancient. The definition follows from it. Nothing can please many, and please long, but just representations of general nature. Shakespeare is, above all modern writers, the poet of nature, the poet who holds up to his readers a faithful mirror of manners and of life; his characters are not individuals but commonly a species, the genuine progeny of common humanity, acting and speaking by the influence of those general passions and principles by which all minds are agitated. Romeo and Juliet passes the duration test before Johnson opens it, since a play still acted and read a hundred and seventy years after its first performances arrives in his study with its credentials partly established; the interest of the Preface is in explaining what the play's survival certifies. On Johnson's account it certifies fidelity to general nature: lovers, fathers, servants, and friends whose passions are the ones the world will always supply. The explanation matters because it reverses the order of neoclassical judgement. A rule-bound critic asks whether the play conforms to the accredited forms and admits its beauties as exceptions; Johnson asks what has pleased many and pleased long, and treats the accredited forms as hypotheses that the play's endurance has already tested.

### What does Johnson mean by calling Shakespeare the poet of nature?

He means Shakespeare copied general human nature, not the manners of a place, a profession, or a fashion. His persons act and speak by the influence of those passions and principles by which all minds are agitated, so that a character in his plays is commonly a species, a person of a kind the world always supplies, not a portrait.

From that foundation the Preface builds its most famous act of acquittal, the defence of the mingled drama. Shakespeare's plays, Johnson wrote, are not in the rigorous and critical sense either tragedies or comedies, but compositions of a distinct kind, exhibiting the real state of sublunary nature, which partakes of good and evil, joy and sorrow, mingled with endless variety of proportion and innumerable modes of combination. The practice is contrary to the rules of criticism, he conceded, but there is always an appeal open from criticism to nature; the end of writing is to instruct, the end of poetry is to instruct by pleasing, and the mingled drama can convey all the instruction of either kind because it includes both, approaching nearer than either to the appearance of life. No play in the canon stood to gain more from this paragraph than Romeo and Juliet. Its design interleaves the registers without apology: a street brawl and a sonnet at a feast, a bawdy nurse and a dying bridegroom, Mercutio's Queen Mab fantasia within hours of the double suicide, gravedigger humour transposed into Peter and the musicians quarrelling over a corpse in Act 4. A criticism that sorted plays into kinds and policed the border would have to record every one of those crossings as a fault. Johnson's framework records them as the play's resemblance to the world, in which, as he put it, the reveller is hasting to his wine at the same moment as the mourner is burying his friend. The objection that mingled scenes interrupt the passions in their progression he dismissed as reasoning so specious that it is received as true even by those who in daily experience feel it to be false. On this play the dismissal has worn well: the musicians' squabble in 4.5 does not relax the catastrophe, it prices it, showing a household where grief is already being processed into arrangements; the comedy is the tragedy's evidence about Verona, not its holiday from Verona.

Variety, in Johnson's vocabulary, is therefore not a decorative abundance but the signature of nature copied faithfully, and it is the first word of praise in his verdict on this play for a reason. The scenes are busy and various because the Preface has already established that variety of proportion and mode is what the real state of sublunary nature presents, and that a drama exhibiting it carries a presumption of truth. When later critics praised the play's contrasts in Romantic terms, as the poetry of youth set against the prose of age, they were decorating a structure Johnson had already described in plainer language and with a plainer justification: the play is various because life is, and its variety is a merit because fidelity, not regularity, is the test.

The duration test, applied to this play's particular transmission, strengthens the point in a way Johnson left implicit. Between Shakespeare's lifetime and 1765 the play had pleased under successive and incompatible regimes of taste: displaced for decades by Otway's Roman adaptation, restored in altered forms, cut and rearranged by Garrick for the mid-century stage, its lovers granted a conscious farewell the text denies them. Johnson's Preface describes works that have passed through variations of taste and changes of manners and received new honours at every transmission, and Romeo and Juliet is among the clearest instances in the canon of a play doing exactly that. The honours were not always paid to the unaltered text, a complication Johnson the editor deplored and Johnson the critic could set aside, since the test measures the fable's hold on audiences rather than the purity of its vehicles. What survived every regime was the story's power to please, and the power, on his theory, is the signature of general nature underneath the changing costumes.

## The Unities Dismissed: Time, Place, and the Play's Four Days

The Preface's second act of acquittal concerns the unities, and it is the passage of Johnson's criticism that reads least like its century. Whether Shakespeare knew the unities of time and place and rejected them by design, or deviated from them by happy ignorance, is impossible to decide and useless to inquire; nothing is essential to the fable but unity of action, and the unities of time and place arise evidently from false assumptions. The false assumption is that a representation must be mistaken for reality to be credited. The spectators, Johnson wrote, are always in their senses, and know, from the first act to the last, that the stage is only a stage, and that the players are only players; delusion, if delusion be admitted, has no certain limitation, since the mind that can take a candlelit room for the plain of Pharsalia can allow an hour to stand for a century. The delight of tragedy proceeds from our consciousness of fiction, and imitations produce pain or pleasure not because they are mistaken for realities but because they bring realities to mind. Unity of action, by contrast, is real, and Johnson found it preserved: a Shakespeare play has commonly what Aristotle requires, a beginning, a middle, and an end, one event concatenated with another, the conclusion following by easy consequence, so that the end of the play is the end of expectation.

Romeo and Juliet is an unusually pure test of this argument because the play commits, in concentrated form, the offence the unities existed to prevent. Its action occupies roughly four days, from a Sunday morning brawl to a Thursday dawn in the tomb, yet within that span it stages a meeting, a marriage, a double killing, a banishment, a second betrothal, a feigned death, a funeral, a journey to Mantua, a suicide pact executed by poison and dagger, and a civic reconciliation, shifting between Verona and Mantua and among street, orchard, cell, chamber, churchyard, and vault. A French neoclassical tribunal would have found the compression incredible and the geography lawless. Johnson's framework finds the compression to be the play's engine and its geography to be no offence at all, since the spectator who never believed himself in Verona cannot be injured by being carried to Mantua. What the framework demands instead is concatenation, and the play supplies it with almost mechanical rigour: the marriage causes the secrecy, the secrecy causes the duel's fatal misunderstanding, the duel causes the banishment, the banishment causes the potion plot, and the potion plot causes the tomb. Johnson's verdict phrase for this quality is the one critics quote least and should quote most: the process of the action is carried on with such probability, at least with such congruity to popular opinions, as tragedy requires. The qualification is Johnson at his most exact. He does not claim the events are likely; he claims they hang together in the sequence audiences accept, each step licensed by the beliefs and customs the play's world holds about honour, obedience, friars, apothecaries, and plague. Probability reassigned from the event to the chain is the precise critical tool this breathless play needs, and Johnson forged it in the act of defending the whole canon at once.

The framework also dissolves, in advance, the puzzle over the play's clocks that later editors would map in detail. The action runs on two inconsistent measures at once. By the short measure, everything happens in about four days, and the text keeps that count ostentatiously: the party on Sunday night, the marriage on Monday, the duel on Monday afternoon, the potion on Wednesday night, the tomb before Friday's dawn. By the long measure, the play implies a slower world: a heroine not yet fourteen whose mother bore her at about her daughter's age, a Capulet who has not danced for thirty years, a Nurse whose memory runs back eleven years to an earthquake and a weaning. Editors have charted the discrepancies and occasionally apologized for them, as if the play's time scheme were a faulty mechanism requiring repair. Johnson's argument removes the bench from which such apologies are issued. If the spectators never supposed themselves transported, and if time is of all modes of existence the most obsequious to the imagination, then the two measures never compete in the only court that matters, the audience's experience: the short clock supplies the urgency the tragedy breathes by, the long clock supplies the settled household world the urgency invades, and no spectator is asked to reconcile ledgers he was never keeping. What Johnson demands is not consistency of calendar but consequence of action, and on that demand the play's four days, crowded as they are, pass without a single wasted hour.

The unity Johnson affirmed in place of the discarded pair can be mapped onto the play joint by joint, and the mapping shows why he used Aristotle's vocabulary with a clear conscience. The beginning is the feud in its ordinary state, with Romeo's Rosaline posture as its private weather: two systems of waste, public and erotic, awaiting a spark. The middle is the conversion of the first system by the second, the marriage turning the feud's heir into its secret son-in-law, so that every subsequent act of violence is also an act of family destruction; the duel, the banishment, and the enforced Paris match are not episodes but the conversion working itself out under rising pressure. The end is the tomb, where both systems close their accounts at once, the feud burying its children and the love completing its logic of secrecy in a darkness where no message can arrive in time. Even the play's apparent subplot, the Paris suit, is load-bearing in this accounting, since without it there is no potion, and without the potion no catastrophe of the specific kind Johnson found irresistible, a catastrophe of timing rather than of intention. Nothing in this structure requires the unities of time and place, and much in it forbids them: a feud is a condition, not an incident, and exhibiting a condition requires the variety of places and hours that the Preface defended. Johnson's Aristotle is thus the real one, the analyst of causal wholes, conscripted against the rule-makers who had borrowed his name for a censorship he never issued. The defence of this play's construction, conducted in the Preface as a defence of the whole canon, fits Romeo and Juliet with a precision that suggests the general argument was always, among other things, a local one.

## "One of the Most Pleasing": The Closing Stricture Weighed

The stricture itself, paraphrased closely, runs as follows. The play is one of the most pleasing of its author's performances. The scenes are busy and various, the incidents numerous and important, the catastrophe irresistibly affecting, and the conduct of the action probable enough, in its congruity to popular opinions, for tragedy. It contains one of Shakespeare's few attempts to exhibit the conversation of gentlemen, the airy sprightliness of juvenile elegance. Its comic scenes are happily wrought. Three of those judgements deserve slow treatment, because each compresses an argument. Pleasing is the first. Johnson's criticism measures drama by the pleasure of audiences ratified over time, since pleasure is the vehicle of the instruction that is poetry's end; to call a tragedy of dead children one of the most pleasing of the performances is therefore not a lapse of taste but a technical verdict, recording that the play performs the office of its kind at the highest rate of success. The word has embarrassed later admirers, who prefer their praise of this play in the vocabulary of passion and poetry, but Johnson's term is the more informative one: it ranks the play by effect delivered, and it places Romeo and Juliet in the small company of works whose effect he found essentially undiminished by their defects.

### Which single sentence carries Johnson's whole praise of the play?

The opening sentence of his closing stricture does: his declaration that the play is one of the most pleasing of its author's performances. In Johnson's critical vocabulary pleasing is a technical term, not a pleasantry, since poetry for him exists to instruct by pleasing, and a play that pleases at this level has performed the full office of its kind.

Busy and various is the second judgement, and it distributes praise across both halves of the mingled design. Busy certifies the incidents: the play never idles, a quality Johnson valued enough to fault Shakespeare elsewhere for plots so loosely formed that slight consideration may improve them. Various certifies the scenes: street, feast, orchard, cell, and tomb, high passion and low comedy, the registers succeeding one another in the endless variety of proportion the Preface had described as the mark of nature. The catastrophe, third, is irresistibly affecting, and the adverb deserves notice from a critic whose Preface complained that Shakespeare's pathetic attempts are routinely blasted by sudden frigidity. Here, Johnson records, the blast does not come: whatever conceits he found sprinkled through the earlier acts, the tomb sequence moved him without reserve, and he said so in the strongest affective language he allowed himself anywhere in the notes on this play. The praise of the gentlemen's conversation, finally, is the stricture's most original observation. Shakespeare, Johnson thought, rarely attempted the talk of gentlemen, the easy commerce of young men of rank; in Mercutio's circle he attempted it and succeeded, catching the airy sprightliness of juvenile elegance. The judgement quietly rebukes the Preface's own generalization that Shakespeare's contests of wit are seldom successful and his gentlemen insufficiently distinguished from his clowns. In this play, Johnson found, the distinction holds: the wit of 1.4 and 2.4 is the wit of a class and an age, sprightly rather than gross, and the finding stands as a small model of his method, the general law amended by the particular case.

The praise of the incidents, numerous and important, rewards the same attention. Numerous is the easy half: few tragedies crowd so many hinges into five acts, a brawl, a betrothal suit, a feast, a balcony vow, a secret marriage, a street duel, a banishment, a forced second marriage, a potion, a funeral, a poisoned exile, a tomb. Important is the discriminating half, and it answers the Preface's own standing complaint that Shakespeare's plots are often loosely formed. In this plot almost nothing is decorative. The feast exists so the marriage can be imagined; the marriage exists so the duel can be a crime against kinship rather than a street accident; the duel exists so the banishment can separate what the marriage joined; the Paris suit exists so the potion can be necessary; the potion exists so the tomb can be a misunderstanding rather than a design. Strike any incident and the chain Johnson praised, one event concatenated with another, loses a link. His two adjectives together therefore record a structural finding that criticism has confirmed ever since: this is the most tightly consequential of the early tragedies, a play whose abundance is the opposite of looseness. The stricture's economy conceals the finding's size, and recovering it is part of what a full account of his verdict owes the reader.

## The Species Gallery: Johnson's Doctrine Applied to the Whole Cast

Johnson left no stricture on the play's other figures, but his doctrine of character, stated in the Preface and proven on the Nurse, invites application to the whole cast, and applying it on his stated principles shows both the doctrine's reach and the play's compliance with it. His test was that a Shakespeare character be a species, actuated by general passions, such as the world will always supply. Capulet passes the test in the form Johnson's notes twice touch without naming. The editor who weighed the guest list's uncle against the dialogue's cousin, and who computed that the old man had been past masking for thirty years while his wife was eight and twenty, was auditing the figure of the father in authority, genial in his hall, absolute in his house, whose hospitality and whose tyranny are the same passion of command at different temperatures: the host who will not have his feast disturbed in 1.5 and the master who threatens his daughter with the street in 3.5 are one species at two hours of its day. Tybalt is the species of honour unmixed, a man Johnson's framework would describe as actuated by a single general passion, the punctilio of the code, and therefore the least individual and most necessary figure in the feud's machinery. Paris is the formal suitor as a kind, all suit and no person, which is why the play can marry him to Juliet in her parents' imagination without ever making him a rival in hers. The Friar is the prudent counsellor of the species Johnson quarried for axioms, the man whose general wisdom is genuine and whose particular applications undo him.

The lovers themselves are the doctrine's hardest case and its best confirmation. Johnson's Preface observes that in other dramatists love is the universal agent by which all good and evil is distributed, whereas Shakespeare, who caught his ideas from the living world, treats love as one passion among many, with no greater influence on the sum of life than any other as it is regular or exorbitant. Romeo and Juliet enacts that demotion of love inside a love tragedy. The catastrophe is not love's work alone; it is the product of love colliding with the feud's hatred, the city's law, the church's machinery, the apothecary's poverty, and the plague's indifference, each passion and institution pursuing its own general course. Johnson's stricture, which distributes its praise among construction, gentlemen, Mercutio, and the Nurse and never once singles out the lovers' passion for commendation, is faithful to that distribution of causes. The silence that struck later readers as coldness toward the most famous lovers in literature is, on his premises, accuracy: the play he read is not a duet but a system, and his verdict grades the system. Whether the grading does justice to Juliet, whose speeches carry the play's highest poetry, is the standing question his assessment leaves open, and the Romantic critics who followed him built their reputations partly in the space that silence made.

## The Field Johnson Entered: A Moral Settlement and a Rule-Bound Objection

Johnson's verdict did not fall on empty ground. By 1765 the play had two settled accounts in circulation, and the stricture's originality is measured by what it declined to repeat from either. The first was the moral settlement associated with Nicholas Rowe, whose 1709 edition opened the century's editorial line and whose brief life of the author set the terms in which the plays were introduced to Augustan readers. Rowe read the tragedy as an engine of instruction whose catastrophe falls where it should: the feuding parents, whose civil rage made the children's world uninhabitable, are punished in the loss of the children, and the play becomes a warning addressed upward, to the governors of households. The reading had the merit of finding a design in the ending and the defect of mistaking the Prince's exhausted aphorisms for the play's own sentence. It governed the play's introduction to the century, and its fingerprints are on every account that treats the tomb as a judgement seat.

The second account was the rule-bound objection, and by mid-century it had found its sharpest form. Charles Gildon, annotating and attacking in the wake of Rowe's edition, had prosecuted Shakespeare's irregularities with a prosecutor's patience. Henry Home, Lord Kames, gave the objection its most respectable philosophical dress in his Elements of Criticism of 1762, three years before Johnson's edition appeared. The objection, in the form Gildon pressed and Kames refined, is that this tragedy's catastrophe does not grow from the characters but befalls them: the lovers are destroyed not by any flaw that ripens through the action but by an accident of transmission, a letter detained by plague, a message that never reaches Mantua. A tragedy whose ending turns on a miscarried letter, the objection runs, violates the deep grammar of the kind, in which suffering must be the consequence of the sufferer's own acts and nature, legible as a moral causation. Of all the charges the century brought against the play, this is the one that has never gone away; it survives, in altered vocabulary, wherever the ending is called a cheat or a contrivance.

Johnson never answered the accident objection head-on, and the honesty of his stricture is partly in its refusal to pretend otherwise. What the stricture does is more interesting than a rebuttal. It concedes the mechanism in the act of praising the effect: the catastrophe is irresistibly affecting, and the process that produces it is probable only in the qualified sense of congruity to popular opinions, a phrase that quietly moves the trial from the machinery to the audience. On Johnson's premises the move is legitimate, since tragedy moves us, as the Preface argues, not by being mistaken for reality but by bringing realities to mind, by exhibiting evils to which we ourselves may be exposed. A miscarried letter is such an evil; every reader lives in a world where messages fail. Kames demanded that the catastrophe be deserved by the action's internal morality; Johnson asked whether it is suffered as human beings suffer, by the collision of intentions with a world that does not forward letters. The adjudication between them has to be split. On the mechanism, Kames wins: the ending is accidental in its trigger, and no amount of concatenation upstream changes what Friar John's quarantine does to the causal chain. On the effect, and on the theory of what tragedy is for, Johnson wins: the accident is precisely what the play is about, a world in which haste, secrecy, and feud have so degraded every channel of communication that a detained letter is not an intrusion of chance but the system's normal product. Johnson did not state that defence in terms; the stage had spent the century making a cognate point in practice, since Garrick's adaptation of 1748, which rewrote the tomb so the lovers could meet and speak before dying, testified that audiences demanded not a different causation but a fuller suffering, the accident retained and its emotional yield increased. The rule critics wanted the play acquitted of accident or condemned for it. Johnson's verdict changed the question, from the regularity of the machine to the nature it mirrors, and the criticism of the play has lived inside his question ever since.

## Mercutio's Allotted Time: Johnson Answers Dryden

The stricture's longest paragraph is a correction of Dryden, and it is the one place where Johnson's verdict on this play engages a predecessor's by name. Dryden had reported a tradition that Shakespeare declared himself obliged to kill Mercutio in the third act lest he should have been killed by him. Johnson treated the anecdote as a pointed sentence in which more regard is commonly had to the words than to the thought: a witty confession of Mercutio's brilliance, not a piece of constructional evidence. Mercutio's wit, gaiety, and courage, he wrote, will always procure him friends who wish him a longer life, but his death is not precipitated; he has lived out the time allotted him in the construction of the play, and Shakespeare could have continued him without difficulty. The full case for the anecdote and its long afterlife belongs to the companion study of [Dryden on Mercutio: 'Shakespeare Killed Him'](/2026/10/02/dryden-on-mercutio-1672/), and it is noticed here only because Johnson's rebuttal is part of his verdict, and a revealing part. Faced with a choice between a charming story about the author's helplessness and a judgement about the finished structure, Johnson chose the structure. Mercutio dies when the play's design has finished with him, at the hinge where comedy must be withdrawn so that its consequences can be suffered; the death is precipitated neither by the author's fear nor by accident, and Johnson's word allotted, with its suggestion of a span measured out in advance, states the constructional reading in a single term.

The constructional reading can be cashed out in the play's own terms, and doing so shows how much Johnson compressed into the one word. Mercutio's span is allotted by three necessities. He must live long enough to establish the world Romeo is leaving, the Verona of wit, honour, and street violence whose codes the lover has been trying to resign from since the feast; the Queen Mab speech and the pun duel of 2.4 are that world's fullest exhibitions, and they require their exhibitor alive and at his height. He must die by Tybalt's sword under Romeo's arm, because only a death Romeo causes by intervening can convert the bridegroom into a killer within the hour of his marriage and make the banishment a consequence of love itself rather than of feud alone. And he must die before the tomb plot assembles, because his register, the register in which every catastrophe is first a joke, cannot be admitted to the play's final movement without dissolving it; the comedy is not dropped from the design but spent, converted into the grief that powers Acts 4 and 5. A character whose death performs three such offices has not been removed by an author's panic. He has been used, completely, which is what allotted means. Johnson's rebuttal also concedes the anecdote its only real claim: Dryden's tradition, he allowed, might easily have reached Dryden's time from Shakespeare's, so the story may be genuine as table talk. What he denies is its jurisdiction. Even a true remark by the author about his difficulties is evidence about the workshop, not about the building, and Johnson's criticism is consistently a criticism of buildings.

The paragraph ends with a stroke of Johnsonian justice that has delighted readers ever since: having defended Mercutio against Dryden's implication, he turns and sentences Dryden himself, whose genius, he wrote, was not very fertile of merriment, nor ductile to humour, but acute, argumentative, comprehensive, and sublime. The sentence is a miniature of Johnson's critical manner, censure and commendation weighed in one period and neither cancelled. It also quietly vindicates the play at Dryden's expense: the sallies of Mercutio that strain Dryden's reach are, by that very fact, exhibits of a kind of genius the Augustan master lacked, and the tragedy that contains them is enlarged by the comparison. Johnson's engagement with Dryden here is mention-length by design in the history of the play's criticism the anecdote belongs to Dryden's account, but as a piece of judging it is Johnson's paragraph that survives, because it replaces gossip with construction.

## The Nurse in Eight Pairs: Johnson's Best Sentence on the Play

The stricture's paragraph on the Nurse is the finest single sentence of character criticism in Johnson's notes on the play, and one of the finest he wrote on any character. The Nurse, he wrote, is one of the characters in which the author delighted; he has, with great subtility of distinction, drawn her at once loquacious and secret, obsequious and insolent, trusty and dishonest. The observation is exact in every pair. Loquacious: the woman of 1.3, whose account of Juliet's weaning expands through the earthquake, the dove-house wall, and her husband's jest, incapable of reaching a point without colonizing the road to it (1.3.25-55). Secret: the same woman keeps the marriage hidden from the Capulet household through two acts of crisis, a confidante whose discretion inside the plot is absolute even while her tongue runs free inside the chamber. Obsequious: she fawns on Romeo, on Juliet, on the Friar, bending to every superior presence. Insolent: she keeps Juliet waiting in 2.5 as a deliberate sport, withholding the news her mistress burns for, and lectures Romeo in the street on his intentions with a freedom no servant's place allows. Trusty: she carries the ladder, the messages, the ring. Dishonest: in 3.5 she counsels Juliet to marry Paris and dismisses the living husband as a dishclout, betraying in one speech the trust of three acts.

### How does the Nurse prove Johnson's rule that a character is a species?

Johnson held that Shakespeare's persons are commonly a species, the progeny of common humanity rather than portraits of individuals. The Nurse is his proof in miniature: her contraries are not one woman's oddities but the traits of the trusted servant in every household, garrulous and discreet, fawning and familiar, faithful in service and faithless in counsel, recognizable in any age.

The sentence's durability rests on its method. Johnson does not describe the Nurse's function, her dialect, or her symbolic place; he identifies the principle of her construction, which is that Shakespeare built her from contraries held in suspension, and that the contraries are all true at once because they belong to a recognizable kind of human being. Later criticism has added the sociology and the psychoanalysis, and has worried the morality of her 3.5 advice at far greater length; it has not improved on the eight paired words as a map of what an actor must hold together in the part. Hagstrum's account of Johnson's criticism stresses that his character judgements proceed from general nature, from what observation finds in the world, and the Nurse sentence is the method at its best: a species identified by its contrary marks, with subtility of distinction praised because distinction, the refusal to blur one trait into its neighbour, is the quality Johnson most prized in a delineator of life. Where the Preface's generalities can feel like a system in search of instances, this sentence is an instance that justifies the system.

Her own great narrative shows what the doctrine catches in performance. The account of the weaning in 1.3 (1.3.25-55) is a masterpiece of circumstantial delay: the earthquake eleven years past, the wormwood on the dug, the child falling forward and cutting her brow, the dead husband's jest repeated because the teller cannot bear to leave it, Juliet's age computed three separate times by three separate routes. The speech is the loquacious half of Johnson's first pair exhibited at full length, and exhibited as a kind of knowledge: this is how a household servant's memory works, by anecdote and association rather than by register, and the comedy is the fidelity. The secret half of the pair is the same faculty turned to plot, the woman who cannot stop talking contriving, across Acts 2 and 3, to say nothing that matters to anyone who must not hear it. Her talkativeness and her discretion are not two traits but one, the garrulity of a woman whose whole social existence is speech, deployed volubly where speech is safe and withheld, at real cost to her mistress's patience in 2.5 and to her mistress's safety in 3.5, where withholding curdles into betrayal. Johnson's phrase subtility of distinction is itself an act of distinction, separating what a coarser criticism merges, and the sentence has lasted because the separation is the character.

## The Objection: From the Preface's Quibble to the Note's Conceit

Against all of this stands the reservation, and it has two storeys. The upper storey is the Preface's famous assault on the quibble. Shakespeare, Johnson wrote, is not long soft and pathetic without some idle conceit or contemptible equivocation; he no sooner begins to move than he counteracts himself, and terror and pity, as they are rising in the mind, are checked and blasted by sudden frigidity. Then the metaphors close in: a quibble is to Shakespeare what luminous vapours are to the traveller, sure to lead him out of his way and engulf him in the mire; a quibble is the golden apple for which he will always turn aside from his career; a quibble was to him the fatal Cleopatra for which he lost the world, and was content to lose it. The passage is the most quoted piece of Shakespeare criticism Johnson wrote, and its violence is deliberate: the quibble is figured not as an ornament misplaced but as an addiction, a malignant power over the poet's mind whose fascinations are irresistible, purchased by the sacrifice of reason, propriety, and truth.

The lower storey is the stricture's version of the charge, and the difference between the storeys is the most important fact about Johnson's objection to this play. In the note on Romeo and Juliet the enemy is named not quibble but conceit, and the complaint is not that wordplay interrupts banter but that suffering itself is made to speak in figures: his persons, however distressed, have a conceit left them in their misery, a miserable conceit. The pathetic strains are polluted with unexpected depravations. Johnson's target has narrowed from the pun to the elaborate figure, from Mercutio's fencing with words to the rhetoric of the desperate: Romeo's oxymorons, Juliet's equivocations, the figured speeches of the tomb. The narrowing matters for fairness. A critic who hated wordplay root and branch would have had to condemn the feast sonnet, the pun duel of 2.4, and Mercutio's dying joke, and Johnson's stricture condemns none of them; it praises the comic scenes in which most of the quibbling lives. His charge against this play is aimed at a specific transaction, the moment when feeling at its highest pitch is exchanged for ingenuity, and terror and pity are checked by the reader's awareness of the author's cleverness. Whether the play commits that transaction is a question of evidence, and Johnson, to his credit, supplied evidence in his notes.

One feature of the Preface passage deserves remark before the evidence is weighed, because it disciplines how the charge should be read. Johnson's assault on the conceit is itself built from conceits, three of them in as many sentences: the quibble as luminous vapour misleading a traveller, as the golden apple of the judgement of Paris, as Cleopatra costing Antony the world. The most figure-drunk paragraph in his criticism is the paragraph against figures. This is not hypocrisy, and treating it as a gotcha misses the distinction his whole practice observes. Johnson's Cleopatra conceit pays for itself: it carries, in a single image, a psychology of addiction, a scale of loss, and a judgement on the bargain, and it arrives in a critical essay whose business is judgement rather than pathos. His objection is never to the figure as such but to the figure bought at the wrong price in the wrong register, ingenuity purchased by the sacrifice of the feeling the scene exists to raise. The metaphors also reveal his method. Terror and pity checked and blasted by sudden frigidity is a report from inside a reader: the charge is phenomenological before it is doctrinal, a description of what happens in the mind at the moment the conceit lands. That is why the charge remains testable when the doctrine that framed it has dated. A reader can rerun Johnson's experiment at any passage, asking whether the figure raises the temperature or drops it, and the experiment, rather than the authority, is the inheritance.

## Testing the Conceit Charge Against the Play's Craft

The full anatomy of the play's wordplay, its servants' opening chain of coal and choler, its duel of puns, its migration from the street to the tomb, is the subject of [Puns and Quibbles in Romeo and Juliet](/2025/01/17/puns-and-quibbles-romeo-juliet/), and the charge is tested here only where Johnson himself laid it. His clearest exhibit is the first he annotated. On Romeo's entrance soliloquy of antitheses, the brawling love and loving hate of 1.1 (1.1.181-193), Johnson remarked that neither the sense nor the occasion of the lines is very evident: Romeo is not yet in love with an enemy, and to love one woman and hate none is no such uncommon state as can deserve all this toil of antithesis. The note is a small masterpiece of Johnsonian auditing. It does not call the lines ugly; it asks what they are for, and finds the expenditure of figure disproportionate to the emotional capital available, since the Rosaline passion is a posture the play itself treats as secondhand. The conceit is unearned, and Johnson's word toil names the disproportion exactly. Against this exhibit, M. M. Mahood's defence of the wordplay in her study Shakespeare's Wordplay, the most sustained modern answer to Johnson, has surprisingly little to say, because Mahood's case rests on wordplay that binds the play's opposed worlds of love and death into a single verbal system; the Rosaline antitheses bind nothing, being borrowed Petrarchan counters that the play will discard the moment Romeo sees Juliet. Round one, on Johnson's chosen ground, goes to Johnson.

The second exhibit reverses the result. Johnson's general law predicts that the play's most distressed persons will forfeit pity by retaining a conceit in their misery, and the play's most distressed wit is Mercutio on the point of death: ask for me tomorrow, Mercutio says, and you shall find me a grave man (3.1.96-97), punning on the word that names his condition as the joke lands; a plague o' both your houses follows within three lines (3.1.99). By the letter of the Preface this should be the frigidity that blasts terror and pity. In performance and in reading it is the opposite: the pun is the character's courage made audible, the last exercise of the faculty that defined him, and the audience's pity is sharpened, not checked, by hearing the wit spend itself on its own extinction. Mahood's account explains why: the wordplay of this play is not decoration laid over its action but the medium in which its two houses, its love and its hate, continually exchange properties, so that a dying joke on grave is the feud's whole logic compressed into a syllable. Johnson's framework contains the instrument that corrects his verdict, since his own test was probability and nature, and a man of Mercutio's established constitution joking at the edge of death is among the most probable things in the play. The adjudication that follows from the evidence is therefore a split verdict with a rule. The quibble and the conceit are faults where they ornament a feeling the scene has not earned, as in the Rosaline antitheses, and instruments where they carry the action's meaning in the speaker's established voice, as in the grave man pun, the shared sonnet at the feast (1.5.92-109), where the conceit of pilgrims and saints is the lovers' negotiation conducted in the only public language available to them, and Romeo's desperate pilot figure in the tomb (5.3.116-118), where the conceit of the sea-sick weary bark steered onto the dashing rocks is not a decoration of his despair but its navigation chart. Johnson wins his chosen case and loses his general law; Mahood wins the law and must concede the case. The stricture's famous sentence survives the audit in reduced but honourable form, as a warning about a real failure mode that this play exhibits early and outgrows.

A principled line inside Johnson's own criticism explains the pattern of wins and losses, though he never drew it in terms. The Preface faults Shakespeare's declamations and set speeches as commonly cold and weak, on the ground that his power was the power of nature, and that when he solicits his invention the offspring of his throes is tumour and obscurity. The conceits that fail Johnson's test in this play are, without exception, monologic: Romeo's solitary antitheses in 1.1, the Chorus speaking alone to the house, Juliet's equivocations soliloquized in dialogue's clothing. The conceits that pass are dialogic, figures exchanged between speakers as moves in a negotiation. The shared sonnet at the feast is the type case: pilgrims, saints, and holy palmers are not Romeo's decoration of a feeling already complete but the medium in which two strangers conduct, in public and in code, the most consequential conversation of their lives, each quatrain an offer and each answer a counter-offer that raises the bid. Figure as action between persons is nature by Johnson's definition, since conversation is life transcribed; figure as embroidery on a solitary passion is invention solicited, and throe-work. The distinction rescues the stricture's charge from blanket error and gives it a jurisdiction: the miserable conceit is the conceit nobody answers. Mahood's structural account reaches the same border from the other side, since the wordplay she shows binding the play's worlds is overwhelmingly exchange, in duels, sonnets, and deathbed rallies, rather than solo display. Two methods that begin in opposite centuries converge on the same map of the play's language, and the convergence is the strongest evidence that the map is true.

Two further exhibits fix the charge's borders, and Johnson's silence about both is itself evidence. The first is the Queen Mab speech (1.4.53-95), the most elaborate conceit engine in the play, a ninety-line fantasia in which Mercutio builds a fairy mythology out of hazelnut chariots and grasshopper wings to mock Romeo's faith in dreams. By any reckoning of ingenuity this is the play's supreme set piece of figure, yet Johnson's stricture places it, by implication, on the credit side, inside the conversation of gentlemen whose airy sprightliness he praised. The exemption is principled: the charge polices the pathetic register, where feeling is the scene's business, and Queen Mab is comic anatomy, a conceit doing diagnostic work in the register where conceits belong. A critic whose objection were to wordplay itself could not have sorted the play this way; Johnson's objection is to wordplay's trespass across the border into grief. The second exhibit is the one his law would most damage and his method most protect, Juliet's potion speech (4.3.14-58), which he left unannotated. Its terrors are figured throughout: the vault imagined as a charnel of her ancestors, the fear of waking mad and plucking at corpses, shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth. Yet the figures are hypotheses, each one a pictured form of a fear the speech is in the act of enumerating, and they alternate with statements of forensic plainness about the mixture's possible failure. A terrified imagination works by picturing; the conceits are the fear's own method, and by Johnson's test of probability they pass, since the speech represents a mind doing exactly what such a mind does. His law, applied mechanically, would condemn the passage his premises acquit. The pattern is the same one the Mercutio paragraph exposed: wherever the audit is rerun with his instruments rather than his conclusions, the play emerges less guilty than the charge sheet, and Johnson's framework emerges as a better critic of the play than Johnson's verdict, which is the most durable compliment a critical system can earn.

Johnson's treatment of Juliet's equivocations in 3.5 belongs to the same audit and reaches the opposite conclusion from his Mercutio paragraph. Her double-edged speech to her mother, mourning Romeo in phrases that Lady Capulet hears as mourning Tybalt and vowing revenge in phrases that promise the reverse (3.5.84-102), struck him as equivocations rather too artful for a mind disturbed by the loss of a new lover. Here the objection is decorum, the fitting of speech to passion, and it is the weakest of his substantive notes: the artfulness is the character's predicament, since Juliet must speak her grief in a house where its object is criminal, and the scene's pain depends on the audience hearing both meanings while her mother hears one. Johnson the auditor of probability, so exact on the oxymorons, here mistakes a dramatic instrument for a psychological implausibility, and the mistake is diagnostic of his limit. Where feeling is direct he judges superbly; where feeling is necessarily dissimulated, his demand for nature read as sincerity misleads him.

## The Moral Lens: Where It Falls on This Play, and Where It Does Not

The Preface's first and gravest charge against Shakespeare is moral. He sacrifices virtue to convenience, Johnson wrote, and is so much more careful to please than to instruct that he seems to write without any moral purpose; he makes no just distribution of good or evil, carries his persons indifferently through right and wrong, and at the close dismisses them without further care, leaving their examples to operate by chance, though it is always a writer's duty to make the world better. Any account that presents Johnson's reading of Romeo and Juliet as a moral reading must start from that indictment. The documents, however, support a more precise and more interesting conclusion. Read against this play, the moral charge is the framework's emptiest drawer. The closing stricture contains no moral judgement on the lovers whatsoever: no word on disobedience, none on the secret marriage, none on the suicides, none on whether the catastrophe distributes good and evil justly. Johnson, who ended his note on Othello in visible distress at its murders and who lectured other plays on their justice, closed this play on pleasingness, probability, and construction. The silence is itself a finding. The century's most formidable moral critic examined the tale his culture used as a warning against disobedient children, the tale Arthur Brooke's poem had prefaced with a sermon against the lovers, and delivered a verdict in which the sermon does not appear. Johnson's moralism, on this evidence, was a real commitment that his reading of the actual play repeatedly declined to enforce.

The deliberateness of the refusal can be measured against the tradition he inherited. The story reached Shakespeare already moralized: Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562, the play's chief source, carried a preface instructing its readers to regard the lovers as a warning, disobedient children who abused their parents' authority, resorted to dishonest counsel, and met the end such conduct earns. Johnson knew the transmission history; his Preface pauses on this very story to remark that Shakespeare followed the English translation where it deviates from the Italian. The sermon was therefore on his desk, part of the documentary furniture of the play he was editing, and the stricture's silence about the lovers' guilt is a silence maintained in the sermon's presence. Rowe's moral settlement, described above, had kept a version of Brooke's sentence in force by redirecting it at the parents. Johnson redirected nothing and sentenced nobody. Where the received reading of the tale was a prosecution, his verdict is a report on workmanship and effect, and the shift is one of the places where the criticism of this play can be watched changing its century.

### Did Johnson blame the lovers themselves for the catastrophe?

No word in his stricture blames them, and the absence is conspicuous. The critic whose Preface charged Shakespeare with making no just distribution of good or evil called this catastrophe irresistibly affecting and moved on to construction, Mercutio, and the Nurse. His remarks on the play fall elsewhere, on a chorus and a heroine's stratagems, not on the lovers' guilt.

The moral lens does flash twice in the notes, and both flashes deserve scrutiny precisely because they are exceptions. The first is the note on the Act 2 Chorus: its use, Johnson wrote, is not easily discovered, since it conduces nothing to the progress of the play, relates only what is already known or what the next scenes will show, and relates it without adding the improvement of any moral sentiment. The criterion invoked is instruction; a sonnet that merely narrates fails the Preface's test that poetry instruct by pleasing. The second flash is sharper. On Juliet's request to be left alone before taking the potion, with her talk of needing many orisons (4.3.2-4), Johnson remarked that Juliet plays most of her pranks under the appearance of religion, and wondered whether Shakespeare meant to punish her hypocrisy. The sentence is the most Augustan thing he wrote on the play and the least defensible: the pranks are the desperate stratagems of a girl of not quite fourteen manoeuvring between a forced marriage and a clandestine one, the orisons line is camouflage inside a household that has left her no honest speech, and to read the camouflage as hypocrisy deserving punishment is to import into the play the conduct-book morality that Brooke's preface had imposed and that Shakespeare's text pointedly omits. A third note shows the moral sense working better. Glossing the Friar's maxim that too swift arrives as tardy as too slow (2.6.15), Johnson extracted its sententia without fuss: precipitation produces mishap. And on Benvolio's partial narration of the fatal duel, he observed that the best minds, in a state of faction and discord, are detorted to criminal partiality (3.1), a moral psychology of the feud more penetrating than any sermon on the lovers. The pattern across the four notes is consistent and worth stating as the section's verdict: Johnson's moral intelligence serves this play well when it reads the feud's adults and badly when it reads the heroine's necessities, and his closing verdict, to its credit, is built entirely from the former kind of attention and from his aesthetic findings, leaving the moralizing notes as minority reports that his own stricture overruled.

## The Store of Axioms: Johnson and the Play's Practical Wisdom

One strand of the Preface's praise is easy to overlook because it praises the plays as a warehouse rather than as dramas. It was said of Euripides, Johnson wrote, that every verse was a precept, and it may be said of Shakespeare that from his works may be collected a system of civil and economical prudence; the plays are filled with practical axioms and domestic wisdom, the sentences by which households and commonwealths are actually steered. On this account an editor's duty includes quarrying the precepts, and Johnson's notes on Romeo and Juliet show the quarryman at work. His characteristic gloss on this play is the extraction of a maxim. The Friar's couplet that too swift arrives as tardy as too slow (2.6.15) he renders, without comment beyond the rendering, as a rule of conduct: precipitation produces mishap. The gloss looks like nothing, three words for a line, and it is Johnson's theory of Shakespearean instruction in miniature, the couplet valued not as poetry but as portable wisdom, a sentence a reader can carry out of the theatre and spend. The play is unusually rich in such currency, and it concentrates the currency in the same speaker. Friar Laurence is the tragedy's axiom engine: wisely and slow, they stumble that run fast (2.3.94); these violent delights have violent ends (2.6.9); virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, and vice sometime by action dignified (2.3.17-18). Johnson's edition, by glossing where it glosses, ratifies the Friar as the play's licensed moralist, the one voice whose sentences are meant to be detached.

The ratification deserves one qualification, which Johnson's own stricture supplies by implication. The play does not endorse its axiom engine; it refutes him by event. Every maxim the Friar utters is true, and the catastrophe happens anyway, through the faithful application of his prudence by a man whose caution engineers the very haste he warns against. Johnson, who extracted the maxims so faithfully in the notes, nowhere remarks in the stricture that the play ironizes its own storehouse, that the system of prudence is exhibited failing in the hands of its ablest practitioner. The omission is the quiet counterpart of his moral silence about the lovers: his editorial practice honoured the play's wisdom literature, and his critical verdict, intent on construction and effect, left unasked the question of what the tragedy thinks of the wisdom it contains. Later readers have made that question central, treating the Friar's sententiae as the play's built-in commentary, continually pronounced and continually outrun. Johnson's notes remain the indispensable starting point for the inquiry precisely because they perform the extraction so cleanly: no reader can see what the play does to its maxims until the maxims have been lifted out and priced, and nobody priced them earlier or more exactly than the editor who believed a system of prudence could be collected from these pages.

## The Small Notes: Johnson Reading Closely

The remaining notes show the judicial critic at line level, and they complicate any portrait of him as a theorist imposing a system. On Capulet's promise that the feast's ladies will be earth-treading stars that make dark heaven bright (1.2.24-25), Warburton had pronounced the reading nonsense and emended heaven to even; Johnson refused the emendation with a question: why nonsense, when beauties are commonly said to eclipse the sun? Both readings, he concluded, are philosophical nonsense and both are equally poetical sense. The note is a small charter of editorial restraint from the critic who is supposed to have bullied the text: where the transmitted words yield poetry, the editor's logic must stand down. His own conjecture at the neighbouring speech shows the other side of the practice. Capulet's promise that Paris will feel such comfort as lusty young men feel when April treads on the heel of limping winter (1.2.26-28) struck Johnson as sound wasted on a poor sentiment, April pleasure being tautologous in young men, and he proposed reading yeomen, whose delight in spring is a farmer's economic joy, the plenty of the year beginning. The emendation has persuaded nobody, but the note is valuable evidence of his method: sentiment audited for its information content, found empty, and repaired by a word that gives the image a reason to exist.

### Why does Johnson puzzle over Romeo's cheerfulness at Mantua?

Because Romeo opens the Mantua scene by reporting that his bosom's lord sits lightly on his throne (5.1.1-3), a dream-fed cheerfulness standing immediately before the news of Juliet's death. Johnson found the three lines very gay and pleasing, then asked why Shakespeare grants his hero this involuntary exaltation at the extremity of unhappiness, and answered with a moral about omens.

His answer to his own question was that Shakespeare perhaps meant to show the vanity of trusting those uncertain and casual exaltations or depressions that many consider certain foretokens of good and evil. The gloss converts a tonal problem into a doctrine, and the doctrine is Johnson's anti-superstition speaking rather than the play; the cheerfulness is better explained by the design, which needs Romeo's hope at its highest so that Balthasar's news can drop him the full distance. Yet even a strained note tells the truth about its writer: confronted by an effect he could feel but not derive, Johnson reached for the moral use, as his Preface said a writer should. Other small notes need no such discount. His observation on the two old Capulets at the feast, weighing the guest list's uncle against the cousin of the dialogue and remarking, dry as a census, that Capulet has been past masking for thirty years while his lady is eight and twenty (1.5), shows the auditor of probability at work inside a joke. His remark that in Italy almost all assassinations are committed during the heat of summer, appended to Benvolio's the day is hot (3.1.2), is the illustrative note as traveller's tale, wrong in its criminology and right in its instinct that the line is doing causal work. And his regret at the tomb, that the poet did not conclude the dialogue with the action instead of staging the Friar's narrative of events the audience already knew (5.3.229), applies the Preface's standing complaint about narration and neglected latter parts to the one passage of this play where the complaint is just: the explanation after the catastrophe is the tragedy's single slack joint, and Johnson found it.

The cousin Capulet note rewards a second look, because it shows the auditor of probability working in his lightest register and reveals how far his consistency checking ranged. The invitation list Capulet sends by his servant names, among the guests, his uncle Capulet; the old man who appears at the feast is addressed in the dialogue as cousin. Johnson ruled for cousin in both places, on evidence drawn from the whole play: the speaker has been past masking for thirty years, the flourish of a man remembering his dancing days, while his wife, by her own account to Juliet, is twenty-eight. The inference is playful and the method is not. Johnson is treating the play as a world whose facts must cohere, cross-examining a guest list against a dance-floor boast and a mother's arithmetic, and correcting the smaller document by the larger pattern. This is the same mind that refused Warburton's emendation two scenes earlier, and the pair of notes together define his editorial conscience on this play: the text is not to be corrected where its poetry is intelligible, and it is to be corrected, quietly and on the record of the play itself, where its documents contradict one another. Editors who came after him built variorum apparatuses on exactly this double principle, usually without noticing that Johnson had stated it by example in the space of a single act.

## Two Endings: The Catastrophe Johnson Praised and the Explanation He Regretted

Johnson's paired judgements on the ending, the catastrophe irresistibly affecting and the Friar's narrative much to be lamented, amount to the discovery that this play has two endings and that they are of unequal value. The first ending is the tomb: Paris dead at the door, Romeo's desperate pilot speech and the poison, Juliet waking to a husband already cooling and finishing the design with his dagger. Of this ending Johnson's praise is unqualified, and it is worth noting that the praise survives his own charge sheet, since the tomb speeches are figured, Romeo steering his sea-sick weary bark onto the dashing rocks in an extended conceit (5.3.116-118) at the very summit of distress. The stricture's affective verdict and its linguistic verdict here part company, and the affective verdict wins in the critic's own sentence order: irresistibly affecting is recorded of the catastrophe as a whole, the conceit charge filed as a general observation about the pathetic strains. A reader who applies Johnson's temperature test honestly will find the tomb the place where his law and his experience most plainly diverge, and will trust the experience, as Johnson, in the stricture's arrangement, seems to have done.

The second ending is the settlement: the Watch, the Prince, the Friar's narrative, the fathers' reconciliation over the bodies, the promised statues in pure gold (5.3.298-303). Johnson's single intervention in this movement is his regret at the Friar's speech, and its terms are exact. He does not object to the reconciliation, the statues, or the Prince's closing sentence that never was a story of more woe (5.3.309-310); he objects to narration, to the play's concluding its dialogue after its action and re-telling, in the Friar's person, events the audience has watched. The objection descends from the Preface's doctrine that narration in dramatic poetry is naturally tedious, unanimated and inactive, and from its complaint that Shakespeare's latter parts are often neglected as the reward comes into view. Applied here it identifies the one movement of the tragedy that operates by report rather than by enactment. Yet the silence around the objection is as significant as the objection. Johnson, the century's moralist, had nothing to say about the settlement's moral content, neither praising the feud's burial as instruction nor censuring the statues as an inadequate distribution of good and evil. His interest in the ending is exhausted by its mechanics and its pathos. The civic moral, on which Rowe's settlement had staked everything, is left unclaimed on the table, further evidence that the stricture's author judged this play in a different court from the one his Preface had built for the canon in general, and that the play was the beneficiary of the change of venue.

## The InsightCrunch Johnson Ledger

Set out in one view, Johnson's remarks on the play form a double column, credits on one side and debits on the other, and the column a reader assembles depends on gathering the Preface's framework, a dozen line notes, and the closing stricture into a single account. The ledger exposes the pattern at a glance. The credit column is filled by the play's construction, its variety, its catastrophe, its gentlemen, its Mercutio, and its Nurse; the debit column is filled almost entirely by its language under passion, its Chorus, and its heroine's dissimulations. Johnson praised what the play does and faulted how its sufferers talk, and the asymmetry is his verdict's signature: he trusted the fable completely and the figures only in part.

A deeper regularity runs under the asymmetry, and the ledger's rows reveal it when read by kind rather than by subject. Every debit attaches to a passage where the play stops enacting and starts declaring: a Chorus that narrates what scenes will show, a Friar who narrates what scenes have shown, a lover who declares a passion in antitheses instead of transacting it, a heroine whose declarations are engineered to be misheard. Every credit attaches to enactment: incidents concatenated, conversation exchanged, a death allotted and suffered, a character held in her contraries. Johnson's distrust, across the whole dossier, is a distrust of the play's statements about itself, and his trust is a trust in its performances. That is the Preface's hierarchy of evidence operating at the scale of a single tragedy, imitation credited because it brings realities to mind and rhetoric discounted because it brings only itself. A reader who grasps the regularity can predict Johnson's judgement on passages he never annotated, which is the mark of a verdict with a method inside it rather than a bundle of tastes.

## What the Verdict Did: The Play's Standing After Johnson

Johnson's edition became the century's standard Shakespeare, and the stricture travelled with it, reprinted in edition after edition until the judgement that the play is one of the most pleasing of the performances had the force of a settled finding rather than one man's opinion; acting editions of the following century still quoted the note entire in their introductions, a survival no other eighteenth-century verdict on the play achieved. The effect on the play's standing was specific. Rowe, the century's first editor-critic, had treated the tragedy as a moral engine whose catastrophe punishes the feuding parents; the mid-century rule critics, Gildon and Lord Kames among them, had marked it down for violating the classical laws and for letting accident, not character, drive the ending. Johnson's verdict displaced both settlements. Against the moralizers it offered a play judged by pleasure, probability, and construction, with the lovers uncondemned. Against the rule critics it offered the Preface's acquittals: the mingled drama defended by nature, the unities dissolved by the spectators' common sense, unity of action affirmed and found present. The Romantics inherited a play thus certified, and when Coleridge lectured on it a generation later he was refining, inside a new vocabulary of organic form, judgements whose licences Johnson had already issued; the succession is traced in the study of [Coleridge's Lectures on Romeo and Juliet](/2026/10/16/coleridge-lectures-romeo-and-juliet/), where the Romantic case begins from ground Johnson cleared.

The transmission of the verdict after 1765 explains part of its authority. Part of the explanation is Johnson's own sentence-making. He wrote the stricture in the definitional manner of his Dictionary, judgement compressed into sentences built to be lifted whole, and editors obliged him for a century because the opening sentence could stand alone in an introduction and carry its credentials with it. Verdicts that require their arguments travel badly; Johnson's travels light, which is one reason the balanced reading of this play, rather than Pepys's hostility or Rowe's moral, became the one the culture kept in circulation. Johnson's edition was reissued under George Steevens's care in 1773 and again in 1778, the notes carried forward into the Johnson-Steevens line that fed the variorum tradition, so the stricture ceased to be one editor's endpaper and became apparatus, the standing comment through which later readers approached the play. Nineteenth-century editions inherited it as furniture; the Pattie stage edition of 1839, a promptbook for Macready's Covent Garden, printed the whole note in its introduction as the play's critical passport, seventy-four years after Johnson wrote it, in a volume whose business was costumes and casting rather than criticism. A judgement quoted entire by a theatrical publisher three generations on has passed the Preface's own test, length of duration and continuance of esteem, in the form Johnson prescribed for poets, applied to a critic. The phrase one of the most pleasing hardened accordingly into something like the century's official finding, and successors defined themselves against it: the Romantic lecturers who recast the play as a poem of youth were answering, among others, a Johnson who had already certified its pleasingness and quarrelled only with its figures.

The final question is the value of the assessment itself, and the audit conducted here supports a firm answer. Johnson's verdict is the most valuable short judgement on the play from his century because its praise identifies the merits that have proved permanent, variety, construction, the Nurse, Mercutio's allotted span, and its blame identifies the one fault line that criticism has never since been able to ignore, the traffic between feeling and figure in the play's language. Its errors are the instructive errors of a system meeting its limit: a moralism that twice mistook a heroine's necessities for her sins, and a law against the conceit that his own evidence refutes at the play's greatest moment of distressed wit. Hagstrum's Johnson, the systematic philosopher of criticism, and Sherbo's Johnson, the annotator deciding case by case, are both present in the notes on this play, and the standing dispute between those portraits can be settled on this play's evidence, because here the two Johnsons can be watched interacting sentence by sentence. The framework, Hagstrum's contribution, selected the questions: mixture, unities, probability, character, instruction. Without the Preface's argued positions, the stricture's phrases would be obiter dicta; busy and various draws its meaning from the mingled-drama defence, congruity to popular opinions from the essay on probability, the Nurse's contraries from the doctrine of species. The occasion, Sherbo's contribution, supplied the answers, and repeatedly supplied them against the framework's expectations: the comic scenes acquitted though the Preface had censured Shakespeare's wit contests, the catastrophe praised though the Preface predicted frigidity, the lovers uncondemned though the Preface demanded moral distribution. A system that selects the questions and a reader who answers them independently is not a failed system; it is criticism working as Johnson said judgement works, improved by practice rather than by submission to dictatorial decisions, including his own. The verdict on Romeo and Juliet is the strongest exhibit for that account of his method, and the verdict is strongest exactly where the annotator overruled the philosopher: in the stricture's refusal to condemn what the fable had acquitted. A balanced verdict is often a timid one. Johnson's is balanced the way a scale is balanced, because the weights on both sides are real.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Johnson ledger, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Johnson ledger is the table in this article that gathers Samuel Johnson's judgements on Romeo and Juliet from his 1765 edition into a single double account: his credits on one side, his debits on the other, each with its source in the Preface, a line note, or the closing stricture. The pattern it exposes is an asymmetry at the heart of his verdict. Johnson's credits attach to what the play does, its construction, its variety, its catastrophe, its gentlemen, its Mercutio, and its Nurse. His debits attach almost entirely to how the play's sufferers talk, the conceits they retain in misery, the Chorus that adds no moral sentiment, and the heroine's dissimulations. The ledger makes that shape visible at a glance: a critic who trusted the fable completely and the figures only in part.

### Q: What did Samuel Johnson call Romeo and Juliet in his edition's closing note?

He called it one of the most pleasing of the author's performances, and the phrase is a technical verdict rather than a compliment. In Johnson's critical vocabulary, poetry exists to instruct by pleasing, and pleasure ratified by audiences over a long duration is the only test available for works that appeal to observation and experience. A tragedy of two dead children that earns the term pleasing has, on his scale, performed the full office of its kind at the highest rate of success. The sentence opens his closing stricture and governs everything that follows in it: the praise of busy and various scenes, of numerous and important incidents, and of a catastrophe he found irresistibly affecting. Later readers have sometimes treated pleasing as faint praise. Johnson meant it as a ranking.

### Q: Why did Johnson praise the play's scenes as busy and various?

Because the two adjectives certify the two halves of his theory of drama. Busy certifies the incidents: the play never idles, and Johnson prized economy of action enough to fault Shakespeare elsewhere for plots so loosely formed that slight consideration might improve them. Various certifies the mixture of kinds: street, feast, orchard, cell, and tomb, high passion beside low comedy, in the endless variety of proportion that his Preface described as the mark of sublunary nature faithfully copied. A play that is busy has earned its length; a play that is various has earned its mixture. Together the words record that Romeo and Juliet satisfied, in one sentence, both the demand for momentum and the demand for fidelity to a world where the reveller hastes to his wine while the mourner buries his friend.

### Q: How does Johnson's defence of mingled drama protect this play's mixture of comedy and tragedy?

The Preface declares that Shakespeare's plays are not, in the rigorous critical sense, either tragedies or comedies, but compositions of a distinct kind exhibiting the real state of sublunary nature, in which good and evil, joy and sorrow, are mingled with endless variety. That declaration acquits Romeo and Juliet in advance of the standard neoclassical charge. The Nurse's bawdry, Mercutio's fantasia, and the musicians quarrelling over the supposed corpse in Act 4 are not border violations between kinds but elements of a single imitation of life, and Johnson adds that there is always an appeal open from criticism to nature. The objection that mingled scenes interrupt the passions he dismissed as reasoning contradicted by daily experience. The play's comedy, on his account, is part of its evidence about Verona, not a holiday from its tragedy.

### Q: Why did Johnson refuse to condemn the play for breaking the unities of time and place?

Because he held that the unities of time and place rest on a false assumption: that a representation must be mistaken for reality by its audience. The spectators, he wrote, are always in their senses, and know from the first act to the last that the stage is only a stage and the players only players, so a mind that can take a candlelit room for a battlefield can allow an hour to stand for days and a stage to stand for Verona and then Mantua. Nothing, he concluded, is essential to the fable but unity of action, and that unity he found preserved in Shakespeare's concatenations of event. Romeo and Juliet, which compresses a courtship, a marriage, two killings, and a double suicide into about four days, is therefore regular where regularity matters and free where freedom costs nothing.

### Q: What did Johnson mean by saying the action has congruity to popular opinions?

He meant that the play's probability is social rather than mechanical. His full judgement was that the process of the action is carried on with such probability, at least with such congruity to popular opinions, as tragedy requires. Johnson did not claim the events of Romeo and Juliet are likely in themselves; a secret marriage, a sleeping potion, and a miscarried letter strain any arithmetic of likelihood. He claimed that each step is licensed by what the play's world believes and practises, about honour and obedience, friars and apothecaries, plague and haste, so that the chain of consequences feels probable to the audience that accepts those opinions. The qualification is one of his most exact critical instruments, and it fits this breathless play better than any demand for literal likelihood could.

### Q: Why did Johnson single out the conversation of gentlemen as one of the play's rare achievements?

Because he believed Shakespeare seldom attempted it. Johnson's stricture calls the play one of the few attempts of Shakespeare to exhibit the conversation of gentlemen, to represent the airy sprightliness of juvenile elegance, and the context is Mercutio's circle: the wit of the street scenes and the Queen Mab exchange, where young men of rank fence with words as a social art. The observation quietly corrects Johnson's own Preface, which had complained that Shakespeare's contests of wit are seldom successful and his gentlemen insufficiently distinguished from his clowns. In this play, he found, the distinction holds: the talk is sprightly rather than gross, marked by class and by youth, and the finding models his method, the general law amended by the particular case under his eye.

### Q: What did Johnson say about the Nurse, and why has the sentence lasted?

He wrote that the Nurse is one of the characters in which the author delighted, drawn with great subtility of distinction at once loquacious and secret, obsequious and insolent, trusty and dishonest. The sentence has lasted because it identifies the principle of her construction rather than her function or her dialect: Shakespeare built her from contraries held in suspension, the gabbling confidante who keeps the marriage secret, the fawning servant who keeps her mistress waiting for sport, the trusty messenger who ends by counselling betrayal. Each pair can be verified against scenes, from the weaning narrative in 1.3 to the dishclout counsel of 3.5, and no later account has improved on the eight paired words as a map of what the part requires an actor to hold together.

### Q: How did Johnson answer Dryden's story that Shakespeare had to kill Mercutio?

He refused to treat the story as evidence. Dryden had reported a tradition that Shakespeare declared himself obliged to kill Mercutio in the third act lest Mercutio should kill him. Johnson replied that in a pointed sentence more regard is commonly had to the words than to the thought, and that the anecdote is a compliment to Mercutio's brilliance rather than a report on the play's construction. Mercutio's wit, gaiety, and courage, he wrote, will always procure him friends who wish him a longer life, but his death is not precipitated: he has lived out the time allotted him in the construction of the play, and Shakespeare could have continued his existence without difficulty. The rebuttal replaces gossip about the author's helplessness with a judgement about the finished design.

### Q: What fault did Johnson find in the play's pathetic scenes?

He found that they are polluted with conceits. His closing stricture allows that the comic scenes are happily wrought, but charges that the pathetic strains are always polluted with some unexpected depravations, and that Shakespeare's persons, however distressed, have a conceit left them in their misery, a miserable conceit. The charge is narrower than the Preface's famous assault on the quibble. Johnson does not condemn the punning of the comic scenes, most of which his praise of the gentlemen's conversation covers; he condemns the elaborate figure that survives into suffering, the moment when a character at the height of passion stops to be ingenious and the audience's terror and pity are checked by awareness of the author's cleverness. Whether the play commits that fault at its great moments is the question his own notes begin to answer.

### Q: Why did Johnson object to Romeo's opening oxymorons about Rosaline?

Because the figures are unearned. Annotating the antithesis chain of Romeo's first soliloquy, the brawling love and loving hate of 1.1, Johnson remarked that neither the sense nor the occasion of the lines is very evident: Romeo is not yet in love with an enemy, loving Rosaline involves no contradiction, and to love one and hate another is no such uncommon state as can deserve all this toil of antithesis. The note does not call the lines ugly; it audits their expenditure, asking what emotional capital the rhetoric has to spend and finding the account nearly empty, since the Rosaline passion is a borrowed posture the play discards the moment Romeo sees Juliet. On this exhibit Johnson's case against the conceit is at its strongest, because here figure really does substitute for feeling rather than carry it.

### Q: Why did Johnson think the Act 2 Chorus served no purpose?

Because it neither advances the action nor improves the audience. The use of this Chorus, he wrote, is not easily discovered: it conduces nothing to the progress of the play, relates only what is already known or what the next scenes will show, and relates it without adding the improvement of any moral sentiment. The note applies two of his standing tests at once. Structurally, a passage that repeats information the scenes are about to supply is dead weight in a fable whose merit is concatenation. Morally, a passage that might have instructed, by framing the lovers' passage from love to death, instead merely narrates. The Chorus sonnet before Act 2 fails both audits, and Johnson's dismissal is the more striking because the sonnet's information, that Romeo has exchanged Rosaline for Juliet, is the hinge of the whole plot.

### Q: What did Johnson mean by suggesting Shakespeare meant to punish Juliet's hypocrisy?

He meant it as a moral explanation of her stratagems, and it is the least convincing note he wrote on the play. Annotating Juliet's request to be left alone with her talk of needing many orisons, on the night before she takes the Friar's potion (4.3), Johnson remarked that Juliet plays most of her pranks under the appearance of religion, and wondered whether Shakespeare meant to punish her hypocrisy. The reading imports into the play the conduct-book morality of its source tradition, in which the lovers are examples to be warned against. Within the play, the supposed hypocrisy is camouflage: a girl of not quite fourteen, trapped between a forced marriage and a secret one, speaking the only pious phrases her household will accept. Johnson's moral framework here mistook a heroine's necessities for her sins, and his own closing stricture, which contains no word of blame for Juliet, in effect overruled the note.

### Q: Why did Johnson call Juliet's equivocations in Act 3 too artful?

On grounds of decorum, the fitting of speech to passion. Juliet's speech to her mother in 3.5 mourns Romeo in phrases Lady Capulet hears as mourning Tybalt, and promises a revenge whose real direction is the opposite of its apparent one. Johnson remarked that the equivocations are rather too artful for a mind disturbed by the loss of a new lover. The objection assumes that a grieving girl would speak plainly, and it is the weakest of his substantive notes on the play, because the artfulness is the character's predicament: Juliet must utter her grief in a house where its object is a criminal enemy, and the scene's pain depends on the audience hearing both meanings while her mother hears one. Johnson, so exact an auditor of probability elsewhere, here mistook a dramatic instrument for a psychological implausibility.

### Q: Why did Johnson regret the Friar's long narrative at the end of the play?

Because it tells the audience what it has just watched. Annotating the Friar's speech beginning I will be brief (5.3), Johnson wrote that it is much to be lamented that the poet did not conclude the dialogue with the action, and avoid a narrative of events which the audience already knew. The note applies two of his standing complaints from the Preface: that narration in dramatic poetry is naturally tedious, being unanimated and inactive, and that Shakespeare's latter parts are often evidently neglected as the end approaches. The Friar's summary, delivered to the Prince while the bodies cool, is the tragedy's single slack joint on Johnson's reading, a passage where the play stops enacting and starts minutes-keeping, and his regret has been shared by most editors and directors since, many of whom trim the speech in performance.

### Q: How did Johnson defend the line about earth-treading stars against Warburton's emendation?

By refusing to let logic overrule poetry. Capulet promises Paris that the feast will show earth-treading stars that make dark heaven bright (1.2). Warburton had pronounced the reading nonsense and emended heaven to even. Johnson answered with a question: why nonsense, when it is commonly said that beauties eclipse the sun, and when Pope himself had used the thought and the word? Both the old reading and the new, he concluded, are philosophical nonsense, but they are both, and both equally, poetical sense. The note is a small charter of editorial restraint from a critic often accused of bullying the text: where the transmitted words yield a recognized poetic figure, the editor's demand for literal consistency must stand down, and the emendation that repairs nothing should not be admitted.

### Q: What did Johnson conclude about Benvolio's account of the fatal fight?

That it is partial, and instructively so. Benvolio narrates the duel of 3.1 to the Prince with a bias toward Romeo that the audience, having watched the fight, can measure. Johnson's note observes that the charge of falsehood against Benvolio, though produced at hazard, is very just, and that the author perhaps meant to show how the best minds, in a state of faction and discord, are detorted to criminal partiality. The remark is Johnson's moral intelligence working at its best on this play: not a sermon on the lovers but a psychology of the feud, which corrupts even its most peaceable witness into an advocate. It also certifies Benvolio's narration as a deliberate effect rather than a convenience, since Johnson reads the distortion as the author's design for exhibiting what faction does to testimony.

### Q: Did Johnson think the play taught a moral lesson?

His framework demanded one and his reading declined to find the play guilty of lacking it in the usual way. The Preface's first charge against Shakespeare is moral: he sacrifices virtue to convenience, makes no just distribution of good or evil, and seems to write without any moral purpose, though a writer's duty is to make the world better. Applied to this play, the charge surfaces only in the margins of Johnson's notes, in the Chorus condemned for adding no moral sentiment and in the orisons note on Juliet. His closing stricture, the considered verdict, contains no moral judgement on the lovers at all: no word on disobedience, the secret marriage, or the suicides. The play's moral instruction, such as Johnson found, lies in its exhibition of faction, haste, and partiality, absorbed from the action rather than announced by the author.

### Q: Where does Johnson's case against the conceit succeed, and where does it fail?

It succeeds where the figure substitutes for feeling, and fails where the figure carries it. His chosen exhibit succeeds: Romeo's Rosaline antitheses in 1.1 expend toil of antithesis on a passion the play itself treats as borrowed, and the charge of the unearned conceit stands. The general law fails at the play's most distressed wit. Mercutio dying makes his grave man pun (3.1), and by the letter of Johnson's doctrine the jest should check the audience's pity with sudden frigidity; in experience it sharpens pity, because the pun is the character's courage exercising itself to extinction, probable by Johnson's own test of nature. The same defence covers the feast sonnet, where the pilgrim conceit is the lovers' negotiation, and Romeo's desperate pilot figure in the tomb, which charts his despair rather than decorating it. Mahood's structural defence of the wordplay wins the law; Johnson keeps his case.
