In 641 an army of a few thousand men pitched camp beside a captured fortress at the point where the Nile valley opens into the Delta. They had just taken the strongest inland position in Egypt and had not yet obtained Alexandria, which was still under negotiation. The camp was a practical arrangement for a force that needed somewhere to sit while the campaign finished. Within a century it was one of the largest cities in the Islamic world, and within four centuries it had become the southern quarter of a capital that has never since ceased to be Egypt's capital. That trajectory, from tents to a metropolis, is the subject of this article, and it deserves a name: the garrison that became a capital thesis, that Egypt's great capital began as an army encampment and that every decision shaping it followed from that origin.

The most important thing to establish at the outset is that it was a choice. The conquerors had Alexandria available: a functioning capital with palaces, a harbour, an administration, water supply and everything a governing power might want. They did not use it. Understanding why explains not only the founding of one city but the reorientation of an entire country, since the decision to govern from inland turned Egypt away from the Mediterranean it had faced for a thousand years and toward the east, permanently.

A note on the evidence, because this subject has an unusual profile. The narrative sources for the founding are late and legendary in their details. The archaeology is extensive but was damaged by a century of destructive digging before proper excavation began. And a single extraordinary documentary find, an archive of discarded writings from one of the city's synagogues, illuminates the medieval city in a level of detail available for almost nowhere else in the medieval world. The result is a place we know very well in some centuries and hardly at all in others.

## Why Not Alexandria?

The decision to found a new settlement rather than occupy the existing capital is the founding fact of the city and the one most often skipped, so it deserves the fullest treatment.

The strategic argument comes first and is decisive. Alexandria is a maritime city, and its defensibility depends on control of the sea. The conquerors had no fleet, and the Byzantine navy remained uncontested for years afterward, as the seaborne expedition that retook the city in 645 demonstrated. Placing a governing centre in a port that an enemy fleet could reach at will, and that could be supplied from the sea by whoever held it, was an obvious risk. Placing it inland, on a river, beyond the reach of a naval power, removed that risk entirely.

The geographical argument comes second and is the more permanent. The point where the Nile valley meets the Delta is the strategic key to Egypt, because everything in the country moves on the river and this is where the river divides. A force there controls traffic in both directions, can move against the Delta or the valley, and sits at the junction of the routes running east toward Sinai and the Red Sea. The Romans had fortified the spot for exactly these reasons, and the conquerors adopted the same logic.

### Why did the conquerors reject the existing capital?

Because a maritime city cannot be held without a fleet, and they had none. Alexandria could be reached and resupplied by the Byzantine navy at will, as the recapture of 645 proved. An inland site on the river was beyond naval reach and controlled the country's internal communications far better.

The orientation argument comes third. Alexandria connected Egypt to the Mediterranean and to Constantinople. The new state's centre lay in Arabia, its communications ran east through Sinai and south down the Red Sea, and its interests pointed in that direction. A capital at the head of the Delta sat astride those routes, and a canal from there to the Red Sea, reopened almost immediately, connected the province's grain directly to the Arabian heartland.

There is a cultural argument sometimes offered and it should be treated cautiously. Accounts sometimes suggest a reluctance among the conquerors to settle in a large Greek city with an alien urban culture, or a desire to keep the army separate from the conquered population. The second consideration has real support in the general pattern of the conquests, where new garrison foundations were established in Iraq as well, and the practice looks deliberate. The first is more speculative.

The administrative argument is the least discussed and possibly the strongest. An army settled in an existing city becomes entangled with its population, its property markets, its factions and its disputes, and controlling soldiers billeted among civilians is notoriously difficult. A separate foundation kept the force concentrated, payable, disciplined and out of contact with the taxpaying population whose productivity the new government depended on. That separation is treated more fully in the article on the conqueror himself and it is one of the quieter reasons the province stayed productive.

Alexandria did not vanish. It remained a major city, a port and the seat of the patriarchate for centuries, and it retained importance long after losing the administrative function. What it lost was the role that had defined it since its foundation, and its long contraction from a metropolis to a provincial port follows directly from the decision described here.

## The Site

The choice of ground is worth examining in detail, because the specific spot rather than the general region determined how the city developed.

The fortress the conquerors had just taken stood on the east bank at the head of the Delta, a Roman foundation of the early imperial period built to control the river crossing and the junction of routes. Its walls were massive, its round towers substantial, and it enclosed a settlement of its own with churches and a resident population. Portions of it survive within the modern city, sunk below present ground level because the surrounding city has risen around them across fourteen centuries.

The camp was pitched immediately north of the fortress, on open ground between the walls and the desert edge to the east, with the river to the west. That position gave it several practical advantages. It was defensible in the sense of having the fortress at its back and the desert on one flank. It had water access to the river. It had room to expand eastward onto ground nobody was farming, which matters enormously for a settlement that grew as fast as this one did. And it did not displace any existing agricultural land, which would have created immediate friction with the population whose productivity the new administration needed.

The topography was more varied than a modern visitor might expect, with low hills and depressions along the desert margin, and the settlement grew across this uneven ground rather than on a flat plain. That is part of why it never acquired a regular plan.

The relationship to the river changed over time and had consequences. The Nile's course shifted westward across the medieval centuries, which progressively increased the distance between the city and the water. A settlement that began beside the river ended up separated from it by a growing strip of newly deposited land, and the practical difficulties of water supply and river access increased accordingly. This is a slow process invisible in any single generation and significant across several.

The fortress itself remained standing and occupied, with its churches functioning and its Christian population in place. The new city grew around and beside an older settlement rather than replacing it, which is why the quarter contains, within a few hundred metres, a Roman fortress, medieval churches, a synagogue and one of the earliest mosques in Africa.

## The Name

The city's name has two competing explanations and the disagreement is old, unresolved, and more interesting than either answer.

The first derivation takes the name from an Arabic word meaning a tent or a large pavilion, and this reading is supported by the foundation story that the tradition attaches to it. In that story, the commander was preparing to move on after the fortress fell when a dove was found nesting on his tent. He declined to disturb it, ordered the tent left standing, and the camp that grew around it took its name from the tent that had been spared.

The story is charming and it is a foundation legend of the most familiar type: it explains a name with an incident, it displays the founder's character, and it converts an administrative decision into a memorable scene. Foundation legends do exactly this everywhere, and the presence of one is not evidence about a name's actual origin.

The second derivation takes the name from a Latin word for a ditch or entrenchment, passing through Greek into Arabic. A Roman military term for a fortified camp arriving in Egypt with Roman military architecture, and then being applied to the camp that grew beside a Roman fortress, is entirely plausible on linguistic and historical grounds.

### Which explanation of the name is correct?

Neither is settled. The Arabic word for a tent fits the foundation story and the settlement's origin as a camp; a Latin term for an entrenchment, borrowed through Greek, fits the site beside a Roman fortress and the general transmission of Roman military vocabulary. Specialists differ and the evidence does not decide it.

The city was also known by a form of the general Arabic name for Egypt itself, and the two names were used together and separately in different periods, which is a source of confusion in the sources. The same word served for the country and for its capital, which is not unusual and which means that a reference in a medieval text can be ambiguous.

The naming question has a wider significance worth noting. A settlement that acquires the name of the country it governs has become identified with it, and the transfer of that name from this foundation to its successors, and eventually to the modern capital in colloquial usage, is a small piece of evidence for the continuity of the site's function across many changes of dynasty and formal name.

## The Layout: A City Built by Allotment

Fustat was not planned in the way a Roman colony was planned, with a surveyed grid laid out before occupation, and understanding how it was actually organized explains almost everything about its subsequent shape.

The organizing principle was the allotment. Ground was assigned to the tribal groups making up the army, each receiving an area within which it settled according to its own arrangements. The number of these allotments is given in the sources as somewhere over twenty, and their approximate positions can be partly reconstructed from later references.

Within each allotment, development was internal to the group. Families built where they chose, subdivided as they grew, and left space for the lanes and open areas the community needed. Nobody imposed a street plan, and the result was an organic pattern of irregular lanes, dead ends, courtyards and blocks that grew by accretion rather than by design.

At the centre sat the congregational mosque and, around it, the quarter assigned to a mixed group not attached to any single tribe, drawn from various origins and organized under the army's banner rather than by descent. That central mixed area is significant: it means the city had, from the beginning, a zone where the tribal organization did not apply and where people of different origins lived together.

### Was Fustat a planned city?

Only at the largest scale. Ground was allotted to tribal groups in blocks, and within each block development was organic, with families building and subdividing as they chose. There was no surveyed street grid. The result was an irregular city of lanes and courtyards that grew by accretion, unlike a Roman planned foundation.

The consequences of the allotment system ran for centuries. The tribal identity of quarters persisted long after the tribal organization of the army had dissolved, appearing in place names and in references to districts. Property rights established at the founding, and their subdivision by inheritance across generations, produced the extraordinarily complex pattern of ownership visible in later documents, where a single building might be held in fractional shares by many parties.

The system also explains the density. A fixed allotment with a growing population can only be developed upward and inward, and this is precisely what happened, producing the multi storey housing that struck later visitors so forcibly.

Public buildings and infrastructure developed later and were fitted into an existing fabric rather than being provided for in advance. Markets, baths, additional mosques, water arrangements and administrative buildings all appear in spaces the city made for them, which is why the medieval city had no monumental centre in the way a planned foundation does.

## How It Grew

The transformation from camp to city happened faster than the founders can have anticipated, and the drivers are identifiable.

Administration was the first. The camp became the seat of the province's government almost immediately, which meant officials, records, courts, treasuries and everyone who deals with them. A governing centre generates a service population several times its own size.

The army's own settlement was the second. Soldiers drawing stipends from the province's revenue are consumers with reliable income, and a concentration of them creates markets, workshops, transport and everything else that supplies consumption. The conversion of a field force into a garrison population with money to spend is an economic event.

Migration was the third and it ran in two directions. Arabs arriving from the peninsula in subsequent waves settled in the new city, since that was where the community and the stipends were. Egyptians came in from the countryside and from provincial towns for the same reasons people always move to a capital: work, opportunity and proximity to power.

Trade was the fourth and eventually the largest. The city's position, at the junction of the river, the Delta, the routes to the Red Sea and the roads east, made it a natural entrepot, and the reopening of the canal connecting the Nile to the Red Sea plugged it directly into the Indian Ocean trading world. Goods from the Mediterranean, from Africa, from Arabia and from as far as China moved through it.

Industry was the fifth and it fed on the rest. A large urban population needs pottery, glass, textiles, metal goods and food processing, and a trading city can export what it makes. The pottery industry in particular reached a scale visible in the archaeology as enormous deposits of production waste.

The rate of growth can be inferred from the physical evidence. The multi storey housing that later visitors described with astonishment is the signature of a city whose population outran its ground area, and its appearance indicates that the allotments were fully built up and being developed vertically within a comparatively short time.

The city's success also created its successors, which is the paradox of the following section. Each new ruling group founded a new administrative quarter adjacent to the existing city rather than inside it, because the existing city was full.

## The Chain of Foundations

The relationship between Fustat and Cairo is widely misunderstood, and the actual sequence is a chain of four foundations rather than a replacement of one city by another.

The first was the original garrison settlement, established beside the fortress in the 640s, which grew into the commercial and residential centre of the country.

The second came with the change of dynasty in the middle of the eighth century, when the incoming regime established a new administrative quarter immediately to the northeast. Its purpose was to house the new governing group and its troops separately from a city associated with the previous order, and it functioned as an official annexe rather than as an independent city.

The third came in the later ninth century, when a governor who established effective independence founded a new quarter further north, laid out on a more ambitious scale with palaces, gardens, a hippodrome and a great congregational mosque that still stands. That foundation belongs to the article on [the Tulunids](/2010/07/01/tulunids-first-muslim-dynasty/), and its relevance here is the pattern: a new ruling group again built beside rather than within.

The fourth came in 969, when a new dynasty arriving from the west founded a walled palace city further north again, intended as a royal enclosure rather than as a general settlement, which eventually gave its name to the modern capital. That foundation belongs to the article on [how the Fatimids founded Cairo](/2010/12/01/how-fatimids-founded-cairo/).

The crucial point is that through all of this the original city remained the population and commercial centre. The successive foundations were administrative and courtly, housing rulers, troops and officials, while the ordinary business of the country continued in the older settlement. Travellers of the tenth and eleventh centuries describe a great commercial city in the south and a closed royal enclosure to the north.

### Did Cairo replace Fustat?

Not at first. Cairo was founded in 969 as a walled palace enclosure for a new dynasty, its court and its troops, while the older city remained the commercial and residential centre for another two centuries. The relationship reversed only after the twelfth century, when the older city declined and the palace city opened to general settlement.

The reversal came in stages. The damage of the twelfth century, discussed below, weakened the southern city. The dynasty that replaced the Fatimids opened the northern enclosure to general settlement and fortified the whole area with a single wall and a citadel, which shifted the centre of gravity decisively north. And the progressive movement of the Nile westward left the southern site increasingly awkward for river access while the northern area developed.

By the later medieval centuries the older city was a diminished district of the greater capital, its buildings decaying, parts of it used for rubbish disposal and industry. The name survived, the mosque continued in use, and the archaeology accumulated undisturbed beneath, which is why so much can now be recovered.

## The Burning of 1168

The event most often given as the end of the city requires careful handling, because the traditional account and the archaeological picture do not fully agree.

The narrative account is dramatic. With a Crusader army approaching in 1168 and the Fatimid state in crisis, the vizier ordered the older city evacuated and set alight, so that the invaders could not use it as a base against the walled capital to the north. The fire is described as burning for an extended period and destroying the city.

The archaeological picture is more complicated. Burning layers exist and are real, and the extent of destruction across the whole site is harder to demonstrate than the narrative implies. Excavation has also shown that parts of the city were already in decline before the twelfth century, with abandonment and rubbish accumulation in some quarters, which suggests the fire accelerated a process rather than initiating it.

The scholarly position is therefore that the event happened, that it was serious, and that presenting it as the sudden death of a great city overstates both its extent and its role. Cities of this size rarely die from a single event, and this one had structural problems already: the river had moved away, the political and commercial centre was shifting north, and the successor foundations were absorbing functions.

The deliberate destruction of one's own city to deny it to an enemy is a recognized military practice and the decision is comprehensible in its context, whatever its consequences. It was also a decision taken by a regime in its final crisis, which fell within a few years.

The aftermath is well documented in the archaeology. The site was not resettled at its former density. Parts became industrial zones, parts became dumping grounds, and the accumulation of refuse over the following centuries created the mounds that dominated the area until modern times. Those mounds preserved what lay beneath them and were themselves systematically destroyed in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries when they were dug for fertilizer, which is the single greatest loss to the archaeology of the site.

## What Excavation Has Revealed

The archaeology of this site is among the most important for the medieval Islamic world and among the most damaged, and both facts need stating together.

The damage came first. The site was covered by enormous mounds of accumulated refuse and collapsed building material, and from the nineteenth century these were dug commercially for the nitrogen rich soil used as agricultural fertilizer. That operation removed and destroyed an unquantifiable proportion of the site's stratigraphy, scattering objects onto the antiquities market without record. A great many museum pieces described as coming from this city arrived by that route and have no findspot.

Systematic excavation began in the early twentieth century and revealed something unexpected: not a ruin field of monumental buildings but a dense residential city with well built houses, and the houses became the most valuable thing the site produced.

The typical house revealed by excavation is organized around a central courtyard, entered through a bent passage that prevents a view into the interior from the street, with rooms opening onto the courtyard and often a raised main reception room on one side. Construction is of brick, floors are frequently paved, and drainage arrangements are elaborate.

### What did houses in Fustat look like?

Courtyard houses of several storeys, entered by a bent passage that blocked the view inside, with rooms opening onto a central court and a principal reception room raised on one side. Excavation found well built brick construction, paved floors, piped water, latrines and cesspits, indicating a considerably higher standard of urban living than expected.

The water and sanitation arrangements are the most striking finding. Excavated houses show piped water supply, basins, drains, latrines and cesspit arrangements, indicating an urban infrastructure well beyond what medieval European cities of the period offered. This discovery substantially revised assumptions about domestic conditions in a medieval Islamic city.

Multi storey construction is attested both by the archaeology and by travellers' descriptions, and the descriptions give heights that strain belief, running to many storeys with families occupying separate levels. Whether the highest figures are literal is doubtful and the general picture of vertical development is confirmed by the excavated foundations, which are substantial enough to carry several floors.

Industrial areas emerged clearly, with pottery kilns, glass working debris and metalworking waste concentrated in identifiable zones, generally at the margins where the smoke and smell were least troublesome. The scale of the ceramic industry in particular is visible in the volume of production waste.

The imported material is the finding with the widest implications. Chinese ceramics of several periods have been recovered in quantity, alongside local imitations of them, which establishes directly that this city was plugged into the Indian Ocean trade routes carrying goods from East Asia. A pot from China in a house in Egypt is a piece of evidence about world trade that no text supplies as concretely.

Excavation continues at various parts of the site, and successive campaigns by different teams have worked different areas, producing a picture assembled from patches rather than a single coherent plan of the whole city.

## The Synagogue Archive

The single most remarkable body of evidence about this city came out of a storeroom in one of its synagogues and it transformed the study of medieval Mediterranean society.

Jewish practice held that writings containing the name of God should not be destroyed, and the community's response was to deposit worn out and discarded documents in a storeroom rather than dispose of them. Over centuries, one such storeroom in this city accumulated an enormous mass of material, and because the climate is dry, it survived.

The contents were not what anyone expected. Alongside religious texts, which were what the practice was designed to protect, the deposit contained the ordinary paperwork of a community: business letters, contracts, marriage documents, court records, accounts, personal correspondence, shopping lists, school exercises and legal opinions. Hundreds of thousands of fragments, spanning roughly the tenth to the thirteenth centuries and beyond.

The material was removed to European and American collections in the late nineteenth century in circumstances typical of the period, and it is now dispersed across many libraries, which has made the work of reassembling and publishing it a project spanning more than a century and still incomplete.

What it shows is a Mediterranean world in operation. Merchants writing from one port to another about cargoes, prices, ships, partners and debts; family letters across long distances; the practical business of marriage settlements and inheritance disputes; the operation of courts and the community's internal governance. The trade network visible in it runs from Spain and North Africa through Egypt to India, with this city as a central node.

Its value extends far beyond Jewish history. It documents the commercial practices, the transport arrangements, the credit instruments, the prices and the social texture of a medieval Mediterranean economy in a way no other source approaches, and historians of the wider region depend on it. A community's reluctance to throw away paper produced the best economic archive of the medieval Islamic world.

## Who Lived There

The population of the city changed character across its history, and the sequence explains a great deal about how Arab Egypt actually formed.

At the founding it was an army: several thousand men organized by tribal group, with their dependents, settled in allotted quarters, drawing stipends from the province's revenue. This was an entirely new population element in Egypt, Arabic speaking, Muslim, and concentrated in one place.

Within a generation it was an army plus a service population. Administration required officials, and the officials at working level were the existing Egyptian bureaucrats operating in Greek and in Egyptian. Supplying a garrison required merchants, carters, builders, bakers and craftsmen, and those came from the surrounding population.

Within a century it was a mixed city. Migration from the countryside brought Egyptians in, conversion began to blur the distinction between the two original populations, intermarriage proceeded, and the tribal organization of the original settlement dissolved into a general urban society even as the quarter names preserved its memory.

Christians were present from the beginning, since the adjacent fortress settlement was Christian and had its churches, and the Christian population of the city itself grew as Egyptians moved in. The churches of the fortress quarter, several of which still stand, served that community continuously.

Jews were present and their community is the best documented of all because of the synagogue archive. It was a substantial, prosperous, internally governed community deeply involved in the Mediterranean trade, with its own courts, schools and charitable institutions, and its documents show constant interaction with Muslim and Christian neighbours in business and in law.

### Did Christians and Jews live in Fustat?

Yes, in substantial numbers and continuously. The adjacent fortress settlement was Christian with churches that still stand, and the Christian population grew as Egyptians migrated in. The Jewish community is the best documented in the medieval Islamic world thanks to the archive its synagogue accumulated, and it was prosperous and commercially central.

The occupational range visible in the documents is wide: merchants at every scale from long distance traders to shopkeepers, craftsmen in every trade, physicians, scribes, teachers, officials, transport workers, labourers and the poor supported by charity. This was a full urban society rather than a garrison with attachments.

Population figures are not available and should not be quoted. Estimates in modern literature vary by large multiples, medieval sources give numbers that are rhetorical rather than statistical, and the standard methods for estimating urban populations from area and density depend on assumptions that the multi storey construction here makes especially uncertain. What can be said is that contemporaries regarded it as one of the largest cities they knew.

## What the City Made and Traded

The economy is the aspect of the city best illuminated by combining the archaeology with the documents, and the picture is of a manufacturing and trading centre of the first rank.

Pottery was the largest industry by physical evidence. Kilns, wasters and production debris occur in enormous quantities, and the range extends from ordinary domestic wares to sophisticated glazed and lustre decorated pieces. The industry served the city, the country and an export market.

Glass production is attested by working debris and finished pieces, and Egyptian glass had a long established reputation that the industry inherited from earlier periods.

Textiles continued the industry that had operated in Egypt for millennia, adapted to new patronage and new requirements including inscribed bands produced under official arrangement, and the trade in cloth is one of the most prominent subjects in the commercial documents.

Metalwork, woodwork, leather, paper and sugar refining all appear, the last becoming a major Egyptian industry in the medieval centuries.

The trade is where the documents contribute most. The commercial letters show a network running west to North Africa, Sicily and Spain, north across the Mediterranean, south up the Nile toward Nubia, east through the Red Sea to Arabia, and onward to India. Goods, credit, partnerships and information moved along it, and the city was a central clearing point.

### What industries operated in Fustat?

Pottery above all, attested by kilns and vast quantities of production waste, alongside glass, textiles, metalwork, leather, paper and sugar refining. The city manufactured for itself, for Egypt and for export, and combined that production with a role as a central node in trade running from Spain to India.

The Indian Ocean connection deserves emphasis because it is easy to underestimate. Chinese ceramics recovered from the site in quantity, and local imitations of them made in the city's own kilns, demonstrate both the reach of the trade and its influence on local production. A potter working in Egypt copying a Chinese glaze is evidence of a connected world.

The commercial instruments visible in the documents are sophisticated: partnerships of several forms, credit arrangements, agency relationships, bills and transfers, and a body of commercial law administered by courts of several communities that merchants used according to convenience. This was a mature commercial economy operating across religious and political boundaries.

## Water, Waste and the Practical City

The unglamorous infrastructure of the city is where the archaeology has been most surprising, and it deserves its own treatment.

Water came from the river, and getting it from there to a large city on rising ground was the standing engineering problem. Carriers with animals moved water in bulk, a trade employing large numbers, and the excavated houses show cisterns and storage arrangements for holding it. Piped distribution within buildings is attested, and the later medieval period saw more ambitious lifting and conveyance works serving the wider capital.

The river's westward migration made this progressively harder. A city founded beside the water found itself further from it in each century, lengthening the haul and increasing the cost, and this is one of the slow structural pressures behind the site's eventual decline.

Waste disposal is the aspect the site is most famous for, because the accumulation of it created the mounds that covered the ruins. Domestic refuse, industrial waste, ash and building debris built up over centuries in and around the settlement, and the resulting deposits reached considerable depths. From the perspective of an archaeologist this is a gift, since sealed refuse deposits are among the most informative material available. From the perspective of the inhabitants it was a growing problem.

Sanitation within houses was better than expected. Latrines connected to cesspits appear regularly in the excavated buildings, along with drains carrying waste water away, and the arrangements are systematic rather than improvised. This is one of the findings that changed scholarly assumptions about domestic conditions in a medieval Islamic city.

Streets were narrow, irregular and often covered, which is the standard pattern in a hot climate and the natural outcome of organic development within fixed allotments. Wheeled traffic was limited and goods moved by animal, which is why the lanes did not need to be wide.

Fire was the standing hazard of a dense city built with substantial timber elements, and the city experienced it repeatedly across its history, of which the twelfth century episode is only the most famous.

Building materials were brick, timber, plaster and reused stone, with stone from earlier structures appearing in later buildings as it does everywhere in Egypt. The construction techniques are those of Egyptian urban building generally, adapted to multi storey work.

## Studying the Founding of Fustat

For a student, this topic organizes around a small number of things worth carrying.

Be able to give the founding date as around 641 to 642 and to place it in the campaign sequence, after the fall of the inland fortress and before the handover of Alexandria. Be able to explain why the conquerors did not use the existing capital, with the naval argument first and the geographical argument second. Be able to describe the allotment system and explain why it produced an irregular city. Be able to state both derivations of the name and say that the question is unresolved. Be able to sequence the four foundations and explain that the successors were administrative annexes while the original remained the population centre. And be able to describe what excavation revealed about housing, water and sanitation, since that is the finding that most changed the field.

The transferable habit here concerns the difference between a founding and a growth. The founding of this city was a small administrative decision about where to camp an army, taken in the middle of a campaign, with no evidence that anyone intended a capital. What made it a capital was everything that followed: the administration settling there, the army becoming a consumer population, migration, trade and industry. Cities are rarely founded in the sense that the word implies; they are sited, and then they grow or they do not.

A second habit concerns negative evidence. The most important fact about this city's early centuries is that the conquerors did not use Alexandria, and absences of that kind are easy to overlook because nothing happened. Asking what a historical actor did not do, and why, frequently produces more explanation than cataloguing what they did.

## What the Founding Did Not Settle

A final analytical section, because several questions the founding is often credited with deciding were in fact left open for centuries.

It did not settle whether Egypt would become Muslim. The city was a garrison of a few thousand in a country of millions, the settlement offered protected status to the existing population in exchange for payment, and the fiscal logic of that arrangement pointed away from rapid conversion. The religious transformation took centuries and belongs to its own articles.

It did not settle whether Egypt would speak Arabic. The administration operated in Greek and in Egyptian at working level for two generations, and the switch of the central accounts came roughly sixty years after the founding, with the language of daily life following over several centuries more.

It did not settle the relationship between Egypt and the caliphal centre, which remained contested throughout the following centuries, produced repeated friction over revenue and appointments, and eventually produced effectively independent rule exercised from Egypt itself by governors who had ceased to behave like governors.

It did not settle the fate of Alexandria, which remained a major city, a working port and a patriarchal seat for a long time afterward, and which declined gradually across several centuries rather than abruptly at any single moment.

It did not settle the city's own form, which developed organically across centuries with successive rulers adding quarters beside it rather than reshaping it.

What it did settle, and settled permanently, was where Egypt would be governed from. That single question, decided by where an army pitched its tents in 641, has never been reopened, and every regime since has governed the country from within a few kilometres of the same spot.

The distinction between what a founding decides and what it merely begins is worth carrying beyond this case. Founding moments are credited with everything that follows them, and they usually decide one or two things while leaving the rest to processes that take generations. Identifying which is which is most of the work of explaining any origin.

## The City's Food and Its Hinterland

A city of this size in a pre modern economy is a machine for consuming the countryside around it, and the arrangements that fed it are worth setting out.

Grain came from the Egyptian countryside, which produced it in quantities that had fed foreign capitals for a thousand years, and supplying a domestic capital was by comparison an easy problem. The river carried it, the existing collection and storage apparatus handled it, and the administration that assessed the tax also managed the movement. Granaries, milling and baking then formed an industry of their own within the city, employing large numbers and subject to official attention whenever prices rose, since bread supply in any pre modern capital is a political question as much as an economic one.

Vegetables, fruit and dairy came from the immediate hinterland, since perishables cannot travel far without refrigeration, and the gardens and cultivated land in the surrounding districts served that market, with the produce moving in daily by donkey and by boat to markets that opened at dawn. The presence of a large urban population reorganizes agriculture in its vicinity toward perishable high value production, and that reorganization is one of the least visible and most reliable effects of urban growth anywhere.

Meat arrived on the hoof from further afield, since animals transport themselves, and the butchery and tanning industries that followed are attested in the archaeology by their waste. Tanning in particular is a noxious trade requiring water and generating smell, and its location within an urban area is always a compromise between the convenience of the tanners and the tolerance of everyone else, which is why such industries cluster at the margins in every pre modern city and why finding them helps map a settlement's edges at a given period.

Fish came from the river and the Delta lakes and was a significant part of the diet, as it had been in Egypt for millennia, supplied by a fishing industry with its own boats, its own markets and its own place in the tax records.

The fuel problem deserves mention because it is easy to overlook and was severe. A city needs fuel for cooking, for baking, for heating water, for glass and pottery kilns and for lime burning, and Egypt has almost no timber. The solution was a combination of imported wood, agricultural residues, dung and whatever else burned, and the constraint shaped which industries could operate at what scale. A large ceramic industry in a country without forests is an achievement of fuel logistics as much as an achievement of craft.

Water has been discussed and was the hardest of the supply problems because of its weight and the river's retreat. A large city consuming water by the thousands of tonnes daily, drawn from a river that was moving steadily further away, generated a transport industry of carriers and animals whose scale is easy to underestimate and whose cost was a standing charge on every household in the settlement.

The general point is that the city's growth transformed its region, and that the transformation ran in both directions, since the region also shaped what the city could become. Land use changed, transport networks reoriented, and a substantial share of the surrounding population's labour went to supplying an urban market. That transformation is invisible in the narrative sources and recoverable from documents and archaeology, and it is a reminder that a city is a regional phenomenon rather than a place.

## The Property System and Its Consequences

The allotment of ground at the founding created a property regime whose effects can be traced for six hundred years, and following it is one of the better ways to understand how the city actually worked.

The original grant was to a group rather than to an individual, and the internal distribution within each allotment was a matter for the group. That produced, from the beginning, a layer of collective claims underneath individual holdings, and disputes over the boundary between the two appear in the sources.

Inheritance then did its work. Islamic inheritance rules divide an estate among multiple heirs in fixed shares, which means that a house held by one man becomes, in the next generation, a house held in fractions by several people, and in the generation after that by many. Over centuries this produces holdings of extraordinary complexity, and the documents record exactly that: parties owning specified fractions of buildings, with the fractions expressed in small denominators.

The practical response was a market in shares. Fractions were bought, sold, exchanged and consolidated, and a substantial part of the surviving property paperwork consists of these transactions. A person assembling a whole building had to acquire shares from many holders, which is a slow and expensive process and one that discouraged redevelopment.

The physical consequence is visible in the archaeology and in the city's form. Where property is fragmented and consolidation is difficult, buildings are repaired and subdivided rather than replaced, plots stay the shape they were, and the street pattern established at the founding persists for centuries because nobody can assemble enough ground to change it. This is a general phenomenon in cities with partible inheritance and it is well illustrated here.

The rental market followed from the same conditions. A mobile urban population and a fragmented ownership structure produce widespread tenancy, and the leases in the documents show the terms, the durations and the obligations in detail.

There is a further consequence for the historian. A property regime this complex generates enormous quantities of paperwork, because every transaction involving a fraction of a building requires documentation and every inheritance requires a division. The richness of the surviving record is partly a function of how complicated the ownership had become, which is a pleasing instance of an administrative difficulty producing an evidentiary benefit.

The system also explains the density and the vertical development. When the ground area is fixed by allotments established once and consolidation is impractical, a growing population builds upward, and the multi storey housing that astonished visitors is the architectural expression of a legal arrangement made in the 640s.

## The Fortress and the City Beside It

The relationship between the new foundation and the older settlement it grew beside is one of the more revealing aspects of the site and it is usually passed over.

The Roman fortress was not an empty ruin when the conquerors arrived. It enclosed a functioning settlement with a Christian population, churches, and the ordinary apparatus of a garrison town that had existed for centuries. Its surrender in 641 transferred it intact, and its inhabitants were not displaced.

What happened next is the interesting part. The new settlement grew beside the fortress rather than inside it, which left the older community in place with its buildings and its life continuing. Two populations, two religions and two languages occupied adjacent ground under a single authority, and the arrangement lasted.

The physical consequence is the quarter's distinctive character. Churches of considerable age stand within and around the fortress walls, functioning continuously. A synagogue serving a community documented in extraordinary detail stands nearby. The congregational mosque of the new city stands a short distance away. All of these are within a few hundred metres and all of them are still in use.

The social consequence was interaction. The commercial documents show members of all three communities doing business together, using one another's courts when convenient, lending, partnering and litigating across religious lines as a matter of routine. This is not a picture of separate quarters with high walls between them but of an ordinary mixed urban society with distinct communal institutions.

The administrative consequence was that the older settlement's population became part of the new city's population, and the boundary between the two dissolved over generations even as the communal institutions persisted. The fortress quarter is a district of the city rather than an enclave within it.

There is an instructive contrast with what did not happen. The conquerors did not clear the fortress, did not convert its churches, did not expel its inhabitants and did not build over it. Given how frequently conquests elsewhere have involved exactly those things, the restraint deserves noting, and it fits the general pattern of the Egyptian settlement described throughout this cluster: change as little as possible, keep the productive population in place, and put the new element beside rather than on top of the old.

## The Ceramics and What They Prove

The pottery recovered from this site is worth a section of its own, because ceramics carry more historical information per fragment than almost any other class of object.

Pottery survives when nothing else does. It breaks and does not decay, it was produced and discarded in enormous quantities, and its styles change quickly enough to date deposits. A site with abundant ceramics is a datable site, and this one has ceramics in extraordinary abundance.

The local production is the first story. Kilns and production waste establish a large industry manufacturing everything from unglazed domestic wares to elaborately decorated glazed pieces, and the technical range visible in the material indicates skilled specialization and access to the materials and fuel a ceramic industry requires.

The imported material is the second and more consequential. Chinese ceramics of several periods have been recovered in quantity, which is direct physical evidence of goods travelling from East Asia to Egypt, and their presence in ordinary domestic contexts rather than only in elite ones indicates a trade of real volume rather than a trickle of curiosities.

The imitations are the third and the most interesting. Local potters produced wares copying the appearance of the imported Chinese pieces, attempting with different materials and techniques to achieve similar effects. That is a documented instance of technological and aesthetic transmission across the whole width of Asia, and it is visible in the archaeology without any text mentioning it.

The distribution of ceramic types across the site helps map its development, since different quarters yield different assemblages and different date ranges, and the resulting picture is one of the ways the settlement's growth and contraction can be traced.

The market problem qualifies all of it. Enormous quantities of ceramics from this site reached collections through the commercial digging of the refuse mounds, without findspots, and material described as coming from here is therefore of two very different evidentiary classes: excavated pieces with context, and market pieces without. The second class is far larger and far less useful, and confusing the two has produced errors in the literature.

What the ceramics establish overall is a city that manufactured at scale, consumed imported goods from the far side of the world, and adapted foreign techniques to local production. That is a description of a connected, sophisticated economy, and it is derived entirely from broken pots.

## Why This City Mattered Beyond Egypt

A closing perspective, because the significance of this foundation runs past its own country.

It was one of the largest cities of the medieval Islamic world in a period when that world contained the largest cities on earth, and its scale alone gave it weight in the economy and the culture of the whole region.

It was a central node in a trading system connecting the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean, and goods, credit and information moving between those two worlds passed through it. The commercial documents from its Jewish community are the principal source for how that system actually operated, which means the medieval Mediterranean economy is understood substantially through the paperwork of one city's merchants.

It was a manufacturing centre whose ceramics and textiles travelled widely, and whose imitations of imported wares document technological transmission across Asia.

It was the seat from which Egypt was governed during the centuries when Egypt was among the wealthiest provinces of successive empires, so decisions taken there had consequences across the eastern Mediterranean.

And it was the origin of the modern capital, which has been among the largest cities of its region continuously since, so the decision about where to camp an army in 641 has a direct line to a metropolis of the present day.

The scholarly significance is a further matter. The combination of a dry climate, a buried and sealed site, and an accidentally preserved documentary archive makes this one of the best evidenced medieval cities anywhere, and models of medieval urban life, commerce and domestic conditions built on its evidence are applied to places that preserved far less.

That evidentiary richness is worth appreciating as an accident. The city was buried under rubbish because it declined, its documents survived because a community would not throw away paper, and its material culture survives because Egypt is dry. None of this was preservation in any deliberate sense, and all of it is why the settlement founded by an army in a hurry is now among the best understood cities of its age.

## The Quarter Names and What They Preserve

A small subject with disproportionate value: the names of the city's districts are a historical source in their own right.

Quarter names derived from the tribal groups allotted ground at the founding persisted in use for centuries, appearing in property documents, in descriptions of the city and in references to where people lived. That persistence is remarkable given that the tribal organization of the army dissolved within a few generations as the settlement became a mixed urban society.

What names of this kind preserve is a founding arrangement long after the arrangement itself has gone. A district called after a group that no longer exists as a group is a fossil, and reading a city's quarter names is one way of recovering an earlier layout that nothing else records.

Other names derived from functions, from markets, from prominent buildings and from families, and the layering of these different naming conventions across a map allows something of the city's development sequence to be reconstructed. A quarter named for a tribe is probably original; one named for a market or a building is probably later.

The practical use of this evidence is in locating the allotments, which the narrative sources describe without giving positions that can be plotted. Combining name references with topography, with the positions of known buildings and with excavated street patterns allows partial reconstruction, and the resulting maps are approximate and contested.

There is a wider point about urban continuity here. Names outlive the things they name more reliably than any other feature of a city, because they are transmitted by use rather than maintained by anyone. A street name can preserve the memory of a gate demolished six centuries earlier. Applied across a whole city, this makes toponymy one of the standard tools for reconstructing urban history where documents fail.

The Egyptian case has a further layer, since the general name for the country attached itself to this city and then transferred to its successors, so that the modern capital carries in colloquial use a name that was originally the country's. That transfer is a small piece of evidence for the continuity of the site's function across every change of dynasty and formal name.

## The Long Decline

The city's contraction across the later medieval centuries is a slower and more interesting story than the fire that gets the attention.

The first pressure was hydrological. The Nile's westward migration progressively separated the settlement from the river, lengthening the water haul, complicating river access for goods, and leaving newly deposited land between the city and the water that developed differently from the older fabric. This is a slow process, invisible in any decade and decisive across four centuries.

The second was political. The centre of government moved north in stages with each new foundation, and while the older city kept its commercial primacy for a long time, the accumulation of court, administration, troops and the patronage that follows them in the northern quarters shifted the balance eventually.

The third was the crisis of the twelfth century, including the recorded evacuation and firing, which damaged the fabric and dispersed the population at a moment when the political situation offered no capacity for reconstruction.

The fourth was the reorganization that followed under the succeeding dynasty, which opened the northern palace enclosure to general settlement, enclosed the wider area within new fortifications and built a citadel on the heights to the east. That programme created a single greater capital with its centre well to the north, and the southern district was left outside the new centre of gravity.

The fifth was economic reallocation. Industry, particularly the dirty industries of pottery, glass and tanning, concentrated in the southern district as residential quality declined, which accelerated the decline further in the ordinary way that industrial land use does.

The sixth was refuse. As the district became peripheral it became convenient for the disposal of the greater city's waste, and the mounds that eventually covered the ruins accumulated over centuries.

The result by the later medieval period was a diminished, partly ruined, partly industrial district on the southern fringe of a great capital, with its mosque still functioning and its churches still serving a Christian population, and with the archaeology of an earlier metropolis sealed beneath the rubbish.

That sealing is why the site matters so much to archaeology. A city that is continuously rebuilt destroys its own past; a city that is abandoned and buried preserves it. The decline that ended the settlement is the reason its earlier centuries can be recovered.

## The Documents of Daily Life

Beyond the synagogue archive, the city has left a body of ordinary paperwork that illuminates its society, and the categories are worth setting out because each answers different questions.

Property documents are the most abundant and the most revealing about how the allotment system evolved. Sales, leases, gifts and inheritance divisions show buildings and parts of buildings changing hands, and the fractional shares that appear routinely, where a person owns some portion of a house, are the arithmetic of generations of inheritance operating on ground allotted once at the founding.

Rental agreements show a substantial market in urban accommodation, which is what one expects in a dense city with a mobile population, and they include the terms, the durations and the obligations of both parties.

Commercial documents record partnerships, credit, agency and the movement of goods, and they show a commercial law in operation across communities, with parties using the courts of one community or another according to convenience and expectation.

Legal and administrative papers record disputes, court proceedings, official appointments and the ordinary business of a governed population, including taxation, which is the state's most persistent form of paperwork everywhere.

Personal correspondence is the least abundant and the most vivid, recording family matters, travel, illness, business worries and the ordinary anxieties of people whose names would otherwise not survive.

School exercises and scribal practice pieces appear, which tell you about literacy and education, and they are the sort of thing that survives only where discarded paper is preserved.

The combined picture is of a densely documented urban society in which written instruments governed property, commerce, marriage and dispute, and in which literacy was widespread enough for that to function. That level of documentation is characteristic of the medieval Islamic Mediterranean generally and it survives here to an exceptional degree because of the climate and the accident of the archive.

The caution attached to all of it is the standard one. Documents survive because they were deposited, discarded or buried in particular circumstances, and the surviving sample reflects those circumstances rather than the society's actual distribution of paperwork. A community that preserved its writings is over represented relative to one that did not.

## From Camp to City: The First Fifty Years

The transition that this article is about happened within roughly two generations, and reconstructing its stages shows how a temporary arrangement becomes permanent.

The first stage was the camp itself, a field arrangement of tents and temporary shelters for an army that had not finished its campaign. Alexandria was still under negotiation, a Byzantine counter-attack was possible and eventually came, and nothing about the situation in 641 suggested permanence.

The second stage was the decision to keep the army there rather than dispersing it or moving it to the coast. That decision was probably not taken formally at any single moment; it was the accumulated result of the campaign ending, the administration needing a seat, and the troops being settled with allotments and stipends. The allotment of ground is the moment at which a camp becomes a settlement, because assigning permanent property rights to a piece of land is an act with no temporary version.

The third stage was building in permanent materials. Tents became huts, huts became brick houses, and the archaeology shows substantial construction within a comparatively short time. Once a population invests in permanent building, relocation becomes expensive and unlikely.

The fourth stage was the arrival of the service population. Merchants, craftsmen, carters and labourers came because there was a market, and their arrival converted a military settlement into a town with an economy of its own that did not depend on the army.

The fifth was the administrative relocation. Moving the province's records, treasury and offices from the old capital to the new settlement made the arrangement institutional, and the officials who came with the apparatus were a further population.

The sixth was the second generation. Children born in the city, growing up there, inheriting shares in property allotted to their fathers, and marrying into local families, made the settlement a home rather than a posting. Nothing makes a place permanent faster than a generation that has never lived anywhere else.

By the end of that sequence the question of whether to govern Egypt from the coast had ceased to be open, not because anyone decided it but because the alternative had become impractical. This is how most capitals become capitals, and the absence of a founding decision is the normal case rather than the exception.

## The Sources for the Founding

What is actually known about the founding, as opposed to what is told about it, depends on a small and problematic body of evidence.

The principal narrative source is a work composed in Egypt in the ninth century, roughly two hundred years after the events, which preserves a great deal of local tradition about the conquest and the early city, including material about the allotments and the assignment of quarters. It is the closest thing to a founding account that exists and it was written by someone with access to Egyptian traditions that have not survived elsewhere.

Its limitations are the usual ones for this literature. It is late, it depends on transmitted material of variable quality, and it has an interest: questions about who received which allotment had continuing consequences for property claims, and accounts of the founding were not neutral among the families descended from the original settlers.

The general conquest narratives and geographies supply parallel material, frequently disagreeing on details, and the later Egyptian topographical literature preserves descriptions of the city's quarters and monuments assembled from earlier sources.

Documentary papyri are the control and they are the earliest evidence. They show the administration operating, the languages in use and the personnel involved, and they include material produced in the city within a few years of its founding. They cannot tell you where a tent stood.

Archaeology is the other control and it is the only source for the physical city. Excavation has established construction techniques, house plans, water arrangements, industrial areas and imported goods, and it has confirmed some things the texts report while complicating others. What it cannot do is date the founding precisely, because the earliest layers are the hardest to reach under a continuously occupied and later heavily disturbed site.

The synagogue archive begins several centuries after the founding and is therefore no help for it at all, which is worth stating because the archive's fame can create the impression that the city is well documented throughout. It is superbly documented from the tenth century and thinly documented before.

The practical result is a familiar profile for this series. The framework is secure: a camp was established beside the fortress in the early 640s, ground was allotted to tribal groups, a congregational mosque was built at the centre, and the settlement became the seat of government. The details, including the naming, the dove, the exact date and the assignment of specific quarters, are traditional rather than documented.

## What Survives on the Ground

The physical remains of the city are more substantial than its reputation as a lost site suggests, and knowing what is where explains the state of the evidence.

The Roman fortress survives in part, with round towers and stretches of wall standing within the modern quarter, sunk below present ground level. It is the oldest visible element and the anchor for the whole site.

The congregational mosque stands and functions, rebuilt many times so that essentially nothing of the original fabric remains while the foundation and the site are continuous. Its footprint marks the centre of the original settlement.

The churches of the fortress quarter stand and function, several of them of considerable age, along with the synagogue whose storeroom produced the archive. This concentration of religious buildings from three traditions within a few hundred metres is the quarter's most striking feature.

The excavated areas lie mainly to the east and south of the standing monuments, where open ground exposed by the removal of the refuse mounds allowed archaeologists to work. Walls, house plans, wells and street lines are visible in the excavated zones, and successive campaigns have worked different parts of the site.

The unexcavated remainder lies under the modern city and under areas used for other purposes, and much of it is inaccessible. The proportion of the ancient city that has been properly investigated is small.

The losses are the largest part of the story. The refuse mounds that covered and preserved the site were dug commercially for fertilizer across a long period, removing stratigraphy and scattering objects onto the market without record. The material that reached collections by that route is enormous in quantity and useless for context.

Conservation and presentation of what remains is a continuing project, complicated by the site's location within a dense urban area, by groundwater, and by the competing requirements of archaeology, active religious buildings and the surrounding population.

## The Other Garrison Cities

Setting this foundation beside the comparable ones established during the same decades clarifies what was standard practice and what was specific to Egypt.

The pattern was general across the conquests. Rather than occupying existing cities, the conquering armies established new settlements: two major foundations in Iraq, one in North Africa somewhat later, and this one in Egypt. Each began as a camp for a settled army, each was organized by allotment to tribal groups, each acquired a congregational mosque at its centre, and each grew into a substantial city.

The reasons were consistent. Keeping the army concentrated made it payable and disciplined. Keeping it separate from the conquered population reduced friction and protected the productive base the new state depended on. Placing it where the strategic logic pointed rather than where the previous regime's capital happened to be allowed the new government to site itself for its own purposes.

What was specific to Egypt is the geography. The Iraqi foundations sat at the desert margin controlling access to a river system, which is a comparable logic, and the Egyptian one sat at the single point that controls a country shaped like a corridor. No other conquered province had a geography that concentrated so much strategic value in one spot, which is why the Egyptian foundation's site proved so much more permanent than the others.

The comparison also illuminates the naval factor. Egypt was the only one of these provinces where the previous capital was a great port vulnerable to a hostile fleet, and the decision to move inland has no exact parallel elsewhere because the problem had no exact parallel.

The subsequent histories diverged considerably. The Iraqi foundations both became major cities and both were eventually eclipsed, one by a new imperial capital founded nearby in the eighth century, which is structurally the same story as the Egyptian one and worth noticing. The pattern of a garrison city being superseded by a purpose built royal capital in its vicinity recurs, and it reflects a general truth about how dynasties relate to the settlements they inherit.

The North African foundation followed the same model and became the base for expansion further west, and its relationship to the older coastal cities of the region mirrors this city's relationship to Alexandria.

Reading these foundations as a group rather than individually shows that Egypt's capital was not an improvisation but an application of a policy, and that the policy was sound enough to produce four cities of the first rank.

## The City in Travellers' Accounts

Descriptions by visitors are the most vivid source for the medieval city and require the usual caution, since a traveller records impressions rather than measurements.

Several geographers and travellers of the tenth and eleventh centuries describe the city, and the features that struck them are consistent: its size, the height of its buildings, the density of its markets, the volume of goods passing through it, and the contrast between it and the walled royal enclosure to the north.

The building heights are the detail most often quoted and most often doubted. Accounts describe residential blocks of many storeys with numerous families in each, and the figures given strain credulity at the upper end. What the archaeology confirms is substantial multi storey construction with foundations capable of carrying several floors, so the phenomenon was real and the numbers are probably inflated in the way travellers' numbers usually are.

The markets receive consistent attention, described by commodity and by quarter, and the descriptions match the picture from the commercial documents of a city handling goods from across the known world.

The gardens and the river frontage appear in the accounts, and one traveller's description of produce and prices is detailed enough to be useful economically, though again the figures are impressions.

The contrast with the northern royal foundation is drawn explicitly by visitors who saw both, and it establishes the relationship this article has argued for: a great commercial city in the south, a closed palace enclosure in the north, coexisting rather than succeeding one another.

The reliability of this material improves considerably when it can be checked against the documents and the archaeology, and the general result of such checking is that the qualitative descriptions hold up while the quantities do not. A traveller who says the markets were vast and the buildings tall was reporting accurately. A traveller who says a building had fourteen storeys was reporting an impression.

There is also a category of description worth distinguishing, which is the account written by someone who did not visit and compiled from earlier sources. Medieval geographical literature contains a great deal of this, and identifying which descriptions are first hand is one of the standard problems in using it.

## The Mosque at the Centre

The congregational mosque built at the founding is treated fully in its own article, and its urban function belongs here because it explains how the city was organized.

A congregational mosque in an early Islamic city is not simply a place of worship. It is the assembly point of the whole community for the weekly gathering, the place where the governor addresses the population, where official announcements are made, where legal proceedings are frequently held, where teaching takes place and where people meet for reasons that have nothing to do with religion. It is the functional equivalent of a forum, a courthouse and a town hall combined.

Placing it at the centre of the settlement, in the quarter assigned to the mixed group not attached to any tribe, therefore did something specific. It created a common space belonging to nobody's allotment, where the whole community gathered regardless of tribal affiliation, in a city otherwise organized entirely by descent group.

The mosque's growth tracks the city's. It was enlarged repeatedly across the centuries as the population outgrew it, and each enlargement is a datum about demographic pressure. Successive rulers extended, rebuilt and embellished it, which is the standard way a ruler demonstrates legitimacy through the community's principal building.

Its survival tracks the city's decline in reverse. The settlement around it contracted, was burned, was abandoned in parts and became partly a rubbish field, and the mosque continued in use throughout, rebuilt after damage and maintained by successive authorities. A building that outlives its city is unusual and it happens when the building's function is more durable than the settlement's economy.

Secondary mosques multiplied as the city grew, serving quarters and groups, and their distribution is one of the ways the extent of settlement at different periods can be reconstructed. A quarter with its own mosque is a quarter with a settled population large enough to need one.

The relationship between the mosque and the successive royal foundations to the north is worth noting. Each new ruling group built its own congregational mosque in its own quarter, which fragmented the community's assembly and is a small piece of evidence about how separate those foundations were meant to be. The older mosque continued to serve the older city, which remained the larger population.

## Governing From a Garrison

What it meant in practice to run Egypt from this settlement is worth setting out, because the arrangement was unusual and its consequences ran for centuries.

The governor's residence and the administrative offices were established in the city, and the province's records, treasury and courts operated there. The apparatus doing the actual work was the inherited one, staffed by Egyptians and operating in Greek and in Egyptian for the first two generations, and the physical relocation of that machinery from Alexandria to the new city is itself a significant administrative event that the sources barely mention.

The army's presence made the city a political actor. A settled garrison drawing stipends from provincial revenue is a constituency with interests, and the history of Egypt under the early caliphate includes episodes where that constituency mattered, over pay, over appointments and over the province's alignment during the civil conflicts. Governors had to manage the soldiers as well as the province.

The distance from the centre shaped everything. Instructions took weeks to arrive, revenue took longer to remit, and verification of what had been collected against what was sent was imperfect. This produced the standing tension between province and centre discussed in the article on the province's first governor, and it produced the eventual outcome that a governor with sufficient local standing could rule effectively independently, which is what happened in the ninth century.

The concentration of the Arab population in one city had a demographic consequence that shaped Egypt's whole subsequent development. Because the conquering population was urban and concentrated rather than dispersed across the countryside, the interaction between it and the Egyptian population happened at a boundary rather than through a mixed rural society. Conversion, intermarriage and language shift proceeded outward from the city rather than everywhere at once, which is one reason those processes took as long as they did.

The city also became the place where an Egyptian who wanted advancement had to go. Once the administration was Arabized, around two generations after the conquest, a career required Arabic and required proximity to the government, and both were found in one place. The magnetic effect of a capital on ambition is a general phenomenon and it was particularly strong here because the alternative centres had lost their functions.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Five claims about this city circulate widely and each misleads in a specific way.

The first is that Cairo replaced it. Cairo was founded more than three centuries later as a walled palace enclosure for a dynasty and its troops, and the older city remained the commercial and residential centre of Egypt for two centuries after that. The relationship reversed slowly and for several reasons, and describing it as a replacement compresses three hundred years into a moment.

The second is that the city was destroyed in a single fire. Burning layers exist and an evacuation and firing in the twelfth century is recorded, and the archaeology shows decline in some quarters before that event and continued occupation in others after it. The fire accelerated a process rather than ending a city at a stroke.

The third is that the name's origin is settled by the story of the dove and the tent. That story is a foundation legend, the competing derivation from a Latin term for an entrenchment is linguistically serious, and specialists have not resolved it.

The fourth is that the settlement was a primitive camp that slowly became civilized. The archaeology shows a city with multi storey courtyard houses, piped water, drains and latrines, sophisticated ceramics and international trade goods within a comparatively short time. Whatever the founding conditions, this was quickly an advanced urban environment.

The fifth is that it was an Arab city in the sense of being exclusively Arab. It was founded as a garrison of Arab tribal groups and became within a century a mixed city with Egyptian Christians, a substantial Jewish community and a converting Muslim population of local origin, and its documents show the three communities in constant commercial and legal interaction.

A sixth belief is less a myth than an omission. Popular accounts treat the site as archaeologically lost, and while the losses were severe, systematic excavation has recovered a great deal and continues to do so, and the synagogue archive makes the medieval city one of the best documented in the world.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended is that Egypt's capital region was fixed by a decision about where to camp an army, and that everything about the resulting city followed from its origin as a garrison.

The site was chosen for military and strategic reasons: beyond the reach of a naval power, at the point controlling the country's internal communications, on the routes running east. Those reasons have held for fourteen centuries, and every capital of Egypt since has stood within a few kilometres of the spot.

The layout followed from the allotment of ground to tribal groups, which produced an organic and irregular city rather than a planned one, and which shaped its property patterns, its quarter names and its density for as long as it lasted.

The growth followed from the functions the site acquired: administration, a stipendiary garrison population, migration and above all trade, which turned a camp into one of the largest cities of its world within a century.

The chain of successor foundations followed from the same logic in reverse. Each new ruling group built beside the existing city rather than within it, because the existing city was full and because a new regime wanted its own quarter, and the accumulation of those foundations northward is the physical history of the modern capital.

The decline followed from a combination of the river moving away, the political centre shifting north, and a deliberate burning in a moment of crisis, and the site's afterlife as a rubbish field is the reason its archaeology survived to be recovered.

The qualifications are real. The founding date is approximate and the founding itself was a process rather than an act. The name's origin is unresolved. The population was never counted and no figure should be quoted. The extent of the twelfth century destruction is disputed. And a great deal of the site was destroyed for fertilizer before anyone excavated it properly.

What remains is the observation this article opened with. An army pitched camp beside a captured fort because it needed somewhere to sit, and the spot became the permanent centre of a country that had been governed from elsewhere for a thousand years. Nobody decided to found a capital. They decided where to put the tents, and the tents are still there in the sense that matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the city of Fustat?

Egypt's first Islamic capital, founded around 641 to 642 as the camp of the conquering army beside the captured Roman fortress at the head of the Delta, on the site now within Old Cairo. It grew within a century into one of the largest cities of the Islamic world, a manufacturing and trading centre handling goods from Spain to India, with multi storey courtyard housing, piped water and elaborate drainage. It remained Egypt's commercial and residential centre for more than five hundred years, even after successive rulers founded new administrative quarters to its north, and it declined only after the twelfth century as the capital consolidated northward. The modern quarter preserves its congregational mosque, the fortress remains and adjacent churches.

### Q: Why did the Arabs found Fustat?

Because the existing capital could not be safely held. Alexandria is a maritime city whose defensibility depends on control of the sea, and the conquerors had no fleet while the Byzantine navy remained uncontested, as the seaborne recapture of the city in 645 demonstrated. An inland site on the river was beyond naval reach. The chosen spot, at the point where the valley meets the Delta, controlled the country's internal communications, sat on the routes east toward Sinai and the Red Sea, and matched the strategic logic that had led the Romans to fortify it. Keeping the army concentrated and separate from the taxpaying population was a further consideration, and the same pattern produced new garrison foundations in Iraq and North Africa.

### Q: Who founded Fustat?

Amr ibn al-As, the commander of the conquering force, established it as the camp of his army after the fall of the inland fortress in 641 and before the handover of Alexandria in 642. He assigned ground to the tribal groups making up the force, built the congregational mosque that still bears his name at the centre, and made the settlement the seat of the province's government. Whether any of this was intended as the founding of a capital is doubtful: the arrangement began as a practical solution for where to put an army during an unfinished campaign, and it became permanent through a sequence of decisions and circumstances rather than by design. His career and governorship are treated in his own article.

### Q: What does the name Fustat mean?

Two derivations compete and neither is settled. The first takes it from an Arabic word for a tent or large pavilion, which fits the settlement's origin as a camp and is supported by the traditional story of a dove nesting on the commander's tent, so that he left the tent standing and the camp grew around it. The second takes it from a Latin term for a ditch or entrenchment, borrowed through Greek, which fits a site beside a Roman fortress and the general transmission of Roman military vocabulary into the region. The dove story is a foundation legend of a familiar type and its presence is not evidence about etymology. Specialists differ and the linguistic evidence does not decide it.

### Q: How did Fustat grow into a city?

Through five drivers acting together. Administration came first, as the settlement became the seat of provincial government with its officials, records, courts and treasury. The garrison itself was second: soldiers drawing stipends are consumers with reliable income, and a concentration of them generates markets, workshops and transport. Migration was third, bringing Arabs from the peninsula and Egyptians from the countryside. Trade was fourth and eventually largest, since the site sat at the junction of the river, the Delta and the routes to the Red Sea, and the reopened canal connected it to the Indian Ocean world. Industry was fifth, with pottery, glass, textiles and metalwork serving the city and an export market.

### Q: What happened to Fustat?

It declined across several centuries for several reasons and was not destroyed at a stroke. The Nile migrated westward, progressively separating the settlement from the river and complicating water supply and river access. The political centre moved north through a chain of successor foundations. A recorded evacuation and firing in 1168, ordered to deny the city to an approaching Crusader army, caused real damage, though excavation shows decline in some quarters beforehand and occupation continuing in others afterward. The succeeding dynasty then reorganized the whole capital northward behind new walls and a citadel. The southern district became partly industrial, partly a dumping ground, and the accumulating refuse sealed the archaeology beneath it.

### Q: Was Fustat the first Islamic city in Egypt?

Yes, and the qualification matters. It was the first settlement in Egypt founded by and for the Muslim conquerors, and its congregational mosque was the first mosque in Egypt and in Africa. It was not the first city in the area, since the Roman fortress beside it enclosed an existing settlement with its own churches and population that continued to function throughout. Nor was it the last new foundation, since three further administrative quarters were established to its north over the following three centuries, each by a new ruling group. What distinguishes it is that it was the original and that it remained the population and commercial centre while the later foundations were courtly and administrative.

### Q: How did Fustat become Cairo?

Not by replacement but by absorption over centuries. Cairo was founded in 969, more than three hundred years later, as a walled palace enclosure for a new dynasty, its court and its troops, and it was not initially a general settlement. The older city remained the commercial and residential heart of Egypt for roughly two more centuries, and travellers who saw both describe a great trading city in the south and a closed royal enclosure to the north. The relationship reversed after the twelfth century, when the older city was damaged and declining while the succeeding dynasty opened the northern enclosure to general settlement, enclosed the wider area behind new walls and built a citadel. The southern district became the fringe of the greater capital.

### Q: Where exactly was Fustat located?

On the east bank of the Nile at the head of the Delta, immediately north of the Roman fortress whose remains stand within the quarter now called Old Cairo, on the southern fringe of the modern capital. The camp occupied open ground between the fortress walls and the desert edge to the east, with the river to the west, on somewhat uneven terrain along the desert margin. The position was chosen because it controls the point where the Nile valley opens into the Delta, the junction of river traffic in both directions and of the land routes running east toward Sinai and the Red Sea. Every capital of Egypt since has stood within a few kilometres of the site, which is the strongest evidence that the choice reflected geography rather than preference.

### Q: How were the tribal quarters of Fustat organized?

By allotment. Ground was assigned in blocks to the tribal groups making up the army, somewhat over twenty of them according to the sources, and within each block development was internal to the group, with families building and subdividing as they chose. There was no surveyed street grid and no imposed plan, so the result was an organic pattern of irregular lanes, courtyards and dead ends. At the centre, around the congregational mosque, lay a quarter assigned to a mixed group not attached to any single tribe, organized under the army's banner rather than by descent, which gave the city a common zone from the beginning. Tribal quarter names persisted long after the tribal organization itself had dissolved.

### Q: What did houses in Fustat look like?

Courtyard houses, frequently of several storeys, built in brick. The characteristic plan places a central courtyard at the heart of the house, entered from the street through a bent passage that prevents anyone outside seeing in, with rooms opening onto the court and usually a principal reception room raised on one side. Floors were often paved. The most surprising finding of excavation was the infrastructure: piped water supply, basins, drains, latrines and cesspit arrangements appear systematically rather than exceptionally, indicating a standard of domestic amenity well beyond what was assumed. Vertical development followed from fixed allotments and a growing population, and travellers described residential blocks of many storeys housing several families each.

### Q: Why was Fustat burned in 1168?

According to the narrative accounts, the Fatimid vizier ordered the city evacuated and set alight as a Crusader army approached, so that the invaders could not use it as a base against the walled capital to the north. Destroying one's own city to deny it to an enemy is a recognized military practice and the decision is comprehensible in a regime facing collapse, which this one was, falling within a few years. The archaeological picture is more complicated than the narrative implies. Burning layers exist, and demonstrating destruction across the whole site is harder, while excavation shows some quarters already in decline beforehand and others occupied afterward. The fire accelerated a process rather than ending a city at a stroke.

### Q: What has excavation at Fustat revealed?

A dense residential city rather than a ruin field of monuments, and that finding reshaped assumptions about medieval Islamic urban life. Excavation exposed courtyard houses of good construction with piped water, drains, latrines and cesspits, indicating amenity beyond what was expected. It identified industrial zones with pottery kilns, glass working debris and metalworking waste, concentrated at the margins. It recovered Chinese ceramics in quantity alongside local imitations, establishing direct participation in the Indian Ocean trade. Successive campaigns since the early twentieth century have worked different areas, producing a picture assembled from patches. The great limitation is that refuse mounds covering the site were dug commercially for fertilizer beforehand, destroying stratigraphy and scattering objects without record.

### Q: How big was Fustat at its height?

No reliable figure exists and any specific number should be treated with suspicion. Medieval sources give populations that are rhetorical rather than statistical, modern estimates vary by large multiples, and the standard method of estimating from built area and assumed density is unusually unreliable here because the multi storey construction makes density assumptions guesswork. What can be said is qualitative and consistent: contemporaries who had seen other great cities regarded this one as among the largest they knew, its markets and buildings impressed visitors from across the Islamic world, and the physical extent revealed by excavation and by the distribution of remains indicates a settlement of the first rank for its period.

### Q: What industries operated in Fustat?

Pottery above all, attested by kilns, wasters and enormous quantities of production debris, producing everything from ordinary domestic wares to sophisticated glazed and lustre decorated pieces for export. Glass working is attested by debris and finished objects, continuing an Egyptian industry of long standing. Textiles carried on the industry that had operated in Egypt for millennia, adapted to new patronage. Metalwork, leather, woodwork, paper and sugar refining all appear, the last becoming a major Egyptian industry in the medieval centuries. Alongside manufacturing, the city functioned as a central node in trade running west to North Africa and Spain, north across the Mediterranean, south up the Nile and east through the Red Sea to India.

### Q: Was Fustat a planned city or an unplanned one?

Planned only at the largest scale. Ground was allotted in blocks to tribal groups, which is a planning decision, and everything below that level was organic. Within each allotment families built where they chose and subdivided as they grew, leaving the lanes and open spaces the community happened to need, with no surveyed street grid imposed by any authority. The result was an irregular city of narrow lanes, dead ends and courtyards that grew by accretion, quite unlike a Roman planned foundation with its grid and its monumental centre. Public buildings, markets, baths and infrastructure were fitted into an existing fabric as they were required, which is why the medieval city had no formal civic centre.

### Q: Did Christians and Jews live in Fustat?

Yes, continuously and in substantial numbers. The adjacent fortress settlement was Christian, with churches that still stand and function, and the Christian population of the wider city grew as Egyptians migrated in from the countryside. The Jewish community is the best documented in the entire medieval Islamic world, thanks to the archive its synagogue accumulated, and it was prosperous, internally governed, and deeply involved in Mediterranean commerce. The documents show constant interaction between the communities in business and in law, with parties using the courts of one community or another according to convenience. The quarter's concentration of a Roman fortress, medieval churches, a synagogue and an early mosque within a few hundred metres is its most striking feature.

### Q: What is the Geniza and what does it say about Fustat?

A storeroom in one of the city's synagogues where discarded writings were deposited rather than destroyed, following the practice that texts containing the name of God should not be thrown away. Over centuries it accumulated hundreds of thousands of fragments, preserved by the dry climate, and the contents were far broader than religious texts: business letters, contracts, marriage documents, court records, accounts, personal correspondence, school exercises. Spanning roughly the tenth to the thirteenth centuries, it documents a Mediterranean trading world running from Spain to India with this city as a central node, and it records commercial practice, credit, transport, prices and social life in a detail no other medieval source approaches. It was removed to European and American collections in the late nineteenth century and remains dispersed.

### Q: How did Fustat get its water supply?

From the Nile, by carriers with animals moving it in bulk from the river to the city, a trade that employed large numbers, with cisterns and storage arrangements in houses for holding it and piped distribution within buildings attested by excavation. The problem grew harder over time because the river migrated westward across the medieval centuries, progressively lengthening the haul and increasing the cost, and leaving newly deposited land between the settlement and the water. Later medieval developments in the greater capital included more ambitious lifting and conveyance works. The declining convenience of water supply is one of the slow structural pressures behind the site's eventual contraction, and it operated invisibly across generations.

### Q: Why did the Abbasids build al-Askar next to Fustat?

For the reason every new ruling group in early Islamic Egypt built beside rather than within: the existing city was full, and a new regime wanted its own quarter for its own troops and officials, physically separate from a settlement associated with the order it had displaced. The new quarter, established immediately to the northeast after the change of dynasty in the middle of the eighth century, functioned as an official annexe housing the governing group rather than as an independent city, while the older settlement remained the commercial and residential centre. The same logic produced two further foundations further north over the following two centuries, and the accumulation of those quarters is the physical history of the modern capital.
