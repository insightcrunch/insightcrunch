The Code on Wages 2019 was sold to Parliament and to the public as a consolidation, a tidying of four ageing wage statutes into a single orderly text. Consolidation it certainly is. But a reader who stops at the tidying misses the statute. Buried in the definitions clause of the Code on Wages 2019 sit two changes that reach further than anything else in the four labour codes: a minimum wage that for the first time attaches to every employee in India rather than only to those whose work a government has listed, and a single definition of wages whose proviso quietly rewrites the arithmetic of every salary structure built on a small basic pay. The first change is the headline. The second is the reform. This article works through both, provision by provision, and tests the Code against the four statutes it repealed.

The four repealed statutes were each built for a different decade and a different problem, and none of them spoke to the others. The Payment of Wages Act, 1936 regulated the manner and timing of wage payment for a relatively narrow class of employees, fixing wage periods, pay days and the permissible deductions from a pay packet. The [Minimum Wages Act 1948](/2013/06/15/minimum-wages-act-1948-explained/) empowered governments to fix floor rates, but only for employments entered in a schedule, so that coverage depended on an administrative act of listing rather than on the fact of employment. The Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 distributed a share of profits through the machinery of allocable surplus, confined to establishments above a size threshold and to employees below a wage ceiling. The Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 required equal pay for men and women for the same work or work of a similar nature and barred discrimination in recruitment. Four statutes meant four definitions of the central concept, wages, each drawn for its own purpose, and an employer of any size maintained parallel computations under each.

The failure of that arrangement was not primarily a failure of rates. It was a failure of coverage and a failure of definition. On coverage, the schedule in the Minimum Wages Act made the most basic protection in Indian labour law wait upon a notification: work that nobody had listed carried no statutory floor at all, however badly it was paid, and the litigation of seven decades clustered around the listing question, around whether a given process or institution fell inside the schedule, rather than around the adequacy of any wage. On definition, the divergence between statutes invited structuring. Because provident fund, gratuity and bonus each fastened on a wage concept, and because the basic wage was the component to which those computations were tied in practice, the rational employer shrank the basic component and enlarged the allowances around it, so that the part of the pay packet carrying statutory weight became a small fraction of the whole. Nothing in the four statutes, read separately, forbade that arithmetic. It took a single definition with a ceiling on exclusions to answer it, and no single one of the four Acts could supply that answer because each defined wages only for itself.

There was also a failure of machinery. The claims window under the Minimum Wages Act was six months, a period short enough to defeat precisely the workers the Act existed for, who learn of a right slowly and organise more slowly still. The Equal Remuneration Act spoke only of men and women, a framing that the law on gender had outgrown. The Payment of Wages Act left large classes of higher-paid employees outside its payment discipline altogether, because its protections were tied to a wage ceiling. Each statute, in other words, carried a limitation that its own text could not cure, and the case for replacement rather than amendment was that the limitations were architectural. Parliament accepted that case in 2019, and the manner of the acceptance matters for understanding what the Code is.

## The four statutes in their own time

Each of the repealed Acts had been, in its moment, an advance, and the Code's design is clearer when the four are seen as solutions laid down in layers rather than as a single failed system. The Payment of Wages Act, 1936 grew out of the Royal Commission on Labour's documentation of wage practices in colonial India: wages paid late, paid in kind at truck shops, cut by fines that bore no relation to any loss, and forfeited on pretexts. The 1936 Act answered with procedure. It fixed responsibility for payment on the employer, required wage periods and pay days, and enumerated the deductions an employer could lawfully make, fining being hedged with limits and a welfare purpose. It was a statute about the manner of payment, and it left the level of wages entirely alone.

The Minimum Wages Act, 1948 was the level statute, and its origins explain the schedule. Post-independence India faced sweated trades in which bargaining power was so unequal that the state accepted fixation as a necessity, but fixation required knowledge, committee by committee and trade by trade, of conditions the young republic's governments did not yet possess. The schedule was the instrument of that caution: employments were added as their conditions were studied, and the Act's advisory committees became the forum in which rates were argued. The compromise was defensible in 1948 and indefensible as a permanent settlement, because the caution that justified listing hardened into the boundary of the right itself. Around the same period the Committee on Fair Wages supplied the conceptual ladder the fixation machinery would climb for seventy years, distinguishing the minimum wage, which preserves efficiency and covers the bare subsistence standard it described, from the fair wage above it and the living wage at the top, a ladder that tribunal jurisprudence elaborated and that the floor wage debate under this Code still echoes.

The Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 codified a dispute that tribunals had been conducting case by case since the nineteen forties, whether labour was entitled to a share of profits beyond wages and on what formula. The Full Bench formula of the Labour Appellate Tribunal had distributed available surplus after prior charges, and bonus claims became a recurring industrial dispute of the first rank. The 1965 Act converted the practice into a code: allocable surplus computed from gross profits after statutory deductions, a minimum bonus payable regardless of surplus, a maximum, and the set on and set off accounts by which a lean year's shortfall and a fat year's excess were carried against each other. Bonus was thereby transformed from an ex gratia payment and a recurring grievance into a deferred wage with rules, and it is that transformation, intact in its essentials, that Chapter IV of the Code inherits.

The Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 implemented the directive principle in Article 39(d) of the Constitution, equal pay for equal work for both men and women, and the obligation India had assumed under the International Labour Organization's Equal Remuneration Convention of 1951, which the country ratified in 1958. Its operative rule was simple to state and difficult to move: same work or work of a similar nature, paid at the same rate, with no reduction of anyone's wage to achieve the equality, and no discrimination in recruitment except where women's employment was restricted by law. Its record across four decades was the thinnest of the four statutes, a reminder that a rule about comparison depends on comparators, job segregation defeats comparison, and an inspectorate visiting for other purposes rarely reconstructed a payroll by gender. The Code's recast of the rule on the ground of gender, examined below, is best read as the fourth layer settling over the third, carrying the duty and the difficulty together.

### Why were four separate wage laws replaced by one code?

The immediate answer is that the Second National Commission on Labour, which reported in 2002, recommended gathering India's labour statutes into a few codes organised by subject, with wages the natural first group because the four wage statutes shared one concept and one administrative machinery.

A single definition of wages could then run across minimum wages, payment discipline, bonus and equal pay, and the anomalies produced by four divergent definitions would disappear with the divergence. The deeper answer is coverage: only a fresh statute could abolish the schedule without unravelling the fixation machinery built on top of it.

The Commission's recommendation waited seventeen years for enactment. A Code on Wages Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in 2017, examined by the Standing Committee on Labour, and lost when that House dissolved. The Bill returned in July 2019 and moved quickly: passed by the Lok Sabha on 30 July 2019 and by the Rajya Sabha on 2 August 2019, it received the President's assent on 8 August 2019 as Act 29 of 2019. It was the first of the four labour codes to be enacted, a full year ahead of the other three, and that sequence was itself a statement of priorities. Wages were the least contested of the four subjects and the one where the consolidation argument was hardest to answer, because the four predecessor statutes overlapped so visibly. Even so, enactment and operation proved to be different events, separated by more than six years, and the interval between assent and commencement shaped the Code's reception as much as its text did: for most of that interval the Code existed as a settled future, definite enough to plan around and remote enough to defer.

The speed of the 2019 passage drew criticism that belongs to the record. A Bill that restructured the wage law of the entire workforce was taken through both Houses in ten days at the end of July and the start of August, and the debate, such as it was, ran largely on the consolidation claim itself, which few were positioned to oppose in principle, rather than on the definitional proviso, which few outside the payroll profession had yet modelled. The 2017 Bill had received the slower treatment, with examination by the Standing Committee on Labour, and the 2019 text carried forward much of what that examination had produced; but the interval between introduction and enactment in 2019 was measured in sitting days, and the statute's most consequential number, the fifty per cent in the proviso, entered the law with less parliamentary arithmetic devoted to it than its effect on provident fund accumulations would later warrant. That is an observation about process, not about merit, and it explains a feature of the Code's afterlife: the argument about the definition, which in a slower passage might have happened on the floor, happened instead across six years of commentary, draft rules and industry modelling while commencement waited, so that by the time the Code operated, its central provision was among the most thoroughly pre-litigated texts in Indian labour law, debated everywhere except in the chamber that passed it.

What the Code kept from its predecessors deserves emphasis, because the replacement was conservative in its machinery even where it was radical in its definitions. The fixation of minimum wages remains with the appropriate Government, Central or state according to the sphere, exactly as the 1948 Act arranged it, and the Code retains the two methods by which rates have always been fixed: the committee method, under which committees and sub-committees hold inquiries and advise the government, and the notification method, under which the government publishes its proposals, considers representations, and notifies the rates. The discipline that rates be reviewed and revised at intervals not exceeding five years is carried over from the old Act without alteration. The bonus chapter preserves the allocable surplus architecture of the 1965 Act, its set on and set off machinery and its linkage of bonus to profits above a guaranteed minimum. The claims route, an application to a notified authority rather than a suit, survives from the predecessor statutes. A reader who knew the old machinery will recognise most of the new building. What changed is the ground on which it stands: who is covered, and what counts as wages.


## What the Code discarded, and why the schedule mattered most

The largest single deletion in the Code is a list. Section 5 of the Code on Wages provides that no employer shall pay any employee wages less than the minimum rate notified by the appropriate Government, and Section 6 obliges the appropriate Government to fix those rates for employees generally. Nothing in the chapter confines the duty to listed employments. The schedule, the device by which the 1948 Act had rationed its own protection, is simply absent, and with it goes the threshold question that structured minimum wage law for seventy years. Under the old Act the first question in any case was classificatory: was this employment scheduled, was this process part of a scheduled employment, was this institution inside or outside the list. Under the Code the first question is arithmetical: what rate has been notified for this class of work in this area, and was the employee paid it. The disputes do not vanish. They migrate, from coverage to level and from coverage to definition, which is where this article will follow them.

Section 6 directs the fixation exercise itself. The appropriate Government fixes a minimum rate for time work, a minimum rate for piece work, and a guaranteed time rate for piece workers, so that a worker paid by the piece is assured a floor measured by time. Rates may be fixed by the hour, by the day or by the month. The fixation provisions differentiate by category of skill, separating unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled and highly skilled work, and by geographical area, and they take account of the arduousness of the work, its temperature, humidity, hazards and underground character. Section 7 addresses the components of a minimum wage, a basic rate together with a cost of living allowance that tracks prices, which is the statutory home of the variable dearness allowance familiar under the old regime. Sections 13 and 14 complete the chapter's working conditions: the appropriate Government fixes the hours constituting a normal working day, with a weekly rest day, and an employee who works beyond the normal day earns overtime at not less than twice the normal rate of wages. None of this machinery is novel. Its significance lies entirely in its reach. A fixation apparatus that once served a schedule of listed employments serves, under the Code, the whole employed population.

### Who gained coverage when scheduled employments were abolished?

Every employee whose work had never been entered in a state schedule gained a statutory floor for the first time: workers in small and unlisted trades, in new forms of work that no committee had examined, and in services that fixation machinery had never reached. The gain is a change in legal status rather than in any pay packet.

A rate must still be notified for the class of work, and the worker must still be able to invoke it, but the argument that the work stands outside the Act altogether is no longer available to an employer.

The universality change is best understood against the oddity it replaced. The 1948 Act was a statute of undoubted moral force whose protection was switched on occupation by occupation, so that its coverage map was the accumulated product of decades of committee work, state priorities and litigation. Two workers doing comparable work at comparable pay could stand on opposite sides of the line because one trade had been scheduled and the other had not, and the Act offered no principle by which to defend the difference. The Code removes the line. Its minimum wage chapters speak of employees as such, and the fixation duty in Section 6 is owed to the workforce at large, differentiated by skill and area but not gated by a list. For the aspirant, this is the first of the two changes to hold: universality is the proposition the Code's supporters cite, and it is genuinely a first in Indian law, because no earlier statute had attached a minimum wage to employment as such.

Universality has a second, quieter consequence that runs through the rest of the Code. Because the wage chapters apply across employments, the Code's payment discipline in Chapter III, its bonus chapter and its claims machinery also operate on a general canvas rather than within the pockets the old statutes had marked out. The Payment of Wages Act had confined its protections by a wage ceiling, so that an employee paid above the ceiling stood outside its rules on pay days and deductions. The Code drops that ceiling for its payment chapter: the rules on wage periods, payment deadlines and permissible deductions are written for employees generally. Consolidation and universality are therefore the same decision viewed from two sides. Four statutes became one, and the one speaks to everyone.

Section 7, on the components of minimum wages, carries forward the three forms the old Act had used, and the choice among them shapes how a rate behaves between revisions. A minimum wage may consist of a basic rate together with a cost of living allowance adjusted to the cost of living index; or of a basic rate, with or without such an allowance, together with the cash value of concessions in respect of supplies of essential commodities at concession rates, where authorised; or of an all-inclusive rate that folds the basic, the allowance and the cash value of concessions into one figure. The first form is the one that dominates practice, and its virtue is that it separates the rate into a part revised by committee deliberation and a part that moves with an index, so the worker's protection against price rise does not wait upon the five-year revision cycle. The cost of living allowance is computed against the index series appropriate to the workforce, the consumer price index for industrial workers and the corresponding series for agricultural labour in the agricultural sphere, and state practice in operating it under the old Act, notification at six-monthly intervals keyed to average index movement, is the practice the Code inherits with the section. A rate expressed in the all-inclusive form, by contrast, freezes the price protection into the number and is correspondingly disfavoured where a fixation authority expects years to pass between revisions. The details are technical, and they are where minimum wage law lives in practice: two states may notify rates that look comparable on the day of notification and diverge steadily thereafter because one has indexed the allowance component and the other has not, a divergence no reading of the basic rates alone will reveal. The floor wage provision interacts with the components in the same quiet way, since the comparison a state must satisfy, that its rates stand at not less than the floor, is made on the notified rates for the area, and the composition of those rates decides how long the satisfaction lasts between adjustments.

Fixation in practice has a texture the statutory methods only partly describe. The committee method is tripartite by design, employers, employees and independents sitting together, and under the old Act the advisory board structure at the Centre and in the states gave that tripartism a permanent address; the Code retains the boards, as a later section of this article explains. In operation, fixation cycles have run on two clocks at once: the revision of the basic rate at intervals within the five-year ceiling, and the adjustment of the cost of living component against a consumer price index, which state governments have commonly notified twice a year, so that a worker's minimum wage has typically moved in small indexed steps between the larger revisions. The Code alters neither clock. What it alters is the population the clocks serve, and the number of separate fixation exercises a state must maintain: one general fixation, differentiated by skill and area, replaces the schedule-by-schedule accumulation of rates, each with its own notification history. Consolidation therefore simplifies the state's side of the machinery as much as the employer's, a point usually lost in accounts that treat the Code as a compliance measure alone. The risk runs the other way as well. A single fixation exercise covering every class of work asks more of the committee stage than a trade-specific inquiry did, and a government that fixes thinly for the general case has fewer visible gaps to be pressed on than one that had simply never listed a trade.

The guaranteed time rate in Section 6 earns its place in the fixation scheme by answering the oldest evasion in piece-rate work. A worker paid by the piece, the rolled beedi, the stitched garment, the packed carton, bears the whole risk of slow orders, poor material and interrupted supply if the wage is measured only in output; a rate per piece set against an assumed pace can be satisfied on a good day and starve on a bad one through no fault of the worker's hands. The Code's scheme answers by requiring both a minimum piece rate and a guaranteed time rate, so that the piece worker's earnings are underwritten by a floor measured in time, and the employer who engages labour by the piece buys the labour's time as well as its product. The old Act had carried the same pair of rates, and the jurisprudence and committee practice under it, particularly in the beedi and home-based trades where the employment relation itself had to be established against the denial that any employer existed, forms the background the Code inherits. Home-based work remains the frontier of the fixation duty under any scheme, because the establishment sits in a dwelling, the workforce is largely women, the intermediary contractor is the visible face of the engagement, and the definitions, employee, employer including employment through a contractor, establishment, do the heavy lifting of coverage. It is not accidental that the Code's definitional scheme reaches expressly through contractors: the piece-rate trades were where the schedule system's coverage failed hardest in practice even after listing, and the Code's answer is to make the duty follow the wages wherever the engagement is routed.

## Who counts as an employee, and what counts as an establishment

Universality operates through the Code's definitions, and they are drawn wide. An employee, under the Code, is a person, other than an apprentice engaged under the Apprentices Act, employed on wages by an establishment to do any skilled, semi-skilled or unskilled, manual, operational, supervisory, managerial, administrative, technical or clerical work for hire or reward, on express or implied terms. An establishment is any place where any industry, trade, business, manufacture or occupation is carried on. An employer includes a person who employs an employee directly or through another person, including a contractor, together with the other persons the definition names, from the occupier of a factory to the legal representative of a deceased employer. Three consequences follow. The first is that the minimum wage chapters are not confined to manual work or to blue-collar employment; the managerial and clerical employee stands inside the same fixation duty as the operative, which the schedule system had obscured by classifying employments rather than persons. The second is that the route through a contractor does not take the worker outside the Code; the definition of employer reaches the arrangement expressly, and the responsibility provisions in the claims chapter pursue the dues accordingly. The third is quieter: because the definitions speak of establishments rather than of industries listed in a schedule, the premises question under the Code is the modest one of whether work is carried on at a place, not the litigated one of whether the activity resembles a scheduled entry. Read with Sections 5 and 6, the definitional scheme converts the minimum wage from a benefit attached to listed trades into an incident of employment itself, which is the precise sense in which the brief for this article describes the change as universality. The boundary cases move accordingly, to the edges of employment as a status: the genuinely independent contractor, the apprentice under the apprenticeship regime, and the platform worker whose arrangements the social security code addresses by scheme rather than by fixation, each of which will be argued at the definition's edge rather than at a schedule's edge.

## The definition of wages in Section 2(y)

The definition sits in Section 2(y) of the Code, and it is uniform: the same words govern the Code on Wages and, by adoption, the other three labour codes, so that a computation made for provident fund under the social security code begins from the same base as a computation made for minimum wages under this one. The definition proceeds in three movements. First, inclusion: wages means all remuneration expressed in money, or capable of being so expressed, payable to a person employed in respect of his employment or work done in it, and it includes basic pay, dearness allowance and retaining allowance, if any. Second, exclusion: the definition then lists eleven heads that are not wages, among them any bonus payable under any law which does not form part of the remuneration under the terms of employment; the value of house accommodation and of the supply of light, water, medical attendance or other amenity; any contribution paid by the employer to a pension or provident fund, with the interest on it; conveyance allowance and travelling concession; sums paid to defray special expenses entailed by the nature of the employment; house rent allowance; remuneration payable under an award, a settlement or an order of a court or Tribunal; overtime allowance; commission; gratuity payable on termination; and retrenchment compensation and other retirement benefits or ex gratia payments on termination. Third, the proviso, which disciplines the exclusions: where the payments under the specified exclusion heads, clauses (a) to (i) of the definition, exceed one-half of all the remuneration, or such other percentage as the Central Government may notify, the amount of the excess is deemed to be remuneration and is added back into wages.

Two further refinements complete the provision. A second proviso directs that, for computing wages for the purposes of equal remuneration across genders and of payment of wages, certain excluded heads, conveyance allowance, house rent allowance, award or settlement remuneration and overtime allowance, are taken into account, a rule that prevents the exclusion list from being used to manufacture a gender gap in the comparison or to shrink the sum whose payment the Code polices. And an explanation provides that where remuneration is paid partly in kind, the part in kind, so long as it does not exceed fifteen per cent of the total wages, is deemed to form part of the wages, which both recognises payment in kind and fences it.

The second proviso deserves a paragraph of its own, because it shows the draftsmen thinking about manipulation in two registers at once. The general definition excludes house rent allowance, conveyance allowance, overtime allowance and remuneration under an award or settlement; the proviso directs that those four heads be counted back in when wages are computed for two purposes, equal remuneration across genders and the payment of wages. The mischief addressed is distinct in each case. In an equal pay comparison, an employer who paid men and women the same basic wage while loading the difference into house rent allowance would, on the unmodified definition, produce identical wages for both and defeat Section 3 on paper; counting the allowance heads back in makes the comparison run on the substance of the packet. In payment discipline, the sum whose timely payment Chapter III polices, and from which Section 18 deductions are made, would shrink to basic pay and dearness allowance if the excluded heads stayed out, leaving the greater part of many packets outside the pay-day and deduction rules; the proviso keeps the chapter fastened to the money the employee waits for at the month's end. Neither limb of the proviso changes the general base for provident fund or gratuity, and commentary that treats the four heads as read back into wages for all purposes overstates it. The provision is a surgical qualification, aimed at the two places where exclusion would have been an instrument rather than a classification.

The three heads the definition expressly includes repay a glance, because two of them are obvious and the third is a fossil that explains the first two. Basic pay and dearness allowance are the historic core of the Indian wage packet, the components to which provident fund and gratuity had always been keyed, and their naming preserves that centrality. Retaining allowance is the payment made to retain the services of skilled workers through the off-season of a seasonal industry, the sugar factory being the standard example, where the plant stands idle for part of the year and the employer pays to keep the mechanic and the pan-man attached against the next crushing season. Its presence in the definition, carried from the wage definitions of the older statutes, is a reminder that the wage concept in Indian law was drafted in the first instance for factories and plantations, long before the salary structures of the services economy existed to be regulated. The Code retains the term without embarrassment, and correctly: seasonal industry has not vanished, and the worker retained through the off-season is paid precisely for not working elsewhere, a payment the definition treats as wages because it is remuneration in respect of the employment, seasonal rhythm and all.

### How does the add-back proviso in Section 2(y) work?

The proviso is a ceiling on exclusions, not a command about salary design. An employer may structure pay in any proportions it chooses, but when the excluded components named in clauses (a) to (i) together come to more than half of the total remuneration, the law disregards the excess for statutory purposes and treats it as wages anyway.

The practical consequence is a floor under the statutory wage base: whatever the structure says, wages for the Code's computations cannot fall below half of the whole package.

The worked table below applies the definition to a representative package. The illustration counts the employer's provident fund contribution within total remuneration, as cost-to-company structures do, and assumes provident fund is contributed on the full statutory wage base, so that the direction and scale of the change can be seen; an individual payslip will differ with the ceiling an employer applies and the components it uses.

| Component or result (rupees per month) | Low-basic structure | After the Section 2(y) test | Restructured structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basic pay | 18,000 | 18,000 | 24,000 |
| Dearness allowance | 2,000 | 2,000 | 6,000 |
| House rent allowance, clause (f) | 18,000 | 18,000 | 15,000 |
| Conveyance allowance, clause (d) | 4,000 | 4,000 | 3,000 |
| Special expenses allowance, clause (e) | 8,000 | 8,000 | 5,000 |
| Overtime and commission, clauses (h) and (i) | 7,840 | 7,840 | 4,840 |
| Employer provident fund contribution, clause (c) | 2,160 | 2,160 | 2,160 |
| Total remuneration | 60,000 | 60,000 | 60,000 |
| Excluded payments under clauses (a) to (i) | 40,000 | 40,000 | 30,000 |
| Excess over one-half of total remuneration | 10,000 | added back | nil |
| Wages for statutory purposes | 20,000 | 30,000 | 30,000 |
| Employee provident fund at 12 per cent of wages | 2,400 | 3,600 | 3,600 |
| Gratuity accrual for one year, at fifteen days of wages per year | 11,538 | 17,308 | 17,308 |

Read the three columns as a before, a during and an after. The low-basic structure in the first column was lawful under the repealed statutes and common across Indian industry: a basic pay of eighteen thousand rupees inside a package of sixty thousand, with the balance carried by allowances, so that every computation tied to basic pay ran on thirty per cent of the real remuneration. The second column shows the same structure after the statutory test is applied. The excluded payments total forty thousand rupees against a ceiling of thirty thousand, one-half of the package; the ten thousand rupees of excess is deemed to be wages; and the base on which provident fund, gratuity and bonus are computed rises from twenty thousand to thirty thousand without a single contractual term having changed. The third column shows the structure an employer reaches when it redesigns rather than submits to deeming: basic pay and dearness allowance are raised to thirty thousand between them, the allowances are trimmed to exactly half the package, and the statutory base arrives at the same thirty thousand by design. The endpoint is identical. The proviso therefore does not dictate a payslip; it dictates a minimum base, and leaves the employer to reach that base by restructuring or by operation of law.

Two features of the proviso repay attention. The first is that it is expressed through clauses (a) to (i) only. Gratuity and retrenchment compensation, clauses (j) and (k), stand outside the tested pool, because they are terminal payments that arise on the ending of employment and cannot sensibly be counted in a monthly structuring test; the ceiling polices the recurring components of a package, which are the components that were being inflated. The second is that the proportion is not frozen in the text. The statute says one-half, or such other percentage as the Central Government may notify, so the single most consequential number in the Code can be moved by notification without returning to Parliament. Commentary that states the fifty per cent figure as though it were an unalterable feature of the design misreads the provision: it is a statutory default with a delegated power attached, and the durability of any percentage statement depends on the notification position.

## A definition built for four codes

The definition's reach extends beyond this Code because the consolidation was a family of statutes, not a single one. The same words were written into the Code on Social Security, the Industrial Relations Code and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, so that the base computed under Section 2(y) of this Code is the base from which provident fund contributions, gratuity and maternity benefit are computed under the social security code, and the base relevant to the wage-linked thresholds elsewhere in the project. That is why the definition, and only the definition, could not have been enacted inside any one of the old statutes. A ceiling on exclusions written into the Minimum Wages Act would have bound minimum wage computation and nothing else; the structuring problem would have survived in provident fund, in gratuity and in bonus, each of which had its own definition and its own threshold arithmetic. The reform had to sit at the level of the concept itself, and the concept could only be unified by replacing the statutes that had fragmented it.

The structuring the proviso answers deserves a precise description, because it was neither fraud nor sharp practice in its ordinary form. Salary design in the decades before the Code was pulled by three lawful incentives. Provident fund contributions were computed on basic wages and dearness allowance under the provident fund statute, so a smaller basic component meant smaller contributions and, for the employee, a larger monthly packet. Gratuity accrued on the last drawn basic pay and dearness allowance, so the same design deferred and shrank that liability as well. House rent allowance, by contrast, carried an income tax exemption for the employee within its own rules, so enlarging the allowance side of the package could be presented, honestly, as tax-efficient pay design rather than as evasion. A package in which basic pay stood at a quarter or a third of the whole was therefore not an aberration but an equilibrium, produced by the interaction of definitions that no single employer had written and no single statute had coordinated. The significance of the fifty per cent ceiling is that it breaks the equilibrium at its base: whatever the tax treatment of an allowance, and whatever the payslip calls a component, the statutory wage base cannot be designed below half of total remuneration. Employers retained freedom over the shape of pay above that floor, and the freedom matters, because performance-linked and variable components were deliberately left within the exclusion heads, variable commission and overtime among them, subject to the ceiling. What the Code withdrew was not flexibility but the use of flexibility to separate the payslip from the statutory computations that ride on it.

## Restructuring is a legal act, not a payroll preference

Because the add-back operates by deeming, an employer can lawfully do nothing and let the statute recharacterise the package; but where an employer chooses to restructure, the change is a change in the terms on which people are employed, and the law of the employment relation has something to say about it. A salary structure is a term of the contract of employment, and for the large class of employees who were workmen under the repealed Industrial Disputes Act, wages, including the period and mode of payment, stood in the Fourth Schedule of that Act among the conditions of service that an employer could not alter without the notice of change Section 9A required, twenty-one days' notice in the prescribed manner, with the change contestable as an industrial dispute. The same architecture of notice and contest attends changes under the industrial relations framework that succeeds it. A restructuring that holds total remuneration steady while moving its components, enlarging basic pay and trimming allowances, is in aggregate neutral and yet not component by component: the employee's house rent allowance falls, with consequences for the tax exemption that allowance carried, the provident fund deduction rises, and the cash in hand each month falls, even though the employer's cost and the package's total are unchanged. Whether such a change improves or worsens the employee's position is a function of time, the loss being monthly and the gain deferred to retirement and to gratuity, and employees were entitled in law to have the change put to them through the notice machinery rather than discovered in a payslip. The Code itself does not regulate the transition of contracts; Section 61 gives its provisions overriding effect over inconsistent terms in any agreement or contract of service, which secures the statutory computations against a contract that purports to define wages lower, but it does not by itself rewrite the contract's own distribution of the package. The distinction mattered in the transition. The statute's base changed by force of law on commencement; the payslip's shape changes by agreement, by notice, or by the slow route of new entrants engaged on the new structure while legacy structures are deemed upward by the proviso. Commentary that announced that every salary structure in India had to be redrawn by a date conflated the two events, and the conflation was the source of much of the confusion the transition produced.

## What the definition does to a pay packet, in both directions

The debate that followed the Code's enactment condensed into a claim and a counterclaim, and both are true of different payslips. The claim was that take-home pay would fall. Where provident fund is contributed on the statutory wage base, a base enlarged by the add-back proviso enlarges the employee's own contribution, which is deducted from the monthly packet; an employee whose structure stood at twenty thousand of wages inside a sixty thousand package contributes twenty-four hundred rupees a month in the illustration above, and contributes thirty-six hundred once the base is deemed to be thirty thousand. If the total cost of the package is held fixed, that extra twelve hundred rupees a month comes out of the cash the employee receives, and the fall in take-home pay is real arithmetic, not advocacy. The counterclaim was that retirement savings would rise. The same twelve hundred, doubled by the employer's matching contribution, enters the provident fund, and gratuity, which accrues at fifteen days of wages for each completed year of service, accrues on the enlarged base as well: seventeen thousand three hundred and eight rupees a year in the illustration against eleven thousand five hundred and thirty-eight. What the Code redistributes, on a fixed package, is the timing of pay: less of it monthly and in hand, more of it deferred and on retirement.

Neither side of that debate holds for every structure, and honest treatment of the Code requires the qualifications to be stated as plainly as the headline. An employer that contributes provident fund only up to the statutory wage ceiling under the provident fund law, rather than on the full wage base, will see little change in contribution cost whatever the add-back does to the definition, because the ceiling, not the base, is doing the limiting. An employee already structured with basic pay and dearness allowance at or above half the package sees no add-back at all and no change in either direction; the proviso bites only on structures built below the halfway line, which is to say on the structures it was aimed at. Bonus moves with the base too, since the bonus chapter computes on wages, so an enlarged base enlarges bonus where the employee remains within the chapter's eligibility ceiling, a third effect that the take-home debate largely ignored. The Code's definition is therefore best described not as a rise in labour cost or a cut in take-home pay but as a compression of the space in which structuring could separate the two: after the add-back, statutory computations track at least half of what the employee receives in remuneration, and the distance between the payslip's largest number and the law's operative number can no longer be stretched at will.

### What did the Supreme Court say about allowances and provident fund wages?

Regional Provident Fund Commissioner v Vivekananda Vidyamandir (Supreme Court, 28 February 2019) held that allowances paid universally, necessarily and ordinarily to all employees across the board form part of basic wages under the Employees' Provident Funds Act, while payments that are variable, linked to extra work or output incentives, or not paid to every employee do not.

The judgment was delivered months before the Code received assent, and it remains the closest existing guide to how an add-back dispute under the Code will be argued, because it answers the same question from the other side: which components of a package are wages in substance whatever the payslip calls them.

The sequence matters for any assessment of the Code. The judiciary had already refused to let labels govern the provident fund base, in a line of authority of which Vivekananda Vidyamandir was the consolidation; Parliament then wrote the refusal into a general definition applicable across all four codes, replacing case-by-case characterisation with a numerical ceiling. The two approaches coexist uneasily and will have to be reconciled in litigation, because the Code's proviso is mechanical, half of total remuneration regardless of the character of each allowance, while the provident fund jurisprudence is qualitative, asking whether an allowance is universal and ordinary or variable and special. Which governs where the provident fund statute's own definition of basic wages continues to operate is a question the delivered text does not foreclose, and it should be flagged as open rather than resolved by assertion in either direction. For the student of the Code, the reliable propositions are narrower: the structuring device was judicially suspect before it was statutorily capped, and the cap, not the case law, is what an examiner or a payroll department applies first.

## The floor wage in Section 9, and what it is not

Section 9 of the Code directs the Central Government to fix a floor wage, taking into account the minimum living standards of a worker, after consultation with the Central Advisory Board, and permits different floor wages for different geographical areas. Appropriate Governments must then fix their minimum rates at not less than the floor wage for the area, and a rate already higher than the floor when the floor is fixed is protected against reduction on that account. The provision is the Code's only centralising device, and it is consistently misdescribed in the summary literature as the introduction of a national minimum wage. It is not that, and the distinction is structural rather than pedantic. A national minimum wage would be a rate payable to an employee, enforceable by the employee against the employer as the wage itself. The floor wage is a rate addressed to governments: it fixes the least rate a state may lawfully notify, and the employee's enforceable entitlement remains the rate the appropriate Government has notified for the work. The floor constrains fixation; it does not itself fix any worker's wage.

The provision's other limitation is the absence of a formula. The statute commits the floor to the Central Government's judgment of minimum living standards, arrived at after consultation, and prescribes no basket of needs, no calorie or consumption norm and no indexation rule from which a figure could be derived or against which a figure could be tested. India arrived at the Code with a long needs-based tradition, from the Fair Wages Committee through the fifteenth Indian Labour Conference norms to the expert committee of 2019 chaired by Anoop Satpathy, which recommended a national minimum wage of three hundred and seventy-five rupees a day on a needs-based method, a figure the government did not accept. The Code acknowledges that tradition only in the phrase minimum living standards, and leaves the arithmetic to the executive of the day. The floor wage the Centre inherited as a working reference was the advisory National Floor Level Minimum Wage, revised to one hundred and seventy-six rupees a day with effect from 1 June 2017 from the earlier one hundred and sixty, as the Ministry of Labour and Employment informed the Rajya Sabha in February 2024; the Code converts that advisory instrument into a statutory constraint, but the constraint's bite in any year depends on a number the statute does not supply. A reader should therefore treat any account that reports the floor wage as though it were a wage in the hand with suspicion, and should treat the level at which the floor is fixed, whenever figures are cited, as a fact about a notification, not about the Code's text.

The ancestry of the floor is instructive. The National Floor Level Minimum Wage began in 1996 as a purely advisory figure, recommended by the National Commission on Rural Labour and maintained by periodic executive revision, addressed to the states as a request that no minimum wage be fixed below it. Advisory status meant the figure's discipline ran through persuasion and through the periodic conferences of labour ministers, and states fixed below it in the years when the request carried no consequence. The revisions of that advisory figure were themselves events in the politics of wages, contested by unions as inadequate and by state governments as unfunded instruction, and the figure of one hundred and seventy-six rupees a day that the Ministry cited to the Rajya Sabha in February 2024 had stood unrevised since June 2017, a fact that illustrates the advisory instrument's weakness better than any argument. Section 9 takes that figure's institutional ghost and gives it legal effect: a floor fixed under the Code binds the fixation of every appropriate Government, and a state notification below it is not merely regrettable but unlawful. The conversion of advice into constraint is the provision's whole content, and it is a genuinely federal instrument, because it disciplines the states through a number the Centre must itself fix transparently and defend, after advice from a board on which the states sit. What Section 9 does not convert is the number's method: the advisory figure had been derived, over its life, by adjusting a base for price movement, and the Code prescribes no derivation at all, so the first statutory floor will be an act of executive judgement wearing the authority of a tradition it does not enact. Readers of the Code should hold the two halves of that sentence together, because commentary on the floor wage habitually prints only one of them.

Set against those qualifications, the floor wage still does real work. State minimum wages in India vary widely, and a part of the variation reflects differences in prices and a part reflects differences in political priority; a binding floor compresses the second kind of variation by ruling the lowest notifications out of bounds, and the protection for existing higher rates ensures the compression works upward only. The device is modest because the Code's settlement is federal: fixation remains with the governments closest to the labour market, and the Centre is given a boundary to police rather than a rate to set. Whether that settlement was too modest is a question for the assessment below; that it was deliberate is plain from the text.

The tradition the floor wage provision declines to enact has its own well documented content, and setting it out shows what the omission costs. The fifteenth session of the Indian Labour Conference, in 1957, adopted norms for a need-based minimum wage: a standard working class family taken as three consumption units, food requirements calculated on a reference intake of twenty-seven hundred calories for an average adult, clothing at eighteen yards per person per year, housing at the minimum rent charged by government for low income housing, and fuel, lighting and miscellaneous items at twenty per cent of the total. In Workmen v Management of Reptakos Brett and Company, in 1992, the Supreme Court revisited those norms and added a further twenty-five per cent to cover children's education, medical requirements, recreation and contingencies, treating the norms as a floor of decency against which fixation could be judged. Committees after that, down to the Satpathy committee of 2019, worked within the same architecture, updating prices and debating the consumption basket. The constitutional backdrop points the same way: Article 43 of the Constitution directs the state to endeavour to secure a living wage, and Articles 39 and 43 together furnished the moral grammar of the fixation jurisprudence. Section 9 of the Code takes from all of this exactly one phrase, minimum living standards, and no norm, no basket and no percentage. Fixation of the floor therefore remains an executive act reviewable, if at all, at the margins, and the needs-based tradition survives as argument rather than as rule. A reader comparing the Code with its predecessors should register the choice accurately: on rates, the old system was thick with norms and thin on coverage; the Code is universal in coverage and deliberately thin on the norms by which its central rate is to be set.

## The advisory boards in Section 42

Chapter V of the Code constitutes the advisory machinery through which fixation politics is institutionalised. Section 42 requires the Central Government to constitute a Central Advisory Board and every state government to constitute a State Advisory Board. The Central Board is composed of representatives of employers and of employees in equal number, independent persons not exceeding one-third of the total membership, and five representatives of state governments nominated by the Centre; one-third of the members must be women, and the chairperson is appointed from among the independent members. The boards advise on the fixation and revision of minimum wages and connected matters, and the Central Board's remit runs expressly to providing increasing employment opportunities for women and to the extent to which women may be employed in establishments or employments the government specifies, a pairing that makes the board the Code's standing forum on women's work as well as on rates. The floor wage in Section 9 is fixed after taking the Central Advisory Board's advice, so the board is the one deliberative body standing between the executive and the Code's most politically sensitive number. The structure is the old Act's tripartism carried into the Code and given a statutory gender composition, and its retention, like the retention of the committee method, marks the boundary of the consolidation's ambition: the Code replaced the statutes, and kept the forums in which wages had always been argued.

## The federal design of fixation

The Code leaves the map of fixation where the old law had it, because labour sits in the Concurrent List and the wage statutes had always been worked by two levels of government. The appropriate Government for establishments in the central sphere, the railways, mines, oilfields, major ports, central public sector undertakings and authorities under the Central Government among them, is the Central Government; for everything else it is the state government. Each fixes rates for its sphere, each maintains its advisory board, each appoints the authorities and the Inspector-cum-Facilitators who work the claims and inspection chapters. The floor wage is the single thread drawn across that map, and its modesty is the modesty of the constitutional settlement: the Centre may police the bottom of the state distribution, and may not occupy the field. Two practical consequences deserve notice. The first is that a national picture of minimum wages under the Code remains a composite of state notifications, varying with local prices, local politics and local administrative energy, with the floor disciplining only the lowest tail; a worker's entitlement is read from the notification of the government of the place of work, and national debate that speaks of the minimum wage in the singular is debating an abstraction. The second is that the quality of the Code's universality will be decided in the states, because universality multiplied the classes of work for which each state must fix and periodically revise rates. A state that fixes carefully by skill and area, and revises the cost of living component on its established rhythm, gives the right content; a state that notifies a single undifferentiated rate for all work has complied with the letter while thinning the fixation tradition the Code inherited, in which differentiation was the substance of the committee's work. The Code cannot be graded on this axis from its text. It supplied the duty and the floor; the map's filling-in belongs to the governments that share the field, which is why the series treats the Code as a framework statute whose success is federated in advance.

Agriculture and domestic work show the federal design under its greatest strain, and they are worth a paragraph because they are where universality is most often tested. Agricultural wages were scheduled employments in most states under the old Act, fixed with the seasons and the operations of the agricultural year in view, and domestic work appeared in the schedules of some states and not of others, the unevenness of that coverage being one of the standing exhibits in the case against the schedule. Under the Code both stand inside the fixation duty without qualification, and the rate for each must still come from the appropriate Government's notification, differentiated as the fixation provisions allow. The distance between a right and a rate is at its widest in these employments: the workforce is dispersed, the workplace is a field or a household, inspection is impractical at scale, and the claim route depends on a worker reaching an authority with some record of the engagement. The Code's answers are the answers of its general design, the burden of proof placed on the employer in a claim, the register and proof duties moderated for the smallest domestic employers, the collective application. Whether those answers reach the household and the harvest is the empirical question the Code's first decade will answer, and it is not answered by the statute's undoubted improvement in coverage on paper.

## Equal remuneration recast as a rule about gender

Section 3 of the Code provides that no employer shall discriminate among employees on the ground of gender in matters of wages, by paying less or paying more, for the same work or work of a similar nature done by any employee, and that no employer shall reduce the rate of wages of any employee in order to comply; the section reaches into recruitment as well, barring discrimination against women, subject to the law restricting women's employment in specified work. Section 4 commits disputes about whether work is of the same or a similar nature to an authority notified by the appropriate Government. The substance is that of the statute this Code repeals, read closely in [Equal Remuneration Act 1976 explained](/2016/02/01/equal-remuneration-act-1976-explained/), and the recast changes the protected ground rather than the protected act: where the 1976 Act framed the duty as one of equal pay for men and women, the Code frames it as a bar on discrimination on the ground of gender, a formulation whose coverage extends beyond the binary the older statute assumed. The change is characteristic of the Code's method. The operative rule is carried forward almost intact, and the improvement, where there is one, lies in a wider definition wrapped around familiar machinery. The enforcement record of the equal pay rule across five decades was thin under the old Act, and nothing in the recast alters the machinery that produced that record; the honest statement is that the Code widens the rule's reach on paper and leaves its realisation to the same inspectorate and claims route as everything else in the statute.

The comparator difficulty that limited the old Act survives the recast, and an honest account of Section 3 should name it. A claim of unequal pay requires work of the same or a similar nature against which the claimant's packet can be measured, and workplaces in which men and women are sorted into different occupations present few clean comparisons, so the discrimination the section reaches most readily is the crudest kind, different rates for the same job in the same establishment. The Code's contribution at this point is partly in the second proviso to the wage definition, examined above, which prevents the comparison from being gamed by allowances, and partly in the dispute route in Section 4, which assigns the characterisation question, whether two bodies of work are of a similar nature, to a notified authority rather than leaving it wholly to litigation. The older jurisprudence on the phrase, which read similarity by the nature of the duties rather than by identity of designation, carries across the repeal as persuasive background. Section 3 should therefore be read as a consolidation of a half-century-old duty in a wider formulation, not as a new departure, and expectations of it should be set accordingly.

## The payment discipline of Chapter III

Chapter III gathers the payment rules that the Payment of Wages Act had carried since 1936 and strips away that Act's wage ceiling, so the discipline binds for employees generally. Section 15 permits payment in current coin or currency notes, by cheque, by crediting the bank account, or by electronic mode, completing the movement away from cash that the old Act had begun by amendment. Section 16 provides that the employer shall fix the wage period, which shall not exceed one month. Section 17 sets the deadlines: where the wage period is daily, payment at the end of the shift; weekly, on the last working day of the week; fortnightly, before the end of the second day after the fortnight ends; monthly, before the expiry of the seventh day of the succeeding month; and where employment is terminated, the wages earned shall be paid before the expiry of the second working day. Deductions from wages are confined to an enumerated list in Section 18, fines, absence from duty, damage or loss attributable to the employee's neglect or default, house accommodation, recovery of advances and loans, income tax and payments ordered by a court among them, with the total deductions in a wage period capped at fifty per cent of the wages, and fines hedged by their own limits and by a requirement that they be credited to a welfare fund applied to purposes approved for the employees. The chapter is the least discussed part of the Code and, for the employee paid late or short as a matter of routine, the most immediately useful, because its duties are unconditional: they do not depend on a notification fixing a rate, and a breach is visible on the face of the payslip. The evidentiary base for those duties sits in Section 50, on records, returns and notices: every employer must maintain a register of the persons employed, with muster roll and wage particulars, must display at a prominent place in the establishment a notice carrying an abstract of the Code, the category-wise wage rates, the wage period, the day and time of payment, and the name and address of the Inspector-cum-Facilitator having jurisdiction, and must issue wage slips to employees. A small exemption releases employers of not more than five persons engaged for agriculture or domestic purposes from those duties, leaving them bound to produce reasonable proof of payment when an Inspector-cum-Facilitator demands it. The design is the old one, that a statute paid in cash to workers with little paper needs the paper created by law, and the display duty in particular converts the workplace notice board into the cheapest enforcement instrument in the Code.

## The bonus chapter and its inherited architecture

Chapter IV of the Code, Sections 26 to 41, carries the Payment of Bonus Act into the consolidation with its architecture substantially intact and one change of expression. The chapter applies to establishments employing twenty or more employees, as the 1965 Act did. Section 26 makes an employee who draws wages up to an amount per mensem notified by the appropriate Government, and who has worked for not less than thirty days in an accounting year, entitled to an annual minimum bonus of eight and one-third per cent of the wages earned, or one hundred rupees, whichever is higher, and this minimum is payable whether or not the employer has an allocable surplus in that year; where the employee's wages exceed the notified ceiling, the bonus is computed as if the wages were the notified ceiling or the minimum wage, whichever is higher. Where there is allocable surplus, Section 26(3) sets the maximum bonus at twenty per cent of the wages earned, and the proportions between minimum and maximum continue to be worked through the set on and set off mechanism the old Act devised. Section 29 preserves the disqualifications for dismissal for fraud, riotous or violent conduct, theft, misappropriation or sabotage, and adds a ground the 1965 Act did not contain, conviction for sexual harassment. The chapter's thresholds are expressed through notification rather than frozen in the text, the eligibility ceiling being an amount the appropriate Government notifies, which gives the provision an adjustability the old Act achieved only by amendment. What the chapter does not do is alter the profit-sharing philosophy beneath it: bonus under the Code remains a deferred share in surplus above a guaranteed statutory minimum, exactly the compromise the 1965 Act struck, and disputes about allocable surplus remain what Section 46 declares them to be, industrial disputes.

The set on and set off machinery, which the chapter carries over, is the device that reconciles a guaranteed minimum with a profit ceiling across years of unequal fortune. Where the allocable surplus in an accounting year exceeds what a twenty per cent bonus would absorb, the excess is set on, carried forward to succeeding accounting years up to a statutory limit of the surplus, and brought into account in a year when surplus falls short; where the surplus is deficient, the amount needed to fund the minimum bonus is set off against the surpluses of better years on the same terms. Chapter VI adds the audit provisions that keep the surplus honest: the balance sheet and profit and loss account of a corporation or company, duly audited, are presumed accurate for the chapter's purposes unless the authority or a Tribunal orders clarification at the instance of a trade union or employees, and employers who are not corporations or companies must have their accounts audited as the chapter requires, with the authority empowered to have an audit done at the employer's expense where the employer fails. The machinery is unglamorous and entirely inherited, and its retention tells the reader something the consolidation's critics on both sides missed: within the bonus chapter, Parliament did not think the 1965 settlement broken, and amended it at the points of threshold, disqualification and nomenclature rather than of principle.

For small establishments the Code's universality stops at this chapter's door, and the boundary is worth noticing because it is deliberate. The minimum wage duty and the payment discipline reach every employee; the bonus chapter reaches establishments of twenty or more. The consolidation therefore did not flatten every threshold in the predecessor statutes, and accounts of the Code that describe it as applying uniformly to all workplaces in all respects overstate the design. What was universalised was the floor under wages and the rules for paying them; profit-sharing kept its size gate, as it had since 1965.

## Claims, enforcement and the Inspector-cum-Facilitator

The enforcement scheme of Chapter VI and Chapter VII decides whether the rights in the earlier chapters are receipts or remedies. Section 45 entrusts claims arising under the Code, for non-payment of wages, short payment of minimum wages, unpaid bonus and unauthorised deductions, to an authority appointed by the appropriate Government, not below the rank of a Gazetted Officer. The claim may be filed by the employee, by a registered trade union of which the employee is a member, or by the Inspector-cum-Facilitator, and a single application may cover any number of employees, which turns the claims route from an individual ordeal into a usable collective instrument. The authority may direct payment of the amount due together with compensation not exceeding ten times that amount, is charged to endeavour to decide within three months, and unpaid amounts are recovered as arrears of land revenue on the authority's certificate, so a successful claim comes with the revenue machinery behind it. An appeal lies within ninety days under Section 49. The single most worker-facing change in the chapter is its limitation period: three years from the date the claim arises, with power to condone delay for sufficient cause, against six months under the Minimum Wages Act and the shorter window of the Payment of Wages Act. Six months had been the quiet repealer of minimum wage rights for seventy years; a worker paid short in a distant district typically learned of the claim, found advice and reached an authority on a slower clock than that. Three years does not solve the information problem, but it removes the statute's contribution to it.

The comparison with the claims machinery the Code replaced shows the upgrade precisely. Under Section 20 of the Minimum Wages Act, the authority could direct payment of the shortfall on a minimum wage claim together with compensation not exceeding ten times the amount of the excess, a power with real teeth; but the same section capped compensation on claims of other kinds at ten rupees, a figure that had been nominal since 1948 and that reduced the claims route, for deductions and delayed payment, to recovery of the bare amount after the trouble of claiming it. The Code generalises the ten-times power across the claims it entrusts to the authority, so that unauthorised deductions and unpaid bonus stand under the same multiplication as short-paid minimum wages, and it replaces the six-month window that governed the old claims with the three-year period already noticed. The appellate tier is built to the same pattern: the appeal under Section 49 goes to an appellate authority appointed from officers at least one rank above the claims authority, must be filed within ninety days with a power to condone delay for sufficient cause, and carries its own direction that the appeal be disposed of, so far as endeavour can secure it, within three months, with outstanding dues then recovered through the same certificate machinery as the original order. The scheme's coherence is its argument: claim, compensation, appeal and recovery are one administrative ladder, climbed without a lawyer's necessity at any rung, and the civil courts are barred from the parallel route, as noted below. The ladder's weakness is the one administrative ladders in India habitually have, that the authorities are officers of the same governments whose fixation and inspection machinery the claims will often embarrass, and the statute's answer to that structural discomfort is the multiplicity of the remedy itself: where the authority will not move, the prosecution chapter and the appellate tier remain, and the burden of proof, as the next paragraphs show, does not rest on the claimant.

Section 51 recasts the inspector as the Inspector-cum-Facilitator. The appropriate Government appoints such officers and assigns their jurisdiction; the inspection scheme under which they work may provide for web-based inspection and for the selection of establishments to be generated through randomised methods, a design aimed at the discretion and predictability that made the old inspector regime a byword; and the office carries an advisory function alongside the enforcing one, the statute directing the officer to advise employers and employees on compliance. The same chapter tempers prosecution for the lesser contraventions: for a first offence of those kinds, the employer is to be given an opportunity to comply within a specified period before any prosecution is launched, an opportunity denied where a contravention of the same nature is repeated within five years, and the underpayment offence itself stands outside that indulgence. The penalty scale is in Section 54. Paying an employee less than the amount due attracts a fine of up to fifty thousand rupees on a first offence; a repetition of the same offence within five years attracts imprisonment of up to three months, or a fine of up to one lakh of rupees, or both. Other contraventions attract a fine of up to twenty thousand rupees. Offences are compoundable by a gazetted officer notified for the purpose, except those punishable with imprisonment alone. Set against the predecessor penalties, the scale is a large nominal rise and a small practical one. The Minimum Wages Act punished its contraventions under Section 22 with imprisonment extending to six months or a fine extending to five hundred rupees, a ceiling that belonged to the price level of 1948 and had become, long before repeal, a figure of embarrassment rather than of deterrence. The Code's fifty thousand rupees for a first underpayment and one lakh with imprisonment on repetition move the numbers into the same century as the payrolls they police, yet for any establishment of size they remain smaller than the saving a single month of underpayment across a workforce can produce, which is why the section's compensation power, ten times the amount due at the instance of the workers themselves, is the enforcement provision with economic weight. The design choice is legible and defensible: the Code makes underpayment expensive through restitution multiplied, administered by an authority the worker can reach, and reserves the criminal scale for repetition, rather than multiplying fines that the old system had shown it would rarely collect.

Two judgements follow from this scheme. The first is that the Code raises the ceiling of what a claim can yield, ten times compensation and a three-year window, while keeping monetary penalties modest by any comparison with the sums an audited payroll can misdirect; deterrence rests on the compensation, not on the fine. The second is that the advisory turn in enforcement, facilitation first and prosecution after an opportunity to comply, is a wager that most non-compliance is structural rather than wilful. The wager fits the definition chapter, which expects payrolls to be rebuilt, better than it fits the employer who simply pays below the notified rate, and the statute's own answer is the escalation built into the second offence. This article states the law as enacted; it is general information on a statute, not legal advice for any particular employment.

The Inspector-cum-Facilitator's powers, stated in Section 51, follow the established pattern of Indian labour inspection with the facilitation layer laid over it. The officer may enter an establishment, inspect persons, documents and registers, examine the employer and employees, and require the production of the records the Code obliges the employer to keep, and the inspection scheme may assign inspections through web-based and randomised methods so that the officer's itinerary is generated rather than chosen, a reform aimed at the visit that was negotiated in advance and at the discretion that made the old inspector a figure of grievance for employer and worker alike, the worker because the visit could be managed, the employer because it could be threatened. The advisory duty is written into the same appointment: the officer is to supply information and advice to employers and employees on the most effective means of complying with the Code. Critics of the design, in Parliament and outside it, read the renaming as the softening of enforcement into consultancy, and pointed to the opportunity-to-comply procedure as the operative proof. Supporters answered that an inspectorate whose only tool was prosecution had produced neither compliance nor credible prosecution under the old Acts, and that a scheme that reserves prosecution for the employer who will not comply on notice concentrates the sanction where it deters. The Code does not resolve that argument; it equips both sides of it. What it removes is the worker's alternative forum shopping between the inspector's visit and a suit, for the Code bars the civil courts from entertaining suits for the recovery of minimum wages, deductions, discrimination in wages and bonus in so far as the sum claimed forms the subject of the claims chapter or could have been recovered under it, funnelling the money claims into the authority under Section 45. The funnel is only as good as the authority, a point the claims chapter's own timelines, three months aimed at for decision, nine months not contemplated, implicitly concede.

Three further provisions decide how the claims scheme bears on the parties in practice. Section 43 fastens responsibility for payment of dues on the employer, and, where the employer is a company or other body, pursues the failure to those who stand behind it in the manner the section prescribes, so that incorporation does not strand a wage claim. Section 44 provides for dues that remain undisbursed, including where an employee has died: the amount is paid to the person nominated by the employee, and where there is no nominee it is deposited with the authority the Code prescribes, to be dealt with under the rules. Section 59 then allocates the burden of proof in the claims themselves: where a claim is filed for non-payment or short payment of remuneration, wages or bonus, or for deductions not authorised by the Code, the burden of proving that the dues were paid lies on the employer. The allocation follows the paper. The registers, wage slips and bank records sit in the employer's custody, and the old system's hardest practical question, how a worker paid in cash proves a negative, is answered by making the employer's records the evidence. Around these provisions stand two general guards. Section 60 voids any contract or agreement by which an employee relinquishes a right to an amount or to bonus due under the Code, in so far as it purports to remove or reduce the liability to pay, which is the statutory descendant of the rule the courts had long applied to consent pleas under the old Act: an agreement to accept less than the minimum was no answer to a claim. Section 61 gives the Code overriding effect over anything inconsistent in any other law, award, agreement, settlement or contract of service. Read together, the three sections and the two guards describe the Code's theory of enforcement, which is realistic about where proof and power sit: the claim is collective, the window is long, the compensation is a multiple, the proof is the employer's to produce, and the exit routes by contract are closed.

## Savings, repeal and the six years between assent and force

Section 69 of the Code repeals the four predecessor statutes and provides that actions taken under them, notifications issued, appointments made, wages fixed and amounts payable, are deemed to have been taken under the corresponding provisions of the Code in so far as they are not inconsistent with it, the standard saving by which a replacement statute avoids restarting the administrative world. The repeal was not, however, an event of 2019. Section 1(3) left commencement to notification, and the Code waited with the other three codes until the four were brought into force together on 21 November 2025, from which date the twenty-nine subsumed statutes of the labour code project stood repealed and the savings provisions took hold; the Central Government has notified the Central Rules under the codes, on 8 May 2026, while state rules, which labour law cannot operate without, remained incomplete long after commencement in much of the country. The long interval between enactment and operation, and the unusual position of a legal system running repealed statutes on a long fuse, is the subject of the companion study of [the labour codes implementation delay](/2013/09/15/labour-codes-implementation-delay/), and is noticed here only because commencement is part of this Code's record: the text Parliament passed in a week in 2019 governed, in the event, from the winter of 2025, and every statement in this article about what the Code does is a statement about the law in force from that date.

The interval had a legislative texture of its own, which the savings question partly reflects. A code that has been enacted but not commenced occupies an unusual position: its provisions are law in the statute book and not law in the workplace, and the repeals it carries are promises rather than events. The Central Government published draft central rules under the Code for public consultation during the interval, and state governments prepared draft rules of their own at uneven speeds, so that a body of subordinate text accumulated around a statute whose operative date kept receding; employers modelled their structures against drafts, unions campaigned against a commencement that had not happened, and the old Acts continued to govern every actual pay packet, their repeal already printed in a code anyone could read. Commencement, when it came on 21 November 2025, therefore did not introduce an unknown statute. It activated a familiar one, and the savings provisions met a legal system that had been rehearsing the transition for years, which is one reason the continuity questions the preceding paragraphs describe have practical rather than theoretical answers in most workplaces: the rates, appointments and proceedings that carry across under Section 69 had been administered under the old Acts in full view of the text that would deem them its own.

Within the savings scheme, the practical questions are three, and the Code's text answers each in the same register. Wage rates notified under the Minimum Wages Act continue as rates under the corresponding provisions until revised, because the saving deems fixation under the old Act to be fixation under the Code; an employer cannot argue that commencement dissolved the rates. Appointments of inspectors and authorities carry across on corresponding lines. Proceedings and claims in being at commencement continue under the savings, with the Code's longer limitation available for claims arising under it. The drafting choice throughout is continuity by deeming, and its cost is the one deeming always carries: where the old and new provisions do not correspond exactly, and the definitions chapter is the conspicuous place where they do not, the question whether a carried-over computation survives in its old form or must be remade on the new base is left to be fought over, with the not-inconsistent qualification as the battlefield.

The correspondence qualification carries the real difficulty of the transition, and it is located exactly where this article has located the reform. A fixation notification under the old Act corresponds to a fixation under the Code, and an inspector's appointment corresponds to an Inspector-cum-Facilitator's, because the institutions match. A computation does not correspond in the same way. Provident fund contributions made and credited on the old base are closed transactions and the saving protects them; a gratuity liability computed on the wage as the old statutes understood it at the date of termination is likewise a closed computation. But obligations that straddle commencement, the bonus for an accounting year that began under the repealed Act and closes under the Code, the claim filed after commencement for short payment before it, the fixation revision due in the first cycle after commencement, each requires the decision-maker to say whether the corresponding provision is found and whether the old treatment is inconsistent with the new, and the answers will not all run the same way, because inconsistency in the definition chapter means something different from inconsistency in a penalty. The courts will work through these cases with the general savings law of the General Clauses Act behind Section 69, which preserves rights accrued and liabilities incurred under a repealed enactment unless a contrary intention appears. The contrary intention question, whether the Code's new base was meant to recompute entitlements that accrued under the old one, is the transition's deepest issue, and the text's silence on it, beyond the deeming formula, is the silence the first litigation under the Code will fill.

### What happens to claims and proceedings begun under the repealed Acts?

The repeal in Section 69 is coupled with a saving that deems things done under the repealed Acts to have been done under the corresponding provisions of the Code, so far as they are not inconsistent with the Code itself.

Notifications fixing wages, appointments of authorities and inspectors, and pending proceedings therefore carry across the commencement line rather than lapsing with the statutes that created them, and a claim properly in being under the old law is not defeated by the repeal itself.

## The shape of the statute

For the reader navigating the Code for the first time, its architecture is worth a map. The Code runs to sixty-nine sections in nine chapters. Chapter I, Preliminary, holds the short title and commencement, the definitions in Section 2, the non-discrimination rule in Section 3 and the decision of same-work disputes in Section 4. Chapter II, Minimum Wages, Sections 5 to 14, holds the payment duty, fixation, components, procedure, the floor wage, hours and overtime. Chapter III, Payment of Wages, Sections 15 to 25, holds the mode and timing of payment, the deductions and the fines chapters of the old payment law recast as one. Chapter IV, Payment of Bonus, Sections 26 to 41, is the longest single subject block, reflecting the accounting machinery it carries. Chapter V is the single Section 42 on advisory boards. Chapter VI, Sections 43 to 50, gathers the payment of dues, the claims machinery, the audit provisions, the appeal and the records duties. Chapter VII is the Inspector-cum-Facilitator in Section 51. Chapter VIII, Offences and Penalties, carries the penalty scale and compounding, and Chapter IX, Miscellaneous, closes the Code with the burden of proof, the bar on contracting out, the overriding effect, the rule-making powers and, in Section 69, the repeal and savings. The architecture itself makes an argument about what a wages statute is: the chapters on getting the wage fixed and defined come first and are short, the chapters on paying it and accounting for it are long, and the enforcement chapters are last and dependent. A consolidation's table of contents is rarely read as a text, but this one rewards it, because the proportions disclose the design's centre of gravity more honestly than the preamble debates did: brief principles, elaborate machinery, and a definition placed in the preliminary chapter, where definitions sit, doing the work of a charging provision without ever being mistaken for one.

## The case law the Code inherits

Replacement statutes inherit their predecessors' jurisprudence where the new text carries the old obligation forward, and the wage obligation is the Code's clearest inheritance. In Crown Aluminium Works v Their Workmen, in 1958, the Supreme Court had treated the duty to pay a minimum wage as an obligation that does not yield to an employer's plea of incapacity: an industry unable to pay the minimum wage has no right to exist, a proposition the Court repeated across the fixation case law of the period. In People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India, in 1982, the Court held that paying less than the statutory minimum wage amounts to forced labour within Article 23 of the Constitution, and Sanjit Roy v State of Rajasthan, in 1983, applied the same Article to strike down a statutory exemption that had let a famine relief statute pay below the minimum. Those holdings concern the obligation, not the schedule, and they transfer to the Code undiminished: a rate notified under Section 6 carries Article 23 behind it, and the universality of the Code's coverage widens the field over which the forced labour proposition runs, since the class of work standing outside any statutory minimum has been abolished along with the schedule. What does not transfer automatically is the fixation jurisprudence built on the schedule's edges, the classification disputes of the old Act, which lose their subject matter; and the needs-based norms developed in the tribunal case law, from the Fair Wages Committee's floors to the consumption units of the fifteenth Indian Labour Conference, survive as the interpretive background against which floor wage notifications will be judged rather than as rules the Code enacted. The comparison between the codes' settlement and the older dispensation as a whole is drawn in [labour codes versus the old laws](/2013/09/01/labour-codes-vs-old-laws-comparison/); the point for this article is narrower, that on the question the courts treated as constitutional, the Code changed the reach of the right and not its nature.

One distinction from the older jurisprudence will matter in the Code's fixation disputes and is worth isolating. The absoluteness of Crown Aluminium Works attached to the minimum wage, the floor of the Fair Wages Committee's ladder; at the level of the fair wage above it, the tribunals treated the employer's capacity to pay as a legitimate consideration, so that the same Court that would not hear incapacity pleaded against the floor heard it readily on the size of an increment. The Code legislates the floor and leaves the territory above it to collective bargaining and adjudication, in which the older distinction continues to do its work. Challenges to notified rates under the Code will accordingly be fought on the fixation considerations the Code itself names, skill, area, arduousness and the cost of living component, and on the procedure of committee and advice, rather than on any statutory formula the court could apply in the government's place. The needs-based norms enter that litigation as they entered the old, as the background of reasonableness against which a rate is defended or attacked. The Code's courts, in short, inherit a jurisprudence that is strict about the existence of the floor, deferential about its level, and unchanged by the repeal in either respect; what the repeal changed is the class of employments over which the strictness runs.

## An honest assessment of the replacement

Measured against its predecessors, the Code on Wages is an improvement of a specific and limited kind: it removes defects that were internal to the old design, and it leaves standing the conditions that limited every one of the old statutes from outside. The internal defects were real. A minimum wage that depended on a schedule was a coverage lottery. Four divergent definitions of wages were an invitation to structure around the law. A six-month limitation period was a denial mechanism dressed as a procedure rule. A payment statute with a wage ceiling abandoned precisely the employees whose employers were most sophisticated. The Code cures all four, and adds a claims route with collective filing, a compensation ceiling of ten times the amount due, an anti-discrimination rule framed on gender rather than on men and women, and a floor under state fixation. A replacement that fixes the internal defects of its predecessors and invents no new ones has done the work replacement statutes are for, and the verdict of this series is that the Code on Wages clears that bar.

The limits are equally identifiable, and each sits where the Code meets a world it did not remake. Enforcement capacity is the first. Universality multiplies the population holding the right faster than it multiplies the officers charged with realising it, and the Code's answer, randomised web-based inspection and an advisory inspectorate, is directed at the integrity of inspections rather than at their number. A right held by every worker in the country, policed by an inspectorate sized for a scheduled fraction of them, is a right whose value will be set in practice by the claims authority and the trade union filing the collective application, not by inspection. The second limit is the Code's dependence on subordinate legislation and notification for its operative numbers: the floor wage figure, the bonus eligibility ceiling, the percentage in the add-back proviso, and the detailed rules on forms and registers all sit below the statute, so the Code as enacted is a frame whose daily content arrives by instrument, and assessments of its impact made from the text alone are assessments of a design. The third limit is definitional litigation deferred rather than avoided: the proviso is mechanical, and mechanical rules at the boundary, the treatment of variable pay, of remuneration in kind above the fifteen per cent fence, of components that fit no exclusion head, will generate the Code's first decade of disputes, as the schedule generated the old Act's. None of this is an argument that the old law was better. It is an argument that the Code should be credited with the reform it in fact made, the definition and the coverage, and not with the transformation of wage payment practice that only capacity and notifications can deliver.

For industry, the Code has been, in practice, a payroll event more than a legal one. The passage from enactment to commencement was filled with structure modelling, and payroll teams and compensation consultants circulated the add-back workings as slide decks long before any rule required a payslip to change; readers working through the same transition material can open the workings in a [PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and follow the computation line by line. The structural question those decks worked on, whether existing salary structures had to change and by when, has a two-part answer that is often given as one. The definition binds by its own force for the Code's computations from commencement, and a structure that fails the fifty per cent test is not made compliant by being left alone, because the deeming operates whether or not the contract is redrawn. But the provident fund and gratuity consequences of the enlarged base run through the rules and notifications of the social security framework, the terrain of [the Social Security Code 2020](/2013/08/15/social-security-code-2020-explained/) and its wage-linked contributions, so the date on which a given employer's contributions rise in practice depends on that framework's instruments as much as on this Code's text. Employers who restructured early did so for certainty; employers who waited were not, for most of the interval, in breach of a rule that had commenced. Treating either course as the only lawful one misstates the transition.

For the student and the aspirant, the Code repays study at exactly the points where this article has lingered. The examinable propositions are few and sharp: universality in place of the schedule; a single definition of wages with an exclusion list, a fifteen per cent fence on payment in kind, and an add-back above fifty per cent; a floor wage that constrains states and is not a national minimum wage; a claims window of three years with compensation up to ten times the amount due; an inspectorate renamed and partly redesigned; and a bonus chapter that keeps the 1965 compromise, twenty or more employees, eight and one-third per cent at the minimum, twenty per cent at the maximum, with a new disqualification for conviction for sexual harassment. Each of those propositions is a place where the Code departed from a predecessor or consciously retained one, and the retention points, the committee and notification methods of fixation, the five-year revision ceiling, the allocable surplus machinery, are as examinable as the departures.

The position of the small establishment in the coverage debate deserves correction, because the discourse around the codes persistently misplaced it. Debate about consolidation was dominated by organised industry and its compliance systems, and small trades figured in it mostly as an objection, the claim being that universal duties would crush the unit too small to bear them. Whatever the force of that claim about compliance cost, its premise about coverage was wrong in law. Universality under the Code reaches the small establishment for the minimum wage and the payment discipline exactly as it reaches the large one, and the old schedule system had in practice reached much small employment as well, trade by scheduled trade. The genuinely new burden for the small unit is not the existence of a floor but the definitional one, where a proprietor paying a handful of employees in unstructured cash-and-kind arrangements meets a statutory base, a register duty moderated only at the smallest domestic scale, and a claims authority whose compensation can multiply a short payment tenfold. The Code made no small-employer carve-out from the wage floor, and that absence is of a piece with its central conviction, inherited from Crown Aluminium Works, that a wage below the minimum is not made acceptable by the smallness of the enterprise paying it. If the conviction imposes costs, the statute's answer is that they are the costs of employing labour at all, and the debate about them belongs to the level of the floor, where the advisory boards argue it, and not to the existence of the duty.

The Code's relation to bargained wages deserves a sentence in any assessment, because the minimum wage in India has never been only a protection for the unorganised. In the organised sector, settlements and awards have historically been struck by reference to the statutory floor and its tribunal elaborations, the needs-based norms supplying the argument's grammar even where the outcome stood well above any notified rate, and revision of the floor moved the base from which bargaining began. A universal floor, applicable without the threshold argument about whether an employment is scheduled, strengthens that reference function for the organised workforce at the same time as it creates the entitlement for the unorganised, and the floor wage's level will be watched by negotiators for the same reason it is watched by state governments: as the one number in the system that is national in reach. None of that makes the floor a bargained wage or the Code an incomes policy. It locates the Code's influence correctly, at the base of a structure whose upper storeys are built by negotiation, adjudication and the market, and whose base the statute underwrites. A reform of the base is not a small thing merely because the building above it is not the statute's work.

A replacement statute is also defined by what it leaves alone, and the Code's silences are part of its settlement. It did not enact a living wage, although Article 43 of the Constitution names one as an endeavour; the living wage remains a directive above the statutory minimum, invoked in argument and not payable on a claim. It did not disturb the appropriate government map or the Concurrent List distribution that produced the state-by-state pattern of rates, preferring the floor wage's boundary to any centralisation of fixation. It did not convert the bonus chapter into a general profit-sharing entitlement for all establishments, keeping the twenty-employee gate of 1965. It did not attempt to reach the genuinely self-employed or the platform workforce through its wage chapters, leaving those arrangements to the scheme-based architecture of the social security code. And it did not index the floor wage or prescribe the basket from which a floor could be computed, although a half-century of committee norms lay ready to hand. Each silence was a choice for enactability and for continuity with the federal and industrial settlement the four old statutes had embodied. The Code is therefore best characterised as a statute that universalised a floor and unified a definition while conserving almost everything else about how Indian wages are fixed, argued and enforced. That is a narrower achievement than the consolidation's publicity suggested, and a more durable one than its critics allowed.

The closing verdict follows the One Test with which this article opened. The Code on Wages did two different things, and they deserve different grades. As consolidation, it is competent and overdue: four statutes, four definitions and four partial coverages became one statute, one definition and one canvas, and the claims and payment chapters are better law than the fragments they replaced. As reform, its centre of gravity is a definition. The universal minimum wage changes the status of the uncovered worker; the definition of wages changes the cost of employing every covered one, reaching into provident fund accumulations, gratuity accruals and bonus computations in organisations that had complied with every predecessor statute while structuring around each of them in turn. The definition is the reform, and the consolidation is its vehicle. What the Code could not do by text, supply inspectors, fix the floor's number, or end definitional argument at the boundary, remains undone in the manner this assessment has stated. A statute that abolished the schedule and capped the exclusions has nonetheless earned its place in the series: it is the rare replacement that can be summarised in one sentence, and the sentence is about fifty per cent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws did the Code on Wages replace?

The Code on Wages, 2019 repealed four statutes and took their subjects into itself: the Payment of Wages Act, 1936, which had regulated the timing and manner of wage payment and the permissible deductions; the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, which had empowered governments to fix minimum rates for scheduled employments; the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965, which had governed the annual profit-linked bonus; and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976, which had required equal pay for men and women. The repeal is in Section 69 of the Code, which couples it with a saving that deems actions, notifications, appointments and fixations under the repealed Acts to have been made under the corresponding provisions of the Code, so far as they are not inconsistent with it. The four Acts ceased to operate when the Code was brought into force with the other labour codes on 21 November 2025.

### Q: Does the Code on Wages give every worker a minimum wage?

Yes, as a matter of legal design, and that is the Code's first great departure from the law it replaced. Section 5 forbids an employer to pay any employee less than the minimum rate notified by the appropriate Government, and Section 6 obliges the appropriate Government to fix minimum rates for employees generally. Nothing confines the duty to listed occupations: the Schedule of the Minimum Wages Act, 1948, under which protection waited on an employment being entered in a list, was abolished. The entitlement a worker can enforce remains the rate notified for the class of work in the area, so a rate must have been fixed for the work in question, and the Central Government's floor wage under Section 9 sets the level below which no state fixation may fall. Universality is therefore a rule about coverage, not a promise that a particular sum is payable everywhere.

### Q: What is the uniform definition of wages and why does it matter?

The definition is in Section 2(y), and the same words run through all four labour codes. Wages means all remuneration in money terms payable for employment or work done, and it includes basic pay, dearness allowance and retaining allowance. Eleven heads are then excluded, including house rent allowance, conveyance allowance, overtime allowance, commission, the employer's provident fund contribution, gratuity and retrenchment compensation. The controlling feature is the first proviso: if the payments under the exclusion heads from (a) to (i) exceed one-half of total remuneration, the excess is deemed to be wages and added back. The definition matters because provident fund, gratuity, bonus and every other wage-linked computation under the codes is made on this base, so the proviso decides the real size of the statutory components of a salary, whatever the payslip's labels say.

### Q: How does the wages definition affect salary structure and provident fund cost?

It works through the add-back proviso in Section 2(y). Salary structures built on a small basic pay carried large allowances precisely because provident fund and gratuity were computed on the basic component; a basic of thirty per cent of the package kept those computations at thirty per cent. Under the Code, excluded components beyond half of total remuneration are deemed to be wages, so the statutory base cannot be designed below half the package. Where an employer contributes provident fund on the full wage base, contributions rise in step with the enlarged base, the employee's own contribution rises with them, and gratuity accrues on the higher figure as well. Employers that contribute only up to the provident fund statute's wage ceiling feel a much smaller effect, because the ceiling rather than the definition limits them. Structures already at or above half are untouched.

### Q: Who fixes the floor wage?

The Central Government fixes the floor wage, under Section 9 of the Code. It does so after consultation with the Central Advisory Board constituted under Section 42, taking into account the minimum living standards of workers, and it may fix different floor wages for different geographical areas. The floor wage is then a constraint on the appropriate Governments, Central and state: no government may notify minimum rates below the floor wage for the area, and a rate that already stands higher than the floor when the floor is fixed may not be reduced on that account. The statute prescribes no formula, basket of needs or index for arriving at the figure, so the level of the floor in any period is a fact about the Central Government's notification, not a number the Code itself supplies.

### Q: Does the Code on Wages cover bonus?

Yes. Chapter IV of the Code, Sections 26 to 41, carries the subject of the repealed Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 into the Code substantially in its old form. The chapter applies to establishments employing twenty or more employees. An employee who has worked for at least thirty days in an accounting year and whose wages fall within the eligibility ceiling notified by the appropriate Government is entitled to a minimum bonus of eight and one-third per cent of the wages earned, or one hundred rupees, whichever is higher, payable whether or not the establishment has an allocable surplus. Where there is allocable surplus, the bonus rises toward a maximum of twenty per cent of wages, worked through the set on and set off machinery the old Act devised. Disputes about bonus are treated as industrial disputes under Section 46.

### Q: How long do you have to claim unpaid wages under the Code on Wages?

Three years. Section 45(6) of the Code fixes the limitation period for a claim before the authority at three years from the date on which the claim arises, and the authority may entertain a later application if satisfied that the applicant had sufficient cause for the delay. The claim may be filed by the employee, by a registered trade union of which the employee is a member, or by the Inspector-cum-Facilitator, and it covers non-payment of wages, payment below the minimum rate, unpaid bonus and unauthorised deductions. The three-year period is one of the Code's most practical improvements: the Minimum Wages Act allowed six months for a claim, and the Payment of Wages Act one year, periods that defeated workers who learned of the right slowly or had to organise before they could file.

### Q: What is an Inspector-cum-Facilitator under the labour codes?

An Inspector-cum-Facilitator is the enforcement officer of the Code on Wages, appointed by the appropriate Government under Section 51, and the office has counterparts across the labour codes. The officer inspects establishments, examines records, registers and persons, and enforces the Code's provisions on wages, payment and bonus. Two features distinguish the office from the inspector of the repealed Acts. The first is method: the inspection scheme under which the officer works may be web-based, with the selection of establishments generated through randomised methods rather than chosen by the officer, a design aimed at the predictability and discretion of the old inspection regime. The second is function: the statute charges the officer with advising employers and employees on compliance alongside enforcement, and for a first contravention of the lesser kinds the employer is given an opportunity to comply before prosecution follows.

### Q: Does the Code on Wages require basic pay to be fifty per cent of total pay?

No. The Code does not prescribe any salary structure, and an employer remains free to divide a package between basic pay, dearness allowance and allowances in any proportions. What Section 2(y) does is different: it caps the exclusions. Payments under clauses (a) to (i) of the definition, the allowance and benefit heads, are disregarded as wages only up to one-half of total remuneration; beyond that half, the excess is deemed to be remuneration and added back to wages for statutory purposes. A structure with basic pay at thirty per cent of the package is therefore lawful as a contract, but the law computes provident fund, gratuity and bonus as if the wage base were fifty per cent. The fifty per cent figure is a floor under the statutory base, not a design rule for the payslip, and the proportion itself can be altered by Central Government notification.

### Q: Will the Code on Wages reduce take-home salary?

For some employees it will, and for others it will not change at all; the answer follows the structure, not the statute book. Where provident fund is contributed on the full statutory wage base, the add-back proviso enlarges that base for any structure whose basic pay and dearness allowance stood below half the package, and the employee's own contribution, deducted from the monthly packet, rises in step. If total cost to the employer is held fixed, the monthly cash in hand falls by that rise, while the provident fund accumulation and the gratuity entitlement grow by the same redesign. Where the employer contributes provident fund only up to that statute's wage ceiling, the definition change barely reaches the payslip. Structures already built at or above the halfway line see no change in either direction. The Code redistributes pay across time for the affected structures; it does not reduce the package itself.

### Q: Which salary components are excluded from wages under the Code on Wages?

Section 2(y) lists eleven exclusion heads. They are: any bonus payable under any law that does not form part of remuneration under the terms of employment; the value of house accommodation and of light, water, medical attendance or any other amenity or excluded service; the employer's contribution to a pension or provident fund and interest on it; conveyance allowance or the value of a travelling concession; any sum paid to defray special expenses entailed by the nature of the employment; house rent allowance; remuneration payable under an award, a settlement or an order of a court or Tribunal; overtime allowance; commission payable to the employee; gratuity payable on termination; and retrenchment compensation, any other retirement benefit and any ex gratia payment on termination. Only the first nine heads, clauses (a) to (i), enter the fifty per cent add-back test; the terminal payments in clauses (j) and (k) stand outside it.

### Q: How is bonus calculated under the Code on Wages?

The calculation keeps the machinery of the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965. Bonus for an accounting year is worked on the establishment's allocable surplus, the share of gross profits remaining after the deductions the chapter prescribes. Every eligible employee, one who has worked at least thirty days in the year and whose wages are within the ceiling notified by the appropriate Government, receives at least the minimum bonus of eight and one-third per cent of wages earned, or one hundred rupees, whichever is higher, and that minimum is payable even where there is no allocable surplus. Where surplus permits, bonus rises to a maximum of twenty per cent of wages earned. Where the employee's wages exceed the notified ceiling, the computation is made as if the wages were the ceiling or the minimum wage, whichever is higher. Surplus above the maximum in a good year is set on and carried forward, and a shortfall is set off, across accounting years on the chapter's terms, and bonus is payable within eight months of the close of the accounting year.

### Q: What is the difference between the floor wage and a minimum wage?

They bind different actors. A minimum wage is the rate fixed by the appropriate Government under Section 6 for classes of work in its sphere; it is payable by the employer to the employee, and the employee can claim it under Section 45 if it is not paid. The floor wage is fixed by the Central Government under Section 9, after consultation with the Central Advisory Board, by reference to minimum living standards, and it is addressed to governments rather than to employers: no appropriate Government may notify minimum rates below the floor wage for the area, and a higher existing rate is protected against reduction. An employee cannot claim the floor wage itself against an employer, because the floor wage is not the wage for any job; it is the lowest level to which state fixation is allowed to sink. Accounts that call the floor wage a national minimum wage confuse the constraint with the entitlement.

### Q: Does the Code on Wages change the date by which wages must be paid?

It restates the payment deadlines without the size distinction the old law carried. Section 16 requires the employer to fix a wage period not exceeding one month, and Section 17 sets the day by which wages for each period must be paid: for a daily wage period, at the end of the shift; for a weekly period, on the last working day of the week; for a fortnightly period, before the end of the second day after the period closes; and for a monthly period, before the expiry of the seventh day of the succeeding month. Where an employee's employment is terminated, the wages earned must be paid before the expiry of the second working day from the termination. The Payment of Wages Act, 1936 had allowed establishments employing a thousand or more persons until the tenth day for monthly wages; the Code applies the seventh day across the board, and it extends the discipline to employees generally rather than only to those below a wage ceiling.

### Q: What deductions may an employer make from wages under the Code on Wages?

Only the deductions Section 18 enumerates. They include fines; deductions for absence from duty; deductions for damage to or loss of goods expressly entrusted to the employee, or of money for which the employee is accountable, where the damage or loss is directly attributable to the employee's neglect or default; deductions for house accommodation and for amenities and services supplied and accepted; deductions for the recovery of advances and loans and the interest on them; income tax payable by the employee; deductions required by an order of a court or other competent authority; and contributions to funds such as the provident fund where the employee has authorised or the law requires them. The total amount of deductions in any wage period may not exceed fifty per cent of the employee's wages for that period. Fines are separately hedged: they may be imposed only for acts and omissions specified in a notice exhibited at the establishment, after the employee has been given an opportunity to show cause, and the amount realised must be applied to purposes beneficial to the employees.

### Q: How much overtime pay does the Code on Wages require?

Not less than twice the normal rate of wages. Section 14 provides that where an employee works for more than the normal working day fixed under Section 13, the employee is entitled, for the overtime work, to wages at a rate not less than twice the normal rate. The normal working day itself, the number of hours it contains, the interval for rest and the weekly day of rest are fixed by the appropriate Government under Section 13, so the threshold from which overtime runs is a notified matter, while the doubling is the Code's own floor and cannot be contracted below. The overtime allowance so earned is one of the heads excluded from the general definition of wages under Section 2(y), though the second proviso counts it back in when wages are computed for equal remuneration and for payment of wages purposes.

### Q: Can part of wages be paid in kind under the Code on Wages?

Only within a narrow fence. The explanation to Section 2(y) provides that where an employee is given remuneration partly in kind, the part in kind, so long as it does not exceed fifteen per cent of the total wages payable, is deemed to form part of the wages. The provision recognises the reality of arrangements in which accommodation, grain or other benefits form part of the packet, particularly in agricultural and plantation employment, and it values that part inside the wage for statutory purposes up to the fifteen per cent line. It is not a general licence to pay in goods: Section 15 requires wages to be paid in current coin or currency notes, or by cheque, by crediting the employee's bank account or by electronic mode, and the in-kind element operates as a limited component within a money wage, not as a substitute for one. The fence is the descendant of the truck system prohibitions that wage law was first written to enforce.

### Q: What is the penalty for paying less than the wages due under the Code on Wages?

Section 54 sets the scale. An employer who pays an employee less than the amount due under the Code is liable, on a first offence, to a fine extending to fifty thousand rupees. If the same offence is committed again within five years, the penalty rises to imprisonment extending to three months, or a fine extending to one lakh of rupees, or both. Other contraventions of the Code carry a fine extending to twenty thousand rupees, and offences are compoundable by a gazetted officer notified for the purpose, except those punishable with imprisonment alone. The penalty is separate from the employee's own remedy: on a claim under Section 45 the authority can direct payment of the amount due together with compensation up to ten times that amount, which for most establishments is the larger financial exposure, and it is the compensation, rather than the fine, that the Code relies on to make underpayment expensive.

### Q: Does the Code on Wages cover workers engaged through a contractor?

Yes. The Code's wage duties follow the engagement rather than the label on it. The definition of employer in Section 2 reaches a person who employs an employee directly or through another person, and includes a contractor among the persons it names, so a worker supplied through a contractor is inside the Code's scheme for minimum wages, payment discipline and claims. The payment chapters place responsibility for paying dues on the employer as so defined, and the claims chapter gives the worker the Section 45 route for unpaid or short-paid wages, with the burden of proving payment placed on the employer under Section 59. The design answers the oldest evasion in the piece-rate and seasonal trades, in which the visible engager denied being an employer and the principal denied engaging the worker at all: under the Code, routing the engagement through an intermediary routes the duty with it rather than dissolving it.

### Q: Can one claim application cover many employees under the Code on Wages?

Yes. Section 45 permits a single application to be filed in respect of any number of employees, so a workforce affected by the same short payment, the same unauthorised deduction or the same unpaid bonus can claim together before the authority in one proceeding rather than worker by worker. The application may be filed by the employees concerned, by a registered trade union of which they are members, or by the Inspector-cum-Facilitator. The authority hearing the claim may direct payment of the amounts due together with compensation not exceeding ten times the amount, is charged to endeavour to decide within three months, and can set recovery in motion as arrears of land revenue on its certificate where the direction is not obeyed. The collective application is one of the Code's deliberate strengths, because wage underpayment is rarely an individual event: it is usually a payroll practice, and the claims route is shaped to meet it at that scale.
