The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 is the statute that was supposed to end dowry, and its central feature explains why it did not. The Act does not merely punish the family that takes dowry. It punishes the family that gives it, with the same minimum sentence, in the same section, in the same words. A bride's parents who pay under pressure and then complain under this statute begin their complaint by describing their own offence, punishable with at least five years of imprisonment. Everything else about the statute, its careful definition, its officers, its lists of wedding presents, its reversed burden of proof, operates in the shadow of that single drafting decision. Enforcement of the law against dowry in India runs overwhelmingly through other provisions, above all the cruelty offence in the criminal code, and this Act survives in practice largely as the place where the word dowry is defined for the benefit of those other laws. That is the finding this article establishes, section by section, and it is a finding about design rather than about effort or intention.

The puzzle is worth stating plainly because so much writing on the subject avoids it. Dowry has been a criminal offence in India since 1961. The punishments have been raised twice, the definition has been widened twice, a reverse burden of proof has been added, a class of officers has been created to prevent the practice, and the surrounding criminal law has been rebuilt with offences for cruelty and for dowry death. Across the same decades the practice has persisted in every region and, by most accounts, has spread into communities and income groups where it was once weak. A statute can fail for many reasons: weak penalties, hostile police, slow courts, social approval of the conduct it bans. Each of those explanations is available here and each contains some truth. But the Dowry Prohibition Act has a further defect that none of the enforcement explanations reach. Its natural complainant is its co-accused. The bride's family is the actor best placed to report a dowry transaction, and the statute makes that family criminally liable for the very transaction it would report. Section 7(3), inserted in 1986, softens the point in one narrow way: a statement made by the person aggrieved does not itself subject that person to prosecution under the Act. The protection covers the statement, not the payment. The payment remains an offence, and everyone in the system, complainant, police officer and magistrate alike, understands that it does.

Parliament has never revisited the symmetry. The amendments of 1984 and 1986 changed nearly everything else in the Act: the definition, the penalties, the classification of the offences, the burden of proof, the machinery of prevention. The equal punishment of giver and taker survived both rounds untouched, and it survives in the text unamended. Whether it should is a genuine question, and this article states the defence of the symmetry at full strength before giving a verdict, because the defence is not foolish. Dowry is a transaction with two willing parties in many cases, and a law that punished only the receiving side would treat the giving family as victims of a bargain they themselves struck, often eagerly, in competition with other families for a groom of a desired status. The argument against the symmetry is equally serious: in most cases the giving family is not free, because refusal carries the cancellation of the marriage and a social cost that falls on the daughter, and criminalising the coerced side removes the only realistic witness the offence has. Both arguments appear in what follows, in the mouths of the people and institutions that have actually made them.

## The Dowry Prohibition Act in one view

The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 is Act 28 of 1961. It received the assent of the President on 20 May 1961 and was brought into force on 1 July 1961. As enacted, it extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, an exception that belonged to the constitutional arrangements of the time; the reorganisation of 2019 carried the central statute book, this Act included, into the successor Union territories. The Act is short. In its amended form it has ten sections, and a reader can hold the whole design in view at once, which is one reason its central defect is so visible. It applies to every marriage in India whatever the religion of the parties, because it attaches to the transaction and not to the ceremony: the only religious carve-out anywhere in the text is the exclusion of dower or mahr, in the case of persons to whom the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) applies, from the definition of dowry itself.

Section 1 names the Act and deals with extent and commencement. Section 2 defines dowry, and the definition is the load-bearing provision of the statute, the one other laws borrow. Section 3 punishes giving, taking and abetting the giving or taking of dowry. Section 4 punishes demanding it. Section 4A, added in 1986, punishes advertising it. Section 5 declares every agreement for the giving or taking of dowry void. Section 6 provides that dowry received by anyone other than the woman herself is held in trust for her and must be transferred to her within a fixed period, and punishes the failure to transfer. Section 7 confines trials to senior magistrates and restricts the ways in which a court may take cognizance. Section 8 adapts the criminal procedure code to the Act, and declares the offences non-bailable and non-compoundable. Section 8A, added in 1986, places the burden of proving innocence, in prosecutions for taking or demanding, on the accused. Section 8B, added in the same year, creates the Dowry Prohibition Officers. Sections 9 and 10 distribute the rule-making power between the Centre and the states.

The Act's brevity is itself a datum. Ten sections carry a definition, four offences, a voidness rule, a trust, a cognizance code, a classification, a reversed burden, a preventive office and two rule-making powers, and they do so in fewer words than many single sections of the tax code. The compression has a virtue and a cost. The virtue is that the whole policy is visible at once, and a citizen can read the statute that governs one of the largest financial events of a family's life in a few minutes. The cost is that everything the Act leaves open, and it leaves open the proof of connection, the meaning of customary, the working content of the officers' functions, has had to be settled by courts and by rules rather than by the text, and the settlement has taken decades. The sections that follow take the architecture in the order in which a dispute meets it: what dowry is, what the Act does about each stage of it, how a case under it is begun and proved, and where the real enforcement went when it left.

Two features of this architecture deserve notice before the detail begins. The first is that the Act is a prohibition statute and not a family law statute. It does not regulate marriage, does not touch the validity of any marriage, does not give the woman a civil claim to maintenance or residence, and does not create a compensation fund. Its entire method is criminal: define a transaction, forbid it, punish both ends of it, and attach a trust obligation to the proceeds. The second is that the Act's own enforcement machinery is strikingly thin compared with the machinery Parliament built around it later. The cruelty offence of 1983 and the dowry death offence of 1986 sit in the general criminal code, are investigated by the ordinary police in the ordinary way, and are tried as part of the ordinary run of sessions and magistrate work. The Dowry Prohibition Act, by contrast, channels its prosecutions through restricted cognizance rules and a class of specialist officers whose role is preventive and evidentiary rather than executive. The statute that defines the wrong is not the statute that polices it, and the gap between the two is where the article's central finding lives.

## Before the Act: state laws, private members and a reluctant Centre

Dowry was legislated against in India before it was legislated against by India. A province of Sindh attempted a restraint law in the 1930s, the Bihar Dowry Restraint Act was enacted in 1950, and the Andhra Pradesh Dowry Prohibition Act followed in 1958. The standard verdict on all of them, recorded in the later histories of the central Act, is that they failed to affect the practice: the sums continued to be negotiated, the ceremonies continued to be staged, and the statutes were invoked rarely if at all. The reasons matter for understanding the central Act, because the central Act inherited them. A restraint law aimed at a transaction both families have agreed to perform faces a complainant problem from its first day. Neither party to a completed dowry bargain has an interest in reporting it, and the person with the strongest interest in its exposure, the bride, is the person with the least independent power inside either household. The state laws also suffered from the limits of their reach, since marriage markets do not respect state boundaries, and from penalties pitched at a level that treated the practice as a nuisance rather than a serious wrong.

Behind the statutes stands a longer history that the standard accounts of the Act rehearse, because it explains what Parliament thought it was prohibiting. In the classical law the marriage rite of kanyadan, the gift of the bride, was accompanied by varadakshina, cash or gifts in kind made to the bridegroom. The dakshina in its classical form was offered out of affection and regard, was voluntary in character, and carried no element of compulsion or of bargain: it completed a religious act rather than pricing one. The accounts agree on the direction of the change that followed. The voluntary element drained out of the practice and the coercive element entered it, as the gift hardened into a negotiated entitlement of the groom's family, scaled to the groom's education, profession and prospects, and demanded as a condition of the marriage rather than offered at it. What had been a token became a price, and the price became enforceable by the sanctions a family can apply inside a marriage negotiation: delay, humiliation, withdrawal of the match, and, after the wedding, pressure on the bride. The practice also travelled. A custom associated in its developed form with particular regions and communities spread, across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, into communities among whom it had been weak or absent, carried by the same marriage markets that made a groom with a salaried post or a professional degree a scarce and priced asset. It is this transformed practice, the demanded and negotiated transfer, that the word dowry names in the Act, and the distinction between the dakshina and the demand is the ancestor of the distinction the Act itself draws between the voluntary present and dowry. A legislature prohibiting dowry in 1961 was therefore not prohibiting gift-giving at weddings. It was prohibiting a market in grooms that had grown up inside a religious form, and every line of the Act, the definition's insistence on connection with the marriage, the exemption for undemanded presents, the offence of demanding, is addressed to the market and not to the form.

At the Centre the question was raised early and answered slowly. Private members brought proposals before the first Lok Sabha, and in the course of discussion on a non-official bill in 1953 the Law Minister assured the House that a government bill would be prepared in consultation with the state governments. The assurance produced delay rather than a bill. The proposal was held back, on the reasoning recorded in the standard accounts, until the Hindu Succession Act had been enacted, on the theory that giving daughters a legal share in family property would weaken the economic logic of dowry. The Hindu Succession Act came in 1956, and the government of the day then took the view that separate dowry legislation was not a matter of urgency. Pressure from inside and outside Parliament continued, and the Dowry Prohibition Bill was at last introduced in the Lok Sabha on 24 April 1959. It was referred to a joint committee of both Houses, the committee reported with amendments, and neither House would accept the other's position on the reported bill. The disagreement had to be resolved in the manner the Constitution reserves for deadlock between the Houses: the bill was considered at joint sittings held on 6 and 9 May 1961, passed there, and received presidential assent on 20 May 1961.

The enactment history is not a curiosity. It explains the character of the text. A bill that takes two years and a joint sitting to pass is a bill on which opinion is divided along lines that do not follow party, and the division in 1959 to 1961 ran between those who wanted a declaratory prohibition with modest penalties and those who wanted the practice attacked as a serious crime. The text that emerged bears the marks of the compromise. It prohibited the transaction in absolute terms and punished both sides of it, which satisfied the abolitionist instinct, and it pitched the original punishments low: under the Act as enacted, giving or taking dowry under Section 3 carried imprisonment of up to six months, or a fine of up to five thousand rupees, or both. Demanding dowry under the original Section 4 carried the same ceiling, and the original Section 4 contained a further inhibition that later Parliaments removed: no court could take cognizance of a demand offence except with the previous sanction of the state government or of an officer the state government specified. A prohibition that requires the executive's permission before its central offence can be prosecuted is a prohibition designed to be used sparingly, and for its first two decades that is how this one was used. The strengthening of the Act in the 1980s, examined in detail below, is best read as Parliament returning to a text it had originally made deliberately weak and rebuilding almost every part of it except the one part, the symmetry, that a later generation would identify as the flaw.

## The definition: Section 2 word by word

Section 2 provides that in the Act, dowry means any property or valuable security given or agreed to be given, either directly or indirectly, in either of two channels: by one party to a marriage to the other party to the marriage, or by the parents of either party to a marriage or by any other person, to either party to the marriage or to any other person. The giving must occur at or before or any time after the marriage in connection with the marriage of the said parties. The definition then excludes dower or mahr in the case of persons to whom the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) applies. An explanation preserves the meaning of valuable security from Section 30 of the Indian Penal Code. Every phrase in that compressed sentence has been litigated, and each does identifiable work.

The subject matter is property or valuable security, which between them cover cash, jewellery, land, vehicles, household goods, shares, and documents creating legal rights. There is no minimum value and no list of included items. The transaction covered is a giving or an agreement to give, so the offence of giving or taking is complete on agreement even if nothing changes hands, and the definition reaches promises extracted for the future as readily as transfers made on the wedding day. The giving may be direct or indirect, which reaches the routed payment: money passed to a relative of the groom, a deposit made to a third party, a property settled on a nominee. The channels are deliberately wide. The giver may be a party to the marriage, a parent of either party, or any other person at all, an uncle, a family friend, an employer advancing a loan against the marriage. The recipient may be either party or any other person. The definition therefore does not describe a payment by the bride's father to the groom. It describes any transfer of value, by anyone, to anyone, that bears the required connection to a marriage.

The connection is the contested element, and its drafting history is the history of the courts narrowing the Act and Parliament widening it again. As enacted in 1961, the transfer had to be at or before or after the marriage as consideration for the marriage of the said parties. Consideration is a contract word, and it invited the reading that property counted as dowry only where it was the price of the marriage itself, the quid pro quo without which the marriage would not have taken place. Property demanded years into a marriage, or demanded for purposes unconnected with the wedding bargain, could be argued out of the definition on that footing, and defendants argued it successfully. The Dowry Prohibition (Amendment) Act, 1984, Act 63 of 1984, in force from 2 October 1985, substituted the words in connection with the marriage of the said parties for the consideration formula, and omitted the original Explanation I, which had carried the first version of the presents carve-out inside the definition itself. The Dowry Prohibition (Amendment) Act, 1986, Act 43 of 1986, in force from 19 November 1986, completed the widening on the time axis by substituting or any time after the marriage for the bare words or after the marriage. The amended definition therefore reaches a transfer made at any point in the marriage's timeline, before it, at it, or at any time after it, provided the connection with the marriage is shown. How the courts have handled the connection requirement under both the old and the new wording is treated in its own part below, because the case law on the point is the most consequential judicial writing on the Act.

### What is a valuable security under the definition?

A valuable security is a document whereby a legal right is created, extended, transferred, restricted, extinguished or released, or whereby a person acknowledges a legal liability or the absence of a legal right. The meaning is borrowed from Section 30 of the Indian Penal Code. Share certificates, bonds, promissory notes and title documents count alongside cash and goods.

The mahr exclusion deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the definition's only acknowledgement of personal law. Mahr is the sum the husband is obliged to pay the wife under Muslim law as an incident of the marriage contract, an obligation running in the woman's favour and enforceable by her. Parliament excluded it because its direction is the opposite of dowry's: it is a protection for the woman priced into the marriage, not a price extracted for the groom. The exclusion is narrow. It covers dower or mahr where the Shariat applies, and it gives no shelter to transfers dressed in other vocabulary. A payment demanded from the bride's family does not leave the definition by being called a gift, a settlement, or by any other name the parties choose; the test is the connection with the marriage, not the label.

Two elements of the definition do quiet work that repays separate attention. The first is the phrase given or agreed to be given. Because an agreement to give is within the definition, the transaction offences do not wait for delivery: a concluded bargain for dowry is itself dowry, and the giving and taking are complete in law when the bargain is struck, which matters for prosecutions founded on negotiations that collapsed before anything moved. The phrase also reaches the instalment structure of real dowry practice, in which part of the price is paid at the wedding and the balance is promised against festivals, a child's birth, or the groom's next posting; the promised balance is dowry from the moment of the promise. The second element is the reach of indirectly. Dowry in practice is seldom handed across a table in a labelled envelope. It is routed: a deposit placed in a relative's account, a property purchased in a nominee's name, a loan from the bride's father that nobody expects to be repaid, fees and expenses of the groom's education or foreign travel discharged by the bride's family. The word indirectly makes each of these a giving within the section where the connection with the marriage is proved, and it prevents the Act from being defeated by the elementary precaution of interposing a person or an account between the giver and the groom's household. The prosecution's difficulty with such transfers is not the definition, which covers them, but the proof of the connection, which must be built from circumstances because the parties to a routed transfer rarely document its purpose.

Three short applications fix the definition's reach better than further abstraction. A father pays a sum to the groom's father a month before the wedding, after a negotiation in which the match was conditional on the payment: the sum is dowry, given by a parent of one party to a person other than either party, before the marriage and in connection with it, and both the payment and the receipt are offences under Section 3. The same father, at the wedding, gives his daughter jewellery which she wears away from the ceremony, no demand having been made: the jewellery is a present to the bride and, if listed, within the exemption, and in substance it is her stridhan, her absolute property, from the moment of the gift. Two years into the marriage the husband's family presses the wife to bring a further sum from her parents for a business the husband is starting, with taunts and threats when she hesitates: the demand, on the Rajinder Singh test, is a demand for dowry if its connection with the marriage is proved, the passage of time and the business purpose being no answer, and the harassment that presses it is separately cruelty under the penal provision, which is where the complaint will in practice be laid. The three cases are the daily fare of this branch of the law, and the definition answers each of them; what the definition cannot do is make the first case's most natural witness willing to speak.

One further consequence of the definition's width is easily missed. Because the giver and the recipient can each be any person, the definition is indifferent to the direction of the transfer as between the two families. A transfer from the groom's side to the bride's side, made in connection with the marriage, is within the words just as a transfer the other way is. The statute's prohibition is formally symmetrical in its definition as well as in its penalty, and prosecutions have occasionally been brought on transfers running in the less usual direction. The social practice the Act confronts runs overwhelmingly one way, and the case law and the statistics reflect that, but the text itself describes a market in marriage payments and forbids the market, rather than describing an exaction by one sex upon the other and forbidding the exaction. That choice of frame, made in 1961 and retained through both amendments, shapes everything the Act can and cannot do.

## The offences, grouped by what they target

The Act creates four penal provisions and one restitutionary provision with a penal edge. They are best understood by the moment of the transaction each one attacks. Section 3 attacks the completed bargain, the giving and the taking. Section 4 attacks the demand, which precedes any bargain and may never ripen into one. Section 4A attacks the advertisement, the public offer that starts the market moving. Section 5 attacks the agreement, denying it legal existence. Section 6 attacks the retention, the keeping of dowry by someone other than the woman to whom the statute says it belongs. Read together they show a legislature that understood the practice as a system and tried to criminalise every stage of it. Read against the enforcement record they show something else as well: the stages most often prosecuted are not the stages this Act covers best, because the demand that ends in violence is prosecuted as cruelty, and the transaction that ends in a complaint is prosecuted, where it is prosecuted at all, alongside that cruelty charge rather than on its own.

Section 3(1) provides that if any person, after the commencement of the Act, gives or takes or abets the giving or taking of dowry, that person shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than five years, and with fine which shall not be less than fifteen thousand rupees or the amount of the value of such dowry, whichever is more. A proviso permits the court, for adequate and special reasons to be recorded in the judgment, to impose a sentence of imprisonment for a term of less than five years. Three features of the provision carry the article's argument. The first is the symmetry itself: gives, takes and abets stand in one clause under one punishment, and the giver's minimum is the taker's minimum. The second is the severity of the floor. Five years is a heavy minimum for a consensual transfer of property within a family, heavier than the maximum available for many offences of violence, and it reflects the judgment Parliament reached in 1984 when it replaced the original ceiling of six months with this floor: that dowry was to be treated as a grave social crime, not a matrimonial irregularity. The third is the fine's structure. By tying the minimum fine to the value of the dowry where that value is higher, Parliament made the penalty scale with the transaction, so that the larger the bargain, the larger the unavoidable fine. The abetment limb completes the net: the relative who negotiates, the intermediary who carries the money, the elder who presses a hesitant family to pay, each abets the giving or the taking and is within the section.

The provisos that run through Sections 3, 4 and 4A, permitting a sentence below the statutory floor for adequate and special reasons recorded in the judgment, are the safety valve of the penalty scheme, and they carry a discipline of their own. The reasons must be adequate and special, which excludes the routine mitigating circumstances of any sentencing hearing, and they must be recorded in the judgment, which exposes them to appellate correction. The valve matters most in Section 3, where the floor is five years and where the convicted person may be an elderly parent on either side of the transaction, because it is the only route by which a court can fit the sentence to the party's real role in a bargain the statute treats as uniform. Sentencing practice under the Act therefore reproduces, at the penalty stage, the judgment the offence definition refuses to make, between the family that extorted and the family that submitted. Courts have the tool; the proviso's existence is a standing admission, inside the text, that the uniform tariff does not fit the uniform offence, and that a measure of the asymmetry the section denies has to be reintroduced by the judge if the punishment is to be just.

Section 4 punishes the demand. In its amended form it provides that if any person demands, directly or indirectly, from the parents or other relatives or guardian of a bride or bridegroom, any dowry, that person shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than six months but which may extend to two years, and with fine which may extend to ten thousand rupees, with a proviso permitting a sentence below the six-month floor for adequate and special reasons to be mentioned in the judgment. The demand offence is the Act's preventive weapon, because it requires no transfer at all: the asking is the crime. It is also, in the brief that accompanies this article's commission, described as one of the offences the amendments created, and that description needs correction, because the correction is itself instructive. The demand offence is original to the Act of 1961. The original Section 4 punished a demand made to the parents or guardian of a bride or bridegroom with imprisonment of up to six months, or a fine of up to five thousand rupees, or both, and it carried the sanction proviso under which no court could take cognizance except with the previous sanction of the state government or its specified officer. What the amendments did to the demand offence was not to create it but to rebuild it: the 1984 amendment removed the sanction requirement, and the recast provision, completed by the 1986 amendment, widened the class of persons from whom a demand may be made to include other relatives, replaced a low ceiling with a floor of six months and a ceiling of two years, and raised the fine. The distinction between creating an offence and unshackling one matters, because the demand offence's first twenty-three years were spent behind a permission requirement that all but guaranteed its disuse, and the pattern of the Act's early failure is written in that proviso as plainly as anywhere.

Section 4A, inserted by the amendment Act of 1986, addresses the market's public face. It provides that if any person offers, through any advertisement in any newspaper, periodical, journal or through any other media, any share in his property or of any money or both as a share in any business or other interest, as consideration for the marriage of his son or daughter or any other relative, that person shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than six months but which may extend to five years, or with fine which may extend to fifteen thousand rupees, again with a proviso for a lesser sentence on reasons recorded. The section equally punishes printing, publishing or circulating such an advertisement. The provision was aimed at the matrimonial advertisement that quoted property and income as inducements, a recognised feature of the marriage market of its period, and at the newspapers that carried the offers. Its importance in the scheme is diagnostic rather than numerical: prosecutions under it are rare to the point of invisibility, but its presence shows Parliament identifying, correctly, that dowry is sustained by a public market in grooms in which price information circulates openly. A legislature that bans the advertisement has understood the practice as a market. A market, however, does not close because its advertising is banned, and the section's quiet history is an early lesson in the limits of the Act's method.

Section 5 is one sentence: any agreement for the giving or taking of dowry shall be void. The provision does quiet but real work. It means that a dowry bargain, however solemnly documented, cannot be enforced in any court; the groom's family cannot sue for an unpaid instalment of an agreed dowry, and the bride's family cannot sue to recover a promised payment withheld. It also means the transaction has no lawful paper trail, which drives it into cash and kind and into the evidentiary darkness that later defeats prosecutions. Section 6 is the Act's one provision written from the woman's side of the transaction, and it is the provision families actually use. It provides that where any dowry is received by any person other than the woman in connection with whose marriage it is given, that person shall transfer it to the woman within three months, computed by three rules: if the dowry was received before the marriage, within three months after the date of the marriage; if it was received at the time of or after the marriage, within three months after the date of its receipt; and if it was received when the woman was a minor, within three months after she has attained the age of eighteen years. Until transfer, the recipient holds the dowry in trust for the benefit of the woman. The three-month period is itself an amendment artefact: the original Act allowed one year, and the amendment Act of 1984 substituted the shorter period with effect from 2 October 1985.

The trust in Section 6 is not decorative. Section 6(2) provides that a person who fails to transfer the property within the period specified, or fails to transfer property as required on the woman's death, shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than six months but which may extend to two years, or with fine which shall not be less than five thousand rupees but which may extend to ten thousand rupees, or with both. Section 6(3A), inserted in 1986, adds the provision with practical teeth: where a person is convicted under Section 6(2), the court shall, in addition to the sentence, direct that person to transfer the property to the woman or her heirs within a period specified in the order, and on failure the amount equal to the value of the property may be recovered from that person as if it were a fine and paid to the woman or her heirs. The retention offence therefore carries its own restitution mechanism, and a complaint under Section 6 is one of the few routes by which a woman or her family can use this Act to get property back rather than merely to get someone punished. That the provision is used at all, in a statute whose other provisions sleep, confirms the article's thesis from the opposite direction: the one section of the Act that does not require the complainant to confess an offence is the one section with a working life.

### What happens to the dowry if the woman dies before it reaches her?

If the woman entitled under Section 6 dies before receiving the property, her heirs may claim it from the person holding it. If she dies within seven years of her marriage otherwise than by natural causes, the property passes to her parents where she leaves no children, and to her children, held in trust for them, where she does.

The drafting of Section 6(3) rewards attention, because the seven-year line in it is the same line that runs through the whole dowry law cluster. Seven years from the marriage is the period within which an unnatural death engages the dowry death offence and the mandatory presumption in the evidence law. Parliament in 1986 aligned the property provision with the penal provisions, so that the dowry of a woman who dies unnaturally within the period returns to her parents or her children rather than remaining with the household whose members may be accused of causing her death. The alignment is a small masterpiece of legislative coherence, and it sits inside a statute whose larger design, the symmetry of Section 3, works against coherence of purpose. The Act contains both kinds of drafting, and an honest account has to hold the two together.

In practice the sections are seldom charged alone, and the pattern of their combination tells the article's story in miniature. A complaint arising from a dowry dispute, where this Act is used at all, typically joins a count under Section 4 for the demand, a count under Section 3 for any completed giving or taking, and a count under Section 6(2) where property that reached the husband's household has not been transferred to the woman, and it travels in the company of the cruelty charge under the penal code, which carries the narrative of the marriage. The combination is revealing in both directions. The Act's counts give the prosecution the definition, the documentary offences and the restitution direction; the penal count gives it the arrest, the ordinary investigative machinery and the complainant's safety from self-incrimination. Left to itself, the Act's own charge sheet is a thin document, because the transaction it describes was arranged between families in private, was documented, if at all, in the list nobody kept, and can be proved only through witnesses who are parties to it. The offences are also instructive in their sentencing pattern. The transaction offence carries the heaviest floor in the Act, five years, while the demand, the conduct that initiates the harm and that most directly terrorises the bride's family before and after the wedding, carries a floor of six months. A legislature calibrating by harm might have inverted those floors; a legislature calibrating by the symbolism of the completed bargain did what this one did. The advertisement offence, with a floor of six months and a ceiling of five years, sits between them, punishing the market's advertising more heavily than its demands. The pattern is that of a statute whose penalties express condemnation in descending order of the transaction's completeness, rather than deterrence in descending order of the conduct's effect, and it is one more respect in which the Act reads as a declaration against a practice rather than as an instrument engineered for the practice's suppression.

## Dowry, present or stridhan

Three categories of property move at an Indian wedding, and the law treats them as three different things. The first is dowry: value transferred in connection with the marriage, under the statute's definition, which the Act forbids and whose proceeds it impresses with a trust for the woman. The second is the present: value given at the time of the marriage without any demand having been made for it, which the Act permits on conditions, the chief of which is that it be entered in a list maintained under the rules. The third is stridhan: the woman's own property, given to her, earned by her, or settled on her, which is hers in absolute ownership under the general law and which no marriage transfers to anyone else. The categories are conflated constantly, in family argument, in police stations and in print, and the conflation is not innocent, because the ownership consequences of the three categories differ completely. Property that is dowry must be handed over to the woman on pain of prosecution. Property that is a listed present to the groom stays with the groom lawfully. Property that is stridhan was never anyone's but the woman's. The table below states the distinctions in the form in which a family, an advocate or an investigating officer needs them.

The stridhan row carries a doctrinal anchor that the case law fixed early. In Pratibha Rani v. Suraj Kumar, decided in 1985, the Supreme Court held that stridhan is the woman's absolute property, that a husband or relative into whose hands it comes holds it in the position of a trustee, and that a complaint for criminal breach of trust lies where it is dishonestly withheld or converted. The proposition has been restated by the Court in the decades since without variation, and it explains why the practical law of wedding property runs through stridhan doctrine rather than through the dowry statute. A woman claiming her jewellery and her gifts is claiming her own property under doctrines that predate the Act and do not incriminate her parents. A woman complaining of dowry under this Act is reporting a transaction her parents committed. The two claims are often about the same gold, and the choice between them, made daily by advocates across the country, is the symmetry problem wearing its working clothes. The relationship between stridhan and the wider law of women's property, including the absolute ownership that Section 14 of the Hindu Succession Act conferred and the coparcenary rights later added to that Act, is traced in [the account of the Hindu Succession amendment of 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/).

## The presents exception and the list nobody keeps

Section 3(2) contains the Act's most consequential exemption. It provides that nothing in Section 3(1) applies to presents which are given at the time of a marriage to the bride, or to presents given at the time of a marriage to the bridegroom, provided that such presents are given without any demand having been made in that behalf and are entered in a list maintained in accordance with the rules made under the Act. A further proviso governs presents given to the bridegroom by or on behalf of the bride or any person related to the bride: such presents are within the exemption only if they are of a customary nature and their value is not excessive having regard to the financial status of the person by whom, or on whose behalf, they are given. The exemption did not begin in this form. As enacted in 1961, the presents carve-out sat inside the definition in Section 2 as Explanation I, framed in terms of presents given in cash or kind at the time of marriage out of regard for the parties; the 1984 amendment omitted that explanation and rebuilt the exemption in Section 3(2), attaching to it the list condition and, for presents flowing to the groom from the bride's side, the tests of customariness and proportionality to means.

The logic of the exemption is sound and its conditions are precise. A prohibition statute that criminalised every wedding gift would criminalise ordinary affection, so Parliament distinguished the demanded transfer, which is dowry, from the voluntary present, which is not. But a distinction stated only in terms of the giver's state of mind would be unprovable either way, so Parliament attached an objective condition: the present must be entered in a list maintained under the rules. The Dowry Prohibition (Maintenance of Lists of Presents to the Bride and Bridegroom) Rules, 1985, made by the Central Government under Section 9, supply the detail. Rule 2 requires that the list of presents given to the bride be maintained by the bride, and the list of presents given to the bridegroom be maintained by the bridegroom. Each list is to be prepared at the time of the marriage or as soon as possible after it, and must be in writing, contain a brief description of each present, the approximate value of the present, the name of the person who gave it, and, where that person is related to the bride or bridegroom, the relationship. Each list must be signed by both the bride and the bridegroom, with a facility for a brief explanation of a thumb impression or mark where a party is unable to sign, and a relative or other person present at the ceremony may also sign. The architecture is complete: two lists, two custodians, values, givers, relationships, both signatures. A wedding conducted under the rules produces a signed contemporaneous record of every significant transfer, against which any later claim that a transfer was a voluntary present can be tested.

Almost nobody keeps the lists. That is not rhetoric but the uniform observation of the case law and the commentary, and it is the single most repairable defect in the Act's operation. The consequences run in both directions, which is why the list matters to both sides of the debate this article must hold in balance. For the prosecution, the absence of a list removes the exemption's protection: a transfer claimed to be a present, which was not entered in any list, stands exposed as dowry if the connection with the marriage is proved, and the accused cannot retreat behind Section 3(2). For the defence, the same absence removes the one objective document that could have proved voluntariness, and a family that gave genuinely voluntary presents at a wedding, unlisted as almost all are, finds the transfers recharacterised years later in the middle of a matrimonial breakdown. Judges have seen the point. Justice S. N. Dhingra of the Delhi High Court, in observations made in 2007 in the case of Neera Singh v. State, urged that courts and police should insist on the maintenance of the lists the rules require and on disclosure of the source of the funds behind wedding transfers, treating the list as the document that separates the present from the dowry at the moment of the wedding rather than in recollection afterwards. The observation has been quoted in later judgments and in the literature on the Act, because it identifies the cheapest reform available in this field: the record already has a legal form, a custodian and a signature requirement, and lacks only the habit of use.

The two further conditions on presents to the bridegroom reward a closer look, because they are the Act's only attempt to police the scale of giving rather than its fact. A present to the groom from the bride's side must be customary in nature, which ties the exemption to the community's settled practice: the clothes, the ring, the customary cash envelope of a given community pass; the car and the flat, in a community where they are not customary, do not. And the value must not be excessive having regard to the financial status of the giver, a test that makes the same object lawful from a rich family and criminal from a poor one, because the statute's concern is not the object but the pressure its giving evidences and creates. The tests are intelligent and almost entirely unadministered. Customariness is a question of community practice on which evidence is anecdotal, and excessiveness relative to means invites an inquiry into the giver's finances that no party to a wedding welcomes and no officer is tasked to conduct before the event. There is also a question of incidence: the burden of bringing a transfer within Section 3(2) rests on the person who claims the exemption, since the exemption is a proviso to a penal provision and the facts that found it, the absence of demand and the entry in the list, are facts within that person's knowledge. A family that cannot produce the list therefore does not merely lose a piece of evidence; it fails to discharge the burden that the exemption's structure places on it, and the transfer is judged as dowry if the prosecution proves the connection with the marriage.

Why the habit never formed is a question the Act's design answers. The list is required as a condition of an exemption from a penal provision, not as a condition of the marriage, and no official attends an Indian wedding to collect it. The families who would keep lists are the families least likely to transact dowry, and the families transacting dowry have every reason to leave the transfers undocumented, because documentation is evidence. The rule therefore selects against itself: compliance is highest where the prohibition is least needed. A serious enforcement of the lists would require attaching the document to something families already do, registration of the marriage, or to something officials already inspect, and Parliament has never made that attachment. Meanwhile the practical consequence stands. In litigation under the Act, the question whether a transfer was a dowry or a present is decided on oral recollection, family testimony and inference, years after the event, in the middle of a failed marriage, because the one document designed to decide it was never made. Families and their advisers who keep records of a marriage's financial side, the lists, the invoices, the bank transfers and the correspondence, hold them in ordinary files and spreadsheets, and a reader assembling such a record for a legal consultation may find an [office file viewer for opening Excel, Word and PowerPoint records](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) useful for reading what the family has kept. The statute asked for exactly such a record in 1985. The gap between the asking and the keeping is the Act in miniature.

## The machinery: courts, cognizance, classification, burden and officers

Section 7 builds a guarded gate into the Act. No court inferior to that of a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class may try an offence under the Act, and the magistrate may pass any sentence authorised by the Act. No court may take cognizance of an offence except upon its own knowledge or a police report of the facts constituting the offence, or upon a complaint made by the person aggrieved by the offence, or by a parent or other relative of that person, or by a recognised welfare institution or organisation, recognition being given by the central or the state government for the purpose. The gate cuts both ways. It protects families from prosecution at the instance of strangers and busybodies, confining the complaint route to the aggrieved person, close relatives and recognised welfare bodies, and in doing so it concedes the Act's premise: dowry is a wrong inside a family, and the trigger for its prosecution should ordinarily come from inside or near that family. It also narrows the channel through which the symmetry problem might have been circumvented, since a prosecution cannot be set in motion by an unconnected third party who has no offence of their own to confess. The police report route stands open, and police reports do found prosecutions, but a police force that learns of dowry mainly from the families involved is back at the same gate.

The complaint route through recognised welfare institutions deserves a word, because it is the Act's one attempt to give the prosecution a trigger outside the family. A welfare institution or organisation recognised by the central or the state government for the purpose may complain in its own name, and recognition is a formal act of the government concerned. The device was meant to let the women's organisations and welfare bodies that encounter dowry cases in their work carry a complaint that a frightened family would not sign. Its limits are the limits of the rest of the machinery: the institution can open the gate, but the evidence behind the gate remains the family's, and an institution's complaint founded on facts the family will not support in the witness box travels no further than the family's own complaint would have travelled. The forum provision completes the gate's design. By confining trials to Metropolitan Magistrates and Judicial Magistrates of the first class, and by freeing those magistrates from the ordinary sentencing ceilings of their class so that they may pass any sentence the Act authorises, Parliament ensured that the five-year minimum of Section 3 could be imposed by the court that tries the case, without committal to a sessions court. The design is coherent as procedure. It simply guards a door through which, for the reasons this article has given, very few prosecutions arrive.

A prosecution that does arrive takes one of two shapes. On the police report route, the investigation proceeds with the powers Section 8 confers, the accused are proceeded against without arrest unless a magistrate orders otherwise or a warrant issues, and the charge sheet goes to the designated magistrate. On the complaint route, the magistrate examines the complainant and the material offered, and takes cognizance where the complaint is by a person the section authorises and the facts alleged disclose an offence. In both shapes the case then turns on the same evidentiary core: the marriage, the demand or the transfer, the connection between them, and the documents, above all the list of presents that would have separated the dowry from the gifts and that is, in the ordinary case, not there. The procedure is not the obstacle. The record is.

Section 7(3), inserted by the amendment Act of 1986, provides that notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force, a statement made by the person aggrieved by an offence under the Act shall not subject that person to a prosecution under the Act. The provision is the closest Parliament has come to admitting the symmetry problem, and its limits define the problem's persistence. What the aggrieved person says is protected: her statement, and by the same logic the complaint built on it, cannot be turned into a charge against her. What she or her parents did is not protected: the giving remains an offence, and another route to the same facts, a police report founded on independent information, is untouched by the shield. A family deciding whether to complain under this Act therefore faces a statute that promises not to prosecute them for speaking and reserves the power to prosecute them for paying. Lawyers advising such families have drawn the obvious conclusion for four decades, and have routed dowry grievances into the cruelty provision of the criminal code, where no equivalent exposure exists. The 1986 Parliament that inserted Section 7(3) plainly wanted more complaints under the Act. It protected the words and left the act criminal, and the complaints did not come.

### Why is there no limitation period for a dowry prosecution?

Because Section 7(2) says so in terms: nothing in Chapter XXXVI of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the chapter fixing periods of limitation for taking cognizance, applies to an offence under this Act. A complaint may therefore be entertained years after the transaction, a deliberate choice for offences that surface only when a marriage breaks down.

Section 8 governs classification, and its text is more subtle than the summaries of it. It provides that the Code of Criminal Procedure shall apply to offences under the Act as if the offences were cognizable offences for the purposes of investigation, and for all matters other than the matters referred to in Section 42 of the Code and the arrest of a person without a warrant or without an order of a magistrate. The formula is a half-way house. For investigation, the police have the powers that attend a cognizable offence; for arrest, they do not, and no arrest under this Act may be made without a warrant or a magistrate's order. The design reflects an early and persistent anxiety about the Act: that its offences, arising inside families at moments of breakdown, would be used to trigger summary arrests, and that the arrest would become the punishment regardless of the trial's outcome. Parliament's answer was to give the investigation teeth and to withhold the handcuffs. Section 8(2) then provides that every offence under the Act shall be non-bailable and non-compoundable. The non-bailable limb is itself dated: the substituted Section 8 of 1984 declared the offences bailable and non-compoundable, and the amendment Act of 1986 replaced the word bailable with non-bailable, so that from 19 November 1986 the offences have been non-bailable while remaining, as they have been throughout, incapable of being compounded by the parties. The combination is unusual and worth pausing on: an offence too serious to compound and too grave for bail to follow as an entitlement, which the police may investigate fully but for which they may not arrest without judicial authority. Each element was a compromise struck at a different moment, and the composite serves none of the system's users cleanly.

Section 8A, inserted in 1986, carries the reverse burden. It provides that where any person is prosecuted for taking or abetting the taking of dowry under Section 3, or for demanding dowry under Section 4, the burden of proving that he had not committed the offence shall be on him. The provision is confined in two ways that the debate about it often ignores. It applies to the taker and the demander, the two figures Parliament regarded as the aggressors in the transaction, and it does not apply to the giver, whose prosecution proceeds on the ordinary burden. And it operates within a prosecution the state must still found: the prosecution must establish the factual platform, the marriage and the transfer or demand, before the burden of disproof moves. Even so confined, the provision is strong medicine, a statutory instruction to convict unless the accused proves innocence of the specific offence, and its presence in the Act is one of the standard exhibits in the argument that the dowry statutes reverse the ordinary protections of the accused. Its practical footprint is smaller than its terms suggest, for the familiar reason: it assists prosecutions that are brought, and the Act's difficulty has never been the burden of proof. It has been the absence of a complainant willing to start.

Section 8B, also inserted in 1986, creates the Dowry Prohibition Officers. The state government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint as many officers as it thinks fit for the areas specified in the notification. The officer's functions are to see that the provisions of the Act are complied with, to prevent, as far as possible, the taking or abetting of the taking or the demanding of dowry, to collect such evidence as may be necessary for the prosecution of persons committing offences under the Act, and to perform such additional functions as the state government may assign or as may be prescribed. The state government may, by notification and subject to prescribed conditions, confer on an officer any of the powers of a police officer, exercisable for the functions the notification specifies. Each officer is supported by an advisory board of not more than five social welfare workers, of whom at least two shall be women, appointed to advise and assist the officer in the efficient performance of the functions. Sections 9 and 10 complete the scheme by giving the rule-making power to the Centre, which made the lists rules of 1985 among others, and to the states, which may prescribe the officer's additional functions and the conditions of the police powers conferred on the officer.

The central scheme has not stood entirely alone. The Act, though a central statute, operates in a field where states have legislated and amended within their competence, and the bare text of the Act carries the marks. In its application to Haryana, a state amendment has substituted the definition section with a set of definitions of its own, and Bihar amended the demand provision by a state Act of 1976; other states have used their rule-making power under Section 10 to prescribe the officers' additional functions and the conditions on which police powers are conferred. The state layer matters less for its content than for what it reveals about the Act's centre of gravity. The parts of the design that states have touched are the definition and the machinery, the parts a government can adjust by notification and amendment; the part no state has touched, because no state could without repudiating the central design, is the symmetry of Section 3. Variation has occurred at the Act's edges. Its centre has been uniform across India and across six decades, which is consistent with the centre being a settled conviction of the legislatures rather than an oversight none of them noticed.

What happens after the gate deserves a paragraph, because the Act's non-compoundable character shapes it. Once cognizance is taken, the case proceeds as an ordinary magistrate's trial, and it cannot be ended by the parties' agreement: Section 8(2) places the offences outside the compounding table, so a settlement between the families, however complete, does not by itself terminate the prosecution. In practice, matrimonial settlements across this whole field of law are given effect through a different door, the inherent power of the High Court to quash proceedings where continuance would be an abuse of process after a genuine settlement, the route whose boundaries the Supreme Court restated in the Social Action Forum proceedings when it held that quashing on settlement is for the High Court under Section 482 of the procedure code and not for any committee or subordinate forum. The detour is instructive. A statute that forbids compounding in the name of seriousness produces, in a class of disputes that characteristically end in negotiated separation, a traffic of quashing petitions to the constitutional courts, with the delay and expense that the detour implies. The Law Commission's recommendation that the cruelty offence be made compoundable with the court's permission was addressed to exactly this friction, and the friction is at its sharpest under this Act, whose offences are the most purely transactional of the cluster and therefore the most naturally compromised when the marriage itself is compromised into a settlement.

The officer of Section 8B is the Act's most eloquent failure, and the failure is structural rather than personal. The statute creates an office whose core duties are preventive and evidentiary, in a system where prevention has no measurable event to attach to and where evidence collected by a non-police officer must still travel through the police report and cognizance gates the Act itself erected. Appointment depends on a state notification; the conferral of police powers depends on a second notification; the advisory board depends on a further appointment; and the officer's work has no procedural privilege, no power to compel, and no budget line that the statute secures. The design predicts the record. Prosecutions under the Act are brought by police and by complainants, trials turn on family testimony and documents, and the reported judgments under the statute almost never turn on an officer's intervention or evidence. An enforcement office that the enforcement process can route around entirely will be routed around, and the Dowry Prohibition Officer survives as a statutory idea with a Gazette notification in some states and a working existence in few. The criticism that appointments have been patchy and the preventive functions have largely not operated has been recorded in reviews of the Act's working across the decades; the structure of Section 8B is sufficient explanation of why the criticism keeps proving true.

## The penal interface: where enforcement actually lives

By the early 1980s the original Act was widely regarded, inside Parliament as much as outside it, as a dead letter, and a joint committee of Parliament examined the working of the Act and reported in 1982. The committee's significance in the cluster's history is that it refused to treat the Act's failure as a failure of drafting detail. Its examination documented a prohibition that was not being used, and its recommendations reached beyond the Act to the criminal code itself, on the reasoning that a practice enforced by cruelty inside the household had to be met by offences addressed to the cruelty and to its fatal outcomes, and not only by a prohibition addressed to the payment. The committee's work is the hinge between the two eras of dowry law: the era in which the prohibition Act stood alone, and the era, which continues, in which it stands at the centre of a cluster whose operative members surround it. The amendments that followed, in 1983, 1984 and 1986, were taken in Parliament in the committee's shadow, and the division of labour they produced, definition in the pillar statute, conduct and death in the codes, evidence presumptions calibrated to each, was set at that moment and has never been redrawn. The committee's examination set in train the rebuilding of the whole dowry law cluster, and the rebuilding proceeded on two tracks at once. One track amended this Act, in 1984 and again in 1986. The other track, the consequential one, wrote new offences into the general criminal law. The Criminal Law (Second Amendment) Act, 1983, Act 46 of 1983, inserted Section 498A into the Indian Penal Code, making it an offence for a husband or a relative of a husband to subject a woman to cruelty, cruelty being defined to include wilful conduct likely to drive the woman to suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to her life, limb or health, and harassment of the woman with a view to coercing her or any person related to her to meet an unlawful demand for any property or valuable security. The same amendment inserted Section 113A into the Indian Evidence Act, permitting a court to presume abetment of suicide where a married woman died by suicide within seven years of her marriage and her husband or his relatives had subjected her to cruelty. Three years later the amendment Act of 1986, in the same statute that rebuilt this Act, inserted Section 304B into the Penal Code, creating the offence of dowry death, and inserted Section 113B into the Evidence Act, the mandatory presumption that attends it. The recodification of 2023 carried the scheme into the new codes without disturbing its structure: the cruelty offence stands at Section 85 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 with the definition of cruelty at Section 86, the dowry death offence stands at Section 80 of the Sanhita with the same punishment of seven years to imprisonment for life, and the two evidentiary presumptions stand at Sections 117 and 118 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, the discretionary presumption for abetment of suicide and the mandatory presumption for dowry death respectively. The full field of which this cluster forms part, maternity, maintenance, guardianship, succession and the rest, is mapped in [the guide to women's rights laws in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/).

One refinement of the cruelty provision's text matters for the comparison, because it is wider than the dowry offence it displaced. The harassment limb of the cruelty definition is not confined to dowry demands: it covers harassment of the woman with a view to coercing her or any person related to her to meet any unlawful demand for any property or valuable security. A demand for money to fund a business, to buy a vehicle, to settle a debt, is within the cruelty provision if the harassment that presses it is proved, whether or not the demand would satisfy the connection test of Section 2 of this Act. The penal provision is therefore not merely easier to invoke than the prohibition; it covers a broader band of the conduct that marriages actually generate, and it asks the complainant to prove a course of behaviour she has lived rather than a transaction she has signed. The definitional traffic runs in one direction only in the death cases; in the cruelty cases the penal code does not even need this Act's word.

The cruelty provision changed the enforcement of dowry law because it changed what a complainant has to prove and what a complainant has to fear. A prosecution under Section 3 of this Act requires proof of a completed transaction, property that moved, in connection with a marriage, and it exposes the giving side of that transaction to the same conviction. A prosecution for cruelty requires proof of conduct: wilful behaviour of the defined gravity, or harassment to coerce an unlawful demand. The demand need not have been met. No transfer need be proved. The complainant's family incriminates itself in nothing by reporting that their daughter is being harassed for money she has not paid. The provision is also, unlike this Act, an ordinary cognizable offence investigated in the ordinary way, and the judicial response to its working, the safeguard jurisprudence on arrest and the continuing argument about its use and misuse, is the subject of [the article on Section 498A and the judicial response](/2016/03/01/section-498a-judicial-response/), which owns that territory in this series. For the purposes of the pillar statute the point is simpler. From 1983 onwards, a family with a dowry grievance had a choice between a prohibition Act that prosecuted transactions and incriminated givers, and a penal provision that prosecuted conduct and incriminated only the husband's side. The system's actors, complainants, lawyers and police alike, made the choice the incentives dictated, and the enforcement of dowry law migrated to the penal code and has lived there ever since.

The dowry death provision answers the gravest cases, and its outline belongs here because its definition borrows this Act's central word. Section 304B of the Penal Code, and Section 80 of the Sanhita after it, provide that where the death of a woman is caused by burns or bodily injury or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of her marriage, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or a relative of her husband for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry, the death is a dowry death and the husband or relative is deemed to have caused it, punishable with imprisonment of not less than seven years extending to imprisonment for life. The word dowry in the provision bears the meaning this Act gives it, which is the sense in which the prohibition statute functions as the definitional source for the operative law: the section that decides whether a death is a dowry death reaches back to Section 2 of the Act of 1961 for the content of its central term. Section 113B of the Evidence Act, and Section 118 of the Adhiniyam after it, then provide that where the foundational facts are shown, the court shall presume that the accused caused the dowry death, a mandatory presumption that reverses the practical burden in the cases of greatest gravity. The distinction between that provision and its neighbour matters and is preserved across the recodification: Section 113A, and Section 117 of the Adhiniyam after it, say the court may presume abetment of suicide, a discretionary presumption, while Section 113B says the court shall presume the dowry death, an obligatory one. The comparison between the penal provisions built around women in this period, their elements, punishments and presumptions side by side, is drawn in [the comparison of the criminal law amendments on women](/2016/03/15/criminal-law-amendments-women-compared/), and the dowry death offence itself, its proximity test and its case law, is owned by the later article in this series devoted to it. The proximity test belongs to that outline, because it disciplines the presumption: in Kans Raj v. State of Punjab, decided in 2000, the Supreme Court held that the words soon before her death in Section 304B impose no fixed period, that the expression is relative and is to be applied through a proximity test between the cruelty or harassment and the death, on the facts of each case; and in Satbir Singh v. State of Punjab, decided in 2001, the Court set out the ingredients of the offence in the form in which trial courts recite them, an unnatural death within seven years, cruelty or harassment soon before it, and that cruelty or harassment for or in connection with a demand for dowry. The evidentiary companion inserted in 1983 deserves its own sentence in this outline, because its asymmetry with the 1986 presumption is a considered legislative choice rather than a drafting drift. Section 113A of the Evidence Act, and Section 117 of the Adhiniyam after it, provide that where a married woman dies by suicide within seven years of her marriage and it is shown that her husband or his relatives subjected her to cruelty, the court may presume, having regard to all the other circumstances, that the suicide was abetted by the husband or the relative. May in the suicide provision, shall in the dowry death provision: Parliament trusted the court's discretion where the death was self-inflicted and removed it where the death was an unnatural one within the defined conditions. The two presumptions together with the offences they serve form the enforcement core of the dowry cluster, and neither of them is in this Act. What the pillar article must record is the direction of dependence: the offence that carries the dowry law's real deterrent weight defines its central concept by reference to the statute that carries almost none.

The civil route completes the map of where a dowry grievance can actually go, and its existence underlines the criminal statute's narrowness. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 treats a demand for dowry as a form of domestic violence and treats the disposal or withholding of a woman's stridhan and other property as economic abuse, and it empowers a magistrate to grant protection orders, residence orders and monetary relief, including relief directed at the return of her property, without any prosecution and without exposing her family to one; the working of that civil machinery is the subject of [the article on the Domestic Violence Act 2005](/2015/12/01/domestic-violence-act-2005-explained/). A woman advised competently in a dowry dispute is therefore likely to be offered three instruments: the civil application, which can recover property and secure the home; the cruelty complaint, which prosecutes the conduct; and, in the gravest cases, the dowry death provisions. The prohibition Act's own counts, the giving and taking, the demand, the advertisement, figure in the advice mainly as background and as the source of the definition the other instruments use. This article is legal information about a statute's design and record, not advice on any person's case; but the design conclusion needs no lawyer to draw. A prohibition that its intended beneficiaries rationally avoid has failed in a way that higher penalties cannot repair.

## Reading the definition in court

The courts' engagement with the Act has concentrated on the definition, and within the definition on two questions: what connection with the marriage a transfer or demand must bear, and how far the demand offence reaches beyond a completed bargain. On the second question the early authority is L. V. Jadhav v. Shankarrao, decided by the Supreme Court in 1983, which refused to import the whole of the Section 2 definition into Section 4 and held that the demand offence is aimed at the demand itself, at discouraging the very asking for property or valuable security in connection with a marriage, so that a demand made before a marriage, in contemplation of it, is within the section without proof that any property was agreed to be given or ever moved. The holding mattered because it separated the demand offence from the transaction offences and gave Section 4 an independent life as a prohibition on the opening move of the dowry negotiation. On the first question the older case law was written under the consideration formula. In S. Gopal Reddy v. State of Andhra Pradesh, decided in 1996, the Supreme Court, reading the definition in its earlier form, insisted that property becomes dowry within the Act where it is given or agreed to be given as consideration for the marriage, and set aside a conviction under Section 4 where the evidence of the appellant's own demand was not made out. The case is a fair specimen of the pre-amendment and transitional jurisprudence: careful about the nexus between the transfer and the marriage bargain, and reluctant to let the definition swallow every financial unpleasantness inside a family.

### Can the Act reach a demand where the marriage never took place?

Yes. In L. V. Jadhav v. Shankarrao the Supreme Court held the demand offence in Section 4 is directed at the demand itself, made in contemplation of a marriage, and requires neither that the marriage occurred nor that property changed hands. A broken engagement does not close the section, if the demand and connection with the intended marriage are proved.

The modern contest over the definition was fought on different ground: not whether the marriage happened, but why the money was wanted. In Appasaheb v. State of Maharashtra, decided in 2007, the Supreme Court construed the definition strictly and held that a demand for money on account of financial stringency, or to meet urgent domestic expenses, or for purchasing manure, is not a demand for dowry, because the demand must bear a correlation with the marriage and a demand for such purposes does not acquire that character merely because it is made of a married woman or her family. The reasoning protected a boundary: households exchange money and demands for money constantly, and the dowry offences would become instruments for converting every financial dispute inside a marriage into a dowry case if the reason for a demand were irrelevant. In Rajinder Singh v. State of Punjab, decided in 2015, a bench of three judges of the Supreme Court held that Appasaheb, and the 2013 decision in Vipin Jaiswal v. State of Andhra Pradesh which had followed a similar path, do not state the law correctly. The Court held that any money or property demanded by any of the persons the definition names, at or before or at any time after the marriage, is a demand for dowry where it is reasonably connected to the marriage, and in the death cases to the death, unless the facts make it clear that the demand has no such nexus; and that the reason for the demand, the purpose for which the money is wanted, is immaterial where the connection with the marriage is established. A bench of the Court restated the position in Jatinder Kumar v. State of Haryana in December 2019. The arc is the familiar one in this statute's life, run in the courtroom rather than in Parliament: a narrowing construction, defended as fidelity to the text, answered by a widening construction defended as fidelity to the statute's purpose, with the wider reading left in possession of the field.

### How did the courts narrow the definition, and how was it widened again?

In Appasaheb (2007) the Supreme Court held that money demanded for financial stringency or domestic expenses was not dowry, since the demand lacked correlation with the marriage. In Rajinder Singh (2015) a three-judge bench held that reasoning incorrect: a demand reasonably connected with the marriage is a dowry demand whatever its purpose, unless the facts clearly show no such connection.

Between the strictness of Appasaheb and the width of Rajinder Singh, the ordinary run of High Court decisions had to decide the recurring factual pattern of the continuing demand: the television, the scooter, the refrigerator, the sum for a business, demanded not at the wedding but in the months and years of married life that followed. In Pawan Kumar v. State of Haryana the persistent demand for a television and a scooter was held to be a demand in connection with the marriage and therefore a demand for dowry, a holding that treated the timing of the demand as no answer to it where the demand's content was the unpaid price of the match itself. Rajinder Singh later supplied the doctrinal foundation for that instinct at the level of the Supreme Court: the demand's connection with the marriage is the test, its stated purpose is not, and the passage of time between the wedding and the demand goes to proof of the connection rather than to the definition. The two poles of the case law are therefore not really in conflict about the wedding day. They are in conflict about the ordinary financial traffic of a household, and the three-judge settlement protects the prosecution where the demand is the marriage's price still being collected, while leaving the defence its answer where the facts show a transaction unconnected with the match. That is a workable allocation, and it has held.

Two further lines of authority discipline the definition from the side of custom. Payments that are customary in character, made on occasions such as the birth of a child or festivals, in amounts that bear no relation to a marriage bargain, have been held outside the definition, a position the Supreme Court stated in Satbir Singh v. State of Punjab in 2001 in the dowry death context and repeated in Ram Singh v. State of Haryana in 2008, where customary payments made at the birth of a child were held not to be dowry. The customary payments line is the mirror image of the presents exception: both recognise that an Indian marriage is surrounded by flows of gifts and customary transfers that are not prices, and both insist that the law's target is the transfer that functions as a price or an exaction, identified by demand, by bargain, or by a connection with the marriage that the facts establish. The definitional case law, taken as a whole, has left the Act with a wide text and a working test: connection with the marriage, proved on the facts, with the reason for the demand excluded from consideration and customary payments excluded from the category. It is a workable law of definition. The statute's failure lies elsewhere, in the identity of the person the definition makes liable when the connection is proved, and no line of cases can repair that, because the courts did not write it and cannot rewrite it.

A final feature of the case law belongs to proof rather than to definition, and Gopal Reddy illustrates it. The appellant in that case had been convicted of demanding dowry on the strength of letters attributed to him and the testimony built around them; the Supreme Court examined the evidence, found the attribution of the demands to the appellant himself not established against the inconsistencies and the inconclusive expert opinion on the handwriting, and set the conviction aside. The case is routinely cited for its reading of the definition, but its discipline is evidentiary: a demand offence, which requires no transfer and leaves no property trail, stands or falls on the words attributed to the accused, and the Court required those words to be proved to be his, by evidence that survived scrutiny, before the section could bite. In a field where prosecutions are built from family testimony recorded long after the event, that discipline is one of the working reasons convictions are hard to sustain, and it is a legitimate one. The reverse burden of Section 8A does not displace it: the prosecution must still found the case, the demand and its maker, before any burden of disproof can move to the accused.

## The symmetry, defended and answered

Because the article's verdict falls against the symmetry, the defence of it must be put at the strength its holders would recognise, in its own terms, before the answer. The defence begins with a description of the transaction that the contrary argument prefers to soften. Dowry is not, in the standard case, a robbery. It is a bargain struck between two families, each advised by its elders, each aware of the going price for a groom of a given education and employment, and each capable of refusing the match. The bride's family shops in the same market in which the groom's family sells: it seeks the doctor, the engineer, the government officer, it compares the prices quoted for each, and it pays a premium for status exactly as a buyer pays a premium for any scarce and desired asset. On that description, a law that punished only the receiving family would be incoherent. It would declare a transaction criminal and then license one of its two parties, and it would invite every giving family to plead coercion as a formula, converting a market it participated in into a victimhood it could invoke when the marriage soured. The symmetry, on this view, is not an oversight but the statute's moral centre: Parliament judged that the practice survives because both sides sustain it, that the giving family which pays a price for a son-in-law in one marriage is the family that will demand a price for a son in another, and that a prohibition addressed to a practice must address the practice whole. There is also a deterrence argument of a harder kind. Punishing the giver raises the cost of entering the market at all, and a family that knows payment is itself an offence has a legal reason, presentable to relatives and to the other side, for refusing to pay: the refusal can be dressed as obedience to the law rather than as poverty or pride. Reformers who defended the symmetrical design made precisely this point, that the Act hands the bride's family a shield of illegality to hold up in the negotiation, and that removing the giver's liability would remove the shield and leave the family bare before the demand.

The answer accepts the description for a class of cases and denies that the class is the whole. The competition account fits the prosperous, status-seeking bargain, and such bargains exist in number. It fits badly the standard case the enforcement system actually meets: a family of modest means, a daughter whose marriage is treated by her community as an obligation to be discharged, a demand that escalates after the engagement when withdrawal has become ruinous, and a payment assembled from loans, sold land and a father's retirement savings, made not to win a premium groom but to prevent the collapse of the match and the disgrace that the community attaches to the bride. For that family the language of market participation is a fiction, because the alternative to payment is not a different groom at a lower price but a daughter returned to a household that will read her as damaged, and a second search begun under worse conditions. Coercion in such cases wears no uniform and leaves no mark; it is the structure of the choice itself. The recorded protest against Section 3 began from exactly this case. Women's organisations and associations across India protested against the punishment of the giver from the Act's early decades, on the ground that the giver is a victim of the system who gives under forced circumstances, and that a provision which treats the coerced payment and the extorted receipt as the same act mistakes the anatomy of the wrong. The enforcement answer completes the argument, and it is the answer this article has established from the statute's own record: whatever the symmetry's merits as moral expression, its operational effect is to silence the only witness the offence reliably produces. A prohibition whose violation is arranged between families can be proved, in the ordinary case, only by one of the families, and the Act makes that family's testimony a confession. The shield argument, the defence's most practical point, cuts against its makers on inspection: a shield is useful to a family that wishes to refuse, and the family that wishes to refuse is seldom the family the system meets; the family the system meets is the one that paid, under pressure it could not withstand, and then found that the law had priced its complaint at five years of its own liberty. The verdict follows. Symmetrical criminalisation states a true proposition about the practice and a false one about the parties, and as the foundation of an enforcement statute it has failed on the evidence of six decades. A prohibition that punished the taking, the demanding and the retaining with full severity, and treated the giving as the act of a witness to be protected rather than of an accomplice to be charged, would lose the symmetry's moral elegance. It would gain a complainant.

What such a redesign would have to contain can be stated from the Act's own machinery, without invention. The shield of Section 7(3), which protects the aggrieved person's statement, would have to be widened into an immunity for the act of giving where the giver or the woman complains, so that the complaint route ceases to be a confession; the demand offence, already complete on the asking and already free of any sanction requirement, would become the Act's working offence, because it is the one whose proof does not depend on the complainant's own liability; and the Section 6 trust, the Act's one restitutionary success, would be left exactly as it stands, since it incriminates only the retainer. Nothing in that programme requires the practice to be approved, the taking to be excused, or the market to be licensed. It requires only that the statute stop charging its witness. The objection that an immunity would be exploited, that families would pay and then complain to recover the price while keeping the marriage's benefits, is a real objection and would have to be met in the drafting, by confining the immunity to complaints made while the coercion continues or within a defined period, and by preserving liability for the giver who initiates a bargain the other side did not demand. The point for this article is narrower: the redesign has been available, in outline, since the enforcement pattern became clear four decades ago, and the fact that no amendment has attempted it marks the symmetry not as a problem Parliament failed to solve but as a choice Parliament continues to make.

## The misuse debate, stated at full strength on both sides

The debate about the misuse of the dowry laws is conducted mostly about Section 498A of the Penal Code, because that is the provision in daily use, but its terms govern the reputation of the whole cluster including this Act, and no pillar account of the Dowry Prohibition Act can leave it in summary form. The stance of this article is that both sides of the debate are held by serious people on evidence that is genuinely available to them, that the two sides are frequently measuring different things, and that the statistics each side cites must be attributed to their sources and not blended into a single number that neither side produced.

The case that the provisions are misused begins from the structure of the offences. The cruelty offence is cognizable and non-bailable, it names not only the husband but any relative of the husband, and a single complaint can therefore bring parents, siblings and in-laws who live in other households and other cities into a criminal process at the moment of a marriage's collapse. The Supreme Court gave the concern its canonical statement early. In Sushil Kumar Sharma v. Union of India, decided in 2005, the Court upheld the constitutional validity of Section 498A and in the same judgment warned that the provision was intended as a shield and that by misuse of the provision a new legal terrorism could be unleashed; the Court commended the concern to the legislature and the Law Commission. In Preeti Gupta v. State of Jharkhand, decided in 2010, the Court recorded a noticeable tendency to implicate the husband and all his immediate relations in complaints under the section, exhorted courts and the Bar to treat the complaints with care, and observed that a serious relook at the entire provision was warranted by the legislature. In Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar, decided in 2014, the Court moved from observation to direction: it held that arrests under the section had become automatic in practice, observed that the provision's cognizable and non-bailable character had lent it a place amongst the provisions used as weapons rather than shields, and it directed that police officers must apply the checklist in Sections 41 and 41A of the Code of Criminal Procedure before arresting in offences punishable with up to seven years, that the reasons for arrest and for non-arrest be recorded and forwarded to the magistrate, and that magistrates authorise detention only on recorded satisfaction, with departmental action and contempt as the sanctions for default. In Rajesh Sharma v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in July 2017, a bench of the Court went further and directed the constitution of Family Welfare Committees in every district, composed of paralegal volunteers, social workers and retired persons, to which every complaint under the section would be referred and until whose report no arrest would ordinarily be made. The institutional reformers arrived at compatible positions by a different road. The Malimath Committee on reforms of the criminal justice system, reporting in 2003, recommended that the cruelty offence be made bailable and compoundable, so that a matrimonial dispute could end by settlement rather than only by trial. The Committee of Petitions of the Rajya Sabha, in a report presented on 7 September 2011, recommended that the government consider making the offence compoundable. The Law Commission, in its 243rd Report of 2012 devoted to Section 498A, recommended that the offence be made compoundable with the permission of the court and subject to a cooling-off period of three months, while recommending that it remain non-bailable and that the safeguard against arbitrary arrest lie in strict observance of Sections 41 and 41A. The parliamentary committees had walked the same road before it: the Department-Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs recommended compoundability in its 111th Report of 2005 on the Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill and reiterated the recommendation in its 128th Report of 2008, and the Law Commission itself recorded that its own 154th Report had recommended making the offence compoundable and that the preponderance of the views it received in consultation favoured the change. The institutional centre of the debate, across two decades and bodies of very different composition, has therefore not been repeal and has not been the status quo: it has been the conversion of a non-compoundable offence into one that a court may allow the parties to settle, coupled with discipline on arrest. That the conversion has never been enacted, through governments of both national coalitions and through a complete recodification of the criminal law, is itself evidence about the debate: the political cost of being seen to dilute the provision has exceeded, for every government that has faced the choice, the institutional consensus in favour of diluting one feature of it. Organised advocacy has pressed the same case from outside the institutions: the Save Indian Family Foundation and allied men's rights organisations have campaigned for decades on the proposition that the dowry and cruelty provisions are deployed as leverage in matrimonial and property disputes, pointing to the low proportion of prosecutions that end in conviction as evidence that a large share of the complaints cannot have been genuine. The statistics this side uses are court-outcome statistics: acquittals, discharges and the long tails of pending trials, read as a measure of how many cases should never have begun.

The answer from the women's movement is that every element of that case mistakes a failure of proof for a falsehood, and that the baseline against which misuse is alleged is a system in which the underlying violence is registered only in fragments. The All India Democratic Women's Association, intervening in the proceedings that followed Rajesh Sharma, put the argument in its strongest institutional form: it submitted that the low conviction rate in these cases is not because the cases are false but because they are not properly investigated, evidence is not properly collected, statements of material witnesses are omitted, and a large number of cases are compromised under the pressure of delay and of the complainant's circumstances; it pointed to the fact that more than ninety per cent of cases under the section are chargesheeted after investigation, which it read as evidence of the pervasive nature of the violence rather than of its invention; and it argued that screening committees of lay persons would be extrajudicial bodies of questionable competence, akin to community panchayats, interposed between a woman and the criminal justice system. When a three-judge bench in Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. Union of India, decided in 2018, set aside the Family Welfare Committee directions as an exercise beyond the judicial role and restored the Arnesh Kumar safeguards as the governing discipline, the women's organisations' intervention was part of the record on which the committees fell. Individual voices have carried the same argument in print for longer. Flavia Agnes, the women's rights lawyer associated with the Majlis legal centre in Mumbai, wrote in The Tribune in 1999 that it is easy, sitting in a metropolis like Delhi, to pass judgment that the laws are being misused, and that the larger reality is one in which the laws are yet to reach the minimum standards of use. Kirti Singh, a former member of the Law Commission, has opposed dilution as counterproductive and pointed to the complaints that are never registered or never followed up as the system's larger error. Ruchira Goswami of the West Bengal National University of Juridical Sciences has made the methodological version of the argument: that the National Crime Records Bureau's statistics are not compiled in a form that can measure misuse, that they are routinely misinterpreted by treating every acquittal as indicative of a false case, and that an acquittal establishes only that the offence was not proved to the criminal standard. The statistics this side uses are registration and outcome statistics of a different kind: the continuing toll of dowry deaths recorded by the Bureau, the proportion of complaints that survive investigation into chargesheets, and the attrition of cases through compromise and delay.

Both cases are honestly statable, and the verdict this article reaches is narrower than either side's headline. The arrest jurisprudence is settled and justified: Arnesh Kumar's discipline, reaffirmed in 2018, addresses the one abuse that is documented beyond argument, the automatic arrest on complaint, and it does so without touching the offences themselves. The compoundability question is a genuine policy choice on which the Law Commission's middle position, compoundable with the court's permission after a cooling-off period, remains the most carefully reasoned proposal on the table, and it was not carried into the recodified offence, which retains the punishments of the Penal Code provisions and their non-compoundable character. The statistical war is unwinnable as conducted, because acquittal rates cannot show falsity and chargesheet rates cannot show truth; what the Bureau's figures can show, and do, is the scale of the underlying phenomenon against which any misuse occurs. And the whole debate, conducted at this intensity for three decades over the cruelty provision, throws the prohibition Act's own position into relief. Nobody campaigns about the misuse of the Dowry Prohibition Act, because the Dowry Prohibition Act is barely used. The misuse debate is a debate about a working law. This Act's problem is the opposite one.

## Six decades on: the honest ledger

The numbers that can be stated with confidence come from the National Crime Records Bureau's Crime in India reports, and they describe a system in which the prohibition Act is a minor instrument beside the penal provisions it feeds. For 2022, the Bureau recorded 6,450 dowry deaths across India. It recorded 13,479 cases registered under the Dowry Prohibition Act itself. And it recorded more than 140,000 cases of cruelty by a husband or his relatives under Section 498A, the single largest head of crime against women in the Bureau's classification, which is the quantitative form of the migration this article describes: for every case registered under the prohibition statute, roughly ten were registered under the cruelty provision. The Bureau's figures for cases registered under this Act in the years immediately before run in the same band, 12,826 in 2018, 13,307 in 2019 and 10,366 in 2020, so the Act's registration volume is stable at a level that is neither negligible nor remotely proportionate to the prevalence of the practice it bans. At the trial end, the Bureau's 2022 figures record 3,689 dowry death trials completed with 1,231 convictions, a conviction rate of 33.4 per cent in the gravest category of dowry case, and its figures put the dowry death cases pending at the end of that year at about sixty thousand. The Bureau also recorded 359 dowry death cases closed for want of evidence in circumstances where the complaints were found genuine, a figure that captures, in one cell of one table, the evidentiary fragility of offences committed inside houses against the household's own members. The Bureau's 2022 figures also let the attrition of a dowry death case be traced through the system in a single column of numbers. Of the 6,450 dowry deaths registered in that year, 4,148 cases were chargesheeted; the courts completed the trial of 3,689 dowry death cases in the year, counting cases carried forward from earlier registrations; and 1,231 of those trials ended in conviction. Each step of the funnel narrows for reasons the case law and the committee reports have already named: deaths inside houses leave thin forensic records, the witnesses are the household's members and the victim's family, cases are compromised or witnesses turn under the pressure of years of pendency, and the proximity and connection the prosecution must prove are fact-heavy inquiries conducted long after the event. The funnel is the enforcement system's own account of itself, and it describes attrition at every stage rather than failure at any single one.

The geography of the 2022 figures is as instructive as their size. Uttar Pradesh recorded 2,218 of the 6,450 dowry deaths, Bihar 1,057 and Madhya Pradesh 518, so that three states account for well over half the national total; the same states lead the registrations under this Act, with Uttar Pradesh recording 4,807 cases, Bihar 3,580 and Karnataka 2,224 of the 13,479. The concentration is consistent with everything else known about the practice's distribution, and it carries a consequence for reform: the Act's failure is national in design but its costs are regional in incidence, and a remedy addressed to the design, the symmetry, the lists, the officers, would do its work where the deaths are counted. The longer run of the Bureau's reports adds one qualification to the picture of unbroken failure, and honesty requires it. The dowry death figures describe a slow decline across the last decade of the series: the Bureau's reports record 7,634 dowry deaths in 2015 and 6,450 in 2022, a fall of some fifteen per cent across seven years in which the population and the number of marriages grew. A decline in the gravest outcome is not nothing, and it is consistent with several causes acting together, among them the deterrent presence of the dowry death offence and its presumption, the spread of women's education and earnings, and the slow movement of the practice's geography. What the decline does not show is any corresponding fall in the practice itself as the Act's own registrations measure it, nor any rise in the prohibition statute's share of the enforcement work; the cruelty registrations that dominate the system did not fall with the deaths. The fairest summary the figures support is that the cluster of laws built around this Act has pressed down the lethal end of the practice while leaving its ordinary operation substantially intact, and that the pressing was done by the penal provisions and their presumptions, the parts of the cluster that a complainant can use without confessing an offence of her own family's.

None of these numbers measures dowry itself. They measure the fraction of it that reaches a police register, and every serious student of the subject, on both sides of the misuse debate, agrees that the fraction is partial. What the numbers measure reliably is the shape of the enforcement system: a definitional statute generating thirteen thousand cases, a conduct offence generating ten times that, and a death toll, recorded by the state itself, of some eighteen women a day in the year counted.

Set against that ledger, the persistence of the practice is the fact that six decades of prohibition have to answer. Dowry was already condemned as a social evil by the reformers of the nineteenth century, was restrained unsuccessfully by provincial statutes, was prohibited nationally in 1961, and was made the object of escalating criminal sanctions in 1983, 1984 and 1986. It has not withered. Accounts of its trajectory differ in emphasis, some recording its spread into communities where it was once absent and its escalation with consumer goods and professional incomes, others recording regional and class variation and pockets of decline, but no serious account claims that the prohibition has suppressed the practice in the manner of a successful ban. The honest conclusion is the one the brief for this article required to be stated without decoration: prohibition alone has evidently not changed the behaviour. The arguments about what would change it are contested and under-evidenced. One family of proposals works inside the Act: enforce the lists of presents, so that the documentary boundary between dowry and present exists in fact; make the Dowry Prohibition Officers real, with notified powers, staff and a preventive practice; use Section 6's restitution machinery systematically rather than incidentally. A second family works around the Act: marriage registration with financial disclosure, women's independent property and earnings, and the long work of social change in the valuation of daughters and grooms. The evidence base for choosing between these families is thin, because the first has never been tried at full strength and the second operates on timescales that defeat measurement. What can be said is that the Act as designed does not test any of them, because its central transaction offence is one its natural complainants cannot safely use.

The verdict on the statute follows from its own text and record. The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 succeeded completely at the task its drafters treated as preliminary: it named the practice, defined it with a precision that has survived two widenings and sixty years of litigation, and supplied the definition on which the operative offences of cruelty and dowry death depend. It failed at the task its title announces. A prohibition statute under which some thirteen thousand cases a year are registered in a country of India's marriage volume, whose preventive officers exist largely on paper, whose documentary discipline is universally ignored, and whose principal offence punishes the victim's family at the same tariff as the offender's, is not an operative prohibition. It is a definitional source with penal appendages, and its history is the cleanest demonstration in the Indian statute book that a law's failure can be located in a single drafting choice. The choice was made in the joint sittings of May 1961, it was defended then and is defended still on the ground that the giving family is a participant and not only a victim, and no amendment in 1984, in 1986 or since has revisited it. Until one does, the law against dowry in India will continue to be enforced, insofar as it is enforced at all, by the provisions that were written into the criminal codes around this Act, and the Act itself will continue to do the quieter work of telling those provisions what dowry means. The recodification of the criminal law in 2023 left the Act standing untouched, its references to the repealed codes read across to their successors, which is the most recent confirmation of its settled role: Parliament rebuilt the Penal Code, the procedure code and the evidence Act around it, carried its definition's consumers into the new books, and did not disturb the pillar itself.

## For the person facing a demand

The analysis in this article has a practical residue, and the brief for the article requires it to be stated for the reader who arrives with a problem rather than a research question. A demand for dowry is an offence at the moment it is made, under Section 4 of this Act, whether or not anything is paid and whether or not the marriage proceeds; a family that records a demand, its words, its witnesses and its occasion, holds the foundation of a complaint that does not depend on any transfer. A transfer already made is documented, where documentation exists at all, by the lists of presents the 1985 Rules prescribe, by invoices, bank records and correspondence, and the absence of the statutory list is the commonest and most avoidable weakness in the files that advocates in this field receive; a family preparing for a wedding it expects to be contested can still make the lists, signed by both parties as the rules require, and convert the wedding's transfers into a record. Property that has moved divides into the categories the table in this article sets out, and the remedy follows the category: dowry held by another is recoverable through Section 6 and its direction for transfer on conviction; stridhan is the woman's own property, recoverable through the criminal breach of trust prosecution, a civil suit, or the monetary reliefs of the domestic violence statute, which also answers the demand itself as a form of domestic violence and can protect the woman's residence while the property questions are fought. The cruelty complaint under the penal code remains the route that prosecutes the conduct of a marriage, and it is the route that does not expose the complaining family to a charge of its own. Which of these instruments fits a given family's position is a question for advice on its facts; the design point, which the statute book itself makes, is that the routes exist in this order of practicality because the prohibition Act's own route is the one its symmetry obstructs.

Two features of the practical landscape complete the picture. The first is the Dowry Prohibition Officer as a point of first resort. Whatever the office's limits as an enforcement mechanism, the officer is the one functionary in the system whose statutory duty is prevention, and a family facing a demand before a wedding, the moment at which intervention can still change the outcome, is the family the office was designed for; a complaint to the officer also creates the contemporaneous record that later proceedings need. The second is the recognised welfare institution, which Section 7 allows to carry a complaint the family cannot carry itself, and which in practice means the established women's organisations and legal aid bodies that work in this field. Neither feature transforms the Act's position, and this article has not pretended otherwise. They mark the difference between the statute as a dead letter and the statute as a neglected one: the channels exist, they are used at a scale far below the practice they address, and the distance between the two is the measure of the design failure the pillar records.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Dowry Prohibition Act make an offence?

The Act makes four kinds of conduct an offence. Section 3 punishes giving or taking dowry, and abetting the giving or taking of it, with imprisonment of not less than five years and a fine of not less than fifteen thousand rupees or the value of the dowry, whichever is more. Section 4 punishes demanding dowry from the parents, other relatives or guardian of a bride or bridegroom, whether or not anything is paid. Section 4A punishes offering property or money as consideration for a marriage through an advertisement, and printing or circulating such an advertisement. Section 6(2) punishes the failure to transfer dowry, held in trust for the woman, to her within the period the Act allows.

### Q: What counts as dowry under the Act?

Dowry means any property or valuable security given or agreed to be given, directly or indirectly, by one party to a marriage to the other party, or by the parents of either party or by any other person, to either party or to any other person, at or before or any time after the marriage in connection with the marriage of the parties. The definition covers cash, jewellery, land, vehicles and documents creating legal rights, and it covers promises as well as completed transfers, in either direction between the families. It does not include dower or mahr in the case of persons to whom the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) applies.

### Q: Is the person who gives dowry also punished?

Yes. Section 3 punishes giving, taking and abetting in a single clause, and the giver faces the same minimum as the taker: imprisonment of not less than five years and a fine of not less than fifteen thousand rupees or the value of the dowry, whichever is more, subject to the court's power to impose less than five years for adequate and special reasons recorded in the judgment. Section 7(3) protects a statement made by the person aggrieved from itself founding a prosecution of that person, but it does not decriminalise the payment. This symmetry is the Act's most debated feature and the main reason its complaints are so few.

### Q: Are wedding gifts covered by the dowry law?

Presents given at the time of the marriage are protected by Section 3(2) if two conditions are met: no demand was made for them, and they are entered in a list of presents maintained in accordance with the rules made under the Act. Presents given to the bridegroom by or on behalf of the bride or her relatives carry two further conditions: they must be customary in nature, and their value must not be excessive having regard to the financial status of the giver. A transfer that was demanded is not saved by being called a gift, and a present that was never listed loses the exemption's protection, which is why the list matters so much in practice.

### Q: Who is a Dowry Prohibition Officer?

A Dowry Prohibition Officer is an officer appointed by a state government under Section 8B of the Act, by notification in the Official Gazette, for the areas the notification specifies. The officer's functions are to see that the provisions of the Act are complied with, to prevent, as far as possible, the taking or abetting of the taking or the demanding of dowry, to collect such evidence as may be necessary for the prosecution of persons committing offences under the Act, and to perform additional functions the state government assigns or the rules prescribe. The state may also confer specified powers of a police officer on the officer. An advisory board of not more than five social welfare workers, at least two of them women, assists each officer.

### Q: What is the burden of proof in a dowry case?

The ordinary rule applies at the start: the prosecution must establish the foundations of the case, the marriage, and the transfer or the demand. Section 8A then reverses the burden for the two offences Parliament treated as the aggressor's. Where a person is prosecuted for taking or abetting the taking of dowry under Section 3, or for demanding dowry under Section 4, the burden of proving that he had not committed the offence is on him. The reverse burden does not apply to a prosecution for giving, which the prosecution must prove in the ordinary way. In dowry death cases a separate and stronger device operates: the presumption in the evidence law, under which the court shall presume that the accused caused the death once the foundational facts are shown.

### Q: How does the dowry law relate to dowry death?

Dowry death is a separate offence, created by Section 304B of the Indian Penal Code in 1986 and carried into Section 80 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. It applies where a woman dies by burns or bodily injury or otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of her marriage, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment in connection with a demand for dowry; the husband or relative responsible is deemed to have caused the death, and the punishment runs from seven years to imprisonment for life. The connection with this Act is definitional: the word dowry in the dowry death provision bears the meaning Section 2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act gives it, and the mandatory presumption in the evidence law operates on the same foundation.

### Q: Why are convictions under the Dowry Prohibition Act so rare?

The first reason is structural: the family best placed to complain is the family that gave the dowry, and giving is an offence under Section 3 carrying the same minimum sentence as taking, so a complaint under this Act begins with a confession. Section 7(3) protects the complainant's statement, not the payment. The second reason is substitution: since 1983 the cruelty offence in the criminal code has offered complainants a route that requires proof of conduct rather than of a completed transaction and exposes only the husband's side, so dowry grievances travel there instead. The third reason is evidence: the transactions happen inside families, the presents lists that would document them are almost never kept, and witnesses are relatives of one side or the other.

### Q: What is the punishment for demanding dowry?

Section 4 punishes demanding dowry, directly or indirectly, from the parents or other relatives or guardian of a bride or bridegroom, with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than six months but which may extend to two years, and with fine which may extend to ten thousand rupees. A proviso permits the court to impose less than six months for adequate and special reasons mentioned in the judgment. No payment is required for the offence to be complete: the demand itself is the crime, and it is complete even where the party from whom it is made refuses and nothing is ever paid.

### Q: What must happen to dowry that is received by someone other than the woman?

Section 6 requires the recipient to transfer it to the woman within three months: within three months after the date of the marriage if it was received before the marriage, within three months after receipt if it was received at or after the marriage, and within three months after she turns eighteen if it was received while she was a minor. Until transfer, the recipient holds the dowry in trust for her benefit. Failure to transfer is an offence under Section 6(2), punishable with imprisonment of six months to two years, or a fine of five thousand to ten thousand rupees, or both, and on conviction the court must direct transfer of the property under Section 6(3A).

### Q: Can a dowry agreement be enforced in court?

No. Section 5 of the Act provides in terms that any agreement for the giving or taking of dowry shall be void. A dowry bargain therefore cannot be sued upon by either side: the groom's family cannot recover an unpaid instalment of an agreed dowry, and the bride's family cannot enforce a promise connected with the bargain. The voidness is part of the Act's design against the transaction itself, denying the bargain the law's assistance at every stage. One consequence is practical rather than doctrinal: because the agreement is void and the transaction criminal, dowry moves in cash and kind without enforceable documentation, which deepens the evidentiary difficulty of prosecuting it later.

### Q: Is advertising a marriage offer with property or money an offence?

Yes. Section 4A, inserted by the amendment Act of 1986, punishes any person who offers, through an advertisement in any newspaper, periodical, journal or other media, any share in property or money, or a share in any business or other interest, as consideration for the marriage of a son, daughter or other relative. The punishment is imprisonment of not less than six months and up to five years, or a fine of up to fifteen thousand rupees, with a proviso for a lesser sentence on reasons recorded. Printing, publishing or circulating such an advertisement is an offence under the same section. The provision was aimed at matrimonial advertisements that quoted property and income as inducements in the marriage market.

### Q: Who can set a dowry prosecution in motion?

Section 7 restricts the routes. A court may take cognizance of an offence under the Act only upon its own knowledge, upon a police report of the facts constituting the offence, or upon a complaint by the person aggrieved, by a parent or other relative of that person, or by a recognised welfare institution or organisation recognised by the central or the state government for the purpose. A stranger to the family cannot found a prosecution by complaint. Trials are held only before a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, who may pass any sentence the Act authorises, and no period of limitation applies to offences under the Act.

### Q: Are offences under the Dowry Prohibition Act bailable or compoundable?

Every offence under the Act is non-bailable and non-compoundable, under Section 8(2). The classification has a history: the substituted Section 8 of 1984 declared the offences bailable and non-compoundable, and the amendment Act of 1986 substituted non-bailable for bailable with effect from 19 November 1986. Section 8 also contains the arrest qualification that defines the Act's procedure: the criminal procedure code applies to the offences as if they were cognizable for the purposes of investigation, but no arrest under the Act may be made without a warrant or the order of a magistrate. The offences are therefore grave in classification while their arrests remain judicially controlled.

### Q: What are the lists of presents, and who must maintain them?

The lists are the records required by the Dowry Prohibition (Maintenance of Lists of Presents to the Bride and Bridegroom) Rules, 1985, made under Section 9 of the Act. The bride maintains the list of presents given to the bride, and the bridegroom maintains the list of presents given to the bridegroom. Each list is prepared at the time of the marriage or as soon as possible after it, in writing, and contains a brief description of each present, its approximate value, the name of the person who gave it and, where the giver is a relative, the relationship. Each list is signed by both the bride and the bridegroom. A listed present is what keeps a wedding gift inside the Section 3(2) exemption.

### Q: Does the Dowry Prohibition Act apply to marriages in every religion?

Yes. The Act attaches to the transaction rather than to the ceremony, and it applies to marriages across all religions and forms of marriage in India. Its one religious carve-out sits inside the definition: dowry does not include dower or mahr in the case of persons to whom the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) applies. Mahr is excluded because it is an obligation owed to the woman herself as an incident of the marriage contract, running in her favour, which is the opposite direction from a dowry exaction. The exclusion covers dower or mahr alone. A payment demanded from a bride's family in a Muslim marriage, by whatever name it is called, is within the Act if it bears the required connection with the marriage.

### Q: What did the amendments of 1984 and 1986 change in the Act?

The Dowry Prohibition (Amendment) Act, 1984, Act 63 of 1984, in force from 2 October 1985, substituted the words in connection with the marriage for as consideration for the marriage in the definition, rebuilt the presents exemption in Section 3(2) with its list condition, raised the Section 3 punishment to a minimum of five years with a minimum fine tied to the dowry's value, removed the requirement of prior government sanction that had guarded the demand offence, shortened the Section 6 transfer period from one year to three months, and substituted Sections 7 and 8. The Dowry Prohibition (Amendment) Act, 1986, Act 43 of 1986, in force from 19 November 1986, extended the definition to transfers at any time after the marriage, recast the demand punishment, inserted the advertisement offence in Section 4A, made the offences non-bailable, and inserted the reverse burden in Section 8A, the Dowry Prohibition Officers in Section 8B and the complainant's statement shield in Section 7(3).

### Q: Can a demand made years after the wedding still be dowry?

Yes, if it is connected with the marriage. The definition reaches transfers and demands at or before or any time after the marriage, and the amendment Act of 1986 widened the closing words deliberately. A three-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Rajinder Singh v. State of Punjab, decided in 2015, held that money or property demanded at any of those points is a dowry demand where it is reasonably connected to the marriage, unless the facts clearly show no such connection, and that the reason for the demand is immaterial. That holding displaced the stricter approach of Appasaheb v. State of Maharashtra, decided in 2007, under which a demand for money for financial stringency or domestic expenses was held not to be dowry. Customary payments, such as those made at the birth of a child, stand outside the definition on separate authority.

### Q: How can a woman recover dowry or stridhan that is being withheld?

Several routes exist, and they are not interchangeable. For dowry received by another person in connection with her marriage, Section 6 of this Act obliges the holder to transfer it to her within three months, a complaint under Section 6(2) prosecutes the failure, and on conviction the court must direct transfer under Section 6(3A), with the value recoverable as a fine if the direction is defied. For stridhan, her own absolute property, the routes are a prosecution for criminal breach of trust where the holder dishonestly misappropriates it, on the principle the Supreme Court settled in Pratibha Rani v. Suraj Kumar in 1985, a civil suit for its return, and an application under the domestic violence statute for monetary relief and the return of her property, since withholding stridhan is a form of economic abuse under that Act.

### Q: What is the difference between dowry and stridhan?

Dowry is value transferred in connection with a marriage, by either family or by any person, to either party or to any other person, which the Act prohibits; the transfer is the offence, and where dowry is received by someone other than the woman, the Act impresses it with a trust and requires its transfer to her. Stridhan is the woman's own property: gifts made to her, property she earns, and property she acquires by inheritance, partition or settlement, which she owns in absolute terms and which no marriage transfers to her husband or his family. A holder of stridhan holds it in trust for her and must return it on demand. The practical importance of the distinction is that a claim to stridhan is a claim to her own property and incriminates nobody in her family, while a dowry complaint reports a transaction her family committed.
