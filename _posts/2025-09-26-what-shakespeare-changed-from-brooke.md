## The Verdict First: A Kept Skeleton, a Transformed Play

Almost everything that happens in Romeo and Juliet happens in Arthur Brooke's poem first. The feast, the meeting, the secret marriage, the street fight, the banishment, the forced match with Paris, the sleeping potion, the failed letter, the tomb, the double suicide, the reconciliation of the houses: the plot of the play is the plot of The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet, the 3,020 line narrative poem Brooke published in 1562, more than three decades before the play reached the stage. Anyone who credits the story itself to Shakespeare is wrong, and the change ledger set out in this article begins by conceding the point in full. What Shakespeare changed from Brooke is not the sequence of events. It is everything that decides what those events mean: the calendar they run on, the ages of the people caught in them, the size and voice of the supporting figures, the fate of everyone left alive at the end, and above all the moral lighting in which the lovers are seen. Brooke's poem opens with a prose preface that condemns its hero and heroine before the story starts. The play opens with a sonnet that condemns nobody. Between those two openings lies the whole transformation.

![What Shakespeare changed from Brooke, the entries of the InsightCrunch change ledger - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict defended here is that the changes form a system, not a scatter. Read one by one, the entries in the ledger can look like routine theatrical tightening. Read together, they all push in three directions at once: toward speed, toward sympathy, and toward the stage's embodied present tense. The compression of months into days makes haste, rather than fortune, the tragedy's visible engine. The lowering of Juliet's age from sixteen to not yet fourteen redistributes blame from the lovers to the adults arranging their lives. The enlargement of Mercutio and the Nurse converts narrative information into conflict and comedy that the poem never stages. The stripping of Brooke's moral frame removes the verdict the poem had passed in advance, so the audience must reach its own. And the repeal of the poem's closing punishments, replaced by pardons and golden statues, changes the ending from a penal settlement into a civic one. Where the changes cost the play something real, the ledger says so: the poem's months gave the marriage a lived duration that four days cannot supply, and that loss is recorded alongside the gains. But judged by effect, which is the only honest court for the question of improvement, the transformation is a creative act of the first order performed on borrowed material. Shakespeare's originality in this play is located precisely where the cliché refuses to look: not in the plot he took, but in the pressures he applied to it.

The evidence base is the comparison itself. Quotations from the play follow René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, cited by act, scene, and line. Brooke's poem, [Arthur Brooke's 'Romeus and Juliet' (1562)](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/), receives a study of its own elsewhere in this series; the present article treats it strictly as the play's working source, cited from the text printed in Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, the standard scholarly source edition against which the comparison is made. The critics in dispute are Bullough himself, whose introduction to the poem catalogues the changes with an editor's patience; Kenneth Muir, whose study of the sources argues that the dramatist followed Brooke's sequence with unusual fidelity; and Weis, whose editorial work shows the debt running down to the level of the individual line. Their disagreement about how dependent the play really is runs through the sections that follow and is settled, on the ledger's evidence, near the end.

## The Scaffold He Kept: Plot, Order, and Borrowed Lines

The first fact the ledger must record is how much was not changed. Brooke's poem and the play share not only a plot but a plot in the same order. A young man of one feuding house, disappointed in an earlier love, is taken to a feast at the enemy's house to cure him by comparison. He sees the daughter of that house and forgets the first love on the spot. They meet, they exchange vows at her window, a friar marries them in secret, as much for reasons of statecraft as for pity, a street quarrel kills the hero's friend and then the heroine's cousin at the hero's hand, the hero is banished, the father presses a new marriage on the daughter, the friar supplies a sleeping potion, the message miscarries, the hero buys poison and dies at the tomb, the heroine wakes and follows him, and the feuding fathers are reconciled over the bodies. Remove any one of those beats from Brooke and the poem collapses; remove any one from the play and the play collapses in the same place. The dependence is structural, and no serious account of the play's making can start anywhere else.

Kenneth Muir, in The Sources of Shakespeare's Plays, pressed this fidelity harder than any critic before him. Muir's Shakespeare is a dramatist who worked with Brooke's poem effectively open beside him, following its sequence scene by scene and sometimes lifting its matter with a closeness that verges on transcription. The point matters because it disciplines every claim made for the changes. Shakespeare was not ransacking an old poem for a premise and then inventing freely. He was converting, steadily and in order, a narrative he trusted. The changes that follow in this article are therefore best understood as decisions taken against a respected text, each one a deliberate departure from something the dramatist had chosen to keep in every other respect. A change made by a writer who demonstrably follows his source elsewhere carries more meaning than the same change made by a writer inventing from nothing. The fidelity is the background that makes the departures legible.

Weis's editorial work deepens the point from fidelity of structure to fidelity of language. The notes to his edition repeatedly set lines of the play beside couplets of the poem and show the dramatist translating Brooke's narrative statements into spoken verse, keeping an image, a comparison, even a turn of phrasing, while changing everything about its delivery. The friar's reasoning, the lovers' complaints of fortune, the descriptions of the feast and the tomb all carry such deposits. This is the level at which the improvement question becomes genuinely difficult, because a borrowed line raised into a famous speech is neither pure invention nor mere copying. The ledger's answer is to grade the changes by their effects rather than by their novelty. A change counts as a transformation when it alters what an audience feels, judges, or understands at that point in the story, whether or not its raw material stood in the poem already. By that standard, even some kept lines belong in the ledger, because the play makes them do work the poem never asked of them.

### How closely does the play follow Brooke's order of events?

Beat for beat, and in the poem's own sequence, from the feast to the reconciliation. The dramatist rearranges almost nothing of consequence in the story's spine, and whole stretches of the play convert Brooke's narration into dialogue in the order the poem presents it. The fidelity is the method: a trusted scaffold, altered at chosen joints.

That method explains the peculiar double character of the play's originality, which the remaining sections examine entry by entry. The poem Shakespeare read was written in poulter's measure, the alternating twelve and fourteen syllable couplets that give Brooke's narrative its rolling, sententious gait, and it was mediated by a narrator who addresses the reader directly, warns the young, and apostrophizes the lovers. The play abolishes the narrator, divides his functions among a Chorus that soon falls silent and the characters themselves, and sets every event in the present tense of performance. Before any single change of fact is counted, that change of medium is the largest entry in the ledger, because it converts everything it touches. Narration reports a marriage; the stage performs one. The sections below take the factual changes in turn, beginning with the instrument that powers all the others, the ledger itself.

Fourteen entries, and a pattern runs through them that no single row announces. Every change either removes a delay, removes a judgment, or removes a distance. The delays go first: months become days, three days of sleep become forty-two hours, a season of meetings becomes two nights. The judgments go next: the preface's condemnation, the narrator's warnings, the closing sentences on the Nurse, the Friar, and the apothecary all disappear or soften into pardon. And the distances go last: narration becomes scene, reported conversation becomes a sonnet exchanged face to face, a described purchase becomes a haggle conducted in front of the audience. What remains after the removals is the poem's story running at stage speed, in stage light, with its moral verdicts struck out and its minor figures promoted into people. The ledger's remaining sections argue the entries in groups, starting with the change that governs the physics of all the rest.

The effect column is the ledger's argument, and it repays being read vertically as well as across. Read down the column and the fourteen entries cease to be separate observations and become three campaigns conducted simultaneously. The first campaign is against time: the calendar, the sleep, and the season of meetings are all shortened, until the story's events stand close enough together for each one to cause the next in full view of the audience. The second campaign is against judgment: the preface, the narrator, and the closing sentences are struck out or softened, until no voice in the work is licensed to condemn the lovers on the audience's behalf. The third campaign is against mediation: narration gives way to enacted scene at the feast, at the window, in the duel, in the apothecary's shop, and at the tomb, until everything that matters happens in front of witnesses. The sections that follow take the campaigns in the order the play itself feels them, time first, because time is the pressure under which the other two campaigns operate.

## Months Into Days: The Compression and What It Powers

Brooke's lovers have time. The poem spreads its action across months: the courtship at the feast, the window courtship that follows, the secret marriage, and then a settled season in which the married pair meet by night, with the Nurse's connivance and a ladder of cords, for some three months before the street quarrel destroys their arrangement. Romeus's exile in Mantua is likewise a matter of duration, a separation endured across letters and reports, and Juliet's counterfeit death is planned against a calendar with room in it. The poem's world is one in which the lovers' chief enemy is circumstance wearing the mask of fortune, and its long calendar is part of its meaning: this is a marriage that exists, that has a past and habits, before it is destroyed.

The play takes that calendar away. From the first brawl on a Sunday morning to the dawn that finds the bodies in the tomb, Shakespeare's action occupies some four to five days, and the text keeps striking the hours like a clock the audience can hear. The feast is Sunday night; the marriage is Monday afternoon; the brawl and the banishment fall on Monday; Capulet sets the wedding for Thursday and then, in a spasm of pleased impatience, pulls it forward: "Wednesday is too soon; / A' Thursday let it be" (3.4), and later forward again to Wednesday itself. Juliet drinks the potion on Tuesday night. Romeo hears the false report, buys his poison, and reaches the tomb on Wednesday night. A dedicated study of this machinery in the play, [Time and Haste in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/01/12/time-and-haste-romeo-and-juliet/), maps the clock in full; the point for the ledger is narrower. None of this urgency is in Brooke. It is the single largest factual change Shakespeare made, and every other change in the ledger either serves it or is made necessary by it.

Bullough, cataloguing the changes, treated the compression as the clearest case of the dramatist's instinct improving his material, and the effects support him. Compression converts the story's causality. In the poem, the catastrophe can be read as the slow working of ill fortune across a season, which is how Brooke's narrator reads it, as a fable of fortune's wheel. In the play, events crowd so tightly that each one visibly causes the next: the wedding is moved a day nearer, so the potion is drunk a night earlier, so the letter's failure falls inside a window too narrow for a second attempt, so Romeo arrives at the tomb hours before Juliet wakes. Haste is not a theme the play discusses; it is the mechanism the audience watches. Capulet, meaning to celebrate, kills his daughter by saving a day. The Friar, whose plan is sound on any roomier calendar, is ruined by arithmetic. When the Prince closes the action by observing that heaven finds means "to kill your joys with love" (5.3), the means he names have been, at every joint, a matter of hours.

The compression also changes the lovers. Brooke's pair are, by the time of the crisis, an established married couple with a season of shared life behind them, and the poem's grief at their end is partly the grief of a household broken. Shakespeare's pair have known each other for four days, have spent one night together as husband and wife, and are carried through the action at a speed that forbids reflection. Their love is therefore presented as a condition of youth and velocity rather than of settled attachment, which is why the play can be read both as the supreme celebration of young love and as its supreme warning: the same compression supplies the evidence for both readings. The change also disciplines every scene. Because the calendar has no slack, no scene in the play is allowed to be merely decorative; each one advances the clock or tightens it.

### What happens to the marriage when months of meetings become a single night?

It loses its duration and gains its intensity. Brooke's lovers are a couple with a past; the play's lovers have a wedding, one dawn parting, and no ordinary life at all. The marriage exists in the play as a vow and a secret rather than as a household, which concentrates its meaning into the scenes of promising and parting.

The honest ledger also records what the compression cost. A marriage of three months' standing carries a weight of habit, negotiation, and ordinary tenderness that a four day marriage cannot demonstrate, and Brooke's poem, for all its lumbering couplets, gives its lovers something the play cannot: evidence of what their life together was actually like. The play answers the loss with intensity of focus, and most audiences have accepted the trade without noticing it was offered. But the trade is real, and it explains a persistent undertow in the play's criticism, the suspicion that the lovers are destroyed before they have properly lived. That suspicion is the compression's shadow. Shakespeare chose the shadow deliberately, because the alternative, a leisurely married love destroyed by slow mischance, is Brooke's poem, and it has never held a stage.

## Three Days Become Two and Forty Hours: The Potion's Shorter Sleep

Inside the larger compression sits a smaller one, exact enough to serve as the ledger's measuring instrument. Brooke's friar gives Juliet a sleeping draught whose effect lasts three days: long enough, in the poem's calendar, for the funeral to proceed, for word to travel, and for the machinery of rescue to be set in motion at a narrative pace. Shakespeare's Friar states his terms in a single number, and the number is small. Handing Juliet the vial, he promises that she will lie as if dead while "two and forty hours" run their course: "And in this borrowed likeness of shrunk death / Thou shalt continue two and forty hours, / And then awake as from a pleasant sleep" (4.1). Forty-two hours is not a rounded poetic figure. It is a deadline engineered to fit the compressed calendar, and it converts the Friar's plan from a scheme with margins into a scheme with none.

The effect propagates through the fifth act. A three day sleep could survive a delayed letter, because a second message, a personal visit, even a rumour could arrive inside three days. A forty-two hour sleep cannot survive the failure of Friar John's errand, which is itself a small masterpiece of the compressed world: John was shut up by the health officers of Mantua, "the searchers of the town, / Suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Sealed up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2). The plague delay is Brooke's invention, kept by Shakespeare; what Shakespeare changed is the time available for it to matter in. In the poem the delay is an episode. In the play it is the whole catastrophe, because forty-two hours leave no room for recovery from a single accident. The ledger's pattern holds again: remove a delay, and fortune's role shrinks while human timing's role grows. The tragedy becomes something that happens because a message and a waking miss each other by hours, which is a far more modern terror than the wheel of fortune Brooke's narrator invokes.

There is a subtler consequence for Juliet. Brooke's heroine sleeps through three days of her own funeral and its aftermath, a passive figure at the centre of her own stratagem. Shakespeare's heroine wakes inside a tomb that already contains two corpses and a dying poisoner, and her remaining lines are few, fast, and decisive: "I will kiss thy lips; / Haply some poison yet doth hang on them" (5.3), and then the dagger. The shorter sleep means she wakes into the wreckage rather than after it, and the play's last movement belongs to her reactions rather than to the Friar's explanations. The Friar's own arrival, his offer of a convent, "I'll dispose of thee / Among a sisterhood of holy nuns" (5.3), is refused in a handful of words. Compression, again, has redistributed agency: taken time away from the plan, and given the ending to the woman the plan was meant to save.

## Sixteen Becomes Not Yet Fourteen: Juliet's Age

Brooke states his heroine's age plainly: Juliet is sixteen, a young woman of marriageable years by the standards of her world, wooed by a Paris her parents favour and old enough, in the poem's presentation, to bear a large share of responsibility for the choices she makes. Brooke's preface in fact loads that responsibility onto her. Shakespeare changed the number. His Juliet is a fortnight short of fourteen, and the play insists on the figure with unusual repetition, as if the dramatist wanted no auditor to miss the alteration. The Nurse computes it by the earthquake and the weaning: "She's not fourteen. I'll lay fourteen of my teeth, / And yet, to my teen be it spoken, I have but four, / She is not fourteen" (1.3). Lady Capulet, urging the match, removes any doubt about what the age means: "By my count, / I was your mother much upon these years / That you are now a maid" (1.3). Capulet himself, in the play's first negotiation with Paris, states the fact as an objection: "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years. / Let two more summers wither in their pride / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride" (1.2). Paris's answer, "Younger than she are happy mothers made" (1.2), and Capulet's rejoinder, "And too soon marred are those so early made" (1.2), set the terms the rest of the play will dishonour.

The effect of the lowered age is not mainly pathos, though pathos follows. It is a redistribution of culpability. A sixteen year old who defies her parents, marries in secret, and feigns death can be held, in the moral accounting Brooke's preface performs, substantially answerable for her end. A girl of thirteen, pressed toward a bridegroom she has not chosen, denied any hearing by either parent, and left with a friar's vial as her only instrument, cannot carry the same weight of blame, and the play's adults arrange themselves around her accordingly: a father who moves her wedding day nearer as a surprise, a mother who answers her plea with "Talk not to me, for I'll not speak a word. / Do as thou wilt, for I have done with thee" (3.5), a nurse who advises bigamy as prudence. The age change and the moral change are the same change seen from two sides. Lower the heroine's age, and the preface's verdict on her becomes obscene; the play did not need to argue with Brooke's morality, because it had already altered the fact on which that morality rested.

The age change also serves the compression. Thirteen is an age at which four days can plausibly contain a whole emotional life, because at thirteen there is no settled self for events to accumulate against; everything is first. Brooke's sixteen year old, already a person of formed habits in a courtship culture, requires the poem's longer calendar to change as much as the story needs her to change. Shakespeare's Juliet moves from obedience to defiance, from "I'll look to like, if looking liking move" (1.3) to "If all else fail, myself have power to die" (4.1), inside four days, and the audience accepts the speed of the transformation partly because the play has told it, three times in the first act, exactly how young she is. The number is load-bearing. Remove it, restore Brooke's sixteen, and both the moral geometry and the tempo of the play sag toward the poem's.

## From a Cold Hand to the Third Voice: Mercutio Enlarged

Of all the entries in the ledger, Mercutio's is the one where the word invention comes closest to being accurate, and even here the seed is Brooke's. In the poem, Mercutio exists for a single reported moment at the Capulet feast. Seated near Juliet as the dancing gives way to conversation, he takes her hand, and the poem pauses on a small sensory fact: his hand is cold as ice, where the hand of the disguised Romeus, taken next, is warm. It is a courtier's cameo, a few lines long, functioning in the poem as a foil that lets the heroine, and the reader, register by contrast the living warmth of the hero. Mercutio has no speech, no duel, no death. He is a temperature.

Shakespeare built the play's third voice out of that temperature. His Mercutio is the Prince's kinsman and Romeo's intimate, the speaker of the Queen Mab speech (1.4), the presiding wit of the street scenes, the provocateur of the quarrel in Act 3, and the author of the play's most famous curse. The creation is analysed as a source problem in its own right in [Mercutio Across the Sources: An Invention](/2026/01/30/mercutio-invention-across-sources/); the ledger entry is about effect, and the effects are structural. First, the enlarged Mercutio gives the play's first half a centre of intelligence outside the lovers, a voice that can describe Romeo's love from a position of amused detachment: "You are a lover. Borrow Cupid's wings, / And soar with them above a common bound" (1.4). Brooke's narrator performs that describing function from outside the action. Shakespeare moves it inside, into a character who can later be killed for his trouble, which converts commentary into stake. Second, Mercutio's enlargement rebuilds the street fight, as the ledger's later section shows: because he exists at full size, Tybalt's quarrel can kill him, and his death, not any property of the feud in the abstract, is what turns the comedy off. His dying joke, "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1), and his thrice repeated sentence on both families, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), supply the hinge on which the whole play swings from its first genre to its second. Brooke's poem has no hinge of this kind; its tone darkens gradually, at narrative speed. The play turns in a single scene because a single invented character dies in it.

### What does Brooke's poem actually give Mercutio to do?

Almost nothing, and the almost is the point. He attends the feast, sits near Juliet, and lends her a hand the poem calls cold beside Romeus's warmth. No dialogue, no function in the quarrel, no death. All else the name carries, the wit, the bawdry, the curse, the grave man pun, is the dramatist's construction built on one detail.

The enlargement also solved a problem the poem never faces. A narrative can slow down for description; a stage play about haste cannot. Mercutio's speeches are the play's permitted expansions, the places where language is allowed to luxuriate, precisely because they are performances by a character whose profession is delay. Queen Mab delays the walk to the feast. The wit combat of Act 2 delays the Nurse's errand. Each delay is pleasurable in itself and fatal in aggregate, because the clock the compression installed is running underneath them. When Mercutio dies, the play loses its licence to linger, and the remaining acts are noticeably bare of ornament: the verse itself seems to hurry. A figure invented from a cold hand ends up governing the play's rhythm as well as its tone, which is as clear a demonstration as the ledger offers that Shakespeare's changes are load-bearing rather than decorative.

## The Nurse Rebuilt: Go-Between Into Confidante, Confidante Into Counsel of Surrender

Brooke's Nurse is an instrument of the plot and little more. She serves as the lovers' messenger and facilitator, the channel through which meetings are arranged, and the poem gives her neither a history nor a voice that any reader remembers. She does her narrative work and is done. Shakespeare's Nurse is among the most fully imagined comic figures in the early plays: a widow with a dead daughter of Juliet's age, a body of memories organized around weaning and earthquakes and a husband's joke at a toddler's expense, and a command of delay as an art form. Her first great speech, the weaning narrative of Act 1 Scene 3, is pure addition, and it does quiet structural work: it establishes that Juliet's effective childhood was spent in this woman's care, that the emotional centre of the Capulet nursery is a servant, and that the girl's real confidante will never be her mother. Lady Capulet must ask the Nurse to leave before she can raise the subject of marriage, and then call her back because the conversation cannot proceed without her: the household's true wiring is shown in a stage direction's worth of business.

The rebuilding pays its largest dividend in the scenes of errand and report. Where Brooke narrates that messages passed, the play stages the passing, and the Nurse's delays, her aching bones, her teasing withholdings in Act 2 Scene 5, "Jesu, what haste? Can you not stay awhile? / Do you not see that I am out of breath?" (2.5), turn transmission itself into drama. The full anatomy of that reinvention is the subject of [How Shakespeare Reinvented the Nurse](/2026/01/23/shakespeare-reinvented-the-nurse/); the ledger records the culminating effect, which falls in Act 3 Scene 5. Brooke's nurse never advises her charge to commit bigamy. Shakespeare's Nurse, confronted with the wreckage of the marriage she brokered, counsels exactly that: "I think it best you married with the County. / O, he's a lovely gentleman! / Romeo's a dishclout to him" (3.5). The advice is practical, affectionate, and annihilating, and it lands with its full force only because the figure speaking it has been built, across three acts, into the person Juliet trusts most. Juliet's response, "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend!" (3.5), spoken aside, and her decision to lie to the Nurse from that moment, mark the true end of her childhood. The poem achieves no comparable betrayal, because the poem never gave the Nurse enough self to betray anyone with. Enlargement, in this entry of the ledger, is not decoration added to a function. It is the precondition of one of the play's deepest wounds.

The Nurse's enlargement also completes the play's reallocation of its moral weight. Brooke distributes judgment through his narrator; the play distributes it through relationships, and the Nurse is the relationship in which judgment arrives as love's failure rather than as law's sentence. She is never punished, never exposed, never even answered. She is simply, from the moment of her counsel, outside the secret she created, and her exclusion is the quietest sentence the play passes on anyone.

## The Friar Softened: Suspect Confessor Into Fallible Holy Man

Brooke's preface warns its readers against, among the instruments of the tragedy, "superstitious friars", and the poem's Friar Laurence operates under that cloud. He is a Franciscan of learning and local repute whom the lovers use as their confessor and go-between, and the poem treats his compliance as part of the machinery of misconduct: he marries the pair in secret, supplies the potion, and shares, in the narrator's accounting, the responsibility for where secrecy leads. Shakespeare kept the friar's functions entire and changed his moral valence at every point. His Friar announces a public spirited motive for the marriage in terms Brooke's poem gestures at but the play makes into a thesis: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3). He is given the play's most famous sententious wisdom and its most famous warning, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6), which places the play's supposed moralising in the mouth of a participant rather than a narrator, where it can be ignored by the young and weighed by the audience. He is allowed error, panic, and a moment of frankly admitted fear in the tomb, "I dare no longer stay" (5.3), and he is then acquitted by the play's highest authority in terms that close the question: "We still have known thee for a holy man" (5.3).

The softening has a precise dramaturgical function. Brooke's moral scheme needs its friar to be suspect, because in that scheme the catastrophe must have human agents whose misconduct explains it. Shakespeare's scheme, having removed the preface and its verdicts, cannot use a villainous or foolish friar without rebuilding the moral machinery it had dismantled. What it needs instead is a competent, well intentioned planner whose plan is defeated by hours and infection, so that responsibility drains out of persons and into timing. The softened Friar is that planner. His herb speech in Act 2 Scene 3, with its doctrine that virtue and vice inhere in the use of things rather than in the things themselves, "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, / And vice sometime by action dignified" (2.3), quietly replaces Brooke's whole moral apparatus with a more interesting one: in the play, nothing and nobody is condemned in kind, only in application and outcome. The potion, the marriage, the friar, the haste itself are all instruments whose moral character is decided by what they meet. That philosophy is the play's own, spoken by its most trusted voice, and it is the exact antithesis of the poem's preface, where everything is decided in advance.

## The Preface Against the Prologue: The Moral Frame Stripped

The most quoted sentences in Brooke's volume are not in the poem. They stand in the prose address "To the Reader" that precedes it, where Brooke, presenting his translation, instructs his audience how to receive the tale: as a warning. The lovers are introduced there as "a couple of unfortunate lovers" who gave themselves to "unhonest desire", who neglected "the authority and advice of parents and friends", and who confided in "drunken gossips and superstitious friars", described as the naturally fit instruments of unchastity. The preface promises that the poem will show the "shameful" end of such conduct and commends the story as a glass in which young readers may see their own obedience reflected. Before a single couplet is read, the verdict is in: desire is the crime, disobedience is its form, and the deaths are the sentence. The poem that follows is, whatever its local beauties, the execution of a judgment already pronounced.

Shakespeare's equivalent threshold is the Prologue, and its differences from the preface constitute the most consequential change in the entire ledger. Where Brooke writes prose, the play writes a sonnet, the period's signature form for love poetry, so that the tragedy announces itself in the idiom of the thing it is about. Where Brooke addresses readers with instructions, the Chorus addresses an audience with information. Where Brooke assigns blame to the lovers' desire and disobedience, the Prologue assigns causality to "star-crossed" fortune and to the parents' "ancient grudge", and the lovers themselves are described with a sympathy that has no equivalent in the preface: their love is "death marked", their course a "misadventured" one, their deaths the instrument by which their parents' rage is finally buried. The sonnet even performs a small act of advocacy, asking the audience's patient ears to attend to what the poem's preface told its readers to condemn. Nothing in the play is argued more economically than in this exchange of thresholds: the same story, admitted to the reader's judgment under a prosecution summary in 1562, is admitted under a defence summary a generation later.

### What does Brooke's preface tell the reader to think of the lovers?

That their story is a cautionary spectacle of lust and disobedience, exhibited so that the young will obey their parents and shun secret love. The preface names the lovers unfortunate but culpable, their advisers corrupt, their marriage a cloak for shame, and their deaths a deserved and instructive end. The play's Prologue, on the same facts, asks for pity.

The stripping of the frame continues inside the action. Brooke's narrator recurs throughout the poem, apostrophizing the lovers, moralizing their choices, and addressing young readers over the characters' heads; the poem never lets its audience forget that it is being improved. Shakespeare abolishes the office. His Chorus speaks once more, at the head of Act 2, in terms that have already cooled into plot summary, "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (Prologue to Act 2), and then vanishes from the play altogether, leaving no authorized voice to tell the audience what to think. Judgment is redistributed to the characters, who judge themselves and each other fallibly and in character: Romeo's self-accusations, Juliet's clear eyed accounting of her situation, the Friar's philosophy of use and abuse, the Prince's closing assessment that "all are punished" (5.3). A story that arrived with its verdict attached leaves the theatre with its verdict dispersed among the interested parties, which is a large part of why arguments about the play's meaning have never closed. Brooke settled the moral question before the story began. Shakespeare unsettled it so thoroughly that the unsettling has become the story's afterlife.

It is worth being exact about what was removed, because the removal is sometimes overstated into a claim that the play has no moral vision at all. The play retains sententiae in abundance; the Friar alone supplies enough maxims to stock a commonplace book. What it removes is not moral reflection but moral jurisdiction: no voice in the play is licensed to deliver the author's verdict on the lovers, and the one figure who might have inherited the narrator's office, the Chorus, is retired after two appearances. Brooke's lovers die inside a judgment. Shakespeare's die inside a controversy. The ledger counts the change as the deepest of all, deeper even than the compression, because the compression altered how the story moves while this altered what the story is for.

## Reported Deeds Become Present Action: The Feast, the Window, the Tomb Speeches

Beneath the factual changes runs a formal one that touches every scene: the conversion of narrative into enactment. Brooke's poem tells; the play shows, and in showing it repeatedly invents the specific forms in which the events are now inseparable from their telling. Three conversions stand for the rest. At the feast, Brooke narrates that the lovers met, conversed, and were mutually taken. The play stages their first exchange as a shared sonnet, fourteen lines traded between them in which Romeo's pilgrimage conceit is taken up, corrected, and completed by Juliet: "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this" (1.5). The form is the meaning: two voices constructing one poem between them enact, in miniature, the mutuality the story will assert and the world will refuse. No equivalent exists in the poem, where the meeting is an event the narrator reports; in the play it is an event the audience watches being jointly authored, and the difference in kind is the difference between being told that two people are attuned and hearing the attunement happen.

At the window, the conversion runs the other way, from quantity to concentration. Brooke's lovers meet at Juliet's window repeatedly across their season, and the poem gives those meetings the loose amplitude of an ongoing affair. The play gathers the window's whole function into a single orchard scene (2.2), in which the vows, the name meditation, the refusal of the moon as a witness, and the arrangement of the marriage all occur inside one continuous night, and then answers it with a single dawn parting (3.5), "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5), in which the lark and nightingale debate compresses all the poem's repeated farewells into one argument about birdsong. What the season gave the poem, accumulation, the two scenes give the play in emblem: everything the affair contained is present, distilled into the two encounters every reader remembers. The compression of the calendar made the concentration necessary; the concentration made it art.

At the tomb, finally, the play invents the speeches by which its ending is known. Brooke narrates the deaths with the full rhetorical furniture of his poem, lamenting at length over the bodies. Shakespeare clears the stage to a single actor at a time and gives Romeo a valediction built from apostrophes to the corpse he believes dead, "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace!" (5.3), and Juliet, waking, a sequence of decisions taken at speed among the wreckage. The poem's ending is a ceremony conducted by its narrator; the play's ending is a series of actions performed in near darkness by people who do not know the whole story. That ignorance, the audience's knowledge set against the characters' partial knowledge at every point in the final scene, is the stage's native resource, and Brooke's medium cannot use it. The conversion of narrative into enactment is therefore not one change among the fourteen in the ledger. It is the condition that made the other thirteen worth making.

## The Duel Rebuilt: How Tybalt's Death Acquired a Middleman

Brooke's street fight is a straightforward affair. In the poem, the quarrel between the houses breaks out in the street, Tybalt and Romeus come to blows in a recognizably direct confrontation, and Romeus kills him, an outcome the poem treats as a misfortune of the feud for which banishment is the legal answer. The sequence in the play (3.1) is a machine of Shakespeare's construction, and its added parts are the enlarged Mercutio and a new moral geometry for Romeo. Shakespeare's Romeo enters the quarrel as a secret kinsman who cannot explain himself: "I do protest I never injured thee, / But love thee better than thou canst devise / Till thou shalt know the reason of my love" (3.1). His refusal to fight, unintelligible to everyone on stage, is what admits Mercutio's intervention; Mercutio fights in contempt at the "calm, dishonourable, vile submission" (3.1), is wounded under Romeo's arm while Romeo tries to part the fray, and dies cursing both houses. Only then does Romeo fight, in declared revenge and self knowledge: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate" (3.1), and "fire eyed fury be my conduct now" (3.1). Tybalt falls to a man who has first refused, then mediated, then avenged.

Every term of the tragedy's moral equation is altered by the rebuilding. Brooke's Romeus kills in a brawl and is exiled for a killing that any gentleman of his world might have committed in self defence. Shakespeare's Romeo kills his wife's cousin in hot revenge for a death his own peacemaking caused, having been married to that cousin's family for a matter of hours. The guilt is therefore interior and specific: Romeo can and does arraign himself for it, and the audience watches the catastrophe being authored by the hero's virtues, his love, his loyalty, his reluctance, as much as by the feud. The rebuilt duel also gives the play its structural hinge, Mercutio's death, and its most quoted curse, and it converts Tybalt from the poem's brawling antagonist into an instrument: a man whose single function in the machine is to be in the square at the wrong hour with the wrong temper. Bullough's catalogue notes the play's elaboration of the fight as one of the clearest gains in dramatic causation over the poem, and the ledger concurs. In Brooke, the brawl happens to the lovers. In the play, the lovers, enlarged friend and all, happen to the brawl, and the difference is the difference between misfortune and tragedy.

## Paris at the Tomb: The Death Brooke Never Wrote

Brooke's Paris is a suitor and nothing worse. The poem's County Paris presses his suit through the Capulet household, is promised the bride, mourns the bride's apparent death in the conventional forms, and survives the poem: no errand of his brings him into the tomb's story, and his hands are clean at the end of it. Shakespeare's Paris is given an invented final movement that changes the moral temperature of the entire last act. His Paris comes to the tomb by night with flowers and a page, on an errand of private devotion: "Sweet flower, with flowers thy bridal bed I strew" (5.3). Interrupted by Romeo's arrival, he reads the intruder as a felon come to desecrate the dead, challenges him, and is killed by a man who has begged him, in one of the play's most quietly terrible speeches, to leave the place alive: "Good gentle youth, tempt not a desperate man. / Fly hence and leave me" (5.3). Paris dies on a misreading, asking with his last breath to be laid beside Juliet, and Romeo, granting the request, carries the body into the vault and only then learns whom he has killed: "One writ with me in sour misfortune's book" (5.3).

The addition works in three directions at once. It gives the tomb scene a threshold, a death before the deaths, so that Romeo enters the vault already a killer for the second time that week and the audience measures his remaining choices against fresh blood. It completes Paris, converting the play's most functional figure, the approved bridegroom, into a person at the moment of his extinction, and retrospectively dignifies the suit the play had used as a mere pressure on Juliet. And it supplies the final scene with its pattern in miniature: two young men, each acting on partial knowledge, each convinced of his own errand's legitimacy, destroying one another outside the tomb of the woman both loved. Brooke's ending, for all its ceremony, contains nothing like this concentration. The poem's hero arrives at the tomb with clean hands and a broken heart; the play's hero arrives having killed his rival in the dark within the hour, and the poison he drinks is taken by a man whose desperation has already been priced. Of all the ledger's entries, this is the one most purely invented, owing Brooke nothing but the character's name and function, and it is among the most characteristic: where the poem narrates a catastrophe, the play adds a rehearsal of it at the door.

## The Apothecary's Shop: A Purchase Becomes an Argument

Brooke narrates the poison's purchase as an errand. His Romeus, resolved on death in Mantua, finds the poor apothecary, makes his bargain, and carries the poison away; the poem spends its lines on the hero's determination. Shakespeare keeps the errand's outline and rebuilds it as a scene of persuasion with its own philosophy, the first scene of Act 5 and the hinge of Romeo's return. His Romeo enters Mantua in a state the audience has been prepared to distrust, having announced a dream of joyful news, "I dreamt my lady came and found me dead" (5.1), and receives Balthasar's report as a confirmation to be defied rather than a fact to be checked: "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1). The apothecary is then summoned not as a tradesman but as a remembered face, and the scene becomes a negotiation in which Romeo must argue a starving man out of his own legal peril: "The world is not thy friend, nor the world's law. / The world affords no law to make thee rich" (5.1). The purchase concludes with the play's bleakest economic verdict, delivered over the gold as it changes hands: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1).

The conversion matters because it relocates the tragedy's last cause. In the poem, the poison is procured because the plot requires it. In the play, the poison is procured through an argument about poverty, law, and money that the audience is made to hear and judge: the apothecary's consent is explicitly bought against his conscience, "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1), and Romeo's reply, "I pay thy poverty and not thy will" (5.1), accepts the terms with open eyes. The scene also completes the compression's work in Mantua. Brooke's exile occupies a long narrative stretch of waiting, correspondence, and gradual news; Shakespeare collapses the entire exile into this one scene of report, resolve, and purchase, so that Romeo's return to Verona feels less like a journey than like a detonation. The ledger's pattern, distance removed and delay removed, is visible in miniature: a narrated errand of the poem becomes the play's most concentrated essay on what desperation costs and who is made to sell it.

## The Feud Staged: Narrated History Into Action at Both Ends

Brooke's poem begins as a chronicle. Before Romeus appears, the narrator lays out Verona, the standing of its two great houses, the antiquity of their hatred, and the failure of earlier attempts, by authority and by marriage alliance alike, to reconcile them. The feud in the poem is thus a known condition of the world, established by exposition, and the lovers' story is then set inside it as an instance. Shakespeare abolished the chronicle and staged the condition. His play opens not with information about the feud but with the feud itself at its pettiest level, two servants of each house working themselves toward a brawl over a bitten thumb, and escalates within a single scene through citizens with clubs to the Prince's edict: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). Everything Brooke's narrator explains, the play either enacts or compresses into that edict, and the audience's knowledge of the feud is thereafter experiential rather than reported: the feud is what Verona does, repeatedly, in public, at every social level, whenever the play needs reminding.

The change frames the story at both ends. Because the feud is staged rather than described, its final settlement must also be staged, and the play's close answers its opening with a symmetry the poem's exposition cannot produce: the same Prince who pronounced the death penalty in the first scene presides over the reckoning in the last, and the same hands that were raised in the brawl are joined over the bodies. Capulet's offer, "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" (5.3), is a stage action before it is a sentiment, a handshake the audience watches two old men perform beside their children's corpses. Brooke's reconciliation, narrated as a settlement between houses, is dignified and remote; the play's is physical and costly, purchased in view. The ledger records the conversion under its general law: remove the distance between the audience and the event. At the level of the frame, that law turns a poem about a famous feud into a play that makes its audience sit inside one.

## The Closing Sentences Repealed: Pardons in Place of Punishments

Brooke's poem ends as its preface promised, in judgment executed. After the lovers' deaths and the discovery of the whole story, the poem's Prince distributes sentences across the surviving cast with a magistrate's completeness. The Nurse, whose connivance enabled the secret marriage, is banished from Verona. The apothecary who sold the poison is hanged. Friar Laurence, fearing the court's displeasure, withdraws from the city to end his days in a hermitage, a self imposed exile the poem reports as the close of his story. The families are reconciled and join in raising a monument over the lovers, but the reconciliation is framed by punishments, and the poem's last movement is penal: every agent of the catastrophe is accounted for, and the account is settled in penalties proportioned to guilt. It is the ending of a moral exemplum, and it completes the preface's contract with the reader.

Shakespeare struck the sentences out. His final scene contains not one punishment. The apothecary never reappears after Act 5 Scene 1; his fate is neither staged nor mentioned. The Nurse, last seen in the chamber of lamentation, simply ceases to exist for the play's purposes; no sentence, no dismissal, no line accounts for her. The Friar, who in the poem flees to a hermitage under a cloud, is in the play examined and acquitted in open court: "We still have known thee for a holy man" (5.3). Even the fathers, whose feud the Prince indicts in the tragedy's most comprehensive sentence, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3), are sentenced to nothing but the sight of their children and the loss of them. The Prince's own closing accounting distributes responsibility to the heavens and to himself, "And I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punished" (5.3), which is the play's verdict form in a sentence: punishment exists, but it has already happened, in the deaths, and the law adds nothing to it.

### What punishments does Brooke's ending hand out that the play drops?

All of them. The poem banishes the Nurse, hangs the apothecary, and drives its Friar into exile at a hermitage to end his days. The play punishes nobody: the apothecary vanishes unjudged, the Nurse is simply absent, and the Friar is formally acquitted by the Prince, who calls him a holy man. Justice gives way to reckoning.

What replaces the sentences is the famous exchange of statues. Montague promises to raise Juliet's statue in pure gold; Capulet answers that he will do as much for Romeo: "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie, / Poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3). Brooke's reconciled houses raise a joint monument, and the play sharpens the inherited gesture into a competition of grief, each father bidding in gold against the other even in capitulation, so that the feud's habits of rivalry are visible inside the peace itself. The change is characteristic of the whole ledger. The poem settles its world by judgment from above; the play settles its world by gestures from within, flawed, expensive, and incomplete, and leaves the adequacy of the settlement for the audience to decide. The penal ending would have confirmed the preface's moral scheme and closed the case. The pardoning ending confirms nothing except the cost, which is why the play, and not the poem, is still being argued about.

## Juliet's Night of Fears: Brooke's Apprehensions Become the Great Soliloquy

Not every change in the ledger is a subtraction or a promotion; a few are expansions, moments where the poem plants a sentence and the play grows a scene from it. The finest is Juliet's night with the vial. Brooke's heroine, alone with the friar's draught, is not without misgivings: the poem allows her a passage of apprehension in which the fears that any reader would predict file past, that the mixture might fail, that it might be a true poison given her to protect the friar's secret, that she might wake in the charnel house among the bones of her ancestors before her husband comes. The poem reports the fears in its narrator's voice and moves on within a handful of couplets, because a narrative that must also cover months cannot afford to stop for one night of dread.

The play stops for nothing else. Act 4 Scene 3 gives Juliet the vial, the empty stage, and forty odd lines in which Brooke's reported apprehensions are converted into a mind thinking at full stretch. Each fear the poem names is taken up, examined, and dismissed or endured in turn. The fear of failure is met with a dagger laid ready: "What if this mixture do not work at all? / Shall I be married then tomorrow morning? / No, no, this shall forbid it. Lie thou there" (4.3). The fear of poison is reasoned through the Friar's character: "I fear it is, and yet methinks it should not, / For he hath still been tried a holy man" (4.3). The fear of waking early flowers into the play's most concentrated passage of imagined horror, the vault with its loathsome smells, its shrieks like mandrakes torn from the earth, and the terror that madness will make her play with her forefathers' joints and dash out her brains with a dead man's bone (4.3). The soliloquy ends not when the fears are answered but when they are outvoted, by the name that has outvoted everything else in the play: "Romeo, Romeo, Romeo! Here's drink. I drink to thee" (4.3).

Bullough's comparison registers the soliloquy as a case of the dramatist finding in his source a hint and building on it beyond recognition, and the ledger treats the entry as the purest available specimen of Shakespeare's method with Brooke. Nothing here is invented from nothing, and nothing is merely transcribed. The poem supplies the fears, the order in which they arise, even the remedy of the dagger laid beside the bed; the play supplies the voice. It is also the entry that most directly refutes any account of the changes as simple tightening for the stage. A dramatist interested only in pace would have cut the night to a couplet, as the poem effectively does. Shakespeare instead spent one of his longest solo scenes on a girl deciding to drink, because the compression elsewhere had purchased the time, and because in the play's economy Juliet's interiority is the treasure everything else is spent to display. The ledger's verdicts must make room for expansions like this alongside the cuts: the method is not economy alone, but economy in the service of chosen intensities.

## Improvements or Merely Differences? The Critics and the Adjudication

The brief for this article poses the complication honestly: a change is not automatically an improvement, and a ledger that records only gains is a piece of advocacy, not of scholarship. The critics named by the brief divide on exactly this point, and their division is instructive. Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources remains the standard comparison, reads the changes with an editor's sympathy for the dramatist: the compression, the enlargements, and the dropped moralising appear in his account as the strokes of a theatrical intelligence converting a diffuse narrative into a shaped stage action, and his judgments of individual passages repeatedly award the play the advantage over the poem. Kenneth Muir, approaching from the opposite direction, documented how little of the story Shakespeare altered at all, and his account carries an implicit caution against the improvement narrative: if the scaffold, the sequence, and at times the phrasing are Brooke's, then a good part of what audiences admire in the play was already present in the poem, and the dramatist's genius lies in recognition and selection rather than in transformation. René Weis, editing the play with both predecessors in view, complicates both positions at the level of the line. His notes show Shakespeare following Brooke's language closely in passages where the improvement school would prefer to see free invention, and producing, in those very passages, verse that no reader would trade for the couplets it translates. Dependence and originality, in Weis's evidence, are not opposites. They are the same lines read twice.

Adjudicated by effect, which is the test this article has applied throughout, the dispute resolves in Bullough's favour with Muir's caution absorbed and Weis's evidence as the mechanism. The caution first: the play's plot pleasures, its suspense, its symmetry of design, are substantially Brooke's, and criticism that credits Shakespeare with the story commits the error the series thesis exists to correct. But the question the ledger asks is not who supplied the materials; it is what the changes do. By that test the major changes earn the name of improvements for the work the play sets out to be. The compression makes causality visible as haste. The age change makes the moral arithmetic of blame come out differently, and more justly. The enlargements of Mercutio and the Nurse convert exposition into experience and make the play's hinge and its deepest betrayal possible. The stripped moral frame converts an audience from pupils into judges. The repealed sentences convert a penal exemplum into a civic tragedy. Each improvement is purchased at a price the ledger has recorded: the marriage's duration, the poem's leisurely social breadth, the friar's and nurse's accountability at the end. A difference becomes an improvement, this article argues, when its gains serve the work's governing design and its costs fall where the design can bear them. By that standard the play's changes are improvements in system, executed on a borrowed story, by a dramatist whose deepest originality was knowing exactly what to remove.

One further consequence deserves recording, because it reaches beyond this play. The change ledger describes a recognisable literary method: fidelity to a source's architecture joined to ruthlessness about its emphases. Shakespeare would use the same method on Holinshed, on Plutarch, on Italian tales and old plays for the rest of his career, and the Romeo and Juliet ledger is its earliest full exhibition. Brooke's poem, read beside the play, is therefore not merely a quarry that happened to be emptied. It is the training ground on which English drama's characteristic way of making new work out of old stories first shows its complete pattern: keep the story, change the time, enlarge the servants of the plot into its rivals, strike out the verdict, and let the ending pardon what the source condemned.

## A Poem for Readers, a Play for a Crowd: Why the Changes Take the Shape They Do

The ledger's pattern becomes still clearer when the two works are restored to their first publics. Brooke's poem belongs to the print culture of the early 1560s, a market for translated continental tales presented as moral instruction, and its apparatus is the apparatus of that market: a prose preface that contracts with the reader in advance, a verse argument that summarizes the plot so that no buyer can mistake the goods, and a narrator who supervises the reading experience from inside the text, because a book's reader is alone with the page and may wander. Every feature Shakespeare removed belongs to that apparatus of supervision. The preface, the summary, the warnings, the closing sentences are the poem's way of keeping a solitary reader inside an approved interpretation from the first page to the last.

The play belongs to a public theatre of the 1590s, where interpretation cannot be supervised and must instead be engineered. A crowd cannot be prefaced into obedience for two hours; it can only be shown, at speed, by embodied persons, and trusted or provoked to judge. Read against their publics, the changes stop looking like a list of preferences and start looking like a single act of translation between media. The narrator's information becomes dialogue and dumb show. The preface's verdict becomes the Prologue's neutrality, because a verdict delivered to a crowd before the action would empty the benches of argument, while neutrality fills them with it. The long calendar becomes five days because a performance is itself an afternoon, and a story that mimics its audience's own span of attention feels urgent in a way a season cannot. Even the closing pardons belong to the medium: a reader can be left with sentences to ponder, but a crowd must be sent home with an image, and the play's final image is not a gallows or a hermitage but two old men shaking hands over their children and bidding against each other in gold.

### Why does Brooke's poem need a narrator while the play does without one?

Because the poem's meanings are not safe in its story's hands. Brooke's narrator exists to fix interpretation for a solitary reader: to condemn in advance, to warn at the joints of the action, and to sentence at the end. The play transfers that office to the audience, whose judgment the action is engineered to engage rather than to replace.

This is also the place to record the limit of the improvement verdict, fairly. A supervised reading is not a worthless thing, and Brooke's poem gave its first readers something the play refuses to give anyone: certainty about what the story means. The poem's afterlife as the play's source should not obscure its own coherence as a moralized narrative built for a culture that wanted its tragedies pre judged. Shakespeare's changes are improvements relative to the stage's purposes and to the sympathies of the audience he actually faced. They are not improvements in the abstract, dropped from a height onto a benighted original. The ledger's final column might more honestly be headed not better but otherwise and, for the theatre, decisively better fitted. The distinction matters for the series thesis this article serves: the cliché that credits the whole work to Shakespeare is wrong twice over, first because the story is Brooke's, and second because the transformation, though real and systematic, is a transformation of a made thing, not a creation out of nothing. What the dramatist did to the poem is what the poem's own culture had done to the Italian tale before him, at one remove higher speed and genius: he received a story, kept its bones, and rebuilt its meaning for the room in which it had to live.

## The Kept Accident: Friar John's Errand and the Compression That Weaponized It

One entry belongs in the ledger precisely because nothing in it changed. The failure of the Friar's letter is Brooke's device, inherited whole: in the poem, the messenger friar entrusted with the letter to Mantua is delayed because the house he visits is shut up under suspicion of plague, the doors sealed by the town's officers, and the explanation reaches Verona too late to be of use. Shakespeare kept the device, the plague, and even the sealed doors, giving the messenger a name, Friar John, a voice, and a short scene of his own in which to make the report: "I could not send it, here it is again, / Nor get a messenger to bring it thee, / So fearful were they of infection" (5.2). On paper, the entry reads as fidelity. In effect, it is one of the most violent changes in the ledger, because the compression altered the world the accident falls into.

In Brooke's calendar, a delayed letter is a misfortune with room around it. The exile has lasted long enough for letters to be a routine; other channels exist; the catastrophe assembles itself from several failures cooperating across weeks, and the poem's moral machinery can distribute the blame among fortune, secrecy, and the lovers' own haste of character. In the play's calendar, the same accident is a single point of failure inside forty-two hours. There is no second letter because there is no second day. There is no visit from the Friar because Mantua and Verona are separated by exactly the distance the plot can afford once. The kept accident therefore changes its nature: what was, in the poem, an instance of the world's general unreliability becomes, in the play, the one narrow gate through which the whole tragedy must pass, and the audience feels the narrowness because the compression has taught it to count hours. The Friar's horrified response, and his immediate resolve to carry the news to the tomb himself, arrive already too late by the arithmetic the play has installed. Shakespeare did not need to invent a better catastrophe than Brooke's. He needed only to shrink the room in which Brooke's catastrophe happens until it had nowhere to miss.

## Two Spoilers: Brooke's Argument and the Sonnet That Replaced It

Both works begin by giving away their endings, and the resemblance conceals one of the ledger's subtlest changes. Brooke's volume opens, after the preface, with a formal Argument in verse: a compressed rehearsal of the entire story, meeting, marriage, quarrel, exile, potion, tomb, and reconciliation, set before the poem so that the reader begins the narrative already knowing its whole course. The play's Prologue performs the identical office. "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star crossed lovers take their life" (Prologue): before any character has spoken, the audience knows that the lovers will die and that their deaths will end the feud. Neither author wanted suspense about outcomes. Each wanted the audience's attention freed from the question of what happens next and fixed instead on how, and on what it means. The shared device proves that some of the play's most admired effects, above all its dramatic irony, are inherited architecture rather than invention: Brooke, like Shakespeare after him, builds a tragedy the reader watches from above the characters' knowledge.

What changes is the use made of the foreknowledge. In Brooke, the advance summary serves the preface's supervision: knowing the end, the reader is positioned to judge each step toward it against the moral the volume has already supplied, and the narrator's interventions keep that judgment on its rails. Foreknowledge, in the poem, is a fence. In the play, foreknowledge is a lens. Because the audience knows the lovers are doomed, every happiness is shadowed in real time: the sonnet at the feast is beautiful and unbearable at once, the Friar's wedding blessing doubles as a funeral text in the hearing, and Juliet's "I drink to thee" (4.3) lands as an act of courage performed inside a known grave. Shakespeare also formalizes the spoiler as a sonnet, and the choice of form argues the play's whole case in fourteen lines. The period's lyric instrument for first love is made to carry a death sentence, so that the tragedy's governing collision, between the lovers' idiom and the world's, is enacted before the curtain of the first scene has effectively risen. Brooke's Argument tells the reader what will happen and what to think about it. The sonnet tells the audience what will happen and leaves the thinking, pointedly, to them. Between those two handlings of the same device lies the distance the whole ledger measures: identical information, opposite relations between a work and its judges.

The comparison also settles a small question that hovers over the improvement debate. Critics inclined to credit Shakespeare with the play's irony must concede the device to Brooke; critics inclined to credit Brooke with the play must concede the irony's refinement to Shakespeare. The ledger's pattern accommodates both concessions without strain, because its unit of account is never the device but the device's employment. Again and again the play takes over the poem's furniture, a spoiler opening, a plague delayed messenger, a friar with a pharmacopoeia, a pair of feuding houses reconciled over a tomb, and changes the emotional current that runs through it. Originality of that kind does not photograph well as a list of inventions, which is why the cliché undersells it. It photographs perfectly as a ledger of changes.

## What the Ledger Proves: Originality as Transformation

The series thesis for this group of articles holds that the cliché credits the whole work to Shakespeare, and that the ledger shows precisely what he transformed. The evidence assembled here supports the thesis and refines it. What Shakespeare took from Brooke was not a hint or a premise but a complete, well built story, whose architecture he respected closely enough to follow in sequence and at times in phrasing. What he changed was the story's physics, its calendar and its sleeps and ages; its population, promoting a temperature and a function into Mercutio and the Nurse; its lighting, striking out the verdicts under which the poem's figures move and leaving them in the open air of judgment; and its ending, in which a magistrate's punishments give way to pardons, absences, and competitive gold. The changes are few enough to list in a fourteen row table and large enough to account for the difference between a poem that survives as a source and a play that survives as a world.

That is why the honest answer to the question every source study is really asked, how much of Romeo and Juliet is Shakespeare's, refuses both of the easy figures. Not the plot: the plot is Brooke's, and the play's fidelity to it is the best documented fact in the play's making. Not a mere polish, either: no polish compresses a season into five days, invents the hinge on which a genre turns, rebuilds a heroine's age so that her accusers become her defendants' burden, or strikes a hanging, a banishment, and an exile from an ending and replaces them with a handshake. The accurate figure is the ledger itself: a kept skeleton under systematic transformation, in which the transformation, not the skeleton, is where the play's meanings live. Brooke wrote the story of Romeus and Juliet. Shakespeare, changing what he changed, wrote Romeo and Juliet, and the fourteen entries are the deed of transfer.

A final word belongs to the poem. It is easy, ledger in hand, to treat The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet as a draft that waited three decades for its author, and the habit should be resisted. Brooke's poem held its story in print, carried it across the Channel's chain of translations into English, and gave it the form in which the English stage could find it. Without the poem's fidelity, there is nothing for the play's transformations to work on; the scaffold the dramatist trusted was trustworthy because Brooke built it well. The change ledger therefore ends in a double credit that the cliché never manages. The story is Brooke's gift. The play is what Shakespeare changed from Brooke, and the changes, entry by entry, are the most instructive record the period leaves of how a great dramatist reads.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch change ledger, and what does it record about Shakespeare's work on Brooke?

The InsightCrunch change ledger is the table at the centre of this article, setting fourteen of Shakespeare's changes to Arthur Brooke's poem in parallel columns: the change, what stands in Brooke, what stands in the play, and the effect the alteration produces. It records that Shakespeare kept Brooke's plot and its order almost entire, while transforming the story's calendar, its heroine's age, the duration of the potion's sleep, the size of Mercutio and the Nurse, the moral character of the Friar, the framing verdicts of preface and narrator, the staging of the feast, the window, the duel, and the purchase of the poison, and the whole penal machinery of the ending. The ledger's purpose is measurement. Claims about Shakespeare's originality are usually made in the abstract, as praise or as correction, and both kinds float free of the evidence. Set down entry by entry, the transformation becomes countable, and its pattern, the steady removal of delays, judgments, and distances, becomes visible. The ledger also records the costs, beginning with the lived duration of the marriage that Brooke's months supplied and the play's days cannot.

### Q: Which single change altered the story's meaning most?

The removal of Brooke's moral frame altered the story's meaning most, because it changed what kind of object the story is. Brooke's prose preface condemns the lovers before the poem begins, calling their desire unhonest, their advisers corrupt, and their deaths an instructive sentence, and his narrator maintains that verdict throughout. Shakespeare replaced the preface with a sonnet Prologue that reports the deaths and blames nobody, distributing causality between the stars and the parents' grudge, and he retired the Chorus after two appearances, leaving no voice licensed to judge. Every other change operates inside that decision. The compression means something different once haste is no longer a sin to be punished; the lowered age means something different once the heroine is no longer on trial; the pardoned ending means something different once no magistrate is required to settle the account. A condemned exemplum became an open tragedy by the striking of a frame, and the striking is a single, deliberate act performed at the story's threshold.

### Q: What form does Shakespeare give the lovers' first conversation, and why does the form matter?

Shakespeare gives the lovers' first conversation the form of a shared sonnet, fourteen lines traded between Romeo and Juliet at the feast in Act 1 Scene 5, where Brooke's poem simply narrates that the pair met and conversed. The form matters because it performs the relationship before the story has asserted it. Romeo opens with a pilgrimage conceit, casting his lips as pilgrims and Juliet as the shrine; Juliet takes up his rhymes, corrects his theology, and completes his quatrains, so that the poem they speak exists only as a joint production. Two strangers who can finish each other's verse form have demonstrated, in the medium itself, the attunement the plot will spend four acts asserting. The sonnet also sets the terms of their courtship: formal, witty, governed, a discipline of exchange in which Juliet is from the first line her partner's equal and editor. Brooke's reported conversation can carry none of this, because narration describes mutuality while the shared sonnet enacts it in front of witnesses.

### Q: Why does Shakespeare move Mercutio from the feast's edge to the centre of the play?

Shakespeare moves Mercutio to the centre because the play needs an intelligence outside the lovers through whom the audience can see them, and a death heavy enough to turn the comedy into tragedy at a single stroke. In Brooke's poem Mercutio is a name at the feast, a courtier whose hand, taken by Juliet in the dancing's aftermath, is cold beside Romeus's warmth; he speaks no lines and plays no part in the quarrel. The play builds him into the Prince's kinsman, the Queen Mab speaker, the presiding wit of the street, and the provocateur of Act 3 Scene 1, whose intervention in the quarrel, wounding, and curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), convert Romeo's refusal to fight into revenge and the play's genre into its opposite. Without the enlargement there is no hinge: Brooke's action darkens by degrees, while the play turns in one scene because one invented man dies in it. The enlargement also buys the play its licence to linger, since Mercutio's speeches are delays the audience enjoys, purchased against a clock that is always running.

### Q: How does the play change the length of Juliet's potion sleep, and what does the shorter sleep do?

The play shortens Juliet's potion sleep from the three days Brooke's friar allows to the two and forty hours Shakespeare's Friar specifies in Act 4 Scene 1. The shorter sleep is the compression of the whole action stated as a single number, and it governs the catastrophe's mechanics. Three days of counterfeit death would leave the Friar's plan with margins: a delayed letter could be followed by a second, a rumour could be checked, the Friar himself could travel. Forty-two hours admit no recovery from a single failure, so when Friar John is sealed into a plague suspected house and his letter never leaves Verona, the plan has no redundancy left and Romeo buys his poison on schedule. The change also redistributes the ending's agency. Brooke's heroine sleeps through her funeral and much of its aftermath; Shakespeare's wakes inside a tomb already occupied by Paris's corpse and Romeo's, with the watch approaching, and her last decisions are taken in minutes. Time removed from the plan becomes decisiveness required of the sleeper.

### Q: How does Shakespeare change Capulet's handling of the wedding date?

Shakespeare turns Capulet's handling of the wedding date into a small engine of the catastrophe, where Brooke's poem treats the match's timing as a matter of arrangement rather than of fatal improvisation. In the play, Capulet first refuses Paris on grounds of Juliet's age and two more summers of waiting (1.2), then, after Tybalt's death, fixes the wedding for Thursday in the belief that a hasty marriage will cure his daughter's grief, announcing the decision to Juliet as a settled kindness: "A' Thursday, tell her, / She shall be married to this noble earl" (3.4). When Juliet's feigned repentance delights him, he pulls the date forward again on impulse, from Thursday to Wednesday, a day saved in celebration that is, in the event, the day on which everything is lost, because it advances the potion by a night and desynchronizes the Friar's letter from the waking it was meant to precede. The change makes the father an unwitting agent of the timing he deplores, and it is pure compression: on Brooke's roomier calendar, a day saved is a courtesy; on Shakespeare's, it is a death sentence pronounced as a treat.

### Q: How does Shakespeare change the apothecary scene from Brooke's narrative?

Shakespeare changes the apothecary scene from a narrated purchase into a dramatized argument about poverty, law, and gold. Brooke's poem reports the errand: Romeus, resolved to die, finds the poor Mantuan apothecary, buys his poison, and departs, with the narrative's attention fixed on the hero's determination. The play stages the transaction as a persuasion conducted under pressure in Act 5 Scene 1. Romeo must first remember the man, summon him, and then argue him out of the legal terror that protects him, on the ground that a world which has left him starving has forfeited its claim on his obedience: "The world is not thy friend, nor the world's law" (5.1). The apothecary's surrender is phrased as a distinction between his poverty and his will, and Romeo's acceptance of the distinction is equally explicit, so that the sale of the poison becomes a small, complete essay on what desperation costs and who is made to sell it. Brooke's errand supplies the plot with poison. Shakespeare's scene supplies the tragedy with its last and bleakest piece of social evidence.

### Q: What does Shakespeare cut from Brooke's account of the Friar's fate after the deaths?

Shakespeare cuts the Friar's exile and obscure end, and replaces them with a public acquittal. In Brooke's poem, Friar Laurence, fearing the Prince's displeasure once the whole story is known, withdraws from Verona to a hermitage and ends his days there, a sentence of obscurity that completes the poem's distribution of penalties among everyone who enabled the secret marriage. The flight also confirms the preface's suspicion of friars: the instrument of the tragedy removes himself from judgment before judgment can sit. In the play, the Friar does the opposite. He remains, surrenders himself to the watch, narrates the whole design in a long account that the Prince hears in full, and receives the play's most surprising verdict: "We still have known thee for a holy man" (5.3). The cut is of a piece with the stripping of the moral frame. A penal ending requires the Friar to be sentenced or to flee sentencing; an ending built on reckoning rather than punishment requires him present, heard, and absorbed into the general pardon. Shakespeare kept the man and deleted his disgrace.

### Q: What replaces Brooke's moralising narrator inside the play?

Brooke's moralising narrator is replaced by a brief Chorus that falls silent and by the characters' own fallible judgments of themselves and one another. The poem's narrator condemns in advance, warns at the action's joints, apostrophizes the lovers, and sentences the survivors. The play's Chorus speaks a sonnet before Act 1 and a shorter summary before Act 2, concerned with plot rather than morals, and then disappears entirely, an office abolished in mid performance. Its functions are redistributed. Sententious wisdom passes to the Friar, whose maxims are dramatic utterances that other characters may ignore, and do. Self judgment passes to the lovers, who arraign themselves with a precision no narrator could match, Romeo on his effeminacy and his haste, Juliet on her situation and her choices. Public judgment passes to the Prince, whose closing assessment, "All are punished" (5.3), is a reckoning rather than a verdict. The net effect is the removal of moral jurisdiction from the work: the play reflects on conduct constantly, and authorizes no voice to settle what the reflections mean. Brooke's readers are told; Shakespeare's audiences are empanelled.

### Q: Why does Shakespeare let the Nurse vanish from the ending instead of sentencing her as Brooke does?

Shakespeare lets the Nurse vanish because her punishment has already been administered in the only currency the play uses, exclusion from the secret she created. Brooke's poem banishes her from Verona in its closing distribution of penalties, accounting for her connivance with the magistrate's completeness the poem's moral scheme requires. The play sentences no one, and the Nurse's absence from the final scene is more eloquent than any sentence could be. Her fall occurred earlier and privately, at the moment she counselled Juliet to marry Paris and dismiss Romeo as a dishclout (3.5), after which Juliet lies to her, excludes her from the potion plan, and entrusts her fate to the Friar alone. The woman who arranged the marriage, carried its messages, and knew its whole history is not present to learn how it ended; the play's last sight of her is in the chamber of lamentation over the supposed corpse, grieving sincerely inside a catastrophe she can no longer follow. Banishment would have been a public acknowledgment of her importance. Oblivion is the play's colder and more consistent justice.

### Q: How does the compression change the Friar's plan from a scheme with slack into one with none?

The compression changes the Friar's plan by removing every interval in which a failure could be absorbed, repaired, or overtaken. On Brooke's calendar, the plan operates across weeks and months: a marriage that has existed for a season, a counterfeit death measured in days, an exile within reach of messengers, and time enough, in principle, for second letters, personal visits, and the correction of accidents. The plan's enemies in the poem are fortune and secrecy, working slowly. On Shakespeare's calendar, the same plan is built across four days, with a potion timed to forty-two hours, a wedding advanced by a day in mid operation, a single letter entrusted to a single friar, and a husband in Mantua who will act on the first report he receives. Each compression is individually plausible, and their combination is lethal: the plan has exactly one messenger, one dose, one waking, and one hour at which all of them must coincide. The Friar of the play is not less competent than Brooke's confessor. He is a planner working without slack, and the play's engineering ensures that the first accident, a plague seal on a door, is also the last.

### Q: How does Shakespeare change the fight in which Tybalt dies?

Shakespeare rebuilds the fight in which Tybalt dies from a direct quarrel into a three stage machine whose middle term is Mercutio's death. In Brooke's poem, the street confrontation leads to blows between the principals, and Romeus kills Tybalt in a brawl the poem treats as the feud's ordinary product. In the play's Act 3 Scene 1, Romeo first refuses the quarrel on grounds he cannot disclose, his secret marriage making Tybalt his kinsman; Mercutio, reading the refusal as dishonourable submission, takes the fight up in contempt; Romeo's attempt to part the fray gives Tybalt the opening to wound Mercutio under Romeo's arm; and Romeo then kills Tybalt in declared revenge, understanding his own part in the sequence as he performs it. The change redistributes the guilt. A hero who kills in a brawl is a victim of his world; a hero whose peacemaking kills his friend and whose revenge kills his wife's cousin within the hour is an author of his catastrophe, and the banishment that follows reads as the least of the sentences he passes on himself.

### Q: Does Shakespeare follow Brooke's wording anywhere, or only the plot?

Shakespeare follows Brooke's wording as well as the plot, in more places than the legend of his originality admits. René Weis's edition repeatedly sets lines of the play beside couplets of the poem and shows the dramatist translating Brooke's narrative statements into spoken verse, keeping an image, a comparison, or a turn of phrasing while changing its speaker, its moment, and its charge. The Friar's reasonings, the lovers' complaints against fortune, and descriptions of the feast and the tomb all carry such deposits, matter quarried from the poem and reset at higher pressure. Kenneth Muir's study of the sources argued from this closeness that the dramatist worked with Brooke's text effectively beside him, following its sequence scene by scene. The finding disciplines both camps in the improvement debate. Passages that feel like pure Shakespeare can prove to be Brooke heightened, and passages lifted nearly whole can become, in their new mouths and moments, the most famous lines in the play. The ledger therefore grades changes by effect rather than by novelty: a borrowed line that does new work counts as a transformation.

### Q: Why does Shakespeare open with servants fighting when Brooke opens with narration?

Shakespeare opens with servants fighting because the play must establish its feud as an experience rather than as information, and the brawl of Act 1 Scene 1 teaches the audience everything Brooke's opening narration explains, at the speed of performance. Brooke begins as a chronicler, laying out Verona, the two houses, the antiquity of their hatred, and the failure of past attempts to reconcile them, so that the lovers' story starts inside a condition the reader has been instructed about. The play abolishes the instruction and stages the condition at its lowest social level: two servants of each house nursing an insult over a bitten thumb into drawn swords, citizens joining with clubs, the principals arriving to inflame what their servants began, and the Prince closing the scene with an edict that prices the next disturbance at the brawlers' lives. In a few minutes the audience has seen the feud's pettiness, its reach, its ritual character, and its legal frame, and has felt the city's violence as tempo rather than as background. A narrated feud can be forgotten. A staged one sets the key for every scene that follows.

### Q: How does Shakespeare change the lovers' wedding night from a season of meetings into a single dawn parting?

Shakespeare condenses the season of married meetings that Brooke's poem allows its lovers into one night and one dawn parting, and loads the whole meaning of the marriage into the parting's few minutes. In the poem, the marriage is consummated and then continued: across some three months the pair meet by night with the Nurse's connivance, so that by the time of the street quarrel they possess a shared life with habits, arrangements, and a past. The play's lovers marry on Monday afternoon, spend that night together after the banishment, and part at dawn in Act 3 Scene 5 in the famous debate of the lark and the nightingale, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5), knowing they may never meet again. The single parting must therefore carry what the poem distributes across a season: tenderness, foreboding, the marriage's whole claim on the audience's belief. Shakespeare makes it do so by staging the parting as an argument about evidence, two people disputing the identity of a bird because the verdict decides whether the husband stays or goes into exile and, as it proves, into the sequence that kills them both.

### Q: What does the play lose by cutting Brooke's months?

The play loses the evidence of the marriage as a lived life, and with it a species of grief that Brooke's poem, for all its lumbering measure, can supply and the play cannot. A couple who have met by night across a season, who have arrangements and routines and a household of two maintained in secret, are destroyed as a going concern: their end breaks something that demonstrably existed and worked. Shakespeare's lovers, married for a day and parted after one night, are destroyed as a promise. The play compensates with intensity, with the emblematic concentration of the balcony vows and the dawn parting, and with a speed that makes the love feel like a single sustained act rather than a condition. But the compensation explains a recurring strain in the play's criticism, the suspicion that the lovers perish before they have properly lived, that the tragedy consumes a beginning rather than a marriage. That suspicion is the compression's shadow, and it is the honest debit entry in the change ledger: the play traded duration for velocity, knowing exactly what the trade cost, because the velocity is what its stage could spend.

### Q: How can the change ledger be used in an essay on Shakespeare's originality?

The change ledger can anchor an essay on Shakespeare's originality by replacing the term's usual vagueness with a countable set of transformations. An essay built on the ledger opens by conceding what the cliché denies, that the plot, its order, and at points its phrasing are Brooke's, and then argues its thesis from the entries: that the dramatist's originality operates as a consistent set of pressures applied to borrowed material, the removal of delays through the compressed calendar and the shortened sleep, the removal of judgments through the stripped preface, the silenced narrator, and the repealed sentences, and the removal of distances through the conversion of narration into enacted scene. Each pressure can be evidenced from a row of the ledger and tested against the complication the ledger itself records, the losses, above all the marriage's surrendered duration. The essay's conclusion then writes itself with a precision the topic rarely achieves: originality in this play is not the invention of a story but the transformation of one, and the ledger is the document that proves the transformation was systematic rather than incidental.

### Q: Which of Shakespeare's changes would Brooke's own preface have condemned most?

Brooke's preface would have condemned most the change that governs all the others, the striking of the moral frame itself, because the preface exists precisely to forbid the response the play is engineered to produce. Brooke instructs his readers to view the lovers as a cautionary spectacle of unhonest desire, disobedience, and corrupt counsel, and promises that their end will instruct the young in obedience. A dramatist who replaced that instruction with a sympathetic sonnet, lowered the heroine's age until the blame slid from her shoulders onto her parents', silenced the narrator, pardoned the friar, and closed with golden statues instead of sentences would have seemed, by the preface's lights, to have taken a wholesome warning and converted it into an apology for everything the warning existed to deter. The sympathy is the offence. Brooke's whole volume is built to prevent an audience from admiring the lovers, and Shakespeare's whole play is built to make admiration unavoidable. That the two works share a plot almost entire makes the opposition cleaner: the same events, exhibited by the poem as a sentence and by the play as a loss, measure the distance between two moral worlds.

### Q: Did Shakespeare improve Brooke, or simply make a different work?

Shakespeare improved Brooke for the purposes of the work he was making, and the purposes are part of the judgment, as Geoffrey Bullough's standard comparison argued in awarding the play the advantage on change after change. Judged by effect, the compression converts fortune into visible causality, the age change redistributes blame more justly, the enlargements of Mercutio and the Nurse create the play's hinge and its deepest betrayal, the stripped frame converts pupils into judges, and the pardoned ending converts a penal exemplum into a civic tragedy. Each gain serves the governing design of a stage tragedy about haste and sympathy. The reservation, which Kenneth Muir's demonstration of the play's fidelity enforces, is that the design was achieved on a scaffold of Brooke's building, and that some costs were real: the marriage's duration, the poem's social breadth, the survivors' accountability. A difference becomes an improvement when its gains serve the work's ends and its costs fall where the work can bear them. By that standard the ledger's verdict stands: transformation, systematically in the direction of improvement, executed on borrowed ground.

### Q: What did Shakespeare keep from Brooke that most shaped the play?

The plot's architecture is the great kept thing, and among its parts the plague delayed letter is the piece that most shaped the play's ending. Shakespeare retained Brooke's whole sequence, feast, window, marriage, brawl, banishment, forced match, potion, tomb, and reconciliation, in Brooke's order, and within that architecture he retained the device by which the catastrophe is triggered: a friar's letter, entrusted to a fellow friar, stopped by a plague seal on a Mantua door. In Brooke the device operates inside a roomy calendar as one mischance among several. In the play, because everything around it was compressed, the same device becomes the single point of failure for the entire design, the one narrow gate through which rescue had to pass and did not. The keeping is as instructive as any change in the ledger. Shakespeare's judgment about his source was not that its machinery needed replacing but that its machinery needed pressure, and the retained letter, squeezed inside forty-two hours, generates more terror than any invention he might have substituted for it. Fidelity, in the ledger's accounting, was itself one of his instruments.
