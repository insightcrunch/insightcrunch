## The Pattern That Organizes the Field

American trade legislation after the middle of the twentieth century told a single story in many different statutory forms, and the story had a structure that repeated with enough regularity to give the field its shape. Congress delegates and then objects. The short sentence named a cycle rather than a single event. Congress held the constitutional power over tariffs and over commerce with foreign nations. Congress transferred part of the exercise of that power to the President and to agencies acting under presidential direction. The executive branch used the transferred authority to negotiate with foreign governments, to proclaim rate changes, to investigate injury and unfair practices, and to adjust imports under conditions that Congress had specified in advance. Members of Congress then complained, in hearings, in floor debate, in renewal fights, and in proposals to amend or withdraw the delegation, that the authority had been used too freely, or too narrowly, or in favor of the wrong industries, or at the expense of workers and firms that a different use would have protected. The complaint, examined closely, was a complaint about a delegation that Congress itself had written and, in most periods, had renewed. That tension between grant and regret did not mark a failure to understand the system. The tension was the system. It explained why trade statutes so often combined broad grants of negotiating or adjustment authority with detailed procedures, reporting requirements, investigations, findings, and opportunities for congressional intervention, and it explained why the history of the subject read less as a steady movement toward openness or toward protection and more as a continuing negotiation within the United States government about who would make trade decisions and under what constraints.

The turning point that organized the field came before the period opened, and an account that began in 1950 without looking backward would mistake a mature arrangement for a new invention. Two statutes framed the choice that later Congresses inherited. The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 represented congressional rate setting in its most complete form, with Congress fixing individual rates in the statute itself, product by product, after committee bargaining. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 represented the alternative, with Congress authorizing the President to negotiate reciprocal reductions and to put the results into effect by proclamation without further legislation. Every major statute after 1934 operated in the space between those two models, closer to one pole or the other in particular titles and particular years, but never returning in full to the method of 1930 and never severing the connection to the delegation of 1934. The postwar extensions, the large acts of 1962 and 1974, the implementing bills for multilateral and bilateral agreements, the preference programs, and the renewals of negotiating authority in 2002 and 2015 all presupposed that the President would negotiate and that Congress would set the terms on which negotiation occurred and would judge the results. The prehistory therefore mattered as more than background. The prehistory supplied the constitutional problem, the institutional alternative that the problem produced, and the vocabulary of grant, condition, finding, and renewal in which later disputes were conducted.

## The Constitutional Baseline

Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gave Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations. The placement of the power mattered. The text assigned tariff authority to the legislature by default, in the article that created Congress and enumerated its powers, rather than to the executive article that created the presidency. Executive tariff power therefore existed only by delegation. A President who proclaimed a rate change, adjusted an import, or concluded a trade agreement that altered duties acted because a statute, or a chain of statutes reaching back to a statute, had authorized the action. The point sounded elementary, and generations of trade debate treated the point as settled, but the point decided the terms on which every later controversy proceeded. When the executive branch defended an action, the defense ran through the statutory grant, its percentage limits, its required findings, its consultation procedures, and its expiration date. When members of Congress attacked an action, the attack ran through the same grant, with an argument that the action exceeded the grant, misread its standard, ignored its procedure, or revealed that the grant itself had been drawn too broadly and required revision. Constitutional argument in trade rarely took the form of a claim that the President possessed an independent tariff power that Congress could not control. Argument took the statutory form instead, which was another way of saying that the constitutional allocation shaped even the disputes that never reached a court.

The baseline also explained why trade legislation differed from ordinary domestic legislation in its preoccupation with time. A delegation could be written for a fixed term, and the fixed term gave Congress a scheduled moment of reconsideration that an ordinary permanent statute lacked. The initial grant in the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act ran for three years. Congress renewed the authority repeatedly into the 1960s, and each renewal forced a fresh assessment of rates negotiated, injuries claimed, and promises made to domestic producers at the time of the earlier grant. Expiration operated as a discipline on both branches. The executive branch negotiated in the shadow of a deadline, knowing that foreign governments discounted offers made under authority that might lapse and that Congress would judge the negotiating record when renewal came before the committees. Congress, for its part, faced a choice with consequences that a vote against a freestanding bill did not present in the same form, because refusal to renew left earlier proclamations and agreements in place while withdrawing the capacity to conclude new ones. Later law preserved the pattern in altered form. Negotiating authority lapsed, renewal bills failed, and new grants arrived with new titles and new conditions, and the intervals between lapse and renewal became part of the history in their own right. The constitutional baseline did not dictate the length of any grant or the conditions attached to a grant. The baseline dictated the question that every grant had to answer, which was how much of a legislative power Congress would lend, for how long, and on what terms of return.

Foreign commerce added a second feature to the baseline that domestic taxation lacked. A duty collected at the border was at once a revenue measure, an instrument of protection or liberalization for domestic production, and a term in a relationship with a foreign sovereign that could retaliate, bargain, or grant concessions of its own. Congress regulated that relationship by statute, but the conduct of negotiation with foreign governments belonged, in practice, to the executive branch, which maintained the diplomatic channels through which bargains were proposed and concluded. Trade legislation therefore had to join two functions that the Constitution had placed in different hands. The statutes that resulted were compromises between legislative control and executive conduct, drafted as authorizations with standards, as procedures for consultation and reporting, and as mechanisms that allowed Congress to approve or disapprove a completed bargain without rewriting the bargain line by line. The later invention of fast track procedures for implementing bills, addressed in the delivered treatment of the Trade Act of 1974, grew directly from that structural need. So did the statutory development of a specialized trade representative, an office created in position by the act of 1962, placed in the Executive Office of the President with Cabinet rank by the act of 1974, and renamed and consolidated as the Office of the United States Trade Representative under the reorganization direction of the act of 1979, effective in 1980. The institutional history traced the constitutional problem with unusual clarity. Congress wanted a negotiator strong enough to bargain abroad and accountable enough to answer at home, and legislation built, rebuilt, and relocated the office in pursuit of that combination.

## Legislation Written Line by Line

The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, Public Law 71-361, stood at one pole of the field, and the statute earned that position by its method as much as by its rates. Congress set individual rates in the statute itself, product by product, after committee bargaining. The method had a logic that a later age, accustomed to delegation, could underestimate. Tariff making in the legislative manner treated each rate as a political decision to be argued among representatives of the interests that the rate affected. A producer that sought a higher duty petitioned, testified, and bargained. A member who represented the district where the producer operated carried the request into committee. Opposing interests, including importers, exporters that feared retaliation, and producers that depended on imported inputs, pressed counterarguments through the same channels. The resulting schedule embodied thousands of such settlements, and the schedule carried the legitimacy of enactment, in that every rate had passed through the legislative process and could be defended or attacked as a vote that members had cast. The method also carried costs that became the central exhibit in the case for change. A process that rewarded organized local pressure line by line tended to produce a schedule shaped by the relative strength of petitioners rather than by a general policy toward trade, and a completed schedule offered foreign governments no single counterpart with authority to bargain the schedule down in exchange for concessions abroad. Revision required another statute, with another round of bargaining, at a moment when economic conditions and foreign responses had already moved.

The afterlife of the statute illustrated the weight that a legislative schedule carried even after policy turned against the method that produced the schedule. The column 2 rates of the 1930 act survived only for the few countries without normal trade relations. The narrow survival mattered for the architecture of later law. Rates enacted in 1930 remained as a residual category, a background against which negotiated rates and later normal trade relations status were measured, long after Congress had ceased to write new schedules in the manner of 1930. A statute could therefore persist as a reference point and as a penalty category while losing its place as the working method of tariff making. That distinction between survival and method helped explain why later debates invoked 1930 so often. Members and administrations invoked the act less for the precise level of any single rate than for the institutional memory of a legislature that had tried to govern trade by writing every line itself and had produced a schedule that the political system then spent years negotiating away from, concession by concession, under authority of a different kind. The memory functioned as a warning in both directions. Defenders of delegation warned against a return to line by line bargaining. Defenders of legislative control warned that the failures attributed to 1930 had become a convenient reason to discount congressional judgment in areas where congressional judgment remained the constitutional default.

A fair reading of the 1930 method also had to account for the protection argument on its own terms, stated with the same care that the liberalization argument received. Protection, in the reasoning that supported high legislatively set duties, defended domestic production and the employment that depended on production against competition that operated under lower costs abroad. The argument presented the tariff as a democratic instrument, in that the communities exposed to import competition could petition the institution that represented the communities and could obtain a rate that reflected a legislative judgment about the value of preserving an industry in a place. Liberalization, in the reasoning that supported negotiated reduction, defended exporters, consumers, and the broader gains of exchange against schedules that raised prices at home and invited retaliation abroad. The argument presented reciprocal negotiation as the disciplined alternative, in that reductions granted only in exchange for reductions received imposed a test that unilateral legislative increases escaped. The statutes that followed 1934 did not settle the contest between those reasonings. The statutes institutionalized the contest, assigning liberalization to negotiation under delegated authority and assigning protection a set of statutory remedies, including escape clause relief, adjustment assistance, safeguards, and national security adjustment, that operated within the delegated system rather than outside that system. The design choice was consequential. Once protection had to be claimed through investigations, findings, and presidential decisions under standards that Congress wrote, the old legislative method had been displaced even for the purposes that the old method had served most directly.

## The Delegation That Began the Modern Period

The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, Public Law 73-316, 48 Stat. 943, signed on June 12, 1934 by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, supplied the origin of everything that followed in the period, and the terms of the grant rewarded close reading because later Congresses copied, narrowed, expanded, and surrounded the original terms for the remainder of the century. The act authorized the President to negotiate reciprocal agreements cutting tariffs by up to 50 percent from existing rates. The percentage ceiling performed the classic work of a delegation limit. Congress chose the outer boundary of permissible reduction, and the President chose, through negotiation, where within the boundary each rate would fall and what foreign concession would be obtained in return. The act applied the negotiated rates on a most favored nation basis, so that a concession negotiated with one country extended to other countries that received that treatment rather than remaining a purely bilateral favor. The act put agreements into effect by proclamation without further legislation. The proclamation feature performed the decisive institutional work. A concluded agreement did not return to Congress as a bill, and the delay, amendment, and line by line reopening that a bill would have invited did not occur. Congress controlled the system at the point of authorization and renewal rather than at the point of each agreement, and that relocation of control, from the individual bargain to the governing grant, defined the modern period more than any single rate that any agreement produced.

Secretary of State Cordell Hull was the champion of the act, and the association between the statute and a Secretary of State rather than a Treasury official or a commerce official recorded an early understanding of what reciprocal negotiation involved. Trade agreements under the 1934 model were foreign policy instruments as well as tariff instruments. Negotiation required a department that conducted relations with foreign governments, and the statutory program placed the work where diplomatic conduct already resided. Later law would move the center of trade negotiation, through the creation of a special representative in 1962, the statutory placement of that office in the Executive Office of the President in 1974, and the consolidation of the Office of the United States Trade Representative in 1980. The movement of the office traced a long institutional judgment that trade negotiation had outgrown a single department with broader diplomatic responsibilities, yet the original placement explained the durability of the model. Negotiation by the executive, under legislative authorization, with results effective by proclamation, matched the function to the branch that could perform the function continuously, across sessions of Congress and across the intervals between elections. Opponents of the model answered that the match came at a constitutional price, in that the branch that negotiated also decided, within the ceiling, and that the legislature that bore constitutional responsibility for duties reviewed the program only at renewal, after agreements had altered the conditions on which domestic industries operated. The renewal debates that followed tested that answer repeatedly, and the amendments that Congress attached at renewal, including the escape clause and peril point procedure of the extension of 1951 and the national security clause of the extension of 1955, showed objection being converted into design rather than into repeal.

Duration completed the original design. The initial grant ran three years, and Congress renewed the authority repeatedly into the 1960s. The short initial term distinguished the 1934 act from a permanent transfer and gave the first renewal a character that later renewals retained even as grants lengthened, with the four year extension of 1958 standing as the longest grant in the program history to that date. Renewal forced the executive branch to defend a record. Rates negotiated, countries covered, injuries alleged, and concessions received all entered the renewal record, and members weighed the record against conditions in the districts and states that members represented. That structure of choice, grant for a term, judgment on a record, renewal with amendments or refusal, became the template for the politics of negotiating authority in later generations, including the lapse that began on April 16, 1994, the defeat of a renewal bill in the House on September 25, 1998, the renewal in the Trade Act of 2002, the lapse of that authority on July 1, 2007, and the renewal in the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, enacted as part of Public Law 114-26 and running by the enacted terms to July 1, 2021. The dates changed, the titles changed, and the conditions multiplied, but the constitutional rhythm established in 1934, delegation for a term followed by congressional judgment on the use of the delegation, supplied the rhythm that the later history followed.

## Delegation as the Starting Point

An era account that began with the extensions of the 1950s therefore began in the middle of an argument that the Constitution had framed and that the statutes of 1930 and 1934 had joined. The Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 wrote the escape clause into statute and added the peril point procedure and protections for farm price support programs, and the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955 added a national security clause that President Eisenhower used to impose crude oil import restrictions in March 1959. Each addition answered an objection that the original delegation had generated, and each addition left the delegation in place. To narrate that record as a contest between Congress and the President, with one branch winning and the other losing, would misstate the record that the statutes left. The record showed two branches bound in a single mechanism, in which legislative authorship and executive action each depended on the other, and in which complaint about the use of tariff authority was, on examination, complaint about delegations that Congress had written, renewed, amended, and written again. The mechanism did not guarantee wise rates, fair adjustment, or successful negotiation. The mechanism guaranteed that responsibility for trade outcomes would be shared, disputed, and periodically returned to Congress for judgment, and that guarantee, more than any individual agreement or proclamation, was the inheritance that the period opened with and the subject that the remainder of the article traces through the statutes themselves.

## The Postwar Settlement

The trade legislation of the nineteen fifties rested on a settlement reached before the decade opened. At its center stood a constitutional allocation of authority that delegation never displaced. Article I, Section Eight of the Constitution gives Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations. Tariff authority belonged to the legislature by default, and executive tariff power existed only by delegation, only within the terms Congress set, and only for as long as Congress allowed the delegation to run. Congress decided how much negotiating authority to lend to the President, for how long, on what conditions, and with what protections reserved against the consequences of agreements the President might reach. The extensions of the nineteen fifties were not routine renewals. Each renewal reopened the bargain between openness and protection, and each adjusted the terms, adding safeguards, procedures, and exclusions that recorded the concerns Congress brought to the question at that moment.

The older order against which that settlement defined itself was the order of the Smoot Hawley Tariff Act of nineteen thirty, in which Congress set individual rates in the statute itself, product by product, after committee bargaining. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of nineteen thirty four replaced that method for a defined class of decisions. Signed on June twelfth, nineteen thirty four, by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, and championed by Secretary of State Cordell Hull, the act authorized the President to negotiate reciprocal agreements cutting tariffs by up to fifty percent from existing rates, applied on a most favored nation basis, and put into effect by proclamation without further legislation. The initial grant ran three years, and Congress renewed it repeatedly into the nineteen sixties. That design supplied the legal vehicle through which the United States entered the multilateral arrangement negotiated after the Second World War, and it established the pattern the nineteen fifties extensions followed, time limited delegation, periodic congressional reconsideration, and conditions attached at each renewal.

## A Provisional Arrangement at Geneva

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was signed at Geneva on October thirtieth, nineteen forty seven, by twenty three countries, and it applied from January first, nineteen forty eight, under a Protocol of Provisional Application. Each element of that description carried weight. The agreement was signed at Geneva. It was applied from the first day of nineteen forty eight. The instrument that brought it into application was a protocol, and the application the protocol provided for was provisional. The arrangement was therefore operational without being final in form. Tariff concessions exchanged under it had practical effect, trade flowed under its disciplines, and governments organized their commercial policies around it, yet the legal foundation on which that activity rested announced its own temporary character in its name. Congress legislated repeatedly about the authority under which concessions were negotiated, while the multilateral instrument those concessions served remained, in form, a provisional application of an agreement the legislature had never itself approved.

For the United States, the manner of application mattered as much as the fact of it. The United States applied the General Agreement as an executive agreement under the authority of the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act, the delegated authority first granted in nineteen thirty four and renewed by Congress in successive statutes. It was never submitted to the Senate for advice and consent as a treaty, and Congress never approved the General Agreement of nineteen forty seven itself. Those two negatives defined the American posture for the entire period. No treaty process took place. No statute enacted approval of the agreement as such. What Congress did enact, before and after nineteen forty seven, was authority for the President to negotiate reciprocal trade agreements and to proclaim the resulting changes in duties. The executive agreement rested on that authority. The distinction meant that the American commitment ran through the delegation statutes, and that the conditions Congress wrote into those statutes, including the escape clause and the peril point procedure, formed part of the legal structure within which the United States participated. When Congress amended the delegation, Congress altered the terms on which American participation rested, even though the text signed at Geneva remained untouched.

The provisional character of the arrangement was deepened by the failure of the institution that was supposed to replace it. The planned International Trade Organization, embodied in the Havana Charter of nineteen forty eight, was never approved. The organization that would have given the postwar trading system a permanent institutional home therefore never came into being. In its absence, the provisional arrangement governed for nearly half a century. A protocol designed for provisional application carried the weight of the system decade after decade, through successive negotiating rounds and successive American delegation statutes, because the intended successor institution had not been approved and no other foundation had been put in its place. Congress did not explicitly approve a text of the General Agreement until the Uruguay Round Agreements Act of nineteen ninety four, which approved the General Agreement of nineteen ninety four as part of a congressional executive agreement rather than as a ratified treaty. That later action confirmed by contrast how unusual the earlier posture had been. During the nineteen fifties, the United States participated in a governing multilateral arrangement that rested on executive action under delegated authority, and Congress expressed its control not by approving the arrangement itself but by renewing, conditioning, and at times narrowing the authority that sustained it.

Because the delegation required periodic renewal, the question of trade policy returned to Congress on a regular cycle, and each return gave members an occasion to press for protections that a single permanent grant would have placed beyond easy reach. Protections written into a renewal statute reached directly into the conduct of American trade policy, governing what the President could concede, what had to be examined before a concession was made, and what could be done after a concession had caused injury. The three extension acts of the decade, in nineteen fifty one, nineteen fifty five, and nineteen fifty eight, followed that logic in sequence. The first wrote a safeguard and a pre negotiation check into statute, insulated farm programs, and excluded the Soviet bloc. The second added national security as a ground for adjusting imports, a ground the executive used before the decade ended. The third granted the longest extension in the history of the program to that date and amended the security clause the second had created. Congress kept the delegation, and Congress hedged it, renewal by renewal.

## The First Extension of the Decade

The Trade Agreements Extension Act of nineteen fifty one was signed on June twelfth, nineteen fifty one, by President Truman. The act extended the negotiating authority, and it did considerably more than extend it. In four distinct moves, Congress used the renewal to build protective machinery into the statute, to protect a domestic program from impairment by trade agreements, and to draw a geopolitical boundary around the concessions the United States would grant. The escape clause looked backward to injury already caused by a concession. The peril point procedure looked forward to injury that a proposed reduction might cause. The amendment concerning agricultural imports protected a standing domestic policy against erosion through negotiation. The denial of concessions to the Soviet Union and Soviet dominated states withheld the benefits of the program from a defined group of countries altogether. Together the four moves defined the protective architecture within which delegated negotiating authority operated during the years that followed.

Section seven of the act wrote the escape clause into statute. Under that provision, after a Tariff Commission investigation and a finding of serious injury from a concession, the President could modify or withdraw the concession. Each step in that sequence mattered. An investigation came first, conducted by the Tariff Commission, the body charged with examining the facts of imports, domestic production, and injury. A finding came next, and the finding had to be one of serious injury, a threshold that separated injury attributable to a concession from the ordinary difficulties of competition. Only after investigation and finding did executive power arise, and the power granted was specific. The President could modify the concession, adjusting its terms to relieve the injury, or withdraw it altogether. The escape clause thus converted a diplomatic commitment into a commitment held subject to a statutory condition. A concession granted in negotiation remained a concession, and trading partners received its benefit, but the concession carried within American law a defined route of relief when serious injury was established through the prescribed process. Placing the clause in statute gave domestic producers a procedure grounded in law, with an investigating body, a required finding, and a defined remedy, and it signaled to negotiating partners that American concessions were made within a framework Congress had specified in advance. Congress did not withdraw negotiating authority in the face of injury concerns, and Congress did not renew the authority unconditionally either. The renewal and the safeguard arrived in the same statute, each qualifying the meaning of the other.

## The Peril Point and the Logic of Prevention

The second move in the nineteen fifty one act reached the problem of injury from the opposite direction in time. The act added the peril point procedure, under which the Tariff Commission reported the lowest rate to which a duty could be cut without serious injury before negotiations took place. Where the escape clause responded after a concession had been granted and injury had followed, the peril point procedure intervened earlier, at the stage when the content of a possible concession remained under consideration. The Tariff Commission examined the product and the existing duty in advance and identified a floor, the point below which a reduction would, in the judgment embodied in its report, bring serious injury. A peril point marked the boundary between a reduction the domestic industry could absorb and a reduction that carried danger of serious harm. The report went forward before negotiations, so that the information existed within the government while positions were being formed and offers were being prepared. The pairing of the two procedures revealed a considered approach to risk. Before agreement, the peril point report supplied a measured assessment of how far a duty could fall. After agreement, the escape clause supplied relief when a concession nevertheless produced serious injury as established by investigation. Neither procedure set rates directly in the manner of the nineteen thirty statute, and neither returned Congress to product by product legislation. Both inserted expert findings into a system of delegated authority, channeling protection through investigation and report rather than through the bargaining of a tariff bill.

## Farm Programs and the Soviet Bloc

The third move protected a particular domestic policy rather than a particular industry in the ordinary escape clause sense. The act amended section twenty two of the Agricultural Adjustment Act so that no trade agreement could impair import fees or quotas imposed to protect farm price support programs. The logic followed from the structure of agricultural support. A price support program maintained returns to domestic producers at levels set by policy, and imports entering without restraint at lower prices could defeat that policy by supplying the market on terms the supported price could not meet. Import fees and quotas under section twenty two served as the border instruments that kept the domestic program workable. Without the amendment, a trade agreement negotiated under delegated authority might have cut across those instruments, conceding away at the border the protection the support program required. The amendment foreclosed that result in advance and in general terms. No trade agreement could impair the fees or quotas. The restraint bound the negotiator before any particular agreement was drafted, and it placed agricultural price supports outside the range of concessions reciprocal bargaining could reach. The escape clause relieved injury after investigation, the peril point warned against injury before negotiation, and the section twenty two amendment removed a defined category of protective instruments from negotiation altogether.

The fourth move drew the boundary of the program by country rather than by product or procedure. The act required denial of concessions to the Soviet Union and to Soviet dominated states. The requirement withheld the benefits of reciprocal agreements from the Soviet bloc as a matter of statutory command rather than executive choice in individual cases. A reciprocal trade program rests on exchange, on concessions given for concessions received within a circle of participating countries. The nineteen fifty one requirement defined part of the outside of that circle by law. Countries within the Soviet bloc were not to receive the tariff treatment that negotiation produced for others. That exclusion coexisted with the most favored nation principle that had structured the program since nineteen thirty four, under which concessions were applied on a general basis rather than confined to the negotiating partner alone. The exclusion showed that the general basis had statutory limits, set by Congress, reflecting distinctions among countries that Congress chose to write into the program. For the remainder of the decade, American trade legislation operated within the boundaries the nineteen fifty one act had drawn.

## National Security Enters the Statute

The Trade Agreements Extension Act of nineteen fifty five renewed the program again and added a ground of action with a long later history. The act, cited at sixty nine Statutes at Large page one hundred sixty two, added in its section seven a national security clause, the precursor of section two hundred thirty two. Under the clause, the Director of the Office of Defense Mobilization reported whether imports threatened national security, and on that basis the President could adjust imports. The design resembled the escape clause in its division of labor between inquiry and executive action, but the substance differed. The escape clause turned on serious injury to a domestic industry from a concession. The security clause turned on a threat to national security from imports. The first inquiry was economic and industry specific. The second reached the requirements of defense, a judgment entrusted in the first instance to the official responsible for defense mobilization. The act thereby recognized, in statutory form, that the volume and character of imports could bear on the security of the country in ways the ordinary calculus of commercial injury did not capture, and it provided a procedure by which that bearing could be reported and acted upon. The clause was framed in general terms, applicable to imports as such rather than to a named product, and its practical meaning would be settled by the occasions on which administrations chose to use it.

## Oil Restrictions Under the Security Clause

In March nineteen fifty nine, President Eisenhower used the national security clause to impose restrictions on imports of crude oil, acting by Proclamation thirty two seventy nine. The episode gave the clause its initial demonstration as a working instrument of policy. A provision added in nineteen fifty five as a general safeguard against security threats from imports became, four years later, the legal basis for concrete restrictions on a commodity central to both the civilian economy and defense planning. The sequence illustrated the way the delegation statutes of the period stored authority in advance of its use. Congress wrote the clause without specifying the product to which it might one day be applied. When the administration determined to act on oil, the statutory route lay ready, and presidential proclamation supplied the operative act. No new legislation was required at the moment of decision, because the legislation of nineteen fifty five had already allocated the roles of reporting official and deciding President. The episode also clarified how the security clause differed from the safeguard machinery of nineteen fifty one. Escape clause relief proceeded through Tariff Commission investigation of injury from a concession, peril point reports preceded negotiation, and the security clause proceeded from a report on threat to national security. Oil in nineteen fifty nine was addressed through the third of those channels.

## The Four Year Extension

The Trade Agreements Extension Act of nineteen fifty eight completed the sequence. Cited at seventy two Statutes at Large page six hundred seventy three, the act granted a four year extension of the negotiating authority, the longest grant in the history of the program to that date, and it amended the national security clause the nineteen fifty five act had added. The length of the extension carried its own message. The original grant of nineteen thirty four had run three years, and renewals since then had kept the program on a short legislative leash, returning the question to Congress at frequent intervals. A four year grant did not abandon that pattern, and the authority remained time limited and subject to reconsideration when the term expired. The longer term nevertheless gave negotiators a wider horizon within which to plan and conduct bargaining, and it gave trading partners a longer period during which the authority behind American offers would remain in force. In a system built on reciprocal exchange, the credibility of an offer depended in part on the durability of the authority under which the offer was made, and Congress in nineteen fifty eight chose to supply more of that durability than any earlier Congress in the program had supplied. The amendment of the security clause in the same act showed Congress continuing to work on the machinery of protection even while extending the term of the grant. The clause had entered the law only three years earlier, and Congress returned to its text at the first renewal after its enactment and amended it, treating the clause as a living part of the statutory scheme.

By the end of the decade, the postwar settlement had a recognizable shape. At the international level, the United States participated in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade under a provisional application that had begun in nineteen forty eight, through an executive agreement resting on delegated reciprocal trade authority, without treaty submission to the Senate and without congressional approval of the agreement itself, while the planned International Trade Organization remained unapproved. At the domestic level, the delegation statutes supplied both the engine of negotiation and the brakes upon it, in the escape clause, the peril point procedure, the protection of section twenty two agricultural controls, the denial of concessions to the Soviet bloc, and the national security clause as added in nineteen fifty five and amended in nineteen fifty eight. At the level of practice, the nineteen fifty nine oil restrictions under President Eisenhower showed the security clause in operation. The settlement was durable precisely because it was conditional. Reciprocal negotiation appeared in the extensions themselves, statutory protection appeared in the conditions attached to them, and Congress preserved its constitutional primacy over duties and foreign commerce by keeping the whole structure dependent on grants that expired and had to be renewed.

## From Reciprocal Authority to a New Scale of Negotiation

The two statutes at the center of the postwar trade system did not create executive trade power from nothing, and neither statute removed Congress from the field. Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution gives Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations, so tariff authority remained legislative by default and executive tariff power existed only by delegation. That constitutional starting point explains the form of every grant that followed. Congress decided how much negotiating authority to delegate, for what purposes, under what procedures, and with what protections for domestic interests, while the President conducted negotiations and put authorized results into effect. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and the Trade Act of 1974 each rebuilt that delegation for a different problem. The earlier statute enlarged the scale of tariff bargaining and joined expansion to adjustment and national security. The later statute rebuilt the procedure for approving agreements, strengthened the instruments for responding to foreign practices and import injury, added a preference program, attached an emigration condition to benefits for nonmarket economies, and gave the chief trade negotiator a firmer statutory home. Together, the two measures supplied much of the architecture within which later trade legislation operated.

The older model came from the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, Public Law 73-316, signed on June 12, 1934 by President Franklin D. Roosevelt and championed by Secretary of State Cordell Hull. That act authorized the President to negotiate reciprocal agreements cutting tariffs by up to 50 percent from existing rates, applied the reductions on a most favored nation basis, and allowed the agreements to take effect by proclamation without further legislation. The initial grant ran for three years, and Congress renewed the authority repeatedly into the 1960s. The contrast with the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 was sharp. Under Smoot-Hawley, Congress had set individual rates in the statute itself, product by product, after committee bargaining. The reciprocal program moved the detailed bargaining to the executive while Congress retained control over the existence, size, and duration of the authority. Support for that approach rested on the proposition that reciprocal reductions could open foreign markets for American exporters while lowering duties at home. Resistance rested on the concern that reductions negotiated in general bargains could expose particular American producers to import competition without a legislative vote on each rate. Neither position disappeared after 1934. Instead, Congress wrote qualifications into later extensions, and those qualifications became part of the setting for both statutes considered here.

During the 1950s, Congress preserved reciprocal negotiating authority while adding devices that limited or conditioned its use. The Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 wrote the escape clause into statute in its section 7, under which, after a Tariff Commission investigation and a finding of serious injury from a concession, the President could modify or withdraw the concession. The same act added the peril point procedure, under which the Tariff Commission reported the lowest rate to which a duty could be cut without serious injury before negotiations began. It also amended section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act so that no trade agreement could impair import fees or quotas imposed to protect farm price support programs, and it required denial of concessions to the Soviet Union and Soviet-dominated states. The Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955 added a national security clause in its section 7, a precursor of section 232, under which the Director of the Office of Defense Mobilization reported whether imports threatened national security and the President could adjust imports. President Eisenhower used that authority to impose crude oil import restrictions in March 1959 through Proclamation 3279. The Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1958 provided a four-year extension, the longest grant in the history of the program to that date, and amended the national security clause. By the early 1960s, therefore, the delegation already contained a recognizable balance between negotiated reductions and an escape route for injured industries, a warning procedure before negotiations, protection for farm programs, a denial rule for a defined group of states, and a national security power over imports.

The international legal setting reinforced the need for a new grant. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was signed at Geneva on October 30, 1947 by 23 countries and applied from January 1, 1948 under a Protocol of Provisional Application. The United States applied the agreement as an executive agreement under the reciprocal trade agreements authority. The agreement was never submitted to the Senate for advice and consent as a treaty, and Congress never approved the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of 1947 itself. The planned International Trade Organization, embodied in the Havana Charter of 1948, was never approved, so the provisional arrangement governed for nearly half a century. American negotiators therefore operated inside a multilateral framework that Congress had enabled through delegated authority without approving the underlying agreement as a treaty or as a statute. A broader grant could support a larger round of bargaining, but the grant had to answer the same domestic questions that had produced the escape clause, the peril point, and the national security clause.

## The Trade Expansion Act and the Architecture of Expansion

The Trade Expansion Act of 1962, Public Law 87-794, changed the unit of negotiation. Earlier reciprocal authority had supported agreements that reduced duties from existing rates, while the 1962 act authorized across-the-board tariff reductions aimed at the European Economic Community and the Kennedy Round. The shift in wording carried a shift in method. Across-the-board bargaining treated categories of trade as subjects for a general negotiation rather than leaving every concession to isolated product bargaining of the kind associated with Smoot-Hawley. Congress thereby gave the President authority suited to a negotiation in which the European Economic Community occupied a central place and in which American access to that market formed a principal object of the bargain. The authority also fitted the multilateral Kennedy Round, where reductions by many participants had to be assembled into a single negotiating enterprise. A reader seeking the detailed treatment of the statute, its provisions, and its operation can follow [the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 guide](/2020/08/01/trade-expansion-act-1962-guide/), while the place of the act in the longer sequence can be stated plainly. Congress moved from repeated extensions of a reciprocal program built around limited percentage cuts toward a grant designed for broad reductions negotiated with a large economic bloc and through a multilateral round.

That enlargement did not rest on an assumption that every domestic consequence of lower duties would be beneficial or evenly shared. The act created trade adjustment assistance, and the creation of that assistance formed one of its most consequential contributions. Adjustment assistance recognized, inside the statute that expanded trade, that trade expansion could impose concentrated costs even when a negotiation served broader national purposes. The device differed from the escape clause. An escape clause response modified or withdrew a concession after an investigation and a finding of serious injury, pulling back part of the bargain itself. Adjustment assistance offered a separate response directed toward adjustment rather than toward reversal of the concession. Congress thus placed two different answers to import injury side by side in the development of the system. One answer protected a domestic industry by altering the treatment of imports. Another answer accepted the negotiated opening while providing assistance connected with adjustment to its effects. Advocates of liberalization could point to adjustment assistance as evidence that expansion did not require indifference to displaced workers or struggling firms. Advocates of stronger protection could answer that assistance after injury did not equal prevention of injury and that the safeguard and security powers remained necessary. The statute did not settle that argument. Its contribution lay in giving each side an institution within the same legal framework, so later debates concerned the adequacy, eligibility rules, and generosity of assistance and safeguards rather than whether domestic costs belonged in trade legislation at all.

Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act supplied the national security component of that framework. Its lineage reached back to the national security clause added in 1955 and amended in 1958, yet placement in the 1962 act gave the authority a durable statutory location within a major trade statute. The provision treated imports not only as a commercial matter but also as a possible national security matter, subject to executive adjustment when the statutory standard was met. That placement preserved a distinction that ran through the entire period. Tariff reductions rested on delegated negotiating authority and reciprocal bargains. Safeguards rested on injury caused by imports. Section 232 rested on national security. Each rationale produced a different kind of executive action and a different relation to Congress. President Eisenhower had used the precursor clause for crude oil import restrictions in 1959, before the 1962 act took its place in the sequence. In 2018, President Trump adopted steel and aluminum measures under section 232. Those two uses, separated by almost six decades, show the persistence of the form Congress created, without requiring agreement about the merits of either action.

The act also created the position of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations. The office was formed in 1962 and organized by executive order in January 1963. That institutional step deserves weight equal to the tariff authority because broad negotiations required a negotiator whose assignment centered on trade rather than on the whole range of foreign or economic policy. Before that creation, Secretary of State Cordell Hull had championed the reciprocal program from within the Department of State, and trade authority had operated through the general institutions of the presidency. The new position identified trade negotiation as a continuing presidential function with a named holder. The design did not transfer Congress's constitutional authority to the negotiator, and the position depended on statutory grants for the bargains the negotiator could conclude. The design instead addressed coordination and representation, giving the program a recognizable point of responsibility across agriculture, industry, foreign policy, and domestic adjustment. The later history of the office confirms the importance of the step while also showing that the 1962 creation was only the first stage. The Trade Act of 1974 placed the office in the Executive Office of the President by statute and raised it to Cabinet level, and action under the Trade Agreements Act of 1979 later renamed and consolidated the office as the Office of the United States Trade Representative, effective in 1980.

Taken together, the parts of the Trade Expansion Act formed a coherent answer to the conditions Congress faced. Across-the-board authority matched the scale of negotiation with the European Economic Community and the Kennedy Round. Trade adjustment assistance acknowledged domestic dislocation without making withdrawal of concessions the only response. Section 232 preserved an import power grounded in national security rather than in reciprocity or industry injury. The Special Representative gave the negotiating program a dedicated institutional head. None of those elements abolished the older protective devices, and the act should not be read as a simple victory for one side of the trade argument. A negotiator received authority broad enough for a major round, but the authority operated inside a statute that recognized injury, adjustment, and security as legitimate parts of trade policy. The next major reconstruction, in 1974, kept that recognition and changed the surrounding procedure even more deeply.

## The Trade Act and the Reconstruction of the Bargain

The Trade Act of 1974, Public Law 93-618, addressed a problem that broader negotiating authority alone could not solve. Agreements increasingly reached beyond simple tariff schedules into practices and measures that required implementing legislation, and Congress needed a procedure that allowed an agreement to be considered as a negotiated package while preserving a legislative role in putting the agreement into domestic law. The act created fast track procedures for implementing bills. Fast track changed the relation between negotiation and legislation by establishing the procedural route in advance, before particular implementing bills arrived. The executive negotiated with knowledge of the route an implementing bill would follow, and Congress acted through procedures the statute itself had created rather than improvising a process for each bargain. A full account of the machinery belongs with [the Trade Act of 1974 complete guide](/2020/06/01/trade-act-1974-complete-guide/), but the contribution to the longer system is clear. The 1934 model had relied heavily on presidential proclamation after reciprocal agreements within a delegated percentage limit. The 1974 model supplied a defined legislative path for agreements that required an implementing bill. Delegation remained essential, yet the form of delegation changed. Congress set procedures for consideration of the result, and the President obtained a more predictable legislative process for negotiated commitments that could not rest on proclamation alone.

The practical significance of that procedural creation appeared in the Trade Agreements Act of 1979, the first trade implementing bill passed under the fast track procedures of the 1974 act. The 1979 act implemented the Tokyo Round agreements on subsidies, government procurement, standards, and other nontariff measures. Its passage demonstrated that the route created in 1974 could carry agreements dealing with nontariff subjects through Congress as implementing legislation. The Trade Act therefore did more than renew authority for another round. Congress assembled a toolkit in which negotiation, enforcement, relief, preference, and conditionality each had a statutory instrument, and in which substantive powers operated outside a single negotiation as well as inside one.

Section 301 supplied the enforcement instrument directed at unfair practices, with retaliation as the sanction behind the process. Its place in the act signaled that American trade law would not depend only on persuading partners to lower barriers through reciprocal bargains or on defending domestic industries after imports arrived. Section 301 gave the United States a statutory basis for identifying foreign practices covered by the provision, pursuing their removal, and retaliating when the process reached that point. Supporters of a firmer enforcement approach treated section 301 as a necessary answer to practices that placed American commerce at a disadvantage and that general negotiations had not removed. Supporters of a more restrained approach warned that unilateral retaliation could provoke countermeasures and weaken negotiated rules. The statute adopted the instrument without resolving that policy dispute for all cases. Later uses kept the dispute visible. A semiconductor arrangement with Japan followed a section 301 case in 1986 under President Reagan, section 301 cases ran through the late 1980s, and the Super 301 identifications of 1989 and 1990 followed under President George H. W. Bush. In 2018, President Trump took tariff actions against China under section 301. The sequence shows a power created in 1974 being used by administrations of different parties across different decades, while arguments over unilateral pressure versus negotiated settlement continued around each use.

Section 201 supplied the safeguard instrument in modern form. It carried forward the escape clause idea that Congress had written into statute in 1951, under which relief followed an investigation and a finding of serious injury from increased imports, but the 1974 act placed safeguards within its broader reconstruction of trade remedies. The distinction between section 201 and section 301 mattered. Section 301 concerned unfair foreign practices and retaliation. Section 201 concerned import injury and safeguard relief, without requiring the same focus on a foreign practice as unfair. The distinction between section 201 and section 232 mattered as well. Section 201 rested on serious injury to a domestic industry, while section 232 rested on national security. Congress maintained separate channels because the justifications, investigations, and political meaning of relief differed in each channel. Later uses illustrated the safeguard channel in operation. In 1983, President Reagan provided safeguard relief for heavyweight motorcycles under section 201. In 2002, President George W. Bush adopted steel safeguard measures under section 201 and withdrew the measures in 2003. Those examples show that the safeguard form enacted in 1974 remained available as a lawful alternative to permanent tariff legislation and to action under the national security provision, without proving that relief was warranted in either case.

The act also created the Generalized System of Preferences, adding a nonreciprocal preference program to a system built largely around reciprocal bargaining. That addition broadened the purposes of American trade legislation. Reciprocal agreements exchanged concessions. Safeguards limited imports after injury. Section 301 pressed against foreign practices. A preference program granted more favorable treatment as a matter of policy rather than as one side of an exchange negotiated at the same moment. The program therefore gave Congress and the President another way to differentiate among trading partners and among categories of trade, separate from the rates produced by multilateral rounds or bilateral agreements. Preferences thereby became a regular instrument of trade legislation, and each program required Congress to decide which products and beneficiaries fell inside or outside the favorable treatment.

The Jackson-Vanik provision added a condition of a different kind by linking trade benefits for nonmarket economies to emigration. The provision placed an emigration condition on credits and concessions for nonmarket economies, so access to defined American benefits depended on more than commercial terms. That design marked a departure from a framework concerned only with duties, quotas, subsidies, procurement, and standards. Congress used trade legislation to press a human freedom concern through economic conditionality. Defenders of the linkage argued that credits and concessions were privileges Congress could condition and that emigration gave the condition a concrete and observable test. Opponents of linkage argued that trade benefits should turn on commercial policy and that conditions drawn from another field could complicate negotiations with nonmarket economies. The act embodied the linkage position as law, and later legislation had to work around or through that choice. The point became especially visible when the China permanent normal trade relations statute of 2000 granted permanent normal trade relations to China and ended the annual Jackson-Vanik waiver review for China ahead of Chinese accession to the World Trade Organization. By that later date, the 1974 condition had structured the legal status of trade with affected nonmarket economies for more than two decades, and removing the annual review for one country required another statute.

Finally, the Trade Act placed the office of the Special Representative in the Executive Office of the President in statute and raised the office to Cabinet level. That step completed the middle stage in the three-stage development of the office. The Trade Expansion Act had created the position in 1962, the Trade Act gave the office a statutory presidential home and Cabinet rank in 1974, and the reorganization directed under the Trade Agreements Act of 1979 led to the renamed and consolidated Office of the United States Trade Representative under Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1979 and Executive Order 12188 in January 1980. The 1974 step mattered because statutory placement carried a different weight from creation of a position followed by organization through executive order. Congress recognized the negotiator as part of the Executive Office structure and elevated the rank attached to the office, strengthening the negotiator's standing in dealings with foreign governments and within the American government. Rank and location did not themselves supply negotiating authority, which depended on grants from Congress. The step instead gave the office a clearer institutional base from which to use authority that other provisions supplied, in a system where fast track procedures, section 301 investigations, safeguard proceedings, preference programs, and Jackson-Vanik conditions all required administration and coordination.

The Trade Act of 1974 therefore stands beside the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 as a central statute for reasons broader than any single provision. One statute expanded the bargain and recognized its domestic costs. The other supplied procedures for legislating complex bargains and tools for acting when negotiation alone did not govern the problem. Liberalization and protection both found instruments in each law, which is why neither law can be described accurately as belonging wholly to one side of American trade debate. Congress used both statutes to delegate power, and Congress used both statutes to define the conditions, remedies, institutions, and exceptions that kept that delegation under legislative design.

## Implementation Through the Tokyo Round Codes

The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 occupied a distinct place in the sequence of trade statutes because the act tested a procedure and implemented a set of agreements at the same time. The act, Public Law 96-39, 93 Stat. 144, enacted on July 26, 1979, implemented the Tokyo Round agreements. Those agreements took the form of codes on subsidies, government procurement, standards, and other nontariff measures. The description mattered because the subject matter extended beyond the border duty itself. Earlier reciprocal authority had centered on the reduction of tariff rates, applied on a most favored nation basis and put into effect by proclamation. The Tokyo Round codes addressed conduct behind the border, where a government could affect trade through a subsidy to a producer, through the purchasing decisions of a public agency, or through a technical standard applied to an imported product. Implementation therefore required changes in domestic law and practice that a tariff proclamation alone could not supply, and Congress supplied those changes through an implementing bill.

The procedural path of the bill gave the statute its second significance. The act was the first trade implementing bill passed under the fast track procedures of the Trade Act of 1974. The 1974 act had created fast track procedures for implementing bills, alongside section 301 on unfair practices and retaliation, section 201 safeguards in modern form, the Generalized System of Preferences, and the Jackson-Vanik emigration condition on credits and concessions for nonmarket economies. Fast track connected negotiation abroad to enactment at home. A President negotiated under authority granted in advance, and Congress then considered the implementing legislation under the special procedure. For the Tokyo Round, the arrangement moved from design to use. The House passed the bill on July 11, 1979, by 395 to 7, and the Senate passed the bill on July 23, 1979, by 90 to 4. The margins recorded broad support in both chambers, and enactment followed three days after Senate passage.

The breadth of the vote invites attention to what the codes did. A code on subsidies addressed the circumstances in which government support for production could be treated as a trade distortion and the remedies available when subsidized imports caused injury. A code on government procurement addressed access to public purchasing, an area of commerce that governments had often reserved for domestic suppliers. A code on standards addressed the risk that product requirements, testing, and certification could operate as barriers separate from any stated protective purpose. Each code sought rules for practices that tariff reduction had left largely untouched. Supporters of liberalization, in the tradition associated with Secretary of State Cordell Hull and the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, treated negotiated rules of this kind as a method for expanding trade without surrendering congressional authority, because Congress defined the negotiating authority and enacted the implementation. Defenders of protection for import sensitive industries noted that a rule on subsidies or procurement could constrain domestic policy choices and that concessions without enforceable discipline on foreign practices could leave domestic producers exposed. Congress accepted the negotiated codes through a statute that preserved a recorded vote in each chamber and placed implementation inside domestic law.

Implementation operated in a system where negotiated liberalization and relief provisions coexisted. A producer injured by a surge in imports could look to safeguard procedures under section 201, a complaint about an unfair foreign practice could be framed under section 301, and a complaint about subsidized imports could be framed under the subsidy discipline that the Tokyo Round implementation carried into domestic law. The statute assigned each pressure a channel without resolving the tension between open markets and domestic adjustment, and that assignment helps explain why the vote could be so wide. Implementation also raised a question of administration, because codes on procurement, standards, and subsidies required coordination across agencies. The trade representative stood at the center of that coordination, and the 1979 act addressed the office directly through a reorganization direction.

## The Trade Representative Office in Three Steps

The history of the trade representative office, stated accurately, unfolded in three steps across three statutes, and no single statute performed the whole work. The first step belonged to the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, Public Law 87-794. That act authorized across the board tariff reductions aimed at the European Economic Community and the Kennedy Round, created trade adjustment assistance, and contained section 232 as its national security provision. The same act created the position of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations. The office formed in 1962 and was organized by executive order in January 1963. The creation of a dedicated position separated trade negotiation from the ordinary conduct of foreign relations and gave the President a named official for the Kennedy Round and for the expanding agenda that followed. The position existed, but statutory placement and rank developed later.

The second step belonged to the Trade Act of 1974, Public Law 93-618. That act established the office of the Special Representative in the Executive Office of the President in statute and raised the office to Cabinet level. Placement in the Executive Office of the President located the negotiator close to the President rather than inside a department with a sectoral mission. Cabinet level rank signaled that trade negotiation ranked among the principal functions of the executive branch and that the representative dealt with other governments and with Congress from a position of recognized standing. The 1974 act combined that institutional elevation with substantive tools, including fast track, section 301, section 201 safeguards, the Generalized System of Preferences, and the Jackson-Vanik condition. Institution and authority therefore advanced together. Congress enlarged what the negotiator could seek and clarified where the negotiator sat while seeking it.

The third step belonged to the Trade Agreements Act of 1979, and the step took the form of direction rather than instant creation. The 1979 act directed the President to submit a reorganization plan for the trade functions. Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1979 and Executive Order 12188, issued in January 1980, renamed the office the Office of the United States Trade Representative and consolidated the trade negotiating functions in that office. The sequence is the verified record. The 1962 act created the position. The 1974 act placed the office in the Executive Office of the President in statute at Cabinet level. The 1979 act directed a reorganization, and the plan and executive order that followed from that direction produced the renamed and consolidated office in 1980. A compressed statement that the 1979 act created the Office of the United States Trade Representative misstates each part of the sequence, because creation, placement, and renaming occurred under different authorities in different years.

Consolidation carried practical meaning beyond a change of name. Tariff negotiation, Tokyo Round code negotiation, preference administration under the Generalized System of Preferences, and enforcement work under section 301 each drew on related knowledge about foreign practices, domestic injury, and congressional intent. A consolidated office could maintain that knowledge in one place and present a single negotiating position abroad, while a fragmented arrangement invited conflicting signals without a central arbiter below the President. Placement in the Executive Office of the President, combined with consolidation, supplied that arbiter in the trade representative, subject to presidential direction and to the authority Congress had granted by statute. The record also left the constitutional baseline intact. Article I, Section 8 gave Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations, and executive tariff and negotiating power existed only by delegation.

## Regional Preferences and the Caribbean Basin Program

The Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act of 1983 marked a change in the form of preference granted by the United States. The Generalized System of Preferences, created by the Trade Act of 1974, offered preferences on a general basis to developing countries that met statutory conditions. The Caribbean Basin act moved toward geography as an organizing principle. The act, Public Law 98-67, 97 Stat. 369, approved on August 5, 1983, was the first regionally targeted United States trade preference program. The program granted duty free treatment to designated Caribbean Basin countries. Designation confined the benefit to a defined set of beneficiaries in a defined region, and duty free treatment removed the tariff on covered products from those beneficiaries without requiring a reciprocal reduction from the beneficiary in the manner of a negotiated trade agreement.

A preference program of this kind differed from a free trade agreement in both obligation and logic. Under a reciprocal agreement, each party reduced barriers and accepted rules, and Congress implemented the bargain through an implementing act. Under a unilateral preference, Congress granted a benefit by statute and retained the ability to define coverage, exclusions, and conditions within the statute itself. The beneficiary received improved access to the United States market as an instrument of economic policy toward the region, and the United States did not receive a negotiated tariff concession in return under the same instrument. The regional focus sharpened the instrument. Rather than spreading a preference across all eligible developing countries, Congress directed the benefit toward Caribbean Basin economies whose trade and employment depended heavily on access to the nearby United States market.

Exclusions defined the limits of the grant as clearly as the grant defined its reach. The program excluded import sensitive products, including apparel, petroleum, footwear, and others. Each named category represented a sector where domestic producers faced direct competition from lower cost imports and where duty free entry could concentrate adjustment pressure on particular communities and workforces. Apparel and footwear involved labor intensive production with a long record of import sensitivity in congressional debate. Petroleum involved energy supply and pricing considerations distinct from ordinary consumer goods. The presence of an exclusion list showed that a preference, even when framed as assistance to a region, operated inside the same domestic balance that shaped tariff negotiation. Congress extended a benefit to designated countries and withheld the benefit where domestic sensitivity was judged greatest. The pattern repeated a familiar structure from earlier trade law, where the escape clause in the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 and the protection of farm price support programs under section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act had each limited or conditioned a general movement toward liberalization.

The arguments around the program followed that structure. Supporters of liberalization presented duty free access as a method for encouraging export earnings, employment, and economic stability in the Caribbean Basin. Defenders of domestic production in the excluded sectors presented the exclusion list as the essential safeguard, arguing that a preference without limits would transfer the cost of regional policy onto workers in apparel, footwear, and related industries. Both positions accepted the regional premise, and the disagreement concerned coverage. Congress resolved the disagreement product by product, granting duty free treatment broadly within the program and carving out the products where sensitivity was most pronounced. The program did not negotiate reciprocal liberalization and did not create a free trade area. Its contribution lay in targeting, as a preference aimed at one region, administered through designation and limited by exclusions stated in advance.

## The First Bilateral Free Trade Agreements

The United States-Israel Free Trade Area Implementation Act of 1985 moved United States practice from unilateral preference and multilateral code implementation toward the bilateral free trade agreement as a distinct instrument. The act, Public Law 99-47, 99 Stat. 82, enacted on June 11, 1985, implemented the first United States bilateral free trade agreement. The agreement had been negotiated under the Trade and Tariff Act of 1984, and the agreement entered into force on September 1, 1985. The House passed the implementing bill on May 7, 1985, by 422 to 0. A unanimous House vote for a trade implementing bill recorded an absence of organized opposition to that particular bargain, with a partner whose trade volume posed limited adjustment pressure for most domestic sectors. The form mattered as much as the partner. A bilateral agreement reduced barriers between two parties on a reciprocal basis and rested on negotiated commitments from each side, unlike a unilateral preference that Congress could grant, limit, or withdraw as a matter of domestic policy toward a beneficiary.

The distinction between a free trade agreement and most favored nation treatment required care. Under the reciprocal program from 1934 onward, concessions negotiated with one partner were applied on a most favored nation basis, extending the benefit broadly. A free trade agreement created a preferential arrangement between the parties that did not extend automatically to all other trading partners. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade had governed United States trade as an executive agreement applied from January 1, 1948 under a Protocol of Provisional Application, without Senate advice and consent as a treaty and without congressional approval of the General Agreement of 1947 itself. A bilateral free trade agreement operated within that wider system as an exception for deeper liberalization between the parties, and the Israel agreement tested congressional willingness to approve such an exception through an implementing act.

The United States-Canada Free-Trade Agreement Implementation Act of 1988 extended the bilateral form to the largest trading relationship on the northern border. The act, Public Law 100-449, was enacted on September 28, 1988, and the agreement entered into force on January 1, 1989. The House passed the bill on August 9, 1988, by 366 to 40, and the Senate passed the bill on September 19, 1988, by 83 to 9. The margins remained wide, though the House recorded opposition that the Israel vote had not shown, a difference consistent with the scale of Canada trade and the number of domestic sectors with a direct stake in the terms. Supporters of liberalization emphasized reciprocal access to a major market between two economies already joined by extensive cross border commerce. Defenders of protection emphasized sectoral exposure and the risk that reciprocal openness would accelerate adjustment in industries where Canadian competition was immediate. Congress approved the agreement by large margins in both chambers.

The subsequent status of the Canada agreement added a further point of precision. The agreement stood suspended for as long as the North American Free Trade Agreement remained in effect. Suspension differed from termination. The Canada agreement did not operate while the broader North American arrangement governed trade among the United States, Canada, and Mexico, because duplicative operation of two agreements covering much of the same bilateral trade would have created conflicting obligations and administrative confusion. Suspension preserved the earlier agreement in a dormant state tied to the duration of the later arrangement. The record therefore required the conditional phrasing. The Canada agreement entered into force in 1989, and the agreement stood suspended for as long as the North American Free Trade Agreement remained in effect.

Taken together, the Israel and Canada acts established a bilateral track. Each act followed a recognizable pattern, with negotiation under authority granted in advance, congressional consideration of an implementing bill, and entry into force on a stated date. From the reciprocal program came reciprocity and presidential negotiation under delegated authority, and from the 1974 act came the implementing bill as the vehicle for congressional approval. The bilateral track did not replace multilateral negotiation or unilateral preference. The track added a middle form, deeper than a preference and narrower than a round, suited to partners where Congress was prepared to approve reciprocal duty free trade in full.

## The Omnibus Act, Super Enforcement, and the Harmonized Schedule

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, gathered several distinct reforms into one statute. The act did not implement a single negotiated agreement in the manner of the 1979 act or the bilateral acts of 1985 and 1988. The act instead revised the tools with which the United States conducted trade policy, tightened a procedure for identifying and pursuing unfair practices, modernized the tariff schedule itself, and assigned the Treasury Department a recurring duty on currency practices. The omnibus form allowed Congress to legislate across those subjects in one measure rather than through separate bills that might move at different speeds or fail independently.

The enforcement centerpiece became known as Super 301. The provision operated as section 310 of the Trade Act of 1974, as added by section 1302 of the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988. Section 310 required the trade representative to identify priority unfair practices and priority countries in 1989 and 1990 and to open section 301 investigations against them. The design altered the starting point of enforcement. Ordinary section 301 work could begin with a petition or with a decision by the trade representative to investigate a foreign practice. Super 301 imposed an affirmative identification duty for two stated years, directing the representative to name the practices and countries judged most significant and to initiate investigations on that basis. The time limit mattered. Congress did not create a permanent annual identification duty under section 310 in the form enacted in 1988. Congress required identifications in 1989 and 1990, creating a concentrated period during which the administration had to state priorities publicly and act on the statement through formal investigations. Identifications followed in 1989 and 1990 under President George H. W. Bush, after section 301 cases had run through the late 1980s under President Reagan, including the semiconductor arrangement with Japan that followed a section 301 case in 1986.

Supporters of a firmer enforcement posture argued that negotiated liberalization depended on credibility, that a trading partner that maintained an unfair practice while enjoying access to the United States market gained an advantage that ordinary diplomacy had failed to remove, and that a statutory identification duty forced attention onto the most consequential practices. Defenders of a more restrained posture argued that a public priority list risked provoking retaliation, that unilateral determinations of unfairness could strain the multilateral system built around the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, and that pressure for results within a fixed cycle could push the representative toward confrontation where negotiation required more time. Congress adopted the firmer design for the two stated years while leaving the underlying section 301 authority in place as the vehicle for investigation and possible retaliation, combining a congressional directive on priorities with executive conduct of each case.

The second major element concerned the language in which tariffs were written. The act approved accession to the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System Convention and replaced the old tariff schedules with the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, in use from 1989. A tariff schedule classifies goods, and classification decides the duty that applies at the border. Before harmonization, the United States used its own schedule structure, and trading partners used structures that did not align category by category, complicating comparison, negotiation, and customs administration. A harmonized system supplied a shared classification framework, so that a product category carried a common description and code across participating countries even though the duty rate remained a matter for each country under its commitments and laws. Adoption changed the organization of the schedule without by itself changing the level of protection on any particular product, and a common system reduced the friction created by divergent national schedules for importers, customs officers, and negotiators.

The third element assigned a surveillance and negotiation duty on currency. The act required the Treasury Department to analyze and report semiannually on the exchange rate policies of major trading partners and to open negotiations with any country found to be manipulating its currency. The provision recognized that an exchange rate could affect trade flows as powerfully as a tariff or a subsidy, because a currency held below a level that market forces would produce could lower the price of exports and raise the price of imports without any change in a tariff schedule. Congress placed the duty with the Treasury Department rather than with the trade representative and required regularity through semiannual reporting rather than a single study. A finding of manipulation triggered a duty to open negotiations, connecting analysis to action in the same provision. The design stopped short of prescribing a tariff remedy for currency practices inside the omnibus act itself, and the structure preserved a distinction between the trade enforcement tools under section 301 and section 310 and the monetary channel assigned to the Treasury Department.

Read as a whole, the omnibus act showed Congress legislating on process, classification, and monetary surveillance in the same measure that sharpened enforcement priorities. Trade outcomes depended on whether unfair practices were identified and pursued, on whether goods were classified under a system that traders and officials in different countries could read in common, and on whether currency policies received sustained official attention. The 1979 act had implemented codes for nontariff measures and set in motion the consolidation of the trade representative office, the 1983 act had targeted a preference by region, and the bilateral acts had built a separate track. The omnibus act drew those threads toward administration and enforcement through the priority identification duty for 1989 and 1990, the Harmonized Tariff Schedule from 1989, and the semiannual Treasury reporting duty.

## The Regional Turn

By the early nineteen nineties the structure of American trade law had been built around two tracks that had developed over decades. One track was multilateral, centered on the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and on successive rounds of negotiation among many countries at once. The other track was bilateral and regional, centered on agreements with individual partners or with a defined group of countries. Legislation in nineteen ninety three and nineteen ninety four brought both tracks to a point of decision in the same Congress, and the choices made in those two years set the institutional form that American trade agreements took for the generation that followed. The North American Free Trade Agreement was a regional agreement negotiated alongside a multilateral round, and its implementing legislation arrived first. The Uruguay Round agreements were the product of that multilateral round, and their implementing legislation arrived a year later. Read together, the two statutes show why regional and global liberalization proceeded in parallel rather than in sequence, and why Congress treated the two kinds of agreement through the same legislative device while giving them different places in the history of the trading system.

The regional track had a visible legislative history before North America became its focus. The Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act of nineteen eighty three had established a regionally targeted preference program for designated Caribbean Basin countries, with import sensitive products excluded. The United States Israel Free Trade Area Implementation Act of nineteen eighty five had implemented the first bilateral free trade agreement entered by the United States. The United States Canada Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act of nineteen eighty eight had implemented a bilateral agreement with Canada that entered into force on January first, nineteen eighty nine. That Canadian agreement occupied a distinctive position in what followed, because the fact of a separate agreement with Canada meant that a wider North American arrangement had to define its relationship to the earlier bilateral text. The record preserved in the legislation provided that the Canada agreement stood suspended for as long as the North American agreement was in effect. The suspension arrangement illustrated a general feature of regional lawmaking in this period. Each new agreement did not erase the earlier instrument in a simple act of repeal. Instead, Congress legislated the relationship between instruments, specifying which obligations governed while a broader agreement remained operative.

The North American Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act was enacted on December eighth, nineteen ninety three, as Public Law one hundred three to one hundred eighty two, and the agreement it implemented entered into force on January first, nineteen ninety four. The statute was the congressional act that gave domestic legal effect to an agreement among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Its passage followed the fast track tradition that Congress had created in the Trade Act of nineteen seventy four, under which implementing bills for trade agreements moved through Congress under procedures designed to produce an up or down vote without amendment of the negotiated text. That procedural background mattered because a regional agreement of this scope touched tariff schedules, rules for goods crossing borders within North America, and commitments that required changes to domestic law to be effective inside the United States. Without an implementing statute, the international agreement and domestic law would have remained separate. With the statute, the agreement acquired a defined place in American law on a date certain.

The vote margins in nineteen ninety three recorded a Congress divided more closely on the regional agreement than it had been on earlier implementing bills. The House of Representatives passed the implementing bill on November seventeenth, nineteen ninety three, by a vote of two hundred thirty four to two hundred. The Senate passed the bill on November twentieth, nineteen ninety three, by a vote of sixty one to thirty eight. Those margins contrasted with the broad majorities that had approved the Tokyo Round agreements in nineteen seventy nine, when the House had voted three hundred ninety five to seven and the Senate had voted ninety to four, and with the unanimous House vote for the Israel agreement in nineteen eighty five. The narrower result did not alter the legal character of the act. Once enacted, the North American agreement had the same statutory foundation as its predecessors. The closeness of the vote instead marked the political setting in which regional integration was debated, a setting in which questions about manufacturing employment, agricultural competition, and the movement of production within North America received sustained attention in committee and on the floor. The statute answered those questions in legislative form by approving the agreement and by setting the terms on which its provisions would apply in domestic law from the first day of nineteen ninety four.

To understand the place of the regional agreement, it helps to place its timing beside the multilateral negotiation that ran at the same time. While governments negotiated a North American arrangement, they also negotiated in the Uruguay Round, the multilateral round conducted under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. The two negotiations were not alternatives in a formal sense. A country could pursue tariff reduction and rule making with its immediate neighbors and pursue tariff reduction and rule making with a much larger group of countries during the same years, and the United States did both. Regional negotiation offered a setting in which a small number of parties could address the conditions of trade among economies joined by geography and by extensive cross border production. Multilateral negotiation offered a setting in which a large number of parties could address the conditions of trade on a global scale and could build or revise institutions intended to serve all members. The legislation of nineteen ninety three and nineteen ninety four preserved that distinction. Congress implemented the regional agreement in its own statute and implemented the multilateral agreements in a separate statute, even though the two measures were considered in successive years and even though they rested on related negotiating authority.

## The Last Completed Multilateral Round

The Uruguay Round Agreements Act was enacted on December eighth, nineteen ninety four, as Public Law one hundred three to four hundred sixty five. The House of Representatives passed the bill on November twenty ninth, nineteen ninety four, by a vote of two hundred eighty eight to one hundred forty six. The Senate passed the bill on December first, nineteen ninety four, by a vote of seventy six to twenty four. The act implemented the Uruguay Round agreements and created the World Trade Organization, which entered into force on January first, nineteen ninety five. Each element of that sentence carried institutional weight. Implementation meant that Congress approved the agreements and made the changes to domestic law that the agreements required. Creation of the World Trade Organization meant that the agreements established a permanent organization to administer the multilateral trading system, replacing an arrangement that had rested on a provisional foundation since the late nineteen forties. Entry into force on a stated date meant that the new organization and the agreements it administered began to operate under the new legal structure at the start of nineteen ninety five.

The background against which Congress acted in nineteen ninety four explains why the creation of the World Trade Organization was treated as a structural change rather than as a routine extension of earlier practice. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade had been signed at Geneva on October thirtieth, nineteen forty seven, by twenty three countries, and it had applied from January first, nineteen forty eight, under a Protocol of Provisional Application. The planned International Trade Organization, set out in the Havana Charter of nineteen forty eight, was never approved. As a result, the provisional arrangement governed for nearly half a century. The United States applied the General Agreement as an executive agreement under the authority that the President held under the reciprocal trade agreements program. The General Agreement of nineteen forty seven was never submitted to the Senate for advice and consent as a treaty, and Congress never approved that text itself. Successive rounds of negotiation therefore took place within a framework that functioned in practice as the central institution of world trade while lacking, in American law, a direct act of congressional approval for its founding text and lacking, in international form, the permanent organization that the Havana Charter had contemplated.

The Uruguay Round Agreements Act changed that posture in a specific and limited way that Congress chose deliberately. In that act, Congress explicitly approved a General Agreement text for the first time. The text was the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of nineteen ninety four, a component of the Uruguay Round agreements. Approval came as part of a congressional executive agreement rather than as a ratified treaty. The distinction between those two forms is constitutional and procedural. A treaty requires the advice and consent of the Senate, with two thirds of senators present concurring, before ratification. A congressional executive agreement rests on approval by both houses of Congress through ordinary legislation, signed by the President, and it carries domestic legal force as a statute. By using the second form, Congress placed the approval of the nineteen ninety four text inside the same kind of implementing legislation that it had used for the Tokyo Round agreements in nineteen seventy nine and for bilateral and regional agreements in the nineteen eighties and nineteen ninety three. The choice preserved the role of the House of Representatives in the decision, a role that the treaty form would not have provided, and it connected approval of the international text to the domestic law changes that implementation required in a single legislative act.

The creation of the World Trade Organization completed the movement away from provisional administration. Where the General Agreement had operated under a protocol that described its application as provisional, the new organization possessed a defined institutional existence from January first, nineteen ninety five. The agreements implemented by the nineteen ninety four act were placed under that organization. For American law, the significance lay in the combination of approval and implementation. Congress did not merely take note of an organization created elsewhere. Congress approved the text, implemented the agreements, and provided the domestic legal basis on which the United States participated in the organization. That combination distinguished nineteen ninety four from nineteen forty seven. In nineteen forty seven, executive action under delegated negotiating authority had brought the General Agreement into application for the United States. In nineteen ninety four, a statute enacted by both houses and signed into law approved the successor text and established the terms of participation in the new body.

The Uruguay Round also holds a place in the sequence of multilateral negotiations that no later round had displaced at the vantage of this article. The Uruguay Round was the last completed global round of multilateral trade negotiations. Earlier rounds had reduced tariffs and had, in the Tokyo Round, produced codes on subsidies, government procurement, standards, and other nontariff measures that the Trade Agreements Act of nineteen seventy nine implemented. The Uruguay Round concluded that progression by producing agreements comprehensive enough to support a permanent organization. Describing the round as the last completed global round is a statement about completion rather than about effort. Negotiating activity in the multilateral system did not end in nineteen ninety four. What ended was the pattern in which a single round, launched and concluded as a unified undertaking among the members, produced a package of agreements that Congress then implemented in one act. Later trade lawmaking in the United States therefore worked mainly through other forms, including bilateral and regional implementing bills and the renewal of negotiating authority, rather than through the implementation of another completed global round.

The relationship between the two statutes of nineteen ninety three and nineteen ninety four also clarified the hierarchy of commitments that American firms and agencies had to apply. A trader moving goods within North America looked to the regional agreement and to its implementing act for the preferential terms that governed that movement. A trader engaged in commerce covered by the multilateral agreements looked to the Uruguay Round agreements and to the organization that administered them for the baseline terms that governed among members generally. The two layers coexisted. Preferential regional terms did not displace the multilateral baseline for trade outside the region, and the multilateral baseline did not prevent members of a regional agreement from granting one another treatment more favorable than the baseline within the terms that the multilateral system allowed. Congress legislated both layers without merging them, and the separate statutes, separate votes, and separate entry into force dates preserved the distinction for courts, agencies, and private parties who had to determine which set of commitments applied to a given transaction.

There was also a procedural lesson in the sequence. Negotiating authority had lapsed on April sixteenth, nineteen ninety four, between the enactment of the North American implementing act and the enactment of the Uruguay Round implementing act. The lapse did not prevent completion of the Uruguay Round legislation, because the agreements had been negotiated and concluded under authority and procedures that applied to them, but the lapse marked the end of a period in which new authority was continuously available. A later attempt to renew authority failed when a renewal bill was defeated in the House on September twenty fifth, nineteen ninety eight. Authority was not renewed until the Trade Act of two thousand two. In that longer view, nineteen ninety four stands as the close of an era in which Congress first implemented a major regional agreement and then, within thirteen months, implemented the final multilateral round and approved a General Agreement text directly for the first time. The two acts together form the legislative peak of the postwar multilateral and regional system as it had developed since the reciprocal trade agreements program began.

## Permanent Normal Trade Relations for China

The third statute in this sequence addressed a single country relationship through a device that reached back to the Trade Act of nineteen seventy four. That act had enacted the Jackson Vanik condition, which tied credits and trade concessions for nonmarket economies to emigration conditions and which, in practice, required an annual waiver process for countries subject to its terms. China had been governed under that annual process. Each year, the continuation of normal trade relations treatment for Chinese goods depended on a presidential waiver and on the absence of congressional disapproval, so the tariff treatment applied to a major trading partner rested on a yearly political decision rather than on a permanent statutory grant. The legislation of two thousand changed that basis in advance of a change in China's position in the multilateral system.

The act granting permanent normal trade relations to China was Public Law one hundred six to two hundred eighty six, enacted in two thousand. It granted permanent normal trade relations to China and ended the annual Jackson Vanik waiver review for China ahead of its accession to the World Trade Organization. The sequence in that description is essential. Congress acted ahead of accession, not after it. By granting permanent treatment in advance, Congress ensured that when China entered the organization, the United States would be in a position to apply the multilateral agreements to trade with China on the permanent footing that membership contemplated, and that Chinese goods would receive the normal trade relations treatment that the statute provided without the yearly contingency that the waiver process had imposed. Readers following the full statutory and diplomatic record for this step can consult [the China PNTR guide](/2020/07/15/china-pntr-2000-guide/) for the detailed treatment given to the measure in its own article.

Ending the annual review removed a recurring legislative occasion that had structured debate over China policy for years. Under the waiver system, trade treatment and broader questions about conditions inside China had been joined in a single annual decision, because the waiver was the legal vehicle through which normal treatment continued. A permanent grant separated those questions as a matter of trade law. After two thousand, the tariff treatment of Chinese goods rested on the permanent statute and on the multilateral commitments that accession would bring into operation between the two countries, rather than on a yearly determination. That change did not resolve the broader policy questions that had been aired during the annual reviews. It relocated them. Congress retained its general authority over commerce with foreign nations under Article One, Section Eight, and it retained the statutory tools that trade law provided for particular practices, including the safeguard and unfair practice provisions that earlier acts had created. What Congress relinquished in the China statute was the specific annual leverage that the Jackson Vanik waiver process had supplied for that country.

The China statute also completed, for this period, the connection between bilateral statutory action and the multilateral institution created in nineteen ninety four. The World Trade Organization had entered into force in nineteen ninety five without China as a member. China's accession would extend the organizations membership to an economy whose trade had grown under a separate and conditional American statutory regime. Permanent normal trade relations aligned American domestic law with that prospective extension before the extension occurred. The alignment illustrates how Congress used different legislative forms for different tasks in the same broad project. For North America, Congress implemented a regional agreement. For the Uruguay Round, Congress approved a multilateral text and implemented the agreements that created a permanent organization. For China, Congress passed a focused statute that removed a condition dating from nineteen seventy four so that the multilateral system and bilateral treatment would operate on a consistent permanent basis once accession took place.

Taken together, the three statutes trace a progression in the geography and the legal form of American trade commitments at the end of the twentieth century. The North American act deepened integration with the two countries that share land borders with the United States, building on the earlier Canadian bilateral agreement that it suspended while the regional agreement remained in effect. The Uruguay Round act placed American participation in the global system on an explicitly approved statutory foundation and under a permanent organization for the first time. The China act removed the last annual condition that had kept the treatment of a prospective member of that organization on a temporary footing in American law. Each act used the implementing or granting statute as its vehicle. None proceeded as a treaty submitted for Senate advice and consent alone. That shared legislative form is the thread that connects the regional turn, the final multilateral round, and the preparation for China's entry into the organization that the nineteen ninety four act had helped to create.

## When Negotiating Authority Ran Out

Negotiating authority lapsed on April 16, 1994. The date mattered because of the way trade power had been structured under the Constitution and under the statutes that followed. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations. Tariff authority is legislative by default, and executive tariff power exists only by delegation. A lapse in negotiating authority therefore did not remove a power that the presidency held on its own. It allowed a delegation to run out, and it left the conduct of trade negotiation without a new grant under the procedures that earlier statutes had built. The distinction between an authority ending and an agreement ending was equally important. Agreements already approved and put into force did not disappear when the grant behind later negotiations expired. The lapse governed what could be negotiated next, on what terms, and through what legislative path, rather than undoing obligations that Congress had already enacted into law through implementing statutes.

Trade legislation after the middle of the twentieth century had rested on a repeated bargain between the two branches. Congress set the terms, the limits, and the duration of a delegation, and the President negotiated within those terms. The Trade Act of 1974 had added fast track procedures for implementing bills, a device that gave an agreement negotiated under a delegation a defined route through Congress. When the delegation itself was absent, that route had no new agreement to carry. A promise made by negotiators depended, in the end, on a statute that would approve it, and the existence or absence of advance authority affected how much confidence any counterpart could place in a bargain struck in Washington. The lapse was thus a fact about credibility and procedure at the same time, and not merely a gap in a legislative calendar.

The timing gave the lapse added weight. The NAFTA Implementation Act had been enacted on December 8, 1993, and NAFTA entered into force on January 1, 1994. The Uruguay Round Agreements Act followed on December 8, 1994, after the lapse date, implementing the Uruguay Round agreements and creating the World Trade Organization, which entered into force on January 1, 1995. Congress proved able to approve two very large agreements in that period. The House passed the NAFTA bill on November 17, 1993, by 234 to 200, and the Senate followed on November 20, 1993, by 61 to 38. The House passed the Uruguay Round bill on November 29, 1994, by 288 to 146, and the Senate followed on December 1, 1994, by 76 to 24. The lapse therefore described something narrower than paralysis. It described the absence of a standing delegation under which new negotiations could be opened and carried toward an implementing bill with the procedural assurances that earlier grants had supplied.

## The Failed Renewal and the Eight Year Interval

A renewal bill was defeated in the House on September 25, 1998. The bill was H.R. 2621. Its defeat extended the lapse from a pause between grants into a condition that lasted, in total, from 1994 to 2002. The completed lapses in negotiating authority before October 15, 2020, were two in number, the lapse of 1994 to 2002 and the lapse of 2007 to 2015, and the first of those lapses acquired its length in part through the failure of 1998. Had the House approved H.R. 2621 on September 25, 1998, the interval would have closed roughly halfway through its eventual span. Instead, the vote left the earlier expiration in place, and the years continued to accumulate without a general negotiating grant of the kind that had supported earlier rounds.

The eight year lapse became a structural fact of the field, and it deserves treatment as structure rather than as anecdote. During a lapse, the instruments that Congress had created for purposes other than new negotiation did not vanish. Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 addressed unfair practices and retaliation. Section 201 provided safeguards in modern form. Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 addressed national security. Those instruments allowed presidents to act against particular practices, particular import surges, and particular security claims under authorities that stood apart from the expired negotiating grant. The semiconductor arrangement with Japan in 1986 followed a section 301 case under President Reagan, and section 301 cases ran through the late 1980s, with Super 301 identifications following in 1989 and 1990 under President George H. W. Bush. President George W. Bush imposed steel safeguard measures under section 201 in 2002, and those measures were withdrawn in 2003. A reader who followed only the unilateral record could miss the lapse altogether, because action continued. A reader who followed only the lapse could miss the action, because the government retained tools that did not depend on negotiating authority. Both records belonged to the same field, and the discipline of the period lay in keeping them separate.

Arguments over renewal reflected the division that trade votes had displayed throughout the period. Advocates of liberalization, in the congressional debates of the era, presented delegated authority as the means by which American exporters gained access to foreign markets under rules that Congress had approved in advance, and they treated the absence of authority as a cost measured in negotiations that other governments conducted among themselves. Defenders of protection, in the same debates, presented limits on delegation as a guard against agreements that exposed American labor to competition under terms that Congress could shape only after the bargaining had ended. Neither position belonged to one party alone, as the close coalitions behind the major trade votes demonstrated. The failed renewal of 1998 was a decision, taken by vote, about how much authority to delegate and on what terms, and its consequence was that the question remained unanswered by statute for four more years.

## The Trade Act That Restored Authority

The Trade Act of 2002, P.L. 107-210, was signed on August 6, 2002, by President George W. Bush. The act renewed negotiating authority. Its Title XXI was the Bipartisan Trade Promotion Authority Act of 2002, and the word bipartisan in that title carried its own information about the coalition the statute claimed to represent. The renewed authority covered agreements entered into before July 1, 2007. That formulation mattered. The grant was not open ended, and it did not promise that every negotiation begun under it would end in an enacted implementing bill. It defined a window. Agreements had to be entered into before the terminal date to fall within the grant, and Congress retained the later decision, on each agreement, whether to approve the implementing legislation that would give the bargain domestic legal effect. Delegation and approval remained two separate acts, performed at two separate stages, by design.

Readers who want the mechanics of that authority set out in full can turn to [the trade promotion authority guide](/2020/08/15/trade-promotion-authority-explained/), which treats the 2015 statute and the procedures around it as its governing subject. Within the longer history, the 2002 act occupied a particular place. It was the first general renewal since the lapse of April 16, 1994. It followed the failed renewal attempt of 1998. It arrived in the same statute as a reform and expansion of trade adjustment assistance, a pairing that linked the opening of markets abroad to a domestic program at home. And it framed a period of agreement making whose products were mostly bilateral and regional rather than global. The Uruguay Round remained the last completed global round of multilateral trade negotiations. No successor round of that scale was completed under the 2002 authority. The output of the renewed grant was instead a sequence of individual agreements, each with its own implementing act, its own public law number, and its own date of entry into force.

## Trade Adjustment Assistance Reformed and Expanded

The Trade Act of 2002 reformed and expanded trade adjustment assistance. The program had a long pedigree by then. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 had created trade adjustment assistance, in the same statute that authorized across the board tariff reductions aimed at the European Economic Community and the Kennedy Round and that carried section 232 as its national security provision. The pairing of liberalization with assistance was therefore as old as the modern program itself. From 1962 forward, Congress repeatedly joined the two ideas in one legislative settlement, on the reasoning, stated in debate by proponents of the pairing, that a policy which opened the economy to wider competition owed a defined response to workers whose displacement that competition caused. Opponents of expanded assistance, also speaking in debate, questioned the cost of the program and the fairness of treating trade related displacement differently from displacement with other causes. The statute of 2002 took a position in that long argument by reforming the program and expanding it, at the moment when it also renewed the authority to negotiate new agreements.

The placement of the assistance provisions inside the same public law as the negotiating grant was a legislative fact with practical consequences. A member of Congress voting on P.L. 107-210 voted on both elements together. By 2002 Congress chose to restore negotiating authority only inside a statute that also addressed, in expanded form, the domestic costs that trade agreements were understood to impose. The authority and the assistance rose together, and later arguments about either one rarely stayed separate from arguments about the other.

## A Wave Written One Agreement at a Time

The bilateral wave that the 2002 renewal framed is best read as a list, because a list is what Congress produced. Each agreement arrived as its own implementing act. Each act carried its own public law number. Each agreement entered into force on its own date, or awaited enactment on a timetable that the terminal date of the authority did not by itself control. Jordan came first in the sequence as recorded, under P.L. 107-43 in 2001, with the agreement in force on December 17, 2001, a date that preceded the signature of the Trade Act of 2002 by several months. Then came Chile under P.L. 108-77 and Singapore under P.L. 108-78, both enacted on September 3, 2003, and both in force from January 1, 2004. Australia followed under P.L. 108-286, in force from January 1, 2005. Morocco followed under P.L. 108-302, in force from January 1, 2006. The Central America and Dominican Republic agreement, CAFTA-DR, was enacted under P.L. 109-53. Bahrain followed under P.L. 109-169, and Oman under P.L. 109-283. Peru was enacted under P.L. 110-138, signed on December 14, 2007. The final group, South Korea under P.L. 112-41, Colombia under P.L. 112-42, and Panama under P.L. 112-43, was enacted together on October 21, 2011, implementing agreements signed in 2006 and 2007 before the 2002 authority lapsed.

Read as a chronology, the wave had a clear shape. It began with individual partners, Jordan, Chile, and Singapore, and it moved outward to Australia and Morocco, then to a regional agreement in Central America with the Dominican Republic, then to Bahrain and Oman, then to Peru, and at last, after a long delay between signature and enactment, to South Korea, Colombia, and Panama. The sequence also crossed administrations in its enactment phase, since agreements signed in 2006 and 2007 under one presidency were enacted in 2011 under another. The authority under which an agreement was negotiated, the date on which the agreement was signed, the date on which Congress enacted its implementing bill, and the date on which the agreement entered into force were four different events, and the wave cannot be understood if those events are collapsed into one another.

## Margins, Timing, and the Meaning of the Votes

The votes on the wave were not uniform, and the differences in margin carried information. CAFTA-DR passed the House by 217 to 215, and a margin of two votes meant that the agreement survived by the smallest coalition that could enact it. The closeness stood in a long line of close trade votes. The House had passed NAFTA by 234 to 200. It had passed the bill implementing the first bilateral free trade agreement, with Israel, on May 7, 1985, by 422 to 0, a unanimity that belonged to an earlier and smaller undertaking. A narrow margin did not make an enacted agreement less law than a wide one. It did affect the atmosphere in which the next negotiation was proposed, because each vote taught negotiators and members alike how much room a further agreement would have.

Timing carried information as well. Chile and Singapore were enacted on the same day, September 3, 2003, and entered into force together on January 1, 2004, a pairing that suggested a deliberate grouping of two completed negotiations into one legislative moment. Australia entered into force on January 1, 2005, and Morocco on January 1, 2006, a rhythm of one major entry into force at the opening of successive years that gave the wave a regular public cadence in its middle phase. The later phase lost that regularity. Peru was signed into law on December 14, 2007, after the authority had reached its terminal date on July 1, 2007. The three agreements enacted on October 21, 2011, had been signed in 2006 and 2007, so that four years and more separated signature from enactment for agreements that the authority had covered when they were entered into. Whether an agreement was entered into before July 1, 2007, determined its place under the 2002 grant. Whether and when Congress enacted its implementing bill was a later legislative decision, and the gap between the two events in the final group was the longest in the wave.

That gap was argued in both directions in the debates of the time. Proponents of prompt enactment presented delay as a cost to American exporters and as a signal to partners that a signed American agreement might wait years for approval. Proponents of delay presented the interval as the space in which Congress performed its constitutional role, examining a completed text and deciding whether the bargain served the national interest. The fast track design of earlier statutes had aimed to reduce exactly this kind of uncertainty by giving negotiated agreements a defined legislative path, and the experience of the final group showed the limits of procedure when the political coalition for enactment had to be rebuilt years after signature. Procedure could structure a decision. It could not supply the votes.

## The Second Lapse and the Renewal That Followed

The 2002 authority lapsed on July 1, 2007. The date had been written into the grant from the beginning, as the boundary of the window within which agreements had to be entered into, so its arrival surprised no participant who had read the statute. Negotiations not concluded by that date lost the cover of the delegation, and the field entered its second completed lapse, running from 2007 to 2015. The symmetry with the first lapse was close but not exact. The first lapse had lasted eight years, from 1994 to 2002, and had included a failed renewal vote in 1998 at its midpoint. The second lapse also lasted eight years, from 2007 to 2015, and it included, within its span, the enactment in 2011 of the three agreements signed before the lapse began. Trade legislation therefore continued during the second lapse, in the form of approval for bargains already struck, even as authority to strike new bargains under a general grant remained absent. The same distinction that organized the first lapse, between authority to negotiate and power to approve, organized the second as well.

During the second lapse, the other instruments of American trade law again operated on their own foundations, as they had during the first. President Trump imposed steel and aluminum measures under section 232 in 2018 and tariff actions against China under section 301 in the same year, under authorities that traced to the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and the Trade Act of 1974 rather than to any negotiating grant. Delegated negotiating authority rose and fell on its own statutory cycle of grant, lapse, failed renewal, and renewal. Enforcement and safeguard authorities, once enacted, persisted across the cycles and remained available in years when no negotiating authority existed at all.

Renewal came in 2015. The Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015 was enacted as part of P.L. 114-26. Its name joined the two themes that the 2002 statute had joined, trade priorities on one side and accountability on the other, promotion of negotiation balanced against congressional direction of its terms. The 2015 act renewed negotiating authority after the second eight year lapse, restoring the delegation on fresh terms and for a fresh period defined in the statute itself. With that enactment, the cycle that had begun in 1994 completed a second full turn, lapse and renewal, lapse and renewal, across twenty one years. The pattern had become one of the defining rhythms of American trade legislation in the period since 1950. The earlier reciprocal trade program, under which an initial three year grant in 1934 had been renewed repeatedly into the 1960s, had known no lapse of comparable duration in the record, while the later program twice stopped for eight years at a time.

## Authority in Force on the Article Date

On October 15, 2020, the negotiating authority renewed in 2015 stood in force. The Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, enacted as part of P.L. 114-26, had renewed that authority, and by its enacted terms the authority ran to July 1, 2021. That end date was a schedule written into the statute, a future boundary of the kind that had structured every grant in the series, and not a description of an authority already ended. The distinction mattered for any reader placing the article date inside the longer chronology. October 15, 2020, fell within the window of the 2015 grant, in the same way that a date in 2005 fell within the window of the 2002 grant and a date in 1996 fell within the first lapse. Each date in the chronology belonged to a defined condition, grant or lapse, and the condition in force on the article date was a grant.

Placing the article date in that way also clarifies the completed record that lay behind it. Two lapses stood completed by October 15, 2020, the lapse of 1994 to 2002 and the lapse of 2007 to 2015. Two renewals had answered them, the Trade Act of 2002 and the act of 2015. One failed renewal stood in the record, the defeat of H.R. 2621 in the House on September 25, 1998. Around those turning points, the bilateral wave had supplied the period with its enacted agreements, Jordan, Chile, Singapore, Australia, Morocco, CAFTA-DR, Bahrain, Oman, and Peru, followed in enactment by South Korea, Colombia, and Panama in 2011. The multilateral achievement of the period remained the Uruguay Round, the last completed global round, approved before the first lapse had run a full year. The whole arrangement, grants and lapses, bilateral agreements and unilateral instruments, assistance reformed and expanded alongside authority, formed the field as it stood on the article date, with the next boundary of the cycle fixed by statute for July 1, 2021, and the disposition of authority beyond that date left, as it had always been left, to a future act of Congress.

The table below is the findable artifact of this guide: the statute sequence in one view, with the year, the public law number, the function the statute performed in the system, and the phase of the periodization to which it belongs. The function column uses the three roles the brief of the field supplies. A statute delegated when it transferred a portion of the exercise of the tariff power to the executive. A statute implemented when it approved and put into domestic effect an agreement already negotiated. A statute created or strengthened a remedy when its principal work was to build machinery for relief against injury, unfair practices, or security threats. ## The Third Phase and the Use of Older Authorities

The third phase differs from the first two in the source of the action rather than in the identity of the actor. In the first phase, the President negotiated with many partners at once under authority granted for that purpose. In the second phase, the President negotiated with one partner or one region at a time under the same general system. In the third phase, the President acted without a new negotiation and without a new grant, under provisions enacted years or decades earlier. Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 supplied a national security provision under which the President could adjust imports. Section 201 of the Trade Act of 1974 supplied safeguard relief in modern form. Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 supplied a procedure directed at unfair practices, and the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 added Super 301, which required identification of priority practices and priority countries in 1989 and 1990. The machinery that operated those authorities receives separate treatment in [the tariff law administration guide](/2020/10/01/us-tariff-law-administration/) and that machinery helps explain why a delegation enacted in one decade remained usable in another.

The record of use, stated by administration and year and by instrument, runs across the whole span covered by this article. In 1959 President Eisenhower imposed crude oil import restrictions under the national security clause added by the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955, a precursor of section 232. In 1983 President Reagan granted safeguard relief for heavyweight motorcycles under section 201. In 1986 a semiconductor arrangement with Japan followed a section 301 case under President Reagan, and section 301 cases ran through the late 1980s. Super 301 identifications followed in 1989 and 1990 under President George H. W. Bush. In 2002 President George W. Bush imposed steel safeguard measures under section 201, and those measures were withdrawn in 2003. In 2018 President Trump imposed steel and aluminum measures under section 232 and tariff actions against China under section 301. Each action rested on an earlier grant, and each raised the same constitutional question in a new setting. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations, and executive tariff power exists only by delegation.

The third phase therefore works as a center of gravity for the later record rather than as a wholly separate era. Negotiating authority did not disappear when unilateral instruments gained prominence. Congress enacted the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015 as part of Public Law 114-26, and that authority ran by its enacted terms to July 1, 2021, so it stood in force on October 15, 2020, the date of this article. Authority to negotiate and authority to act alone coexisted at the end of the period, just as both kinds of authority had coexisted since the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 created a multilateral negotiating grant and the section 232 tool in a single statute. The change is one of emphasis and sequence. Earlier grants were used first and most visibly for negotiation. Later use drew more heavily on the parts of earlier statutes that required no partner, no agreement, and no implementing bill.

## Turning Points and What Produced Them

A turning point is a statute or a lapse after which the system operated under different rules. The extensions of the 1950s form the first group. The Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 wrote the escape clause into statute, so that after a Tariff Commission investigation and a finding of serious injury from a concession, the President could modify or withdraw the concession. The same act added the peril point procedure, under which the Tariff Commission reported the lowest rate to which a duty could be cut without serious injury before negotiations began. The act also protected import fees and quotas under section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act and required denial of concessions to the Soviet Union and Soviet dominated states. The Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1955 added a national security clause, and the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1958 supplied a four year extension, the longest grant in the program to that date, and amended that clause. Congress extended the delegation created in 1934, and in the same statutes Congress built reporting, injury, and security procedures into the grant. Extension and qualification arrived together.

The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 forms the second turning point, and the statute discloses a change in the negotiating problem. The act authorized across the board tariff reductions aimed at the European Economic Community and the Kennedy Round, a broader authority than the product by product bargaining embodied in the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930. The same act created trade adjustment assistance, created the position of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations, and contained section 232 in a single public law, Public Law 87-794. The Trade Act of 1974 forms the third turning point, and its cause lay in procedure as much as in substance. The act created fast track procedures for implementing bills, addressing the problem of how Congress would act on agreements negotiated under delegated authority. The act also created section 301, section 201 safeguards in modern form, the Generalized System of Preferences, and the Jackson-Vanik emigration condition on credits and concessions for nonmarket economies. The act placed the Special Representative office in the Executive Office of the President in statute and raised the office to Cabinet level.

The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 tested the new procedure, because that act implemented the Tokyo Round agreements on subsidies, government procurement, standards, and other nontariff measures and was the first trade implementing bill passed under the fast track procedures of the 1974 act. The subject matter of agreements had widened beyond tariff rates, and the 1974 procedure carried an agreement of that wider kind through the House on July 11, 1979 by 395 to 7 and through the Senate on July 23, 1979 by 90 to 4. The 1979 act also directed a reorganization plan for the trade functions, and Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1979 together with Executive Order 12188 in January 1980 renamed the office as the Office of the United States Trade Representative and consolidated the negotiating functions in that office. Later turning points reversed direction rather than widening scope. Negotiating authority lapsed on April 16, 1994, and a renewal bill failed in the House on September 25, 1998. The Trade Act of 2002, signed on August 6, 2002 by President George W. Bush, renewed authority for agreements entered into before July 1, 2007 and reformed and expanded trade adjustment assistance. That authority lapsed on July 1, 2007, and the completed lapses before the date of this article run from 1994 to 2002 and from 2007 to 2015. Congress declined to renew a grant on schedule, and negotiation under that form of authority stopped until a later Congress enacted a new grant, as Congress did in 2015.

## Throughlines, Reversals, and the Machinery of Objection

One throughline runs beneath every phase, and a name for that throughline is the delegation ratchet. Congress moved away from setting individual rates in statute, product by product, as the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 had done after committee bargaining. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 authorized the President to negotiate agreements cutting tariffs by up to 50 percent from existing rates, applied on a most favored nation basis, and to put agreements into effect by proclamation without further legislation. Congress renewed that initial three year grant repeatedly into the 1960s, and later stages widened the field. The 1962 act authorized across the board reductions, and the agreements implemented in 1979 covered subsidies, government procurement, standards, and other nontariff measures. The Uruguay Round Agreements Act of 1994 created the World Trade Organization and marked the first occasion on which Congress explicitly approved a GATT text. Delegation did not return to its starting point after any single grant expired. The trade representative office began as a position in 1962, gained statutory placement and Cabinet rank in 1974, and gained consolidated form in 1980, and that office persisted across lapses and renewals.

Against that ratchet, Congress built objection machinery into its own grants. Early examples are the peril point procedure of 1951, the escape clause that allowed a concession to be modified or withdrawn after an injury finding, and the conditions that the 1951 act attached to farm program protections and to concessions for Soviet dominated states. Fast track procedures in the 1974 act set the terms on which implementing bills would move, so delegation to negotiate coexisted with a timetable and a vote that Congress controlled. The Jackson-Vanik provision conditioned credits and concessions for nonmarket economies on emigration, and the annual waiver review under that framework lasted for China until the legislation of 2000 ended that review. The 1988 act required semiannual Treasury reporting on the exchange rate policies of major trading partners and required negotiations with any country found to be manipulating its currency. Enacted grants also carried stated objectives and consultation requirements as terms on which authority was lent, and Congress retained disapproval devices and the simplest device of all, non-renewal. The lapse that began in 1994, the House defeat of a renewal bill in 1998, and the lapse that began in 2007 show non-renewal in practice. Objection rarely took the form of repeal. Objection took the form of conditions written in advance, reports demanded during operation, votes required at implementation, and silence at renewal.

Adjustment assistance forms the third throughline, and the record presents adjustment assistance as the recurring companion bargain that accompanied large grants of negotiating authority. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 created trade adjustment assistance in the same statute that authorized across the board reductions for the Kennedy Round. The Trade Act of 2002 reformed and expanded trade adjustment assistance in the same statute that renewed negotiating authority after an eight year lapse. A Congress that authorized negotiation on a large scale also legislated for domestic adjustment in the same text, and a Congress that returned to authorization in 2002 returned to adjustment in the same text as well. The recurrence at the two authorization moments of 1962 and 2002 nonetheless identifies a habit of legislation. Delegation outward and provision inward traveled together at the points where delegation expanded or resumed.

The reversals qualify each throughline without breaking any of them. Delegation widened, and then authority lapsed twice for long intervals. The first use of fast track procedure in 1979 produced very large majorities, while later implementing votes narrowed, with the North American Free Trade Agreement passing the House by 234 to 200 in 1993 and the Dominican Republic Central America Free Trade Agreement passing the House by 217 to 215. Multilateral negotiation produced a completed global round in 1994, and the Uruguay Round remained the last completed global round of multilateral trade negotiations at the date of this article, while bilateral and regional agreements multiplied under the authority renewed in 2002. Unilateral instruments operated in some stretches and not in others, with a safeguard action in 1983, an arrangement after a section 301 case in 1986, safeguard measures in 2002 withdrawn in 2003, and actions under two provisions in 2018. Reversal occurred inside the delegated system, on ground that the statutes of delegation had laid out.

## A Periodization of Centers of Gravity

The periodization that this article proposes divides United States trade legislation since 1950 into three phases, stated as an argument about centers of gravity rather than as a claim about exclusive periods. The first phase centers on multilateral negotiation, running from the extensions of the reciprocal program in the 1950s through the Kennedy Round authority of 1962 and the Tokyo Round implementation of 1979 to the Uruguay Round Agreements Act of 1994. The second phase centers on bilateral and regional agreements, beginning with early examples in the 1980s and reaching a sustained wave under the authority renewed in 2002, with implementing acts for Jordan, Chile, Singapore, Australia, Morocco, the Dominican Republic Central America agreement, Bahrain, Oman, Peru, South Korea, Colombia, and Panama. The third phase centers on unilateral action under authorities enacted decades earlier, with a record of use running from 1959 to 2018 and a concentration of such action at the later end of the period. No phase claims that other forms stopped, and the record defeats any such claim in specific counterexamples.

The first counterexample concerns overlap between the first and second phases at their point of greatest activity. The North American Free Trade Agreement was enacted on December 8, 1993 and entered into force on January 1, 1994, while the Uruguay Round was reaching completion and gained implementation through the Uruguay Round Agreements Act enacted on December 8, 1994. NAFTA was negotiated while the Uruguay Round ran, and a regional negotiation and a global round gained implementation within twelve months of each other. Neither sequence waited for the other to finish. The earlier bilateral record points in the same direction. The United States Israel agreement gained implementation in 1985 as the first United States bilateral free trade agreement, and the United States Canada agreement gained implementation in 1988 and entered into force on January 1, 1989, and both enactments preceded the completion of the Uruguay Round. The bilateral form developed inside the multilateral era and expanded after the last completed global round. That sequence is a change in weight rather than a change in kind.

The second counterexample concerns a unilateral grant inside the negotiation era. The Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act of 1983, approved on August 5, 1983, was the first regionally targeted United States trade preference program, and that act granted duty free treatment to designated Caribbean Basin countries, with import sensitive products including apparel, petroleum, and footwear excluded. The Caribbean preference program of 1983 was a unilateral grant inside the negotiation era, because a preference of that kind required no reciprocal negotiation with the beneficiaries and no implementing bill for an agreement. Congress legislated a one sided grant in 1983, in the period between the Tokyo Round and the Uruguay Round and in the same decade as the Israel and Canada agreements. The Generalized System of Preferences, created by the Trade Act of 1974, supplies an earlier instance of the same form inside the multilateral phase. A distinction nonetheless separates those grants from third phase action, because section 201, section 232, and section 301 operate through executive action under delegated authority, while a preference program operates through a statute Congress itself enacted. More than one route remained open in every decade.

The third counterexample concerns unilateral action while a multilateral round was completing. Super 301 identifications occurred in 1989 and 1990 under President George H. W. Bush, as the statute enacted in 1988 required, and section 301 cases had run through the late 1980s under President Reagan. The Uruguay Round was then in progress and reached completion with agreements implemented in 1994, so Super 301 unilateralism in 1989 and 1990 coincided with the final years of that global negotiation. The fourth counterexample reaches back to the start of the modern system. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 created both the multilateral negotiating authority aimed at the Kennedy Round and the section 232 unilateral tool in a single statute, and that same enactment created adjustment assistance and the trade representative position. The two instruments that later phases separate in emphasis began as companion provisions in one text. A periodization that survives its own counterexamples earns a limited claim. Trade legislation since 1950 moved its center from negotiation among many partners, toward negotiation with single partners and regions, and toward action under older grants that required no negotiation at all, and each earlier form persisted after its center passed.

## Congress Delegates and Then Objects

The closing assessment returns to a claim that the whole record supports and that a reader can state in one sentence. Congress delegates and then objects. Delegation is the first half of the sentence because the constitutional baseline leaves no other route to sustained executive action on tariffs. Article I, Section 8 assigns the tariff and foreign commerce powers to Congress, tariff authority is legislative by default, and executive tariff power exists only by delegation. Every major development followed from a decision by Congress to lend that authority in a defined form, whether the form was reciprocal negotiation under the act of 1934 as extended in the 1950s, across the board authority in 1962, fast track procedure and new unilateral instruments in 1974, renewed authority in 2002 after a lapse, or renewed authority in 2015 that ran to July 1, 2021. Objection is the second half of the sentence because Congress attached limits to the lending from the beginning. Peril points and an escape clause in 1951, a national security clause amended in 1958, objectives and consultation requirements as terms of later grants, reporting duties on currency practices in 1988, implementing votes that narrowed from the majorities of 1979 and the House vote of 422 to 0 on the Israel agreement in 1985 to the margin of 217 to 215 on the Dominican Republic Central America agreement, disapproval devices within grant statutes, and non-renewal in 1994 and again in 2007 compose a single pattern expressed through different instruments.

That pattern resolves an apparent contradiction. The same institution that widened delegation also complained about the uses of delegation, and the record shows no contradiction once the statutes are read as continuing negotiations between the branches rather than as settled transfers of power. A grant answered a problem of scale, because Congress could not bargain rate by rate with foreign governments through committee process. A condition answered a problem of control, because a grant without objectives, consultation, reporting, or a terminal date would have transferred the constitutional power in substance while preserving the form of legislation. A lapse answered a problem of consent, because renewal required an affirmative case that a majority in both chambers was prepared to accept, and in 1998 the House declined to accept that case. Adjustment assistance answered a problem of distribution, because the gains and losses from negotiated reductions did not fall evenly, and Congress paired the authorizations of 1962 and 2002 with provision for adjustment in the same statutes. Each element of the objection machinery had a function that the original grant lacked on its own, and the machinery accumulated because each new round of delegation exposed a new point at which control could be exercised without ending delegation itself.

The phases give that pattern a chronology. During the multilateral phase, objection operated mainly inside the grants, through limits, reports, conditions, and implementing votes on agreements with many parties. During the bilateral and regional phase, objection operated agreement by agreement, as each implementing act required a separate legislative decision and margins varied from one agreement to the next. During the phase centered on unilateral action, objection faced a harder setting, because action under section 201, section 232, or section 301 rested on grants enacted in 1962, 1974, and 1988 and required no new implementing vote at the moment of use. The instruments that remained available in that setting were instruments built for moments other than implementation, including oversight of the administering agencies, amendment or repeal of the underlying grants, and control over future renewals of negotiating authority. The record through October 15, 2020 shows those older grants in operation and shows negotiating authority from 2015 in force alongside those grants, and the coexistence of the two records is the point on which the article ends. Trade legislation since 1950 is a history of authority lent on terms, used in forms that the lending Congress did not always foresee, and contested through machinery that the lending Congress itself supplied. Congress delegates and then objects, and the statutes preserve both halves of that sentence in full.

A reader who works through the sequence with a notebook open will find that the statutes reward the keeping: each grant states its own limits, each condition records an objection that an earlier grant produced, and each lapse marks the point at which the objection prevailed for a time. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What trade legislation has Congress passed since 1950?

The sequence opens with the extension statutes of the nineteen fifties, which renewed the reciprocal trade program and added the escape clause, the peril point procedure, and the national security clause. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 authorized across-the-board reductions and created adjustment assistance and the section 232 security authority. The Trade Act of 1974 created fast track, section 301, modern safeguards, and the preference program. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 implemented the Tokyo Round. Congress then enacted the Caribbean preference program of 1983, the Israel agreement act of 1985, the Canada agreement act and the omnibus act of 1988, the NAFTA act of 1993, the Uruguay Round act of 1994, the China permanent normal trade relations act of 2000, the Trade Act of 2002, a wave of bilateral implementing acts, and the renewal of 2015.

### Q: Has trade legislation shifted power to the president?

Yes, by delegation rather than by transfer of ownership. The Constitution assigns the duty power and the foreign commerce power to Congress, and every presidential tariff action rests on a statute in which Congress lent the exercise of that power for a stated purpose, within stated limits, for a stated time. The loans grew broader across the sequence: proclamation authority in 1934, across-the-board negotiating authority in 1962, fast track procedure and retaliation authority in 1974, and repeated renewals thereafter. Congress kept the ownership of the power throughout, and expressed its control through the terms of each grant, through conditions and reporting duties, through the refusal to renew in the nineteen nineties, and through the two completed lapses of negotiating authority, from 1994 to 2002 and from 2007 to 2015.

### Q: What did the Uruguay Round trade legislation create?

The Uruguay Round Agreements Act of 1994, Public Law 103-465, enacted on December 8, 1994, implemented the agreements of the Uruguay Round of multilateral trade negotiations and created the World Trade Organization, which entered into force on January 1, 1995. The act also marked the first occasion on which Congress explicitly approved a text of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, approving the GATT of 1994 as part of a congressional-executive agreement rather than as a ratified treaty. The House passed the implementing bill on November 29, 1994, by 288 to 146, and the Senate followed on December 1, 1994, by 76 to 24. The Uruguay Round remains the last completed global round of multilateral trade negotiations.

### Q: Did trade legislation move from multilateral to bilateral deals?

In its center of gravity, yes, and with named exceptions that the periodization itself records. The early decades of the sequence were organized around multilateral rounds conducted inside the GATT framework, ending with the Uruguay Round. Bilateral and regional instruments nevertheless appeared inside that era: the Caribbean preference program of 1983 was a unilateral statutory grant, the Israel agreement of 1985 was the first bilateral free trade agreement, and NAFTA was negotiated while the Uruguay Round was still running. After the Trade Act of 2002 renewed negotiating authority, the enacted output was a wave of bilateral and regional agreements rather than a new global round. The phases therefore describe where the weight of practice lay in each period, not exclusive periods in which only one instrument was used.

### Q: What was the 1988 omnibus trade legislation?

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, was the decade's comprehensive trade statute. Its best known addition was Super 301: section 1302 of the act added section 310 to the Trade Act of 1974, requiring the trade representative to identify priority unfair practices and priority countries in 1989 and 1990 and to open section 301 investigations against them. The act also approved accession to the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System Convention and replaced the old tariff schedules with the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, in use from 1989. A third element required the Treasury Department to analyse and report semiannually on the exchange rate policies of major trading partners, and to open negotiations with any country found to be manipulating its currency.

### Q: Is GATT a treaty in trade legislation?

No. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of 1947 was signed at Geneva on October 30, 1947 and applied from January 1, 1948 under a Protocol of Provisional Application. The United States applied it as an executive agreement under the President's reciprocal trade agreements authority. It was never submitted to the Senate for advice and consent as a treaty, and Congress never approved the GATT of 1947 itself. The planned International Trade Organization was never approved, so the provisional arrangement governed for nearly half a century. Congress first explicitly approved a GATT text in the Uruguay Round Agreements Act of 1994, which approved the GATT of 1994 as part of a congressional-executive agreement, a form that likewise differs from a ratified treaty.

### Q: Which trade legislation created the trade representative?

No single statute did the whole work; the office was built in three statutory steps. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 created the position of Special Representative for Trade Negotiations, in response to the negotiating demands of the Kennedy Round. The Trade Act of 1974 placed the office in the Executive Office of the President in statute and raised the Special Representative to Cabinet level. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 then directed the President to submit a reorganization plan for the trade functions, and Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1979, carried into effect by Executive Order 12188 in January 1980, renamed the office the Office of the United States Trade Representative and consolidated the trade negotiating functions in it. Answers that assign the creation to one act compress a record that runs across three.

### Q: What is the timeline of US trade legislation?

The timeline begins with the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, which first delegated tariff negotiating authority to the President, and with the GATT applied as an executive agreement from 1948. The extension statutes of 1951, 1955, and 1958 added the escape clause, the farm program protection, and the national security clause. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and the Trade Act of 1974 supplied the across-the-board authority, fast track, and the remedy statutes. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 implemented the Tokyo Round. Preference and bilateral statutes followed in 1983, 1985, and 1988, with the omnibus act of 1988, then NAFTA in 1993, the Uruguay Round act in 1994, and China permanent normal trade relations in 2000. Negotiating authority lapsed from 1994 to 2002 and again from 2007 to 2015, was renewed by the Trade Act of 2002 and the act of 2015, and stood in force on October 15, 2020, running by its enacted terms to July 1, 2021.

### Q: When was the World Trade Organization created?

The World Trade Organization was created by the Uruguay Round Agreements Act of 1994, Public Law 103-465, which Congress enacted on December 8, 1994 to implement the agreements concluded in the Uruguay Round of multilateral negotiations. The organization entered into force on January 1, 1995, giving the postwar trading system the permanent institutional home that the provisional GATT arrangement had lacked since 1948, after the planned International Trade Organization of the Havana Charter was never approved. The act that created it was also the statute in which Congress first explicitly approved a GATT text, approving the GATT of 1994 within a congressional-executive agreement. The Uruguay Round that produced the organization remains the last completed global round of trade negotiations.

### Q: What did the Trade Act create?

The reference is to the Trade Act of 1974, Public Law 93-618, the central statute of the field. It created the fast track procedure under which Congress considers implementing bills for trade agreements under a fixed timetable, without amendment, in exchange for negotiating objectives, notification, and consultation duties imposed on the President. It created section 301, the authority directed at unfair foreign practices, with retaliation among its outcomes, and restated the escape clause in modern safeguard form as section 201. It created the Generalized System of Preferences for developing countries and carried the Jackson-Vanik emigration condition for nonmarket economies. It also placed the Special Representative's office in the Executive Office of the President in statute, at Cabinet level, building on the position first created in 1962.

### Q: What is the Harmonized Tariff Schedule and when was it adopted?

The Harmonized Tariff Schedule is the classification system under which imported goods are assigned the duty rates they pay, organized on the international Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System, which gives trading countries a shared vocabulary of product categories. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 approved United States accession to the convention behind that system and replaced the older tariff schedules with the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, which has been in use from 1989. The change mattered beyond bookkeeping: a shared classification made tariff commitments in agreements easier to specify and compare across countries, and it gave the customs administration and importers a single schedule in place of the accumulated categories of the pre-delegation and postwar schedules.

### Q: What did the omnibus trade act change about unfair practice cases?

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 strengthened the unfair practice machinery of the Trade Act of 1974 by adding a procedure known as Super 301. Section 1302 of the 1988 act added section 310 to the 1974 act, under which the trade representative was required to identify priority unfair practices and priority foreign countries in 1989 and 1990, and to open section 301 investigations against the practices identified. Ordinary section 301 procedure began with a petition or a decision to self-initiate; Super 301 added a statutory identification duty with named years, converting congressional dissatisfaction with the pace of enforcement into a calendar the executive was required to meet. The first identifications followed in 1989 and 1990 under President George H. W. Bush.

### Q: What is the escape clause in trade legislation?

The escape clause is the safeguard device by which a tariff concession granted in a trade agreement may be withdrawn or modified when increased imports cause or threaten serious injury to a domestic industry. Congress wrote it into statute as section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, building on escape clauses that had appeared in agreements by executive practice: after an investigation and a finding of serious injury by the Tariff Commission, the President could modify or withdraw the concession. The device expressed the objection half of the delegation bargain, since each renewal of negotiating authority carried a standing route by which its results could be revisited for injured industries. The Trade Act of 1974 restated the mechanism in modern safeguard form as section 201.

### Q: How long did trade negotiating authority lapse before the renewal act?

Eight years. The authority under which presidents negotiated trade agreements lapsed on April 16, 1994, and the renewal effort of the decade failed when the House defeated H.R. 2621 on September 25, 1998. The Trade Act of 2002, Public Law 107-210, signed on August 6, 2002, finally renewed the authority, in its Title XXI, for agreements entered into before July 1, 2007, and paired the renewal with a reform and expansion of trade adjustment assistance. During the lapse, agreements already enacted remained in force, and the remedy and security authorities of the earlier statutes, section 301, section 201, and section 232, continued to operate independently of the missing negotiating grant. A second lapse of equal length ran from 2007 to 2015.

### Q: What did the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act do?

The Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act of 1983, Public Law 98-67, approved on August 5, 1983, created the first regionally targeted trade preference program in United States law. It granted duty free treatment to designated countries of the Caribbean Basin for eligible products, extending preferences by statute and on conditions Congress set, rather than through a reciprocal bargain negotiated under the trade agreements program. Import-sensitive products were excluded from the grant, among them apparel, petroleum, and footwear. The act matters in the periodization of the field because it was a unilateral and regional instrument enacted in the middle of the multilateral era, a decade before the regional turn marked by NAFTA, and it began the sequence of preference and bilateral statutes that the nineteen eighties produced.

### Q: Which country signed the first bilateral free trade agreement with the United States?

Israel. The United States-Israel Free Trade Area Implementation Act of 1985, Public Law 99-47, enacted on June 11, 1985, implemented the first bilateral free trade agreement in United States practice. The agreement had been negotiated under the authority of the Trade and Tariff Act of 1984 and entered into force on September 1, 1985. The House passed the implementing bill on May 7, 1985, by 422 to 0, a margin that reflected the agreement's modest trade volumes as much as its precedent. The precedent proved durable: the Canada agreement act of 1988 followed the same implementing form, and the bilateral wave enacted after the Trade Act of 2002 extended the form to partners across several continents.

### Q: What is the difference between a trade agreement and a treaty?

A treaty, in the constitutional sense, is made by the President with the advice and consent of two thirds of the Senate, and takes domestic effect through that process. The trade agreements in this field followed a different route, the congressional-executive agreement: Congress delegated negotiating authority to the President in advance, in statutes such as those of 1934, 1962, 1974, and 2015, and the resulting agreements took domestic effect through implementing statutes passed by both Houses by ordinary majorities, or, in the case of the GATT of 1947, through application as an executive agreement under the delegated reciprocal trade authority, without Senate consent and without a congressional vote on the agreement itself. The distinction explains why trade agreements can be extensive in substance while never appearing in the treaty record.

### Q: What round of agreements did the Trade Agreements Act implement?

The Tokyo Round. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979, Public Law 96-39, enacted on July 26, 1979, implemented the agreements concluded in the Tokyo Round of multilateral trade negotiations, which had addressed the nontariff measures that tariff bargaining could not reach, through codes on subsidies, government procurement, standards, and related practices. It was the first trade implementing bill passed under the fast track procedures that the Trade Act of 1974 had created, and it passed by wide margins, the House voting 395 to 7 on July 11, 1979 and the Senate 90 to 4 on July 23, 1979. The same act directed the reorganization that renamed the trade representative's office in 1980.

### Q: How can Congress object to a trade agreement negotiated under fast track?

Congress built its objections into the delegation itself. A grant of negotiating authority states the objectives an agreement must serve, and imposes notification and consultation duties that place the committees of jurisdiction inside the negotiation while it runs. The statute also carries procedural exits: either House may act to withdraw expedited treatment from a particular agreement where the President has failed to meet the statute's conditions, and an extension of the authority may be denied by disapproval. The final objection remains the vote itself, since an implementing bill must still pass both Houses and be presented to the President. Behind those devices stands the strongest one: Congress may simply allow the authority to lapse, as it did from 1994 to 2002.

### Q: What should a student remember about the pattern of US trade legislation?

Three things carry most of the field. First, the constitutional ownership: the power to lay duties and regulate foreign commerce belongs to Congress, and every presidential trade action rests on a statutory delegation, so the statutes are the primary record. Second, the organising pattern: Congress delegates and then objects, writing conditions, safeguards, and renewal dates into its grants, and twice letting negotiating authority lapse for eight years at a time, from 1994 to 2002 and from 2007 to 2015. Third, the periodization: multilateral negotiation, bilateral and regional agreements, and unilateral action under older authorities are centers of gravity rather than exclusive periods, since NAFTA was negotiated during the Uruguay Round and the unilateral instruments were enacted inside the negotiation statutes themselves. The seventy-year table in this guide fixes the sequence.
