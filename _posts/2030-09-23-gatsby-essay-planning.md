Ten minutes that save the essay. That is the claim this article defends, and it names a skill rather than a slogan. A fast plan unpacks the prompt, forms a thesis, and sketches points with evidence before writing begins, and that small sequence changes what the finished essay can do. The planned essay argues a coherent case from its first paragraph, while the unplanned one discovers its case halfway down the page and spends the second half apologizing for the first. Under exam pressure the temptation runs the other way. Ten minutes feel like ten minutes stolen from the writing, and many students decide that starting sooner is the safer choice. It is the costlier one. Writing without a plan does not eliminate the thinking a plan would have done; it postpones it, and postponed thinking arrives at the worst moment, in the middle of a paragraph, with the clock already running. This article teaches the sequence as a learnable method: five two-minute moves that carry a writer from a blank page to a working plan, each move producing something written down, each written product feeding the next. The method belongs to the practical side of this series. The essay hub teaches the whole process of writing about the novel, the thesis article teaches how to build an arguable claim, and the structure article teaches how to arrange points into a shape. This article owns the rapid-planning skill that sits between those components: how to assemble them at speed, under pressure, before the writing starts. The distinction matters because the hub tells a writer what the pieces are and this article tells a writer how to fit them together in ten minutes, which is a different competence. Speed without sequence is just hurry. Sequence is what turns ten minutes from a delay into an investment, and the investment pays out in every paragraph that follows.

The argument against planning sounds persuasive until it meets an actual timed essay. Students who skip the plan say they are thinking while they write, which is true in the sense that everyone thinks while they write. The difference is what the thinking has to accomplish. A writer who planned spends drafting time extending thoughts already formed: turning a sketched point into a paragraph, attaching analysis to evidence already chosen. A writer who did not plan spends drafting time doing two jobs at once, discovering what to say and how to say it in the same breath. That double load produces the recognizable symptoms of the unplanned Gatsby essay: an introduction that restates the prompt in general terms, a second paragraph that wanders into plot summary because the plot is the safest thing to say, a third paragraph that suddenly remembers the question, and a conclusion that introduces a fresh claim because the writer finally knows what the essay wanted to argue. None of these are intelligence failures. They are planning failures wearing the mask of writing problems, and they cost far more than ten minutes to repair once the ink is dry. The evidence for this is not abstract. Markers of timed essays report that essays with a visible plan score more consistently, and every teacher who has timed an in-class essay has watched unplanned essays drift into summary around the halfway mark, the point where initial energy runs out and only structure can carry the argument forward. Structure does not come from nowhere. It comes from the plan, or it does not come at all.

The complication deserves its most generous hearing. Planning can go wrong. Some students plan for twenty minutes and then write in a panic. Some build elaborate plans that turn into first drafts, copying the plan into prose at such length that no time remains. Some treat the plan as a cage, refusing to follow a better idea that arrives mid-essay because it was not on the sheet. These are real failures of planning, and this article does not ask anyone to plan badly for longer. It asks for a specific kind of plan: fast, skeletal, and disposable in the right way. Ten minutes, not thirty. A sheet of rough notes, not a shadow essay. A sequence that produces a thesis and sketched points with evidence, and then releases the writer into the drafting with a map that can be redrawn. The distinction between planning and over-planning is not a matter of minutes but of product. A good ten-minute plan is done when it answers three questions on the page: what the prompt is asking, what the thesis claims, and what evidence each point will use. If those three answers exist, the plan is complete, and continuing to plan past that point is the bad habit critics of planning describe. If those three answers do not exist, the writer has not planned at all, however many minutes have passed.

What follows is the sequence taught as a method, with a worked minute-by-minute example, a before-and-after comparison of planned and unplanned writing, and guidance for prompts that resist the standard moves. The method assumes a timed exam essay on The Great Gatsby, forty-five minutes to an hour of writing time, and a writer who knows the novel. The numbers can flex: with a thirty-minute essay the plan shrinks to five or six minutes, and with a two-hour paper the plan can stretch to fifteen without changing its character. What does not flex is the order of operations, because the order is what makes the sequence fast. Unpacking the prompt first means the thesis answers something real. Forming the thesis second means the points serve the thesis rather than the novel's plot order. Sketching points with evidence third means no paragraph arrives without ammunition. Writers who improvise these operations in a different order pay for it: a thesis formed before the prompt is understood answers a question nobody asked, and evidence gathered before the points are chosen becomes a collection of favorite quotes in search of paragraphs. Order is the whole economy of the ten minutes, and the table below lays it out as a concrete schedule rather than an aspiration. Before the table, one more distinction that governs everything in this article. Planning is often taught as a virtue, something good students do because good students are virtuous. This article teaches it as a technology, a sequence of moves that produces a defined product on a defined schedule. The difference matters because virtues are vague and technologies are testable. A student who treats planning as a virtue will plan in whatever way feels virtuous, at whatever length feels sufficient, and will be surprised when the results vary. A student who treats planning as a technology will run the five moves, check the three products, and know within ten minutes whether the plan is complete. The technology framing also explains why this article can promise speed without promising magic. The sequence does not make a weak reader strong. It makes a prepared reader fast, by giving preparation a form that fits inside an exam. The form is what follows, and it is deliberately mechanical: five moves, two minutes each, three products, one page. Mechanics are what survive pressure. Inspiration does not.

### Why does ten minutes of planning repay itself?

Planning repays itself because a timed essay rewards argued answers, not recalled summaries. Ten minutes spent unpacking the prompt, forming a thesis, and sketching points with evidence gives every paragraph a job before the first sentence exists. The unplanned essay spends that same time later, rewriting itself mid-flow, which costs more.

The return on the ten minutes shows up in four places, and each is visible to the marker. The first is the introduction. Planned introductions state a thesis because the thesis already exists on the plan; unplanned introductions restate the prompt because the writer has nothing else to say yet. A marker forms an early judgment from the introduction, and planned essays win that judgment before the argument has even begun. The second return is paragraph purpose. In a planned essay every paragraph maps to a point on the sheet, so no paragraph drifts. In an unplanned essay paragraphs map to whatever occurred to the writer in the order it occurred, so the middle of the essay is a sequence of accidents. The third return is evidence. Planned writers arrive at each paragraph with quotations or scenes already selected, which means they can spend their drafting time analyzing rather than remembering. Unplanned writers spend drafting time hunting through memory for something to quote, and hunting time is writing time lost. The fourth return is the conclusion. Planned essays end by returning to a thesis the writer has held all along, so the ending sounds like an arrival. Unplanned essays end by finally discovering their thesis, so the ending sounds like a beginning that ran out of paper. None of this requires genius. It requires ten minutes of structure, and the structure is repeatable across any prompt the exam sets.

There is also a psychological return that matters under pressure. Exam anxiety feeds on shapelessness. A blank page plus a ticking clock is the classic anxiety machine, and starting to write immediately can feel like fighting the anxiety by acting. But the relief is temporary, because the writer knows, paragraph by paragraph, that no plan backs the prose. A written plan changes the emotional register of the task. Once the sheet holds a thesis and three sketched points, the essay becomes a set of smaller, named jobs: write point one, write point two, write point three. Anxiety finds less purchase on named jobs than on the shapeless demand to write a good essay. This is not motivational decoration around the method. It is part of why the method works for students who fear running out of time, because fear of the plan costs them twice: once in the minutes they withhold from planning, and again in the confidence they withhold from writing. The fastest way to write more confidently is to know, before the first sentence, what the essay will say.

| Minute | Move | What lands on the page |
|---|---|---|
| 0-2 | Unpack the prompt | The question's key terms underlined, the task word identified, and one sentence in the writer's own words stating what is actually being asked |
| 2-4 | Form the working thesis | One arguable sentence that answers the prompt, plus the because-clause that gives it a reason to exist |
| 4-6 | Sketch the points | Three point headings in logical order, each phrased as a claim rather than a topic label |
| 6-8 | Attach the evidence | One to two remembered quotations or scenes per point, noted in shorthand, with the technique each one invites |
| 8-10 | Order and check | A quick test of the sequence, one phrase each for the introduction's opening and the conclusion's return, and a final glance that every point serves the thesis |

The table's two-minute blocks are calibrated for a forty-five-minute essay, and they scale cleanly. For a thirty-minute essay, halve each block: one minute per move, with the same products in the same order, because the sequence is the method and the minutes are only its container. For a two-hour paper, the plan can stretch to fifteen minutes without changing character, spending the extra time on the evidence stage, where longer papers demand deeper quotation banks. What never changes, whatever the total, is the order of the moves and the definition of done. A plan is complete when the prompt is restated, the thesis is arguable, and each point carries evidence with an analytical move. Students who internalize that definition stop watching the clock during planning, because they know exactly what finished looks like, and they stop planning when they see it.

### What does the ten-minute sequence do to a Gatsby prompt?

It converts a general question into a specific answer by forcing each move to produce a product. The unpacking stage turns the prompt's vocabulary into demands. The thesis stage turns those demands into a claim. The point-sketching stage turns the claim into a paragraph order. Each stage narrows the task, so the writer starts specific instead of discovering it late.

Consider how this works on a familiar prompt: Discuss the presentation of the American Dream in The Great Gatsby. The unplanned writer reads this and reaches for everything known about the dream: Gatsby's parties, the green light, the Valley of Ashes, Daisy, the ending. The material is rich, which is the problem. Without a plan, the essay becomes a tour of every remembered detail in the order the details arrive, and the prompt's key word, presentation, gets lost under the weight of content. The planned writer spends the first two minutes asking what the prompt is actually demanding, and the demands are precise once isolated. Presentation asks how the novel shows the dream, not what the dream is. Discuss asks for evaluation, not a list. The writer's own-words sentence might read: this asks how Fitzgerald presents the dream across the novel and what that presentation implies about it. That sentence is worth its two minutes because everything after it serves it.

The sequence then disciplines the material. A thesis formed at minute three might run: Fitzgerald presents the American Dream as a beautiful illusion that the novel exposes as corrupt, with Gatsby embodying its hope and the Buchanans embodying its price. This is a working thesis, not a final one, and its roughness is a feature. It names a position, arguable because someone could answer that the novel preserves some hope in the dream, and it gives the because-clause its weight: hope on one side, price on the other. The unplanned writer never states anything this explicit before writing, which is why unplanned essays drift into balanced waffling, giving both sides without a position. The plan forces a position early, when it is cheap to adjust, rather than late, when it is expensive.

The point sketch then converts the thesis into paragraph jobs. Point one: the dream's initial presentation as romance and possibility, evidence from the green light and Gatsby's early appearances. Point two: the dream's corruption through wealth, evidence from the parties and the careless rich. Point three: the dream's collapse and Nick's final accounting, evidence from the Valley of Ashes and the closing meditation. Each point is a claim about the thesis, not a chapter of the novel, and each carries its own evidence. The unplanned writer, by contrast, would write paragraphs about the green light, the parties, and the ending as topics, summarizing each and hoping an argument emerges. The plan's three points are already arguments, so the drafting task is extension rather than discovery. This is what the sequence does to a prompt: it turns a question about a theme into a thesis with three jobs, before the clock has taken ten minutes.

### How do you form a working thesis inside a fast plan?

A working thesis forms in two minutes by answering the prompt in one arguable sentence plus a because-clause. Write the answer first, then force it to give its reason. The because-clause converts a restated opinion into a claim with a mechanism, which is exactly what the essay's paragraphs will then prove.

The technique needs a concrete demonstration, because thesis advice usually stays abstract. Take the prompt: How does Fitzgerald present social class in The Great Gatsby? The unplanned response to this prompt is often a sentence like: Fitzgerald presents social class through the different groups in the novel, showing the rich, the poor, and those in between. This is not a thesis. It is a table of contents. It cannot be argued against, because nobody would claim the novel does not contain different groups, and an essay built on it can only summarize the groups in turn. The planned writer's two-minute thesis drill attacks this exact weakness. First minute: answer the prompt in one sentence that takes a position. Something like: Fitzgerald presents class as an unbridgeable divide that money cannot cross. Second minute: attach the because-clause. Because Gatsby's wealth buys him parties but never entry, while the old-money Buchanans waste their advantage without consequence. The sentence is arguable, someone could counter that Gatsby nearly does cross over, and it names a mechanism, wealth without entry, that the points will demonstrate. The full thesis lives on the plan in rough form, and roughness is permitted. The plan is not marked. The essay is.

The drill works because it exploits a truth about timed writing: precision is easier in short sentences. Students who try to form a thesis while drafting produce long, tangled sentences that hide the absence of a claim inside subordinate clauses. The plan's thesis is one sentence, and one sentence cannot hide. If the sentence is just a restated prompt, the writer sees it immediately and can sharpen it. If the because-clause is missing, the writer feels the gap and adds one. This visibility is the point. The plan makes the thesis inspectable before the essay commits to it, and two minutes of inspection is worth more than two paragraphs of repair. Students sometimes worry that a working thesis locks them in, but the plan's thesis is a hypothesis, not a contract. The final two minutes of the sequence include a check precisely so the writer can adjust. What the thesis must not do is remain unformed. An unformed thesis is not freedom; it is a debt, and drafting charges interest.

There is a subtle trap in thesis formation that the plan helps avoid: answering the wrong question beautifully. Some prompts ask about presentation and students answer about importance. Some ask about a single character and students answer about the theme the character represents. The unpacking stage of the sequence guards against this, and the thesis stage tests the guard. A writer who reads the plan's thesis against the plan's own-words prompt sentence can see a mismatch in seconds. The unplanned writer discovers the mismatch, if ever, after the essay is done. The plan's thesis sentence is also where the writer's own reading of the novel enters. A thesis that says something the writer actually believes, however roughly, produces livelier analysis than a thesis assembled from remembered criticism. The plan gives the writer two minutes to find that belief and write it down. The hub article on [how to write a Gatsby essay](/2030/04/01/how-to-write-gatsby-essay/) covers the whole process this thesis serves, but the ten-minute method is where the thesis gets made at speed, which is its own skill.

### How do you note evidence for each point on a one-page plan?

Evidence gets noted in shorthand beside each point, with one to two remembered quotations or scenes per point and the technique each invites. The note is a reminder, not a transcription: a phrase plus the analytical move it supports, so drafting time goes to analysis instead of recall.

The evidence stage is where plans most often fail, and the failure takes a predictable form. A writer sketches three good points and then, under the evidence heading, writes something like: green light quote, Valley of Ashes, Daisy. This is a memory jog, not an evidence plan, and it leaves all the real work for the drafting. When the writer reaches point one, the green light must be recalled, selected, and analyzed from scratch, which is the same labor the plan was meant to do early. A proper evidence note answers three sub-questions in a few words each. Which precise moment? What exact words? What technique does it invite? So the note beside point one on the American Dream plan might read: green light, end of chapter one, "Gatsby believed in the green light", symbolism of distance and desire. The quotation is real and short. The technique is named. The drafting task is now defined: explain how the symbolism of distance and desire serves the point about the dream's presentation as romance. The analysis is still the writer's job, but the materials are assembled.

Closed-book conditions make this stage harder and more valuable. With no text to consult, the evidence note depends on remembered quotations, and remembered quotations are the fragile part of any exam essay. The plan disciplines them. Rather than trusting that the right quote will surface during drafting, the writer commits to specific words on the plan, and the commitment reveals gaps early. If the writer cannot produce a quotation for point two at minute seven, that is information: point two may need a different point, or the evidence may need to shift from quotation to scene. A scene note works the same way: Myrtle's death in the Valley of Ashes, hit-and-run, plot as moral evidence. The technique invited might be symbolism or narrative structure rather than language analysis, but the note still specifies the move. Adjusting at minute seven costs seconds. Discovering the gap at minute thirty costs a paragraph. This is the ten minutes repaying themselves in their most concrete form.

The one-page constraint matters because it forces selection. A plan that lists every quotation the writer knows about a theme is not a plan; it is a panic inventory. The discipline of one to two items per point forces the writer to choose the most apt evidence, and aptness is what markers reward. A single well-chosen quotation, analyzed in depth, outperforms three quoted lines that are summarized and abandoned. The plan's evidence notes should also flag the analytical angle in a word or two, because a quotation without a technique note tends to get quoted and dropped. Note the word choice, the image, the repetition, or the narrative voice beside the quotation, and the drafting paragraph has its analytical spine before it begins. The thesis article's guidance on [building a Gatsby thesis statement](/2030/04/08/gatsby-thesis-statement/) explains why claims need support; the ten-minute plan is where the support gets selected, in shorthand, before the writing starts.

### What does a worked minute-by-minute plan look like?

A worked plan shows each two-minute move producing written content on one prompt. Minutes zero to two unpack the question into the writer's own words. Minutes two to four draft an arguable thesis with its because-clause. Minutes four to six sketch three claim-shaped points. Minutes six to eight attach evidence in shorthand. Minutes eight to ten order and test them.

Take the prompt: "Nick claims he is one of the few honest people he has known. How far do you agree that Nick is a reliable narrator?" Minutes zero to two: the writer underlines the key terms, narrator reliability, honest, how far, and identifies the task word as evaluation. The own-words sentence reads: this asks whether Nick's claim of honesty holds up, and how far his narration can be trusted. That sentence is on the page by minute two, and it already rules out the most common wrong turn, which is writing an essay about whether Nick is a good person instead of whether his narration is reliable. The unplanned writer makes that wrong turn in the first paragraph and spends the essay recovering. The planned writer spends two minutes ruling it out.

Minutes two to four produce the working thesis: Nick presents himself as honest, but his selective sympathy, especially toward Gatsby, makes his narration partial rather than fully reliable. The because-clause: because he admits judgments while claiming reserve, and his account filters events through admiration. This is arguable, defensible, and rich enough to generate points. Note what it does not do: it does not try to be balanced. A thesis that says Nick is reliable in some ways and unreliable in others is true and useless, because no point can prove it. The plan's thesis takes a position, partial rather than fully reliable, that the points will qualify as they go. Qualification belongs in the paragraphs. Position belongs in the thesis. Writers who confuse the two produce essays that never land anywhere.

Minutes four to six sketch the points as claims. Point one: Nick's opening claim of honesty establishes a contract with the reader that the novel then tests. Point two: his obvious admiration for Gatsby colors his presentation of events, from the parties to the aftermath of Myrtle's death. Point three: his harshness toward Tom and Daisy reveals a moral filter that undermines his pose of reserve. Each point is a claim about reliability, not a plot episode, and each one tests the thesis from a different angle. The plan's three points do not march through the novel in chapter order. They orbit the thesis, which is what keeps the essay argumentative rather than narrative. A point shaped like "Nick at Gatsby's parties" is a topic. A point shaped like "Nick's admiration colors his account of the parties" is an argument, and the plan forces the second shape.

Minutes six to eight attach evidence. Beside point one: the opening pages, "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known", direct self-characterization, a claim the novel invites the reader to test. Beside point two: the closing tribute, "Gatsby turned out all right at the end", judgment presented as fact, plus Nick's orchestration of Gatsby's funeral against the world's indifference. Beside point three: "They were careless people, Tom and Daisy", the famous verdict, moral language that exposes the narrator's own commitments. Each note pairs the quotation with the move it invites, so drafting becomes analysis rather than recall. The writer also notes what to avoid: the temptation to quote at length, the need to keep each quotation brief enough to analyze.

Minutes eight to ten order and check. The order is already logical, one to three, but the check asks harder questions. Does each point serve the thesis? Point one tests the honesty claim, point two tests the admiration filter, point three tests the moral filter: yes, all three. Is there a counter-argument to acknowledge? Yes, and it becomes the qualification inside point two or three: Nick's partiality is open, which is itself a kind of honesty. One phrase for the introduction: start with the paradox of the honest narrator. One phrase for the conclusion: return to the verdict that Nick is honestly partial. The plan is complete at minute ten, one page, and the essay that follows has a thesis, three argued points, evidence with analytical moves, and a known ending. The unplanned writer at minute ten has a first paragraph of generalities and a growing sense of trouble. That is the difference the sequence buys.

### How do you handle a prompt that resists the standard plan?

Resistant prompts get the same sequence with adjusted questions at each stage. For unseen or open-ended prompts, spend the unpacking minutes on the task words and the scope. For comparative or passage-based prompts, shape the points around the comparison or the extract. The sequence flexes; the order does not.

Some prompts refuse the three-point thematic shape. An unseen passage prompt, for example, asks for analysis of a specific extract rather than an argument about the whole novel, and a plan built around three thematic claims will misfire. The sequence still applies, but the questions change. The unpacking stage asks what the passage does rather than what the novel means. The thesis stage produces a reading of the passage: this extract presents Gatsby's hope through imagery of distance that the surrounding narration quietly undercuts. The point sketch organizes around the passage's own movements rather than themes: the imagery, the narrative voice, the structural placement. Evidence is the passage itself, which removes the recall problem but adds a selection problem, and the plan's shorthand discipline matters even more when the material is abundant. A comparative prompt needs a different adjustment: the points must integrate both texts under a shared frame rather than treating them in turn, and the plan's point headings enforce that integration from the start.

The hardest resistant prompt is the open-ended one, the kind that invites the writer to discuss a broad statement about the novel with no specified angle. These prompts feel like freedom and function as traps, because without a specified angle the unplanned writer tries to say everything. The plan's unpacking stage is the defense. Two minutes spent asking what the statement is really claiming, which words are doing the argumentative work, and what would count as disagreeing with it will usually surface a specific thesis. A prompt like "The Great Gatsby is a novel about the corruption of the American Dream" contains its own argumentative pressure in the word corruption: the writer can agree, qualify, or reframe, but the plan forces one of those positions onto the page. The structure article's guidance on [Gatsby essay structure](/2030/04/15/gatsby-essay-structure/) shows how points arrange themselves once a position exists; the rapid plan is where the position gets chosen, even for prompts that seem to offer none.

There is one more resistance worth naming: the prompt the writer has effectively pre-answered. Students who have rehearsed a favorite essay sometimes meet a new prompt and bend it toward the rehearsed material. The plan exposes this. When the own-words sentence at minute two describes the actual question, and the thesis at minute four answers a different one, the mismatch is visible on the page. The unplanned writer never writes the own-words sentence and therefore never sees the mismatch, which is why pre-prepared essays score poorly on questions they only half-answer. The plan's first move is a loyalty test between the writer and the prompt. Pass it, and the essay answers what was asked. Fail it, and no amount of beautiful writing about the green light will recover the marks. The plan is not only a time method. It is a honesty method, and the honesty starts with reading the question.

The before-and-after comparison makes the method's value concrete. Take two openings written for the same American Dream prompt. The unplanned opening: "The American Dream is an important theme in The Great Gatsby. F. Scott Fitzgerald presents many different aspects of the dream through his characters. Gatsby himself believes in the dream, while other characters show its darker side. This essay will discuss how Fitzgerald presents the American Dream." Every sentence is true and none of it argues. The marker learns nothing except that the writer has read the novel. The planned opening, written from the thesis on the sheet: "Fitzgerald presents the American Dream as a beautiful illusion and then dismantles it, giving Gatsby its hope and the Buchanans its price. The novel's famous images of longing, the green light across the water, belong to the dream's promise; its images of waste, the Valley of Ashes between the Eggs and the city, belong to the dream's cost. The presentation works by keeping both images in play until the cost overwhelms the promise." The second opening knows what it thinks. The difference is not talent. It is ten minutes, and the plan underneath it.

The comparison extends to the middle of the essay, where unplanned writing most visibly collapses. The unplanned second paragraph typically begins: "At the start of the novel, Gatsby is introduced as a mysterious figure who throws lavish parties." This is plot summary wearing an essay's clothes, and it happens because the writer, having committed to nothing, reaches for the safest available material. The planned second paragraph begins: "The dream first appears as pure romance, and the green light is its perfect image: distant, green, and just out of reach." This is a point from the plan, with evidence attached, and it analyzes rather than narrates. By the fourth paragraph the gap widens further. The unplanned writer, having summarized the parties and the affair, arrives at the ending with no argument to conclude, and writes a conclusion that finally states a thesis nobody has proven. The planned writer arrives at the ending with the thesis already proven three times over, and writes a conclusion that sounds like an arrival. The two essays took the same total time. The planned one spent ten minutes of it before writing, and every paragraph after that shows the return.

Budgeting the plan under pressure is a skill of its own, and it deserves explicit rules. First, decide the plan length before the exam starts, not during it. For a forty-five-minute essay, ten minutes. For an hour, ten to twelve. For thirty minutes, five or six, with the same five moves compressed: one minute per move, shorthand even terser. Second, use a visible timer or the exam clock, and honor it. The plan's discipline cuts both ways: it must end as well as begin. Students who plan beautifully for twenty minutes have not used this method; they have used a different, worse one. Third, write the plan where it cannot be confused with the essay. A boxed corner of the answer booklet, a separate planning page if the exam provides one, or a clearly ruled-off section. Markers sometimes read plans, and a clear plan can earn credit for structure even when the essay runs short, but the main reason is psychological: a separated plan stays a plan and does not become a draft. Fourth, practice the sequence under timed conditions before the exam, not for the first time in it. The method feels slow the first three times and fast thereafter, which is why rehearsal belongs in revision. The article on [revising Gatsby for exams](/2030/07/29/revise-gatsby-for-exams/) treats preparation as a whole; timed planning practice is the specific rehearsal this skill requires.

There are failure modes to watch for, and naming them is part of the method. Over-planning is the first: continuing past the point where the thesis, points, and evidence exist. The symptom is a plan that reads like a draft, full sentences where shorthand would do. The cure is the completeness test: thesis, points, evidence. When all three are on the page, stop. The second failure is the beautiful plan that never gets used: the writer plans carefully, then writes the essay from memory of the novel rather than from the sheet, and the two diverge. The cure is physical: keep the plan visible, glance at it between paragraphs, check each paragraph against its point heading before moving on. The third failure is the rigid plan: the writer discovers a better point mid-essay and refuses it because it was not planned. The plan is a map, not a cage, and a better idea discovered at minute twenty-five is worth more than a planned idea that has gone stale. The cure is the amendment rule: new points must still serve the thesis, and if they do, they belong. If they do not, the thesis may need the adjustment instead. Either way, the plan gets updated, not abandoned. A plan that cannot change is not a plan. It is a script, and scripts break under pressure.

### What makes a thesis arguable under time pressure?

A thesis is arguable when a reasonable reader could disagree with it and the disagreement would matter. Under time pressure, arguability comes from a verb of judgment plus a mechanism: not just what the novel does, but how and to what effect. The because-clause forces both onto the page in one sentence.

The disagreement test is the fastest diagnostic a plan allows. Read the thesis sentence and ask whether anyone could sensibly answer no. If the answer is no, nobody could disagree, the sentence is not a thesis; it is a fact wearing a thesis costume. "Fitzgerald uses symbolism in The Great Gatsby" fails the test, because no reader would dispute it, and an essay built on it can only list symbols. "Fitzgerald's symbols work against the characters who believe in them" passes, because a reader could counter that the symbols sustain the characters' hope, and the disagreement would be worth having. The plan's two minutes for the thesis are, at bottom, two minutes spent making sure the sentence passes this test before the essay commits forty minutes to it. Students often believe arguability requires originality, a reading nobody has ever proposed. It does not. It requires a position, and positions can be entirely conventional. "Gatsby is more admirable than the Buchanans" is not original, but it is arguable, and an essay can prove it well. Originality is a bonus. Position is the requirement.

Verbs of judgment are the machinery. A thesis built on "presents," "shows," or "explores" describes; a thesis built on "exposes," "undermines," "elevates," or "condemns" argues. The difference is visible in a single revision. Weak: "Fitzgerald presents Daisy as a complex character." This is true and inert. Strong: "Fitzgerald presents Daisy as the novel's moral test: every male character reveals himself in how he treats her, and each one fails." The strong version has a verb of judgment hiding inside its structure, reveals, fails, and it names a mechanism, treatment of Daisy as revelation, that the points can demonstrate. Another pair, on the ending. Weak: "The ending of the novel is significant." Strong: "The ending withdraws the hope the novel has built, so that Nick's final meditation reads less as consolation than as an epitaph." The weak version could introduce any essay about anything. The strong version commits to a reading that the points must then earn. Under time pressure, reaching for a judgment verb is the quickest upgrade available, and the plan's thesis line is where the upgrade happens.

The because-clause does the second half of the work. A thesis without a reason is a verdict without evidence, and while the essay will supply the evidence, the plan needs the reason now, because the reason is what generates the points. "Fitzgerald exposes the American Dream as corrupt" is arguable but barren; it gives the points nothing to prove beyond repetition. "Fitzgerald exposes the American Dream as corrupt because its promise depends on excluding the very people it claims to welcome" gives the points their shape: one on the promise, one on the exclusion, one on the resulting collapse. The because-clause is a point-generator, and writers who skip it find themselves at minute six with a thesis and no idea what the three points should be. Writers who write it find the points half-formed already. This is why the sequence puts the thesis before the points rather than alongside them. The thesis is not just the essay's answer; it is the plan's engine.

There is a sophisticated failure mode worth naming: the balanced thesis that refuses to take a position. "Fitzgerald both celebrates and criticizes the American Dream" is true, and it is useless as a plan's engine, because no point can prove a both. The essay that follows will give one paragraph to celebration, one to criticism, and no paragraph to an argument, and the conclusion will restate the balance as if balance were insight. The plan cures this by demanding a verb of judgment, which balance cannot supply. If the writer genuinely sees both sides, the plan's answer is not to deny the complexity but to rank it: "Fitzgerald's criticism of the dream outweighs his attraction to it, and the novel's structure performs that outweighing." Now the complexity lives inside the points, where it belongs, and the thesis has a position the essay can prove. Position first, qualification in the paragraphs: that is the division of labor the plan enforces, and it is the difference between an essay that lands somewhere and one that hovers.

A final note on revising the thesis within the plan. The working thesis is allowed to change during the final two-minute check, and writers should expect it to change. The points often reveal a stronger formulation than the one drafted at minute three. Perhaps the evidence notes show that two points both concern Nick's narration, suggesting the thesis should foreground narration rather than theme. Revising the thesis at minute nine costs seconds and strengthens the whole essay. What the writer must not do is revise the thesis during drafting without updating the plan, because then the sheet and the essay diverge and the plan's guidance goes dead. The rule is simple: the thesis can move, but it moves on the page, in the plan, where the writer can see what the move costs. A thesis that moves deliberately is a hypothesis being refined. A thesis that drifts silently is an essay losing its way.

### How do you pick evidence that earns its place?

Evidence earns its place when it is precise, brief, and analytically productive: exact words you can quote, short enough to unpack, and rich enough to support a technique observation. Pick the quotation that proves the point's claim, not the famous line you happen to remember. One apt quotation analyzed deeply beats three quoted lines summarized thinly.

The famous-quote trap is the most common evidence failure in Gatsby essays. The novel is unusually quotable, and students arrive at exams carrying a cargo of celebrated lines: the green light, the careless people, the boats against the current. These lines are famous because they reward analysis, which makes them tempting, but fame is not fit. A quotation earns its place by proving the point it sits beside, and the most famous line in the novel is worthless if it proves a different point. The plan's evidence stage is where this discipline gets enforced, because the writer must attach each quotation to a specific claim and name the analytical move it invites. A note that reads "careless people verdict, proves Buchanans escape consequences, moral language as judgment" is doing the work. A note that reads "careless people quote" is a souvenir. The difference becomes visible in drafting: the first note produces a paragraph of analysis, the second produces a quoted line followed by summary. Markers can tell which plan the writer used, because they can see which essay analyzes its quotations and which merely displays them.

Precision has a technical meaning on the plan. A precise evidence note names the exact words, the location in the novel, and the technique the words invite. Consider three notes for the same point about Gatsby's self-invention. Imprecise: "Gatsby's past." Better: "Gatsby's invented past." Precise: "'Jay Gatsby sprang from his Platonic conception of himself,' chapter six, elevated diction as self-mythology." The precise note does three jobs: it commits the writer to real words, it places the evidence so the paragraph can orient the reader, and it names the analytical angle, elevated diction, so the paragraph analyzes rather than narrates. Precision on the plan is what makes drafting fast, because the decisions that slow writers down, which words, where, saying what about them, are already made. The shorthand can be terse; it cannot be vague. Vagueness on the plan is just procrastination with a pen.

Brevity is the second test, and it cuts against student instinct. Students tend to quote long, believing that more of the novel on the page means more evidence in the essay. The opposite is true. A long quotation cannot be analyzed word by word, so it gets summarized, and summarized quotation is decoration. A short quotation, a clause or a single image, can be unpacked fully: every word choice examined, every connotation weighed. The plan should favor the short and the sharp. "Her voice is full of money" is seven words that can sustain a paragraph on imagery, synesthesia, and the commodification of Daisy. A full paragraph of Daisy's dialogue cannot be unpacked at the same density. When noting evidence, the writer should prefer the line that fits in the margin of the plan. If the quotation needs more than a line of shorthand, it is probably too long to analyze well, and a shorter fragment from the same passage will serve better.

The third test, analytical productivity, is the one students most often skip. A quotation can be precise and brief and still be dead on the page if it invites no technique observation. The plan's evidence note must name the move: symbolism, imagery, diction, repetition, narrative voice, structural placement. "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past" invites observation about rhythm, the long vowels, the paradox of forward motion and backward pull, the first-person plural that suddenly includes the reader. A note that names "rhythm and paradox" beside the quotation gives the drafting paragraph its spine. A note without a technique invites the writer to quote and move on, which is the dropped-quotation habit markers penalize. The technique note is the smallest unit of planning and one of the most valuable, because it converts every piece of evidence from a display item into an analytical task.

Building an evidence bank during revision makes the plan's evidence stage dramatically faster. Rather than hoping the right quotations surface under pressure, the student prepares a small set of short, multipurpose lines, each one capable of serving several themes. The green-light belief serves the dream, hope, and symbolism. The careless-people verdict serves class, morality, and character. "I was within and without" serves narration, detachment, and Nick's divided position. "The foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams" serves corruption, imagery, and the ending. "Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!" serves delusion, dialogue as characterization, and time. Eight to ten such lines, genuinely memorized with their locations and technique notes, will cover nearly any prompt the exam sets. The plan then becomes an act of selection from abundance rather than a hunt through scarcity, and selection is fast. Students who try to memorize thirty quotations memorize none securely; students who master ten own them. The bank is built during revision, but it pays out on the plan.

Scene evidence deserves the same discipline as quotation evidence. Some points are better served by a moment than a line: Myrtle's death in the Valley of Ashes, Gatsby's funeral with almost no mourners, the Plaza Hotel confrontation. A scene note must be as precise as a quotation note: which scene, what happens in it, and what analytical move it invites. "Plaza confrontation, Tom exposes Gatsby, dialogue as power struggle, Gatsby's 'Her voice is full of money' collapsing under Tom's facts" is a scene note with a technique. "Plaza scene" is a memory jog. The plan treats scenes and quotations identically, because the paragraph treats them identically: both are evidence to be analyzed, not summarized. The temptation with scenes is to retell them, and the technique note is the defense. Name what the scene does structurally or dramatically, and the paragraph will analyze the doing rather than narrate the happening.

### What does the final two-minute check catch?

The final check catches the three failures drafting cannot fix cheaply: a thesis that answers the wrong question, a point that serves the plot instead of the thesis, and evidence with no analytical move attached. It also sets the introduction's opening phrase and the conclusion's return. Two minutes of inspection here replace twenty minutes of repair later.

The first inspection is thesis against prompt. Read the own-words sentence from minute two and the thesis from minute four side by side, and ask whether the thesis answers the sentence. This sounds too simple to matter, and it is the single most valuable two minutes of the sequence, because prompt-drift is the costliest error a timed essay can make. A brilliant essay on the wrong question scores worse than a competent essay on the right one, and drift usually happens at the thesis stage, when a writer with a favorite reading bends the prompt toward it. The check makes the bend visible. If the prompt asks about presentation and the thesis argues about importance, the mismatch shows in seconds, and the fix is a one-sentence revision, not a rewritten essay. Writers who skip the check discover the mismatch, if they ever do, after the essay is finished, when the fix costs everything. The check is cheap precisely because the essay does not exist yet.

The second inspection is points against thesis. Read each point heading and ask whether it proves the thesis or merely tours the novel. The symptom of failure is a point that could appear in an essay on any prompt: "Gatsby's parties," "the Valley of Ashes," "the ending." These are topics, and topics serve the plot. A point that serves the thesis wears the thesis's language: "the parties display wealth as spectacle that excludes even their host." If a point heading does not contain an argument, rephrase it now, while rephrasing costs a few words. The check also tests the set: do the three points overlap, leaving one idea proved three times? Do they leave a gap, with part of the thesis unproved? Overlap and gap are both visible when the headings sit together on the page, and both are fixable in seconds. The unplanned writer discovers overlap at paragraph four, when two paragraphs have said the same thing, and discovers gap at the conclusion, when the thesis claims more than the essay proved.

The third inspection is evidence against analysis. Scan each evidence note and ask whether it names a technique or analytical move. A note that is only a quotation is a warning: the paragraph it feeds will quote and drop. Add the technique word now, while the novel is still fresh in the mind from the evidence stage. This inspection also catches the over-ambitious note, the quotation too long to unpack, and the under-specified note, the scene with no analytical angle. Both get fixed in seconds. The check's discipline here is the same as the evidence stage's, applied with fresh eyes after a few minutes' distance. Distance helps: the writer who returns to the notes at minute nine sees their vagueness more clearly than the writer who made them at minute seven.

The check also performs two constructive jobs. It drafts the introduction's opening phrase and the conclusion's return phrase, the two small extras the one-page plan carries. The introduction phrase is usually the paradox or tension the thesis addresses: "the honest narrator who judges everyone." The conclusion phrase is the thesis restated with earned weight: "honest about his partiality, partial in his honesty." These phrases are not sentences to copy; they are directions for the drafting, so the introduction opens with purpose and the conclusion lands rather than trails off. The second constructive job is the counter-argument slot. The check asks what the strongest objection to the thesis would be, and assigns it a home: a qualification inside point two, a concession in the conclusion. An essay that acknowledges its best objection and answers it is stronger than one that ignores objections, and the plan is where the acknowledgment gets scheduled. Unplanned essays either ignore objections or surrender to them mid-paragraph; planned essays place them.

When the check fails, the protocol matters. A failed check is good news, because it failed before the essay existed. If the thesis mismatches the prompt, revise the thesis sentence; the points may survive with rephrasing. If a point serves the plot, rephrase the point as a claim; the evidence may survive unchanged. If evidence lacks a technique, add the word; the quotation stays. Most check failures are one-line fixes, which is the entire point of checking early. Only rarely does the check reveal a structural failure, a thesis with no possible points or points with no possible evidence, and even then the fix is a fresh two minutes on the failed stage, not a fresh start. The check is not a verdict on the writer. It is maintenance on the plan, and maintenance done now is what the ten minutes are for.

### Why do markers reward the planned essay?

Markers reward the planned essay because its virtues are the marking criteria: a clear thesis, paragraphs with purpose, selected evidence, and sustained analysis. Planning does not add decoration; it delivers the exact qualities examiners are paid to find. An unplanned essay can stumble into these qualities, but a planned one produces them on purpose, paragraph after paragraph.

Consider what a marker actually does with a timed essay. The marker reads quickly, under workload pressure, looking for the qualities the mark scheme names. The first is argument: does the essay have a position it sustains? The planned essay announces its position in the introduction because the position existed before the introduction was written. The unplanned essay often delays its position until the conclusion, if it arrives at all, which means the marker spends the whole essay wondering what is being argued. That wondering is not neutral; it is the experience of a lower band. The second quality is paragraph purpose. Markers notice when every paragraph does a job, because most essays they read have at least one paragraph that does nothing. The planned essay's paragraphs map to the plan's points, so each one advances the case. The unplanned essay's paragraphs map to the order ideas occurred, so some advance the case and some mark time. The marker does not need to see the plan to feel the difference. Structure is perceptible in prose.

The third quality is evidence, and here the marker's judgment is fine-grained. Examiners distinguish between quotation displayed and quotation used, between a line dropped into a paragraph and a line analyzed. The planned essay analyzes because its evidence notes named the technique in advance; the writer arrives at each quotation knowing what to say about it. The unplanned essay is more likely to quote and summarize, because the writer is meeting the quotation for the first time in the drafting. Examiner reports on timed essays return to this distinction year after year: the essays that score well integrate brief, apt quotation into analytical sentences, while weaker essays quote at length and move on. The plan is the mechanism that puts a given essay on the right side of that line. It does not make the writer smarter. It makes the writer earlier, and earlier is enough.

The fourth quality is the hardest to fake and the easiest to plan: sustained focus on the question. Markers penalize drift, the slow slide from the prompt into general discussion of the novel, and drift is the signature failure of the unplanned essay. The planned essay resists drift structurally, because every point was tested against the thesis and the thesis against the prompt before writing began. When the planned writer feels the pull toward an interesting but irrelevant observation, the plan is there to rule it out: does it serve the thesis? If not, it waits for another essay. The unplanned writer has no such test, and the interesting irrelevance goes in, and the marker notes the drift. Focus is not a personality trait. It is a product of the plan's checks, and markers reward the product whether or not they ever see the sheet.

There is also the matter of the plan itself. In many exams the planning page is visible to the marker, and a clear plan creates a halo of competence around the essay. This is not the main reason to plan, but it is a real one. A marker who sees a thesis, three claimed points, and evidence notes reads the essay that follows with the expectation of structure, and expectations shape judgment at the margins. More importantly, a visible plan can earn credit when the essay runs short of time. An essay that ends abruptly but sits beneath a complete plan shows the marker what the argument would have done, and markers are human: the plan's promise softens the essay's truncation. The unplanned essay that runs out of time simply stops, and the marker sees only the stopping. Planning is insurance as well as method, and the premium is ten minutes.

### How do you use the plan while you write?

Keep the plan visible and consult it between paragraphs, checking each paragraph against its point heading before moving on. Tick off points as you complete them, and note any amendment on the sheet so plan and essay stay in agreement. The plan is a working document during drafting, not a relic of the first ten minutes.

The physical arrangement matters more than students expect. The plan should sit where the eyes can reach it without effort: the facing page of the answer booklet, a planning box kept open beside the writing, a ruled-off section at the top of the first page. A plan buried three pages back might as well not exist, because the writer will not turn back to it mid-paragraph, and the essay will quietly diverge. Before drafting begins, the writer should decide exactly where the plan lives and keep it there. This is a small decision with large consequences. The plan's power during drafting is as a reference, and a reference that cannot be seen cannot be consulted. Students who plan on scrap paper that slides under the desk have planned for nothing.

The between-paragraph ritual is the plan's active use. Finishing a paragraph, the writer glances at the sheet, ticks the completed point, reads the next point heading, and checks the evidence note beside it. This takes seconds, and it does three things. It confirms the finished paragraph did its job: does what was written match the claim on the sheet? If the paragraph drifted, the writer knows now, with the next paragraph still unwritten, rather than at the end. It loads the next paragraph's materials into working memory: the claim, the quotation, the technique, all read fresh seconds before they are needed. And it maintains the essay's momentum, because the writer never faces the blankness of "what next?" The next is written down. Writers who skip the ritual often report that the plan "didn't help," but what didn't help was a plan consulted once and abandoned. The plan helps when it is used, and use is a habit, not an event.

Amendments belong on the sheet, in the moment. When a better point arrives mid-essay, or when a paragraph's evidence proves weaker than expected, the writer updates the plan: cross out, add, rephrase. The updated sheet keeps plan and essay in agreement, which preserves the value of the remaining checks. A writer who amends mentally, without touching the sheet, creates two plans, the written one and the real one, and the written one becomes misleading. The amendment rule from the failure-modes section applies here as a positive practice: the plan changes deliberately, on the page, where the change is visible. There is a satisfying aspect to this. The plan with crossings-out and additions is a record of thinking, and thinking visibly improving is exactly what the exam rewards. A pristine plan that the essay ignored is worth less than a messy plan the essay followed.

Time management during drafting interacts with the plan continuously. The writer who is running long, with too much essay left and too little time, should return to the sheet and choose: which point can be compressed, which evidence note can be dropped, which paragraph can do double duty? The plan makes these triage decisions clean, because the essay's structure is visible at a glance. Without the plan, triage happens blindly, and writers under time pressure tend to rush everything equally, producing three thin paragraphs instead of two strong ones. The writer who is running short on material, with time left and points exhausted, should return to the sheet and expand: which evidence note has unanalyzed depth, which point admits the scheduled counter-argument, which conclusion phrase can grow? The plan is a dashboard for the essay's resources, and dashboards exist for exactly these moments. The unplanned writer manages time by feel. The planned writer manages it by reading the sheet.

The conclusion deserves a final consultation. Before writing the last paragraph, the writer reads the conclusion phrase from the plan and the thesis beside it, and checks the essay against both: has the thesis been proved, or only asserted? Does the conclusion have something to return to, or must it invent a thesis now? The planned writer can answer these questions because the answers are on the sheet. The conclusion then does its proper job, restating the thesis with the weight of the proven points behind it, and the essay ends with the feeling of arrival. There is a small discipline that helps here: write the conclusion's first sentence as a direct answer to the prompt, using the prompt's own key terms, and then let the rest of the paragraph earn that answer. Unplanned conclusions often dodge the prompt in their final lines, drifting into general praise of the novel. The plan's conclusion phrase, written ten minutes into the exam with the prompt fresh, keeps the ending loyal to the question that started everything. This is the last return the ten minutes pay, minutes after they were spent, in the final paragraph of the essay. The plan that began as five moves on a blank page ends as the instrument the writer used to finish well. That continuity, from the first two minutes to the last paragraph, is what the method means by ten minutes that save the essay.

### How does a plan handle the prompts students fear most?

Feared prompts, the unseen passage, the strange comparison, the question on a minor character, all surrender to the same sequence because the sequence asks about the task, not the topic. Unpack what is demanded, form a thesis about that demand, and let the points follow the demand's shape. Fear comes from shapelessness, and the plan supplies the shape.

The unseen passage is the most feared and the most manageable. The prompt supplies the material, which removes the recall problem entirely, and the plan's job shifts from selection to organization. Minutes zero to two ask what the passage does: what is its dramatic situation, whose voice controls it, where does it sit in the novel? Minutes two to four form a reading: this passage presents Gatsby's longing through images of distance that Nick's narration quietly frames as futile. The points organize around the passage's own movements rather than imposed themes: the imagery first, the narrative voice second, the structural placement third. Evidence is the passage itself, quoted briefly and often, and the shorthand discipline matters more than ever, because abundance tempts the writer to quote everything. The plan's one-quotation-per-move rule, adapted here to one observation per movement of the passage, keeps the analysis dense. Students fear the unseen passage because they cannot prepare its content. The plan shows that content was never the problem; organization was, and organization is portable.

The minor-character prompt frightens students who have prepared only the major figures. A question on Myrtle, or Wilson, or Jordan, can feel like an ambush to a writer whose evidence bank holds only Gatsby, Daisy, and Nick. The plan's response is to treat the minor character as a lens rather than a subject. Minutes zero to two ask what the character's function is: what does this figure allow the novel to show that the major characters cannot? The thesis forms around function, not biography: Wilson's passivity makes him the novel's measure of what the dream does to those it excludes. The points then follow the function: the character's introduction, the character's key scene, the character's fate as judgment on the world around them. Evidence comes from the character's few appearances, which are few enough to recall precisely, and the technique notes carry extra weight, because with little material the analysis must do more. The plan turns a thin topic into a focused argument, which is exactly what the prompt rewards. The students who fail these prompts fail by apologizing for the character's minor status and padding with plot. The plan forbids both.

The strange comparison, Gatsby set beside an unfamiliar text or an unexpected frame, frightens by doubling the unknown. The plan's comparative discipline from the FAQ applies at speed: find the shared frame in the unpacking minutes, phrase every point as a claim about both sides, and let the frame do the organizing work. If the comparison is genuinely strange, the strangeness is usually the point: the prompt is testing whether the writer can think with the novel rather than about it. The plan's thesis should say so directly. The sequence does not make the unfamiliar familiar. It makes the unfamiliar answerable, which is all the exam asks.

Underneath these cases sits a general protocol for the prompt that seems to defeat preparation. When the writer genuinely does not know the angle, the plan falls back on the novel's permanent resources: narration, imagery, structure. Almost any Gatsby prompt can be approached through one of these doors. A question about an unfamiliar theme can be answered through the imagery that carries it. A question about an unfamiliar character can be answered through Nick's narration of them. A question about an unfamiliar scene can be answered through its structural placement. The plan's unpacking minutes are where the writer chooses the door, and the thesis commits to it. This is not a trick. It is the recognition that a great novel is analyzable from any entrance, and the plan's job is to choose an entrance quickly and walk through it with purpose. Fear tells the writer there is no door. The plan finds one in two minutes.

### What does a finished one-page plan actually look like?

A finished plan is a single page of shorthand: the prompt restated in one line, a working thesis with its because-clause beneath it, three claim-shaped point headings in order, evidence notes with technique words beside each point, and two small phrases for the opening and the close. Nothing on it is a sentence meant for the essay.

The best way to understand the method is to see its product, so here is a complete plan sheet for the American Dream prompt discussed earlier, exactly as it might look at minute ten. At the top, the own-words sentence: "Asks how Fitzgerald presents the dream and what that presentation implies." Beneath it, the thesis: "Fitzgerald presents the American Dream as a beautiful illusion the novel exposes as corrupt, because Gatsby embodies its hope and the Buchanans embody its price." The sentence is rough. The handwriting is rushed. Neither matters, because the sentence is arguable and it has a because-clause, which means it can generate points. A student looking at this thesis knows what the essay will argue. A student looking at a vaguer thesis, "the dream is important in the novel," knows nothing, and the plan has already done its first job by making the difference visible.

The three point headings follow, each a claim. Point one: "The dream first appears as pure romance; the green light is its perfect image." Point two: "Wealth corrupts the dream; the parties turn longing into spectacle." Point three: "The dream collapses; the Valley of Ashes and the ending price it." Beside point one, the evidence note: "green light, ch.1 end, 'Gatsby believed in the green light,' symbolism of distance." Beside point two: "parties, ch.3, 'men and girls came and went like moths,' simile, spectacle without substance; owl-eyes in library, hollowness." Beside point three: "Valley of Ashes, ch.2/8, 'ash-grey men,' imagery of waste; Myrtle's death, plot as moral evidence; final meditation, 'boats against the current,' rhythm as resignation." Each note pairs the material with the move. The writer who drafted this sheet at minute eight knows, for every paragraph, what to quote and what to say about the quotation. The writer who skipped the plan will meet these same decisions during drafting, when each one costs writing time.

At the bottom of the sheet, the two phrases. Opening: "beautiful illusion, then the price." Closing: "hope belonged to Gatsby; the bill went to everyone." And in the margin, the counter-argument slot: "concede Nick's tenderness toward Gatsby, answer that tenderness is the novel's last illusion." The whole sheet holds perhaps ninety words. It took ten minutes to make. The essay it produces will take forty minutes to write, and every one of those minutes will be spent extending arguments rather than discovering them. Students sometimes ask whether a plan this skeletal can really carry an essay. The answer is that the skeleton is the essay's structure, and structure is what carries. The flesh, the sentences, the transitions, the analysis, gets written during drafting, which is its proper home. The plan does not write the essay. It makes the essay writable, which is the more valuable service.

Notice what the sheet does not contain. It contains no introduction draft, no topic sentences written out, no paragraph pre-composed. It contains no alternative thesis, no list of every quotation the writer knows about the dream, no doodles of indecision. Everything on it earns its place by serving the three products: prompt understood, thesis formed, points with evidence sketched. A student comparing this sheet to their own first attempts will usually find their sheets too full, and the remedy is subtraction. Cross out the extra quotations. Compress the sentences to phrases. Keep the page to one sheet. The discipline feels like loss and functions as gain, because a plan the writer can read at a glance is a plan the writer will actually use between paragraphs. The finished plan is not impressive. It is useful, and usefulness is the standard.

## Verdict

The verdict is for the plan, and it is not close. Ten minutes of structured planning produces a stronger Gatsby essay than ten extra minutes of writing, because the essay's quality is decided before the first sentence: in the thesis it argues, the points it proves, and the evidence it analyzes. Consider the alternative honestly. The writer who skips planning gains ten minutes of drafting and loses the only part of the process that guarantees the drafting has a direction. Those ten minutes get spent anyway, in the stalls and restarts and mid-paragraph repairs that unplanned writing always requires, except now they are spent at the highest price, with the clock running and the argument missing. The plan buys its minutes back with interest, in introductions that state a thesis, paragraphs that each do a job, and conclusions that arrive instead of trailing off. The objection that planning steals writing time mistakes motion for progress. Unplanned writing spends its stolen minutes discovering, mid-paragraph, what the essay should have decided before it began, and that discovery is the most expensive thinking an exam allows. A fast plan unpacks the prompt, forms a thesis, and sketches points with evidence before writing begins, and the planned essay then spends its writing time on the only work that earns marks: extending an argument already formed. Students who skip planning are not saving time. They are borrowing it at the worst interest rate the exam offers, and the debt comes due in the middle of the essay, when the clock is already running and the argument is still missing. Plan first, write second, and let the ten minutes do their quiet work. The method asks for nothing the student does not already own: knowledge of the novel, a pen, and ten minutes of discipline. What it returns is an essay that knows what it thinks before it starts thinking aloud, and in a timed exam that difference is the whole difference. The planned essay is not a better essay by accident. It is a better essay by design, and the design takes ten minutes.

One practical rhythm: rehearse the plan alone on Monday, run plan-plus-essay on Wednesday, and review both sheets on Friday, asking what the check caught and what it missed. Use the annotated text to build the evidence bank, use the timed practice to build the speed, and bring both to the exam.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you plan a Gatsby essay in ten minutes?

Run the five two-minute moves in order: unpack the prompt into your own words, form a working thesis with a because-clause, sketch three claim-shaped points, attach evidence in shorthand with the technique each invites, then order the points and check them against the thesis. Each move must leave something written on the page, because a plan that lives only in the head evaporates under pressure. Keep the whole plan to one page, and stop when the thesis, points, and evidence are all present. The sequence is fixed but the minutes flex: five minutes for a thirty-minute essay, up to fifteen for a two-hour paper, with the same moves in the same order. Treat the first attempts as training: the sequence will feel mechanical and slow, and that is the point. Mechanics survive pressure; inspiration does not, and the exam rewards the writer whose method holds when the clock is loud.

### Q: What is the fast planning sequence?

The sequence is unpack, thesis, points, evidence, check, in five two-minute stages. Minutes zero to two turn the prompt into a one-sentence statement of what is actually being asked. Minutes two to four produce one arguable thesis sentence with its reason attached. Minutes four to six sketch three points phrased as claims, not topics. Minutes six to eight attach one to two remembered quotations or scenes per point in shorthand, each with the analytical move it invites. Minutes eight to ten order the points, draft opening and closing phrases, and verify that every point serves the thesis. The order is the method's economy: a thesis formed before the prompt is understood answers the wrong question, and evidence gathered before points are chosen becomes decoration. Writers who respect the order find that each stage makes the next one easier: the unpacked prompt practically writes the thesis, and the thesis practically writes the points. The sequence is a slope, not a staircase, and it runs downhill once the first move is made.

### Q: How do you sketch points and evidence before writing?

Phrase each point as a claim that serves the thesis, never as a topic label, and write the three claims in logical order on the plan. Then beside each claim, note one to two precise pieces of evidence in shorthand: the exact words of a short quotation or the specific scene, plus the technique it invites in a word or two. A note like "green light, end of chapter one, 'Gatsby believed in the green light,' symbolism of distance" is enough; the drafting paragraph then extends the analysis instead of hunting for material. Keep evidence to one page so the selection stays disciplined, and commit to specific words rather than memory jogs like "green light quote." Specificity on the plan is what frees drafting time for analysis. A useful test: cover the novel knowledge in your head and read only the plan. If the plan alone tells you what each paragraph will argue and what it will quote, the sketch is complete. If it only reminds you of topics, the sketch needs another minute.

### Q: How do you budget planning time under pressure?

Decide the plan length before the exam, not during it: ten minutes for a forty-five-minute essay, ten to twelve for an hour, five to six for thirty minutes with the same five moves compressed. Use the exam clock and honor it, because the plan's discipline requires an ending as well as a beginning; planning for twenty minutes is a different and worse method. Write the plan in a ruled-off section or a planning box so it stays separate from the essay and visible during drafting. Glance at it between paragraphs and check each paragraph against its point heading. Students who fear running out of time should note that the plan is what prevents the mid-essay stalls that actually consume time. One more budgeting habit: decide in advance what you will sacrifice if the clock surprises you. Knowing beforehand that the third evidence note is expendable, while the thesis never is, turns panic into triage.

### Q: Why not skip planning to start writing?

Skipping the plan does not eliminate the thinking a plan would have done; it postpones it to the worst moment, mid-paragraph with the clock running. The unplanned writer does two jobs at once, discovering what to say and how to say it, which produces the classic symptoms: a general introduction that restates the prompt, a middle that drifts into plot summary when ideas run thin, and a conclusion that finally discovers the thesis too late to prove it. Ten planned minutes buy an introduction with a real thesis, paragraphs that each have a job, evidence selected before it is needed, and a conclusion that sounds like an arrival. The planned essay spends its writing time extending an argument; the unplanned essay spends it searching for one. Starting sooner feels faster and writes slower. The deeper reason is cognitive load: the mind holds only a few things at once, and the unplanned writer asks it to hold the argument, the evidence, and the prose simultaneously. The plan moves the argument and the evidence onto paper, leaving the mind free for prose.

### Q: How does planning keep the essay from drifting into summary?

Drift into summary happens when a paragraph has no argumentative job, so the writer reaches for the safest material available, which is the plot. The plan prevents this by giving every paragraph its job before drafting begins: each point on the sheet is phrased as a claim about the thesis, and each claim carries its evidence and analytical move. When the writer reaches a paragraph, the task is to extend an argument, not to find one, and there is no empty moment for summary to fill. The plan's evidence notes reinforce this because a quotation paired with a technique note demands analysis, while a bare topic heading invites narration. Planned essays still mention plot, but the plot serves the point instead of substituting for it. The check at minute eight to ten confirms the wiring: if a point cannot be phrased as a claim, it gets rephrased or replaced before the essay starts. Planned essays still narrate when narration serves the argument; the difference is hierarchy. In the planned essay the claim governs and the plot illustrates, while in the drifting essay the plot leads and any claim is incidental. The plan fixes the hierarchy before the first paragraph.

### Q: How do you rehearse ten-minute planning before the exam?

Rehearse the sequence as a standalone drill, separate from full essay writing. Take a fresh Gatsby prompt, set a ten-minute timer, and run the five moves on a blank page; then stop and inspect the sheet as if you were the marker, asking whether the thesis is arguable, the points are claims, and the evidence is specific. Do this three or four times across different prompt types, a theme question, a character question, and an open-ended statement, before ever combining the drill with a full timed essay. The first attempts will feel slow and the sheet will look thin; by the fourth, the moves become automatic and the plan fills the page. Only then add the drafting phase, running plan plus essay in the full exam time. Rehearsal builds the familiarity that makes the sequence fast, and familiarity cannot be borrowed from reading about the method. Keep the rehearsal sheets. Comparing your fourth plan with your first shows exactly which stage improved and which still leaks time, and that diagnosis is worth more than another hour of rereading the novel.

### Q: How should each planning stage be timed?

Give each of the five moves two minutes on a forty-five-minute essay, and treat the boundaries as firm rather than approximate. Unpacking gets minutes zero to two, the thesis gets two to four, points get four to six, evidence gets six to eight, and ordering plus the check gets eight to ten. The firmness matters because each stage has a natural tendency to expand: unpacking tempts endless re-reading of the prompt, and evidence tempts a trawl through everything remembered about the novel. When a stage's two minutes end, move on even if the product is rough; roughness is permitted because the plan is not marked. For shorter essays, compress proportionally rather than dropping stages: one minute per move on a thirty-minute essay keeps the full sequence intact. Never borrow minutes from the plan for drafting or the reverse; the budget is a contract with yourself. If a stage consistently overruns in rehearsal, that is data, not failure: it tells you which move needs its own drill. A writer whose evidence stage always runs long should rehearse evidence selection alone until the two minutes hold.

### Q: What belongs on a one-page plan?

Four things and nothing else: the prompt restated in your own words, the working thesis with its because-clause, three point headings phrased as claims in logical order, and beside each point one to two evidence notes in shorthand with the technique each invites. Add two small extras in the final minutes: a phrase for the introduction's opening and a phrase for the conclusion's return. Everything else is clutter. Full sentences are clutter; shorthand is the plan's language. Favorite quotations with no point to serve are clutter; evidence earns its place by attaching to a claim. Alternative theses are clutter; the plan holds one working thesis, adjustable but singular. The one-page limit is a discipline device, not a stationery constraint: it forces selection, and selection is what makes the evidence apt and the points sharp. When in doubt about an item, apply the deletion test: remove it and ask whether the plan still works. If yes, the item was clutter. Plans improve by subtraction far more often than by addition.

### Q: How do you plan when the thesis is not yet clear?

Write a provisional thesis anyway, and label it as working. The plan's thesis is a hypothesis, not a contract, and a rough position held lightly beats no position held anxiously. Draft the best arguable sentence you can in the two minutes: take the prompt's key terms, attach a verb of judgment, and add the because-clause even if the reason feels thin. Then let the points test it. Often the act of sketching points clarifies the thesis, because a point that will not fit reveals what the thesis should have said. Use the final two minutes to revise the thesis in light of the points; the check stage exists partly for this. What you must not do is leave the thesis slot blank and hope clarity arrives during drafting. Blankness compounds under pressure, while a provisional thesis gives the essay something to argue with, including itself. There is a useful paradox here: the writers most afraid of committing to a thesis are usually the ones with the most to say. The provisional thesis gives their thinking something to push against, and the pushing is what produces the real thesis.

### Q: How do you plan for an open-ended essay prompt?

Spend the unpacking minutes finding the prompt's hidden argumentative pressure. Open-ended prompts feel like freedom and function as traps, because without a specified angle the unplanned writer tries to say everything. Ask what the statement is really claiming, which words do the argumentative work, and what would count as disagreeing with it. A statement about the corruption of the American Dream contains its pressure in the word corruption: you can agree, qualify, or reframe, and the plan forces one of those positions onto the page. Then run the normal sequence: the thesis takes the chosen position, the points prove it, the evidence supports the points. The plan's first move is a loyalty test between you and the question, and it matters most exactly where the question seems to demand least. A practical trick for these prompts: imagine the mark scheme and ask what distinction it rewards. Open-ended questions almost always reward position over coverage, which tells you the plan's thesis must choose, not survey.

### Q: How do you adjust the plan mid-essay?

Treat the plan as a map, not a cage. If a better point arrives during drafting, test it against one rule: does it serve the thesis? If yes, add it to the plan and write it; the amendment takes seconds and the essay gains strength. If no, either the thesis needs adjusting to accommodate a genuinely better idea, or the idea belongs in a different essay. Update the sheet rather than abandoning it, keeping the visible plan and the actual essay in agreement so the check between paragraphs still works. What you must not do is let the essay silently diverge from the plan, because then you are writing unplanned with the time already spent. A plan that cannot change is a script, and scripts break under pressure; a plan that changes deliberately stays a plan. Make the amendment visible: draw the arrow, write the new heading, cross the old one out. The visible change keeps the sheet honest, and an honest sheet is one the writer can trust for the remaining paragraphs.

### Q: How do you order the points in a rapid plan?

Order by logical dependence, not by the novel's chapter sequence. The most reliable order is the one where each point prepares the next: establish the presentation or claim first, deepen or complicate it second, and resolve or judge it third. For the American Dream thesis, romance first, corruption second, collapse third; each point needs the previous one to land. Test the order in the final two minutes by reading the three point headings as a miniature argument: if they flow, the order holds. Avoid the chronological trap, where points march through the novel in plot order and the essay becomes a retelling with commentary. Avoid also the strongest-first temptation unless the later points genuinely build on it; an essay that peaks in paragraph one and declines feels like a deflating argument. The plan's order is the essay's skeleton, and skeletons are load-bearing. Read the three headings aloud as one flowing claim. If you stumble between them, the order needs work; if they run together, the essay's middle will run too. The ear catches logical gaps the eye misses.

### Q: How do you plan evidence from memory in closed-book exams?

Commit to specific words on the plan rather than trusting recall during drafting. Beside each point, write the exact short quotation you intend to use, or if no quotation is secure, name the precise scene and the analytical move it invites. The commitment reveals gaps early: if you cannot produce evidence for a point at minute seven, change the point or shift from quotation to scene before drafting begins. Build the evidence bank during revision by selecting a small set of short, multipurpose quotations, lines like the green-light belief or the careless-people verdict, that serve several themes, and rehearse attaching them to different points. On the plan, note the technique beside each quotation in a word or two so the paragraph analyzes rather than drops the quote. Memory is the fragile part of closed-book essays; the plan is where its fragility gets managed. A final memory safeguard: for each quotation on the plan, note one distinctive word, the word you would search for if you could. That word becomes the retrieval hook, and hooks hold under pressure when whole sentences slip.

### Q: What does over-planning look like and how do you avoid it?

Over-planning is a plan that has become a draft: full sentences where shorthand would do, every paragraph pre-written, twenty minutes gone before drafting starts. The symptom is a beautiful plan and a panicked essay. Avoid it with the completeness test: the plan is done when the thesis, the three points, and the evidence notes are all on the page. At that moment, stop, even if the urge to keep refining is strong. Keep notes skeletal by rule: phrases, not sentences; reminders, not transcriptions. If you find yourself writing the introduction on the planning page, you have crossed the line. The plan's value is structural, not verbal; its job is to decide what the essay argues, not how it sounds. Ten minutes is the budget because it is enough for structure and too short for drafting, and the timer enforces what willpower may not. Watch for the disguised form: the writer who spends five extra minutes checking one detail in memory. Checking is drafting in disguise. Trust the completeness test and move on.

### Q: How do you plan the introduction and conclusion in ten minutes?

Do not draft them; sketch their jobs. In the final two minutes, note one phrase for the introduction's opening move, usually the paradox or tension the thesis addresses, and one phrase for the conclusion's return, usually the thesis restated with the weight of the proven points behind it. The introduction's job is already decided by the plan: state the thesis and map the points. The conclusion's job is likewise decided: return to the thesis as an arrival, not a discovery. Because both jobs are structural, they need phrases, not paragraphs. A planned introduction writes itself in three or four sentences once drafting starts; an unplanned one sprawls because the writer is still deciding what the essay claims. Reserve the actual sentences for drafting time, where they belong. The planned conclusion has one more advantage: because the thesis was proved three times over, the final paragraph can reach outward, connecting the argument to the novel's larger significance, instead of merely restating what was said.

### Q: How do you recover a plan when time runs short?

Protect the plan's core and sacrifice its ornaments. If the exam clock shows less time than expected, compress the sequence rather than abandoning it: one minute per move still produces a thesis, three point headings, and one evidence note per point, which is enough structure to carry a shortened essay. Never skip the thesis stage, because an essay without a thesis cannot be saved by extra drafting minutes; if anything must give, let it be the second evidence note per point or the introduction phrase. If the shortage appears mid-essay rather than at the start, return to the sheet, cross out the weakest point, and redistribute its evidence to the remaining two. A two-point essay with a clear thesis outperforms a three-point essay written in panic. The plan is most valuable exactly when time is shortest, because pressure is when unplanned writing collapses fastest. In the extreme case, when only minutes remain, the plan's thesis plus one well-evidenced point still constitutes an essay with an argument. A fragment of an argument outscores a complete summary, because the mark scheme rewards thinking, not coverage.

### Q: How does the rapid plan connect to the full essay process?

The rapid plan is the assembly stage of the larger process: it takes the components the other articles teach and fits them together at speed. The thesis article teaches what makes a claim arguable; the plan is where you build one in two minutes. The structure article teaches how points arrange into a shape; the plan is where you choose the order under pressure. The essay hub teaches the whole journey from reading to revision; the plan covers the ten minutes where the journey's direction gets set. Think of the relationship as design versus construction: the hub and its companions teach the craft, and the plan is the site drawing you sketch before building. A writer who knows the components but cannot assemble them quickly will still stall in the exam; a writer who can plan fast but knows no craft will assemble weak materials quickly. The skill this article owns is the rapid assembly, and it completes the set. Students sometimes ask which to learn first. Learn the components first, through the hub and its companions, so the plan has quality materials to assemble. Then learn the assembly, so the components reach the page in time.

### Q: How do you plan a comparative Gatsby prompt quickly?

Shape the points around the comparison itself, never around one text at a time. In the unpacking minutes, identify the shared frame the prompt implies: the basis on which the two works can actually be compared. Then phrase each point as a claim about both texts under that frame, so the plan enforces integration from the start. A point heading like "both novels present wealth as spectacle, but only one makes it moral evidence" keeps the comparison inside every paragraph. Attach evidence from each text beside the point in shorthand, keeping the notes brief enough that selection, not collection, is the discipline. The classic failure is the stapled essay, half on one text and half on the other, and the plan prevents it because no point heading is allowed to mention only one side. Speed matters here because comparative prompts double the material; the plan's selection discipline is what keeps the doubled material under control. One comparative pitfall the plan prevents: the lopsided essay that knows one text deeply and the other thinly. The evidence notes expose the imbalance at minute seven, when there is still time to rebalance or narrow the frame.

### Q: How do you stop the plan from becoming a second draft?

Enforce shorthand as a hard rule and the completeness test as a hard stop. Write the plan in phrases and fragments, never in sentences that could be copied into the essay; if a note reads like prose, compress it. Stop planning the moment the thesis, points, and evidence are all present, even if polishing feels productive, because polishing the plan is drafting by another name. Keep the plan physically separate from the essay, in a ruled-off section, so the two cannot merge on the page. And remember the plan's purpose: it decides what the essay argues, not how it sounds. The sentences belong to drafting time. A plan that tries to do drafting's job steals drafting's minutes and delivers a worse version of both products; a plan that stays skeletal does its own job and releases the writer to write. If you catch yourself composing sentences on the plan, stop and ask what decision the sentence is avoiding. Usually it is a thesis decision or an evidence decision, and making the decision directly is faster than writing around it.
