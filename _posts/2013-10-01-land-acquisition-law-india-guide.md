Land acquisition law in India is not one law. A farmer whose field is notified for a highway, a family whose plot is taken for a metro line, a factory owner whose premises are requisitioned during an emergency and a village whose common land is absorbed into an industrial estate may all describe their experience in the same words, that the government took the land, yet each of them stands inside a different legal regime, with a different compensation formula, a different set of procedural safeguards, a different forum for complaint and, in some regimes, no right to object at all. The first and most consequential question in any Indian acquisition is therefore not how much will be paid. It is which law is doing the taking. Everything else, the consent threshold, the impact assessment, the multiplier, the solatium, the rehabilitation package, the authority that hears the dispute, follows from that routing decision, and the routing decision is made by the acquiring government when it chooses its instrument, usually before the landowner hears of the project at all.

![Land acquisition law in India mapped across the general Act, the Fourth Schedule special statutes and state laws - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-52.webp)

The shape of the field is this. At the centre stands a general acquisition law, for a hundred and twenty years the Land Acquisition Act, 1894, and since 1 January 2014 the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, known in practice as the LARR Act. Around that general law stands a ring of special statutes, each written for one sector, each carrying its own acquisition code: thirteen of them are listed in the Fourth Schedule to the 2013 Act, covering national highways, railways, metro railways, coal bearing areas, atomic energy, electricity, petroleum and minerals pipelines, ancient monuments, the Damodar Valley Corporation, mines, tramways, the resettlement of displaced persons and the requisitioning and acquisition of immovable property. Beneath and beside those central laws sits a third layer, the acquisition statutes of the states, industrial development Acts, highways Acts, housing Acts and town planning Acts, together with state amendments to the central law itself, because acquisition of property is a Concurrent List subject on which both Parliament and the state legislatures may legislate. A fourth route exists alongside all three: the state may skip compulsion entirely and buy, or assemble land through negotiated purchase and land pooling, in which case the acquisition statutes never switch on at all and the transaction is governed by consent, contract and the price the parties can agree.

The thesis of this article, and the reason a map is needed, is that the general law has historically been the exception in practice rather than the rule. The projects that displaced the largest numbers of people in independent India, the highways, the dams, the mines, the power stations, the railway lines, were very often acquired under the special statutes, which were drafted for speed and which carried weaker safeguards than the general law, sometimes no rehabilitation obligation at all. When Parliament replaced the 1894 Act with the far more demanding settlement of 2013, with its social impact assessment, its consent thresholds and its doubled compensation, the reform at first changed the legal position for only part of the field, because the thirteen scheduled statutes were expressly excluded from the new Act's operation. What closed much of that gap was not the statute itself but an instrument made under it: first an ordinance, and then, after the ordinance failed politically, a Removal of Difficulties Order of 28 August 2015 that extended the 2013 Act's compensation and rehabilitation schedules to every acquisition under the thirteen scheduled Acts. A reader who understands only the 2013 Act understands the minority position. A reader who understands the schedule of exemptions, and the order that partially closed it, understands where the operative law lives.

This article builds that map. It sets out the constitutional frame, because the power to acquire and the right to property determine what any acquisition statute may lawfully do. It then describes the two general laws, the Act of 1894 whose architecture governed for over a century and supplies the vocabulary the whole field still speaks, and the Act of 2013 whose settlement replaced it, in each case at the depth a map requires and no deeper, because the statute-by-statute treatment belongs to the specialist articles of this cluster. It then works through the special statutes and the Fourth Schedule, the extension mechanism of 2015, the state layer, and the non-compulsory routes, and it closes by testing the two large framings that dominate argument about this field, the development framing and the displacement framing, against the structure the map reveals. The findable artifact is a decision table that routes any acquisition a reader encounters to its regime in two questions and states, for each route, the compensation basis, the consent requirement, the assessment requirement and the rehabilitation obligation that follow.

## The constitutional frame: a power assumed and a right demoted

Every acquisition statute in India rests on two constitutional facts. The first is that the power to take private property for public purposes, the doctrine lawyers call eminent domain, is nowhere granted by the Constitution in terms. The second is that the corresponding right, the citizen's right to property, was removed from the fundamental rights chapter by the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 and re-established outside it, as Article 300A, so that property is a constitutional right but not a fundamental right. Between those two facts sits the whole of Indian acquisition law: a sovereign power that the Constitution regulates but does not create, exercised against a right that the Constitution protects but has deliberately demoted.

### Why did the largest projects escape the general acquisition law?

Because the special statutes were written sector by sector, each designed to let one acquiring authority proceed without the general law's hearings, objections and court references. Highways, railways, mines and power, the works that displaced the most people, were routed through these faster codes for decades, largely outside the general law's safeguards.

The pattern was not a conspiracy but a convenience repeated until it became a structure. Each ministry drafting its sectoral code copied the shortcuts of the last, and because the projects were framed as national necessities, no legislature was ever asked to defend the cumulative result: that the Indian state's most intrusive power over its citizens' homes operated, for most of the land it took, under its least protective procedure. The exemption list in the Fourth Schedule is the fossil record of that accumulation.

The doctrine of eminent domain came to India through colonial legislation rather than through constitutional text. The power itself, the inherent authority of the sovereign to take private property for public use on payment of compensation, was articulated in European legal thought centuries before it reached an Indian statute book, and the East India Company's governments exercised it through instruments such as Bengal Regulation I of 1824 before Parliament at Westminster and then the Indian legislatures built it into general Acts, the Act of 1857, the Act of 1870 and finally the consolidating Act of 1894. When the Constitution came into force in 1950 it did not confer the power. It assumed the power existed, as an attribute of sovereignty, and it regulated the conditions of its exercise, principally through Article 31, which guaranteed that no person would be deprived of property save by authority of law and that property taken for a public purpose would be taken under a law providing compensation. The distinction matters in argument. A challenge to an Indian acquisition almost never denies that the state may acquire. It attacks the law under which the acquisition proceeds, the public purpose asserted, or the compensation the law provides, because those are the points the Constitution actually governs.

Legislative competence over the subject is concurrent. Entry 42 of the Concurrent List in the Seventh Schedule reads, in its entirety, "Acquisition and requisitioning of property." Parliament may legislate on it, and so may every state legislature, and where a state law on the subject conflicts with a central law, the rules of repugnancy in Article 254 decide which prevails, with the central law ordinarily prevailing unless the state law was reserved for the President's consideration and received assent. The concurrency is not a technicality. It is the structural reason the field has three layers. It explains why states maintain acquisition machinery inside their industrial development and highways Acts, why a state may amend the central LARR Act for its own territory with presidential assent, as several states have done, and why two identical projects in two states may proceed under materially different rules. It also sits beside a second entry that practitioners sometimes confuse with it: land itself, its tenure, its transfer and its records, is a State List subject under Entry 18 of the State List, which is why land records, tenancy and ceiling law are state fields, while the taking of land for a public purpose is shared. The map of acquisition law is federal all the way down.

The second constitutional fact, the demotion of property, was the end of a thirty-year contest that shaped the republic's first decades, and its outline has to be understood before Article 300A means anything. As enacted in 1950, the Constitution protected property twice over: Article 19(1)(f) guaranteed citizens the right to acquire, hold and dispose of property, and Article 31 required authority of law, public purpose and compensation for any taking. The very first amendment, in 1951, began the retreat, inserting Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule to shield land reform legislation from the property guarantee. The Supreme Court's early reading of compensation, in State of West Bengal v. Bela Banerjee in 1954, treated the word as requiring a just equivalent of what the owner was deprived of, a full indemnification standard. Parliament answered through the Fourth Amendment in 1955 by barring courts from questioning the adequacy of compensation. The Court in P. Vajravelu Mudaliar v. Special Deputy Collector in 1965 accepted that adequacy was not justiciable while insisting that the principles a statute laid down for fixing compensation had to be relevant to value and not illusory. The contest reached its height in R. C. Cooper v. Union of India in 1970, the bank nationalisation case, where the Court struck down the nationalisation of fourteen banks because the statute's compensation principles were irrelevant to the value of the undertaking taken and its restrictions failed Article 19; [the bank nationalisation case judgment](/2013/01/15/bank-nationalisation-case-judgment/) is examined in its own article in this series, which owns that doctrinal record, and it is cited here only because it is the high-water mark of property as a fundamental right and the decision the political branches then set out to reverse. Parliament's answer came in the Twenty-fifth Amendment of 1971, which substituted the word "amount" for "compensation" in Article 31 and inserted Article 31C to protect laws giving effect to the directive principles in Articles 39(b) and 39(c). The Kesavananda Bharati decision of 1973 upheld the substitution while preserving judicial review of the basic structure. The Forty-second Amendment attempted to widen Article 31C; the Forty-fourth removed the subject from the fundamental rights chapter altogether.

The Forty-fourth Amendment, enacted in 1978 with the property provisions commencing on 20 June 1979, omitted Article 19(1)(f) and the whole of Article 31 from Part III and inserted Article 300A in Part XII, providing that no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law; [the 44th Amendment Act 1978](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/) is the subject of its own article in this series, and the operative consequence for acquisition law is the one stated here. Three consequences follow, and they organise the modern field. First, a person whose property is taken can no longer move the Supreme Court directly under Article 32 for enforcement of the property right; the remedy lies in the High Court under Article 226 or in the forums the acquisition statute itself creates. Second, the constitutional text no longer contains the word compensation at all. The amount payable for a taking is whatever the governing statute provides, and the battle over quantum is therefore fought inside the statutes, which is precisely why the difference between the 1894 Act's formula and the 2013 Act's formula matters so much and why the extension of the 2013 formula to the scheduled Acts was worth a political crisis. Third, Article 300A is not an empty shell. The words "save by authority of law" require a valid enacted law, and the Supreme Court has read the surrounding constitutional discipline into it: in K. T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka, decided in 2011, the Court treated the right under Article 300A as a constitutional right, and a taking under it as still conditioned by public purpose and by the law providing for compensation that is not illusory, so that a statute which took property without public purpose or on principles unrelated to value would remain vulnerable, not under a fundamental right but under the constitutional right itself and the guarantee against arbitrariness in Article 14. The honest summary is that 1978 changed the forum, the standard and the politics of property, but did not leave the landowner remediless.

One further judicial anchor belongs in the frame because it determines how most acquisition challenges actually end. The courts have consistently refused to substitute their view of public purpose for the government's. In Sooraram Pratap Reddy v. District Collector, Ranga Reddy District, decided in 2008, the Supreme Court restated the settled position that public purpose is primarily a matter for the state to assess, that the concept is elastic and varies with the needs of society, and that judicial review intervenes where the power is exercised mala fide or as a colourable exercise, a fraud on the power, taking property ostensibly for a public purpose for what is in substance a private or collateral one. The declaration of public purpose in the statute's own terms is treated as near-conclusive. Challenges on this ground therefore rarely succeed, and the practical litigation in acquisition concentrates instead on procedure, on compensation and, after 2013, on the assessment and consent machinery, where the statute gives the challenger findings to attack rather than a judgment of policy to displace. The distinction between an acquisition for a public purpose and an acquisition for a company, which the 1894 Act policed through a separate Part and an inquiry into whether the work would prove useful to the public, is part of the same doctrine: the state may acquire for a company, but it must do so through the company route, with the company's agreement and cost-bearing recorded, and not by dressing a private acquisition in public purpose language.

## Public purpose: the doctrine that decides what may be taken

If the regime is the first question, public purpose is the second, because every acquisition code in the field conditions the power on it and the contest over its meaning runs through all of them. The phrase does two kinds of work. It is a jurisdictional condition: land may be taken only for a purpose the law recognises as public, and a taking for a purpose outside the condition is unauthorised however regular the procedure. And it is a site of deference: within the condition, the courts have historically left the choice of purpose to the government, intervening only where the asserted purpose is a colourable cover for a private or collateral one. The older statutes left the phrase open-textured, defined inclusively at most, and the judiciary filled it with an elastic jurisprudence in which town planning, slum clearance, industrial development, housing and the establishment of a company's factory township could all qualify, the thread being some public benefit or public utility rather than direct use by the public. Sooraram Pratap Reddy, discussed in the constitutional frame above, is the modern restatement: the purpose's public character is for the state to assess, the declaration is treated as conclusive in the statute's terms, and review polices the boundary of good faith rather than the wisdom of the choice.

The Singur acquisition is the boundary case that gives the doctrine its modern shape, and it belongs on this map because it was run, deliberately, through the general law's company route. The West Bengal government acquired land under the 1894 Act for the Tata Motors small car project, treating the establishment of industry and the employment it promised as the public purpose and processing the acquisition through the machinery for acquisition for a company. After the project's abandonment amid protest, the Supreme Court in Kedar Nath Yadav v. State of West Bengal, decided in 2016, set the acquisition aside, the two judges concurring in the result by reasoning that fastened, in different measure, on the failure to follow the company acquisition procedure the statute required and on the characterisation of the benefit: a taking whose immediate beneficiary was a single private manufacturer, processed without the safeguards the law attaches to company acquisitions, could not stand as a public purpose taking in the form it had taken. The precise division of reasoning belongs to the case law articles; the map point is that the doctrine bit, in the most politically charged acquisition of the decade, not by denying that industrialisation is a public purpose but by insisting that the route determines the safeguards and that the route chosen must be the route the benefit's character requires. The 2013 Act then rewrote the terrain on which the doctrine operates by enumerating public purposes exhaustively in Section 2(1) and by attaching the consent thresholds to the identity of the proponent, converting much of what had been doctrine into text. Where an acquisition proceeds under a special statute, the older doctrinal position largely survives, because the sectoral codes define their purposes by their sectors and the courts' deferential case law was built on cognate language. Public purpose is therefore itself a routing variable: the same project faces a statutory roster and a consent gate under the general law, and an open-textured doctrine under the special codes.

## The land reform shadow: why acquisition law began as agrarian law

The constitutional frame described above cannot be fully understood without the land reform shadow that falls across the field's first three decades, because the property clauses were not cut down in the abstract: they were cut down to save a specific programme. The abolition of zamindari, the great intermediaries' estates created or recognised under the colonial land settlements, was the first legislative project of the republic's state governments, and it was acquisition by another name: the state took the intermediary's entire interest, compensated at rates fixed by the reform statutes, and redistributed the tenurial structure beneath. The Bihar legislation was challenged in State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh in 1952, among the earliest and largest constitutional cases, and the litigation threat to the programme produced the First Amendment's protective architecture: Article 31A, which shielded laws for the acquisition of estates and related agrarian measures from the equality and freedom guarantees, and Article 31B with the Ninth Schedule, which immunised the listed statutes from fundamental rights challenge altogether. The pattern set then, enact, shield, expand the shield, recurs through the property contest: the Fourth Amendment after Bela Banerjee, the Seventeenth Amendment widening the definition of estate after further reform litigation, the Twenty-fifth after Cooper. The Ninth Schedule device itself eventually drew a boundary: in I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu in 2007, a nine-judge bench held that statutes placed in the Schedule after the Kesavananda decision of 24 April 1973 remain open to challenge where they damage the basic structure, closing the era in which the Schedule was an unlimited safe. For the map, the significance is locational. The doctrines an aspirant learns in the property chapter, 31A, 31B, the Schedule, the amount substitution, were forged in agrarian acquisition, and the industrial and infrastructure acquisition that dominates the modern field inherited a constitutional settlement designed for a different conflict. Much of the field's oddity, a demoted property right coexisting with elaborate statutory entitlements, is that inheritance working itself out: what the Constitution ceased to guarantee, the statutes of 2013 restored in legislative form, and the contest moved from the constitutional text to the schedules and notifications where this article has located it.

## The first general law: the architecture of the Act of 1894

The Land Acquisition Act, 1894, Act I of 1894, brought into force on 1 March 1894, was the general acquisition code of India for a hundred and twenty years, and its architecture is still the grammar of the field: the special statutes borrow its concepts, the 2013 Act defines itself against it, and acquisitions begun under it continued to be governed by its transition rules long after its repeal. A map of the field has to show its load-bearing walls, though the full structural survey belongs to [the Land Acquisition Act 1894 explained](/2013/10/15/land-acquisition-act-1894-explained/) in this series, which owns the statute's detailed treatment.

The Act worked through a fixed sequence of executive acts. It opened with a preliminary notification under Section 4, published when land was needed or likely to be needed for a public purpose, which authorised entry, survey and measurement. It then permitted persons interested to object under Section 5A, a hearing inserted by amendment in 1923 and thereafter the landowner's one structured opportunity to contest the taking before it hardened, though the objection went to the government whose project it was and the 5A report was a recommendation the government could and routinely did set aside. A declaration under Section 6 followed, stating that the land was needed for a public purpose, and that declaration was made conclusive evidence of the need, subject only to the colourable exercise doctrine described above. The Collector then issued notices under Section 9, conducted an inquiry and made an award under Section 11, fixing the compensation. Possession could be taken once the award was made, and the land then vested in the government free from all encumbrances under Section 16. A person dissatisfied with the award could require the Collector to refer the dispute to the district court under Section 18, which would redetermine the compensation, with appeals lying upward from there. That sequence, notify, declare, award, possess, refer, is the template against which every Indian acquiring authority's conduct is still intuitively judged.

Compensation under the Act was built on market value at the date of the Section 4 notification, computed under the matters listed in Section 23, to which the matters in Section 24, such as the speculative potential of the taking itself, were excluded, and to which a solatium was added in consideration of the compulsory nature of the acquisition. The solatium stood at fifteen per cent of market value for most of the Act's life and was raised to thirty per cent by the amendment of 1984, the same amendment that raised the interest payable on delayed payment and introduced time limits within which the award had to follow the declaration, on pain of the proceedings lapsing. Interest on the unpaid amount ran at nine per cent for the first year and fifteen per cent thereafter. Two features of this scheme drew the criticism that eventually killed it. The first was that market value was anchored to recorded transactions in a market where consideration was systematically understated to save stamp duty, so the statutory measure reproduced the understatement. The second was the urgency clause in Section 17, which allowed the government, in cases of urgency, to take possession fifteen days after the Section 9 notice and before any award, and to dispense with the Section 5A hearing altogether. Invoked first for defence and emergencies, the clause became a routine instrument for ordinary projects, and its use meant that the single hearing the Act offered could be switched off precisely when the acquiring authority was in a hurry, which was most of the time.

The Act's other structural absence was rehabilitation. It compensated owners of interests in land, and it did so by paying money to the persons interested whom its inquiry identified. It contained no obligation to resettle a displaced family, no livelihood restoration, no consideration for the landless labourer, the tenant, the sharecropper or the artisan whose income died with the acquisition but who held no title the Act recognised. A separate Part, Part VII, governed acquisition for companies, requiring an inquiry into whether the work was likely to prove useful to the public and an agreement by the company to bear the cost, but the safeguards ran to the public character of the purpose, not to the people displaced by it. When the political history of Indian development is written, the gap between the Act's meticulous machinery for valuing land and its silence about the humans on the land is the statute's defining feature, and it is the gap the politics of Singur, Nandigram and the other great land conflicts of the decade before 2013 made impossible to sustain.

## The second general law: the settlement of 2013

The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, Act 30 of 2013, received assent on 26 September 2013 and commenced on 1 January 2014, repealing the Act of 1894 subject to the transition provisions in its Section 24. It is the general law of the field, and the complete treatment of its machinery belongs to [the LARR Act 2013 explained](/2013/11/01/larr-act-2013-explained/) in this series. What a map must record is the settlement's shape, because the shape is what the other regimes are measured against and what the extension of 2015 carried into them.

The settlement rests on four pillars that the 1894 Act lacked or held in weaker form. The first is a Social Impact Assessment. Before acquisition, the government must cause a study, under Chapter II of the Act, of the project's impact on affected families, on livelihoods, on public infrastructure and on the social fabric of the area, carried out in consultation with the local bodies, publicised in the affected area and appraised by an independent Expert Group, which may recommend that the project not proceed where the costs outweigh the benefits and the purpose can be served by other land. A public hearing in the affected area is part of the process. The second pillar is consent. Where land is acquired for a private company, the prior consent of at least eighty per cent of affected families is required; where it is acquired for a public private partnership, the threshold is seventy per cent; acquisition by the government for its own use requires no consent, a distribution of obligations that turns entirely on who the project proponent is. The third pillar is the compensation formula. Market value is determined under Sections 26 to 28 by the most favourable of the measures the Act allows, then multiplied, in rural areas, by a factor between one and two fixed by the state government on a sliding scale by distance from urban centres under the First Schedule, and in urban areas by one, and to the result is added a solatium of one hundred per cent under Section 30, with twelve per cent per annum additional amount from notification to award. The practical effect, widely reported and intended by the drafters, is compensation of up to twice the market value in urban areas and up to four times in rural areas once multiplier and solatium combine. The fourth pillar is rehabilitation and resettlement as an entitlement in the award itself, set out in the Second Schedule: housing for displaced families, land or employment or annuity options, subsistence and transportation allowances, resettlement infrastructure under the Third Schedule, and special provisions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, including additional compensation and a development plan, with acquisition in Scheduled Areas conditioned by the Act's Section 41 on the prior consent of the Gram Sabha and to be taken only as a demonstrable last resort.

Around those pillars the Act built safeguards that qualify the old machinery. The urgency power survives only in narrowed form in Section 40, confined to defence of India, national security and emergencies arising from natural calamity, and even there hedged with conditions. Multi-irrigated cropped land may be acquired only as a last resort and subject to a state-wide ceiling under Section 10, with equivalent land to be developed in exchange. Land that remains unutilised for the period the Act specifies, five years in the enacted text, is to be returned to the original owners or to a state land bank under Section 101. Disputes over the award go in the first instance not to the district court but to a Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority established under Chapter VIII, with appeal to the High Court. And Section 24 carried the old regime's unfinished business across the repeal: proceedings begun under the 1894 Act continued under it where an award had been made, but where an award had been made five years or more before the new Act commenced and physical possession had not been taken or compensation not paid, the proceedings were deemed to have lapsed and fresh proceedings had to begin under the new law, a clause whose two conditions generated a decade of litigation between the readings given in Pune Municipal Corporation v. Harakchand Misirimal Solanki in 2014 and the five-judge restatement in Indore Development Authority v. Manoharlal in 2020, which rejected the earlier reading on the deposit of compensation in the treasury. That litigation belongs to the compensation articles of this cluster; on the map it is enough to note that the transition clause, not any forward-looking provision, produced the new Act's most contested jurisprudence.

### What changed on 1 January 2014 when the 2013 Act commenced?

The general acquisition code changed identity. The Act of 1894 was repealed and replaced by a statute that made social impact assessment, consent thresholds, a multiplied compensation formula with one hundred per cent solatium, and rehabilitation as an award entitlement the default conditions of a lawful taking.

The commencement date matters beyond symbolism, because the transition rules in Section 24 key off it: awards made under the old Act before that date carried the old proceedings forward, while the lapse conditions for older unfinished proceedings were measured against it. Commencement also started the one-year clock in Section 105(3) for extending the new entitlements to the Fourth Schedule statutes, the deadline whose expiry on 1 January 2015 set the ordinance crisis in motion.

The 2013 settlement also redrew the public purpose definition, listing the purposes exhaustively in Section 2(1): strategic and defence purposes, infrastructure, housing for the poor, rehabilitation of project-affected families, industrial corridors, mining and the other specified heads, ending the open-ended elasticity that the courts had protected and replacing a discretionary breadth with a statutory roster. And it made one exclusion that the whole of the next section is about. Section 105 provides that, subject to the extension duty in its third sub-section, the provisions of the Act shall not apply to the enactments relating to land acquisition specified in the Fourth Schedule. Parliament enacted the most demanding general acquisition law in India's history and, in the same statute, walled off the sectors that acquire the most land from it.

## The ring of special statutes and the Fourth Schedule

The Fourth Schedule to the 2013 Act lists thirteen enactments. Read as a roster, it is the map of where Indian acquisition law actually operated for most of the republic's life. The Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958 protects and acquires around monuments. The Atomic Energy Act, 1962 carries acquisition powers for the nuclear programme. The Damodar Valley Corporation Act, 1948 equips the river valley corporation. The Indian Tramways Act, 1886, a survival from the first generation of acquisition statutes, serves tramway undertakings. The Land Acquisition (Mines) Act, 1885 deals with the acquisition of land over minerals, where what is taken is often the right to work the seam rather than the surface. The Metro Railways (Construction of Works) Act, 1978 serves metro construction. The National Highways Act, 1956 serves the national highway network. The Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines (Acquisition of Right of User in Land) Act, 1962 takes not ownership but a right of user beneath land through which pipelines pass. The Requisitioning and Acquisition of Immovable Property Act, 1952 governs requisition, the temporary taking, and the acquisition of requisitioned property that follows in some cases. The Resettlement of Displaced Persons (Land Acquisition) Act, 1948 served the refugee resettlement of Partition. The Coal Bearing Areas Acquisition and Development Act, 1957 serves coal prospecting and mining. The Electricity Act, 2003 carries the works and wayleave powers of the power sector. And the Railways Act, 1989 serves the railway network, with a special chapter inserted in 2008 for designated special railway projects.

These statutes share a design logic, and the logic explains both their existence and their cost. A ministry building a linear project, thousands of kilometres of road or track across hundreds of villages, wanted a code tuned to its own engineering sequence: notify the alignment, hear objections on a short clock, take possession, build, and settle compensation afterwards through its own competent authority rather than through the district Collector and the reference court. Sectoral statutes delivered that. The National Highways Act proceeds by a notification of intention under Section 3A, a hearing of objections under Section 3C confined to the use of the land for the notified purpose, a declaration under Section 3D on which the land vests in the Central Government, and determination of the amount by a competent authority under Section 3G, with an arbitrator appointed by the Central Government under Section 3G(5) for a party dissatisfied with that amount. The Railways Act chapter for special projects follows a similar pattern of intention, objection, declaration and competent authority award. The Coal Bearing Areas Act moves from prospecting notification to declaration to vesting in the Central Government or a government company. In each case the acquisition is designed as an administrative process internal to the executing department, with shorter intervals, narrower objection rights and a compensation determination that, before 2015, typically tracked the 1894 principles without the general law's developing floor of precedent, and with no rehabilitation obligation anywhere in the code.

### How many laws can govern a single highway project?

At least three. The National Highways Act, 1956 supplies the procedure, the competent authority and the arbitrator. The compensation and rehabilitation entitlements come, since the 2015 extension, from the First, Second and Third Schedules of the 2013 Act. Land records and title underneath remain governed by state revenue law.

The layering is not theoretical. A single alignment crosses survey numbers whose records sit in different settlement operations, tenancies recorded under different state codes and, in places, forest land that brings the Forest Rights Act into the file as a fourth layer. The competent authority's award speaks to compensation under one layer while possession, title correction and mutation proceed under others, which is why highway acquisition files so often outlive the road they were opened for.

The safeguard gap that this design produced is the field's central fact, and it should be stated plainly rather than rhetorically. Under the general law after 1984, an objector at least had the Section 5A hearing unless urgency was invoked, the award was made by a Collector institutionally distinct from the project, dissatisfaction carried a reference to a court, and solatium and interest were fixed by statute. Under several of the special codes, the hearing was narrower, the award-maker was the project's own authority, the appellate route ran to an arbitrator appointed by the acquiring government or to the district court only on limited grounds, and nothing in the code required the project to ask what the displacement would do to the people displaced. Nobody enacted this gap as a single decision. It accumulated, statute by statute, as each sector obtained a code written by its own ministry for its own convenience, and the general law's protections quietly ceased to be the standard for the largest takings. The special economic zones episode illustrates the same pattern from outside the schedule: the Special Economic Zones Act, 2005 created no acquisition machinery of its own, yet in practice zone land was assembled by state governments and their industrial authorities using the general law or state industrial statutes, frequently through the urgency route, so the displacement burden of the zones programme was carried by instruments that the zones legislation never had to defend. The lesson generalises. In this field, the statute that authorises the project is rarely the statute that does the taking, and accountability for the taking lives in whichever acquisition code the project is routed through.

One more acquisition power sits outside both the general law and the Fourth Schedule and completes the ring: defence. Land for defence purposes has historically been acquired under the general law, requisitioned under the Requisitioning and Acquisition of Immovable Property Act, 1952, or restricted under the Works of Defence Act, 1903, which imposes building and use restrictions around defence installations without taking title. The cantonment and defence estates layer is a specialist subject in itself; on this map it is enough to mark that military land sits in its own pocket of the field, as it does in most legal systems, and that acquisitions there attract the urgency and security exceptions of every general regime, including the narrowed urgency clause of Section 40 of the 2013 Act, which lists defence first among the purposes for which the urgency power survives.

Why did Parliament, having written the 2013 settlement, exempt the ring from it? The stated reason was continuity: the scheduled statutes governed live national programmes, and subjecting them overnight to social impact assessment and consent would, the government argued, stall projects mid-execution. The structural reason runs deeper, and it is the reason the exemption took the shape it did. Section 105 did not simply exempt the thirteen Acts. It exempted them subject to a duty. Sub-section (3) of Section 105 required the Central Government, by notification within one year from the commencement of the Act, to direct that the provisions of the 2013 Act relating to the determination of compensation in accordance with the First Schedule, and rehabilitation and resettlement in accordance with the Second and Third Schedules, being beneficial to affected families, shall apply to acquisitions under the scheduled enactments, with such exceptions or modifications as do not reduce compensation or dilute those provisions. Parliament's design was therefore a split: procedure would remain with the sectoral codes, but the money and the resettlement, the two things a displaced family actually receives, would be equalised across the whole field by executive notification within twelve months. Whether that design was a bridge or a burial ground for the reform depended entirely on the notification, which is why the notification's history is the most instructive episode in the modern law of this field.

## Urgency across the regimes: the speed variable

Every acquisition regime must answer a question of tempo: how quickly may the state move from decision to possession when it believes delay defeats the purpose? The answers map the regimes' philosophies as sharply as their compensation formulas do. The 1894 Act's answer was the urgency clause of Section 17, under which the appropriate government could direct that possession be taken fifteen days after the Section 9 notice, before any award, and could direct that the Section 5A hearing not apply; in exchange, the statute required tender of eighty per cent of the estimated compensation before possession, a safeguard added by the amendment of 1984. Drafted for famine relief works, defence and genuine emergencies, the clause was progressively normalised until, in the decades before repeal, routine road and industrial acquisitions were being run through it as a matter of administrative habit, and the landowner's one hearing had become optional in practice. The judicial response was confined to insisting that the urgency be real and the direction reasoned, and governments learned to record urgency in formulaic terms that review rarely disturbed.

The 2013 Act's answer was to make urgency exceptional in text: Section 40 confines the power to defence of India or national security and to emergencies arising out of natural calamity, requires the additional conditions the section imposes, and withholds the power from the ordinary run of projects altogether. The special statutes give a third answer, the most consequential of the three: they build the speed into the ordinary process, so that no urgency direction is needed. Short objection windows, hearings confined to a single issue, vesting on declaration and possession on the authority's timetable are not emergency departures in the sectoral codes; they are the standard procedure, available for every project however routine. A reader comparing the regimes should therefore treat tempo as a routing consequence, not a circumstance: the same project acquires a hearing-rich, urgency-poor procedure under the general law and a hearing-poor, permanently urgent procedure under the sectoral code, and the government's choice of instrument is, among other things, a choice of how fast it may lawfully move against the landowner's resistance. The political salience of that choice explains a pattern any observer of the field will recognise, that governments defend the sectoral statutes most fiercely precisely when a project is contested, because contest is when tempo is worth most.

## The extension: how an order did the work of a statute

The one year given by Section 105(3) ran out on 1 January 2015, and the deadline was met, formally, by the ordinance route. On 31 December 2014 the President promulgated an amendment ordinance which, among wider changes that Section 105 had nothing to do with, substituted a new sub-section (3) into Section 105 declaring that the compensation and rehabilitation provisions of the 2013 Act shall apply to the enactments in the Fourth Schedule with effect from 1 January 2015. The wider ordinance, which proposed to exempt five categories of project, defence, rural infrastructure, affordable housing, industrial corridors and infrastructure and social infrastructure projects, from the consent and social impact assessment chapters of the Act, became the centre of a major political confrontation. The amendment bill passed the Lok Sabha and stalled in the Rajya Sabha, where the government lacked a majority. The ordinance was re-promulgated twice. In August 2015 the government announced that it would allow the ordinance to lapse, which it did on 31 August 2015, and the episode entered political history as the occasion on which the opposition's campaign against a supposed pro-corporate land grab forced a governing majority in the lower House to abandon a signature reform; the ordinance and its failure are the subject of a separate article in this cluster and are traced here only to the point the map requires.

The point the map requires is what happened to the Fourth Schedule extension when the ordinance died. It survived, by a different instrument. On 28 August 2015, three days before the lapse, the Central Government issued the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement (Removal of Difficulties) Order, 2015, published as S.O. 2368(E) and brought into force on 1 September 2015. The Order was made under Section 113 of the 2013 Act, the power to remove difficulties in giving effect to the Act, which was available for two years from commencement and therefore still open. Its operative paragraph is short: the provisions of the 2013 Act relating to the determination of compensation in accordance with the First Schedule, rehabilitation and resettlement in accordance with the Second Schedule and infrastructure amenities in accordance with the Third Schedule shall apply to all cases of land acquisition under the enactments specified in the Fourth Schedule. The route by which the extension arrived deserves a moment's attention, because it exposes the fragility built into the original design. Section 105(4) had wrapped the promised notification in parliamentary discipline: a draft of every notification under sub-section (3) was to be laid before each House for thirty days, and if both Houses disapproved or modified it, the notification was not to issue or was to issue only as modified. That discipline was never used, because the extension never travelled as a Section 105(3) notification at all. It travelled first as an ordinance amending the section itself, which no laying requirement governed, and finally as a removal of difficulties order under Section 113, a power drafted for smoothing commencement wrinkles, time-limited to two years, and exercised here to carry the substantive heart of the reform across the schedule boundary months after the statutory deadline for the designed route had passed. Lawyers argued at the time, and the argument is preserved in the litigation record, about whether a removal of difficulties power may properly do work of that magnitude; the order was nonetheless acted on across the acquiring ministries, compensation was computed under it, and no authoritative ruling displaced it. The episode's lesson for readers of Indian legislation generalises: where a statute delegates its own completion to executive action under a deadline, the deadline will be met by whatever instrument lies nearest to hand when the politics of the designed instrument fails, and the durability of the result will depend on acquiescence and enforcement rather than on the elegance of its legal pedigree.

From 1 September 2015, therefore, a family displaced by a national highway, a railway project, a coal block or a metro line became entitled to the 2013 Act's compensation arithmetic and its rehabilitation entitlements even though the acquisition itself proceeded, and proceeds, under the sectoral statute.

### Why are the compensation question and the procedure question different after 2015?

Because the extension was deliberately partial. The 2015 Order carried the First, Second and Third Schedules of the 2013 Act into the thirteen scheduled statutes, equalising what a displaced family receives, but it carried none of the 2013 procedure: no assessment, no consent, no independent appraisal entered the sectoral codes.

The sectoral codes' own notifications, confined objection hearings and competent authorities therefore continue to govern how the taking is done, while a different statute's schedules govern what the taking must pay. Practitioners describe the result as one acquisition with two legal personalities, and challenges are framed accordingly: procedure is attacked under the sectoral code, while quantum and resettlement are claimed under the 2013 schedules, in the same proceeding or in parallel ones.

That partiality is the mature shape of the field, and assessing it fairly requires holding two truths together. In favour of the extension, it achieved by a three-paragraph order the single most valuable equalisation in the system: the money. A highway acquisition after September 2015 cannot lawfully pay the old sectoral rates; the multiplier, the solatium and the rehabilitation package follow the family across the schedule boundary, and the litigation since has largely concerned authorities that applied the extension grudgingly or late, not its validity. Against the extension, it froze into place the procedural inequality that the 2013 Act was enacted to abolish. The family on the highway alignment receives 2013 money through a 1956 procedure: no assessment of whether the alignment should run through their village at all, no consent threshold however private the project, no independent appraisal, and an objection hearing confined to the use of the land for the notified purpose. The namable claim of this article is therefore precise. The general law is the exception in Indian acquisition not because it is rarely used but because, for most of the field's history, the largest takings lived outside it, and when reform came, the instrument that did the practical work of unifying the field was not the celebrated statute of 2013 but the unglamorous order of 28 August 2015, which unified the entitlements while leaving the procedures, where the power imbalance actually operates, exactly where the sectoral statutes had put them.

## The state layer: state statutes, state amendments and the assent route

Below the central map lies the state layer, and for a large share of acquisitions by volume it is the only layer that matters. State industrial development corporations acquire under state industrial Acts, of which the Maharashtra Industrial Development Act, 1961 is the prototype: land is notified for an industrial area, objections are heard by the state's own officers, the land vests in the state and is transferred to the development corporation, and the compensation machinery historically borrowed the principles of the general law while running on the state's own procedure. The Karnataka Industrial Areas Development Act, 1966 follows the same pattern, and most industrialising states maintain an equivalent. State highways Acts perform the same service for state roads that the National Highways Act performs for the national network. Housing boards, urban development authorities and town planning schemes add a further register of instruments, some acquiring outright and some, under town planning legislation of the Maharashtra pattern, appropriating land through the scheme itself with compensation adjusted inside the scheme's accounts. None of these instruments appears in the Fourth Schedule, because the Fourth Schedule lists central enactments. Their exclusion from the 2013 Act runs the other way: the 2013 Act is a central law on a concurrent subject, and state acquisition statutes survive alongside it where they can operate without repugnancy, or under cover of presidential assent where they cannot.

The states also used the amendment route against the central law itself, and the two mechanisms they used mark the constitutional geography exactly. The first is Section 105A, inserted into the 2013 Act by state amendment with presidential assent under Article 254(2), which permits a state to list its own enactments in a Fifth Schedule and exempt acquisitions under them from the Act. Tamil Nadu moved first, inserting Section 105A and a Fifth Schedule covering three state enactments, the Tamil Nadu Acquisition of Land for Harijan Welfare Schemes Act, 1978, the Tamil Nadu Acquisition of Land for Industrial Purposes Act, 1997 and the Tamil Nadu Highways Act, 2001, with the amendment expressed to operate from the commencement of the central Act. Maharashtra followed the same route for the Maharashtra Highways Act. The second mechanism is direct amendment of the 2013 Act's operation in the state: Gujarat's amendment Act of 2016, which received presidential assent, exempted specified categories of project from the social impact assessment and consent chapters for acquisitions in that state; other states, Telangana among them, pursued their own assent-backed amendments in the same period, and several more drafted them. The general information in this article is a map, not advice on any individual acquisition, and any reader tracing a live taking must identify the state instrument in play, because the differences these amendments make are differences in whether an assessment happens and whether consent is asked at all.

Repugnancy doctrine polices the boundary. Where a state acquisition law and the central law occupy the same ground and collide, Article 254 makes the central law prevail to the extent of the repugnancy unless the state law was reserved and assented to, in which case the state law prevails in that state until Parliament legislates again. The assent route is therefore not a loophole but a designed feature of concurrent legislation, the Constitution's own mechanism for letting a state depart from a central concurrent law with the Union's countersignature. Practically, it means the field cannot be mapped from Delhi alone. The correct statement of the law for a given parcel is a function of the project, the acquiring authority and the state, and any account that reports only the central position has answered two-thirds of the question.

## Industrial land: the state channel in operation

Because industry is where acquisition politics burns hottest, the state industrial channel deserves a closer look than its place in the roster suggests. The pattern statute, Maharashtra's Act of 1961, creates a development corporation, declares an area as an industrial area, and then acquires the land within it through a state-run chapter: notice to owners, a hearing of objections by an officer of the state, a declaration, vesting in the state government free of encumbrances on payment or tender of compensation, and transfer to the corporation, which develops and allots the plots. Compensation inside that chapter was modelled on the 1894 principles, and the procedure was designed to deliver a consolidated, litigation-cleared estate to industry in one operation. The Tamil Nadu Acquisition of Land for Industrial Purposes Act, 1997 refines the same pattern with an expedited single-notification process, which is why Tamil Nadu placed it, with its highways Act and its Harijan welfare schemes Act, in the Fifth Schedule under Section 105A when the 2013 Act threatened to overlay the state machinery with the central assessment and consent chapters. Karnataka's industrial areas legislation and the Gujarat industrial development framework follow the family design. The cumulative effect is that a very large share of the land Indian industry actually occupies was never acquired under the general central law at all, and the terms on which it was taken, the hearing given, the compensation paid, the absence of rehabilitation, were set by state codes that the national argument about the 2013 Act barely mentioned. Land banks, the inventories of pre-acquired land that state corporations advertise to investors, are the visible residue of this channel: land taken in an earlier decade under a state code, held by the corporation, and allotted on terms the original owners had no part in setting. Any account of Indian acquisition that confines itself to the central statutes will mislocate the industry story entirely, which is the federal structure of Entry 42 expressing itself in practice.

## How the field acquired this shape: the historical sequence

The map described so far is a sediment. Each layer was laid down by a phase of the Indian state's development, and the phases explain why the layers do not match. The first phase is colonial and extractive in a specific sense: the early acquisition instruments served public works that the colonial state valued, roads, canals, cantonments and railways, and the legislative line runs from Bengal Regulation I of 1824, which authorised the taking of land for roads, canals and other public purposes on payment of compensation assessed by arbitrators, through the general Acts of 1857 and 1870 to the consolidating Act of 1894. The 1894 Act's design choices, government-controlled process, compensation at recorded market value, no rehabilitation, were not oversights. They were the choices of a state that wanted land cheaply for works it had decided on, and they survived independence intact because the successor state wanted the same thing for the same kind of works.

The second phase is the developmental republic. The Constitution's property guarantee was cut down, through the amendments described in the constitutional frame above, principally to clear the way for land reform, the abolition of zamindari and the imposition of ceilings, and the same decades saw the great displacement projects of the planned economy: the river valley schemes of which the Damodar Valley Corporation, created by its own Act in 1948, was the template, the steel towns, the irrigation barrages and the expanding highway and rail networks. Two features of this phase built the modern map. The acquiring state reached instinctively for the sectoral instrument, a corporation Act here, a highways Act there, a coal Act in 1957, so the ring of special statutes is substantially a creation of the planning era. And rehabilitation was handled, where it was handled at all, as an administrative welfare measure attached to particular projects rather than as a legal entitlement, so its quality depended on the project's budget and the state's conscience, and the displaced of the early projects, many of them tribal communities in the submergence zones, received the least. The record of that failure, documented by official committees and by the movements that formed around the Sardar Sarovar project on the Narmada in the 1980s and 1990s, is the factual foundation of the displacement framing tested later in this article.

The third phase is the amendment of the general law without structural change. The amendment of 1984 raised the solatium to thirty per cent, tightened interest and imposed time limits on the award, a real improvement in the money terms that left the process untouched. Through the 1990s the general law continued to serve the expanding infrastructure programme, and the urgency clause carried more and more of the load as governments discovered that Section 17 could convert the Act into a possession-first instrument whenever a project was politically urgent. The fourth phase is the conflict decade that made the old settlement untenable. Economic liberalisation shifted acquisition's centre of gravity from the state's own works to land assembled for private and partnership industry, and the spectacle of a colonial statute being used to displace cultivators for factories and real estate detonated politically: the resistance at Singur in West Bengal, where land acquired for the Tata Motors small car plant was abandoned after sustained protest, the violence at Nandigram in 2007 over a proposed chemical hub and special economic zone, the agitation at Bhatta Parsaul in Uttar Pradesh in 2011, and long campaigns against mining and industrial projects in Odisha and Chhattisgarh together converted land acquisition from an administrative subject into a first-order national controversy. The United Progressive Alliance government answered first with a national rehabilitation policy in 2007, an executive policy without statutory force, and then with legislation: bills introduced in 2007 lapsed with the Lok Sabha, a revised bill followed in 2011, and after standing committee scrutiny and a long parliamentary passage the 2013 Act emerged. The fifth phase, the attempted rollback of 2014 and 2015 and its failure, followed by the equalisation of entitlements through the 2015 Order and the state amendments, has been described above. The field a reader faces is the sum of these phases: a colonial general code replaced only in 2014, a planning-era ring of sectoral codes never repealed, a reform statute whose reach was negotiated down at birth and then partially restored by executive order, and a state layer that continues to legislate. Sediment explains incoherence better than design does.

## Acquisition and requisition: the taking that must be given back

Entry 42 of the Concurrent List couples two powers that the brief for any map of this field must separate, because confusing them produces real-world error. Acquisition extinguishes the owner's title permanently and vests the property in the acquiring state or its transferee. Requisition takes possession and use for a limited period while title remains with the owner, and it carries an obligation to return the property when the need ends, with compensation assessed not as the price of the thing but as payment for the period of deprivation, in substance akin to a compulsory rent together with compensation for damage. A requisitioned building is the state's for the duration; an acquired building is the state's outright. The distinction controls everything practical: the measure of money, the owner's residual rights, the duty to restore, and the point at which a supposedly temporary taking that never ends becomes a colourable acquisition that courts will treat as requiring acquisition law's fuller payment.

The principal central instrument is the Requisitioning and Acquisition of Immovable Property Act, 1952, which appears in the Fourth Schedule because it contains both powers: it empowers the Central Government to requisition property for a public purpose, on notice to the owner, with compensation determined under its own principles and disputes referred to arbitration, and it separately empowers acquisition, of property already requisitioned in defined circumstances or of property needed for a public purpose, on payment of its value. The Act's history carries the doctrine's warning. Requisitioning on a large scale began under wartime defence powers, and the peacetime statute was originally temporary, renewed repeatedly, because legislatures were uneasy about a standing power to take possession without taking title; requisitioned premises in the great cities remained with governments for decades, and the litigation the Act generated is largely about the duty to release property once the purpose is spent and about owners' claims when release never came. The working rule that emerges from the scheme is that requisition is legitimate as a bridge, for an emergency, a temporary camp, a time-bound need, and illegitimate as a substitute for acquisition chosen because it is cheaper. State requisition statutes occupy the same conceptual ground in their territories. On the map, requisition is the field's fourth route alongside the general law, the special statutes and the state instruments, distinguished not by the identity of the taker but by the nature of what is taken: possession for a time, not ownership forever. The family of requisition powers is wider than the 1952 Act: state requisition statutes operate in their territories for housing and public purposes, and modern emergency legislation carries its own versions, of which the requisition power in Section 65 of the Disaster Management Act, 2005, authorising the requisition of resources, premises and vehicles needed for rescue and relief operations, is the most contemporary instance, used at scale during the pandemic period for hospitals, quarantine facilities and transport. The doctrinal continuity across these instruments is exact: the emergency justifies the speed and the informality of the taking, and the temporariness is the constitutional price of that informality, so that every requisition regime carries, in text or in judicial reading, the release obligation and the periodic measure of compensation that separate a lawful requisition from a disguised acquisition.

## The routes that are not compulsion: negotiated purchase and pooling

A map of acquisition law that ended with compulsion would misdescribe how much Indian project land is assembled, and the alternatives matter to the routing question because a landowner approached by the state must first establish whether compulsion is even in play. The first alternative is negotiated purchase. Nothing in the acquisition statutes prevents a government or an authority from buying land by agreement at a negotiated price, and several governments have adopted direct purchase as policy for projects where acquisition litigation was expected to cost more in time than the price premium cost in money: the purchasing authority negotiates through committees at district level, pays a price at or above the statutory formula as an inducement, and takes a registered conveyance, so that title passes by contract and the acquisition code never opens. For the landowner the legal character of the encounter changes completely: no notification, no objection right, no award, and equally no compulsion, no entitlement to the statutory multiplier by right, and no remedy except to refuse and wait for the state to choose between raising the offer and switching to acquisition. The second alternative is land pooling or readjustment, in which owners surrender their parcels into a scheme and receive back a smaller, serviced, more valuable reconstituted plot, with the scheme financing its infrastructure from the land value uplift and from reserved plots sold by the authority. Town planning schemes of this kind have a long statutory history in Maharashtra and Gujarat, and the most prominent recent deployment was the assembly of the Andhra Pradesh capital at Amaravati under the land pooling scheme framed under the Andhra Pradesh Capital Region Development Authority Act, 2014, which pooled tens of thousands of acres by agreement with landowners who received developed plots and annuity payments rather than cash compensation. Pooling's legal signature is consent structured by statute: the scheme is compulsory in its planning consequences once operative, but participation is induced rather than imposed, and the owner's return is equity in the project rather than a price for exit. Both alternatives appear in the decision table below as the private and negotiated route, because for routing purposes what defines them is the absence of the acquisition code and the presence of a bargain, however unequal the bargaining positions may be.

## The machinery across regimes: who decides, and where a dispute goes

Regimes differ most concretely in their machinery, and a reader routing an acquisition should ask three mechanical questions of any regime: who hears the objection, who fixes the money, and where does a dispute travel. Under the 1894 Act the answers were the government itself on the report of its officer under Section 5A, the Collector as award-maker, and the district court on reference, an institutional separation between project and award that was the old law's one structural virtue. The Collector in that scheme, the district's chief revenue officer acting in a statutory capacity, conducted the inquiry, heard claims, apportionment disputes and measurement objections, and pronounced the award; the reference under Section 18 then put quantum before a judge. Under the 2013 Act the Collector remains the process officer, conducting the assessment's administrative stages, the census of affected families and the award, but the dispute forum changed: objections to the award go to the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority, a dedicated forum presided over by a person qualified for judicial office, with an appeal to the High Court, a redesign meant to move acquisition disputes out of the ordinary civil courts' backlog while keeping a judicial appeal. Rehabilitation administration gained its own officers, an Administrator and a Commissioner for rehabilitation and resettlement at state level, charged with the formulation and implementation of the resettlement schemes that the award promises.

### Who is the Collector in land acquisition, and why does the office recur in every regime?

The Collector is the district's senior revenue officer acting in a statutory capacity under the acquisition code in force. Every regime needs a local officer who can identify the land, notify the interested persons, hear claims and pronounce an award, and the district administration is the only machinery that touches every parcel.

What regimes change is everything around the office. The 1894 Act sent disputes from the Collector's award to the district court on reference; the 2013 Act sends them to a dedicated Authority and gives the Collector the assessment and census machinery to run; the special statutes dispense with the Collector's independence altogether and hand the functions to a competent authority appointed for the project. The office's persistence, and its variable independence, together explain much of the field's lived character.

The special statutes answer the three mechanical questions differently, and the differences are the safeguard gap in institutional form. The National Highways Act concentrates the process in a competent authority appointed by the Central Government, typically a revenue officer deputed to the project, who hears the confined objection, declares the vesting and determines the amount; the dissatisfied party's route is not a court reference but an arbitrator appointed by the Central Government, the very party acquiring, with the arbitration then governed by the arbitration law and challengeable on its narrow grounds. The Railways Act's special projects chapter replicates the pattern. The Coal Bearing Areas Act routes compensation disputes to a tribunal constituted for the purpose. In each pattern the award-maker sits closer to the project than the Collector of the general law did, the objection is confined to narrower grounds than Section 5A allowed, and the appellate forum is weaker than a court. None of this is accidental, and none of it was altered by the 2015 extension, which changed the entitlements these authorities must award but not who they are or how they are reviewed. When practitioners say that the special statutes are faster, this machinery is what they mean: fewer independent hands touch the file between notification and possession.

A worked comparison shows what the routing is worth in money, and it is worth doing with one hypothetical parcel carried through the regimes, using the statutory formulas and no invented facts. Take agricultural land with a determined market value of twenty lakh rupees, acquired in a rural area where the state has fixed the maximum multiplier of two. Under the 1894 Act, the award would have been the market value of twenty lakh, plus solatium at thirty per cent, six lakh, a total of twenty-six lakh before interest, and nothing for the family's resettlement, because the Act did not know the family as a unit of entitlement at all. Under a special statute before September 2015, the award would have tracked similar principles through the competent authority, commonly without even the discipline of the reference court's accumulated case law, and likewise without rehabilitation. Under the same special statute after the 2015 Order, the First Schedule arithmetic applies: twenty lakh multiplied by two gives forty lakh, solatium at one hundred per cent adds forty lakh, and the award stands at eighty lakh, with the Second Schedule's rehabilitation entitlements, housing, subsistence, choice of land or employment or annuity, added on top for displaced families. Under the 2013 Act directly, the same eighty lakh and the same entitlements result, but reached through the assessment and consent procedure the special statute never conducts. The family's money therefore depends on when and under which code the taking occurs, in a ratio of roughly one to three between the old world and the new, which is why the routing question with which this article opened is not a lawyer's refinement. It is the difference between the regimes' whole practical content.

## The interfaces: forest rights, Scheduled Areas and parallel clearances

The acquisition map does not exhaust the law that governs a taking, because two further bodies of law attach to particular lands and override or condition the acquisition codes, and a routing exercise that ignores them will misfire on exactly the lands where displacement is most contested. The first is the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, the Forest Rights Act. Where the land to be taken is forest land or land on which forest rights are claimed, the rights recognised by that Act, individual cultivation rights, community rights, and the community right to protect and manage community forest resources, must be recognised and settled, and the diversion of forest land for a project requires, under the framework the Supreme Court enforced in the Niyamgiri decision, Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment and Forests in 2013, that the Gram Sabha of the affected village determine the presence of rights, including religious and cultural rights of the community, before the diversion proceeds. The Niyamgiri Gram Sabhas' rejection of the bauxite mining proposal demonstrated that this is a substantive gate and not a consultation ritual. The Forest Rights Act is not an acquisition statute and appears in no schedule, but on forest land it operates as a condition precedent layer over every acquisition regime, general and special alike.

The second interface is the law of Scheduled Areas. The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 requires consultation with the Gram Sabha or the panchayat before acquisition of land in the Scheduled Areas for development projects and before resettlement of persons affected by such projects, and the 2013 Act hardened the corresponding protection inside acquisition law itself through Section 41, under which acquisition in Scheduled Areas is to be avoided as far as possible, taken only as a demonstrable last resort, and made conditional on the prior consent of the Gram Sabha or the autonomous council concerned, with a development plan and enhanced entitlements for displaced Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe families. A third interface, not a rights layer but a parallel approval track, is environmental clearance: a project large enough to need acquisition usually also needs clearance under the environment impact assessment notification issued under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and the two processes proceed in parallel with different authorities, different hearings and different challenge forums, so that a project may survive acquisition litigation and fall on clearance, or the reverse. The map is therefore three-dimensional at its edges: the acquisition regime supplies the taking, the rights statutes condition it on particular lands, and the clearance regime conditions the project that the taking serves.

## Reading a notification: how a taking announces its regime

Every compulsory taking announces itself in a document, and the document names its regime for a reader who knows where to look. The preliminary notification, whether the Section 4 notification of the old general law, the corresponding notification under the 2013 Act's assessment chapter, Section 3A of the National Highways Act or the prospecting notification of the coal Act, recites the statute under which it is issued in its opening words, identifies the acquiring authority and the purpose, and describes the land by survey number. The recital is the routing answer. A notification that invokes a Fourth Schedule statute tells the reader that the procedure will be sectoral and that the entitlements, after the 2015 extension, will be drawn from the 2013 schedules. A notification under the 2013 Act tells the reader to look for the assessment study, the public hearing record and, for private or partnership proponents, the consent documentation. A notification under a state industrial Act tells the reader that the state code's own objections, vesting and compensation provisions govern and that the central Act enters, if at all, through the assent and repugnancy analysis. Two further features of the paper repay attention: the purpose recited, which determines whether the public purpose limb is even arguable and whether the company route should have been used instead, and the urgency recital, whose presence signals that the hearing stage has been compressed or removed and whose absence under the 2013 Act is guaranteed except for the narrowed Section 40 categories. Advisers who work in this field read the notification before they read anything else, and landowners who receive one hold, in its first paragraph, the answer to the question this article exists to answer.

## The commons problem: the land the map's categories miss

Every regime in this field keys its obligations to interests in land, and a large share of rural India's productive landscape is held in forms that interest-based categories capture badly: village commons, grazing lands, ponds, threshing grounds, pathways and culturable waste recorded in revenue papers as government land, poramboke, gauchar or shamilat, used by the whole village and owned, in the record's language, by the state or the panchayat. When such land is allotted or transferred for a project, the acquisition codes may never be triggered at all, because in the state's own account no private property is being taken: the transaction is an internal reassignment of government land, effected by government order, with no notification, no objection, no award and no compensation to anyone. Yet the economic event is identical to an acquisition for the families who grazed their animals, drew water, held the weekly market or dried their grain on the land taken. The commons problem is therefore the map's known blind spot, and it has generated its own corrective doctrines at the margins. Courts have repeatedly protected village commons against encroachment and diversion on the ground that such lands are held for the community's use and cannot be alienated by executive convenience, directing restoration of illegally occupied ponds and grazing lands in a line of authority of which the Punjab and Haryana litigation over village ponds is representative. The 2013 Act partially internalised the problem by counting among affected families those whose livelihoods depend on common resources and by valuing common property assets within the assessment, and the Forest Rights Act's community rights, including the right to protect and regenerate community forest resources, gave forest-fringe commons a statutory personality they had lacked. But the structural position remains: the closer land stands to the recorded commons end of the spectrum, the more the taking of it escapes the acquisition map entirely and must be fought in the law of records, panchayats and public trust instead. A complete account of which law governs a taking must therefore begin one step earlier than the notification, with the revenue record, because the record's classification of the land decides whether acquisition law is even invited to the event.

## The perimeter of the general law: exclusions inside the settlement

The Fourth Schedule is the 2013 Act's famous exclusion, but the settlement's perimeter is also drawn inside the Act, and a complete map marks the internal boundaries. The Act governs acquisition for public purposes as enumerated, and its machinery is calibrated to the proponent: the consent chapters bite only where a private company or a partnership is the beneficiary, so the largest class of takings, acquisition by government for its own use, proceeds without any consent stage at all, a design choice that the Act's defenders justified by democratic accountability and its critics attacked as the settlement's central concession. The assessment chapter itself yields at the edges: acquisitions under the Section 40 urgency power proceed without it, and the enacted scheme coordinates the social assessment with the environmental impact assessment that irrigation and other regulated projects undergo under environmental law, rather than stacking two full studies on the same footprint. The Act's application to land already owned by government, to transfers between government departments and to the vast category of land recorded as government or village common land in revenue records, raises a further boundary question that litigation continues to work out: acquisition law is triggered by the taking of private interests, and where the state asserts the land is already its own, the conflict moves out of acquisition law entirely into title and revenue law, which is one reason disputes over commons and grazing lands so often surface as record correction cases rather than acquisition references. Finally, the Act left its own schedule open: the Central Government holds a power to add enactments to or omit them from the Fourth Schedule by notification, subject to the parliamentary laying discipline the Act prescribes, so the perimeter of the exemption is itself delegated and adjustable, a quiet provision that means the map's central boundary can be redrawn by the executive without fresh primary legislation, and that any reader relying on the schedule should treat it as a living instrument rather than a fixed wall.

## Consent and assessment as routing variables

Two requirements divide the modern field more sharply than any others, because they exist in only one regime: the social impact assessment and the consent of affected families. Under the 2013 Act, the assessment is the default opening of every acquisition, a study conducted under Sections 4 to 9 through an independent agency in consultation with the panchayat or municipality, published in the local language, appraised by an Expert Group drawn from outside the acquiring department, two non-official social scientists, two experts on rehabilitation and a technical expert among its members, with power to say that the project should not proceed on that land at all. Consent then operates asymmetrically: eighty per cent of affected families for a private company, seventy per cent for a public private partnership, none for the government's own acquisitions. The design philosophy is visible: the closer the proponent is to a private beneficiary, the more the process shifts from administrative decision toward negotiated legitimacy. The special statutes contain neither requirement in any form, and the 2015 extension did not import them. A private developer whose plant is reached through a state industrial Act or whose raw material moves on a railway siding acquired under the Railways Act faces no consent threshold from acquisition law however private the benefit, while the same developer acquiring directly under the 2013 Act must carry four-fifths of the affected families. This asymmetry, more than the money, is what the failed 2015 amendment bill sought to level downward and what state amendments in Gujarat and elsewhere levelled within their territories by exempting listed project categories from the assessment and consent chapters: the political contest over Indian acquisition law since 2013 is, in substance, a contest over these two routing variables, fought state by state and sector by sector because the central amendment failed.

The assessment requirement also connects acquisition law to a question the older law never asked: whether the project should take this land at all. The 1894 Act asked what the land was worth; its Section 5A objection, where it survived, asked whether the land was suited to the purpose; neither asked what the taking would do to the people on the land or whether the public benefit justified the social cost. The Expert Group's power to recommend against proceeding, the statutory direction that irrigated multi-cropped land be taken only as a demonstrable last resort and within a ceiling the state must fix, and the requirement that the assessment identify the families whose livelihoods, not merely whose titles, the project extinguishes, together convert the opening stage of acquisition from a notification ritual into the one moment at which the project's siting is contestable on evidence. The special statutes contain no equivalent moment: the highways objection under Section 3C is confined to the use of the land for the notified purpose and is heard by the project's own authority. A reader comparing regimes should therefore treat the assessment not as paperwork added to the front of the process but as the relocation of the real decision, from the award, where only price is arguable, to the threshold, where the taking itself is.

## Who counts as affected: the entitlement unit across regimes

The deepest difference between the regimes is not procedural at all. It is the answer each gives to the question of whose loss the law recognises. The 1894 Act recognised persons interested in the land, a category built from title and legally cognisable interest: the owner, the mortgagee, the lessee, the holder of an easement, each entitled to a share of the compensation apportioned according to interest. Persons whose livelihoods depended on the land without an interest in it, the agricultural labourer, the sharecropper without recorded rights, the artisan, the grazier, the family member working the fields, stood outside the award, and the celebrated injustices of Indian displacement are substantially the working out of that definition across lakhs of families whose relationship to the land was real but not proprietary. The special statutes inherited the same proprietary unit, usually in narrower form.

The 2013 Act rebuilt the unit. Its operative concept is the affected family, defined in Section 3 to include not only landowners but families whose primary source of livelihood stands affected by the acquisition: agricultural labourers, tenants, sharecroppers, artisans and others working in the affected area for the qualifying period before acquisition, families whose primary source of livelihood for the qualifying period was dependent on forests or water bodies, including gatherers, hunters and fisher folk, and beneficiaries of government assignment of land under land distribution schemes. Rehabilitation entitlements attach to the family so defined, while compensation for the land attaches to title, so the Act runs two registers at once, a property register for the price and a livelihood register for the resettlement, and the census and the assessment exist largely to populate the second register before the award. This is why the extension of the Second Schedule to the Fourth Schedule statutes mattered beyond its money value: it carried the livelihood register, at least as an entitlement on paper, into highways and railways acquisitions whose own codes recognised only owners. It is also where implementation most visibly strains, because identifying livelihood losers through a census in a sectoral acquisition run by a project authority, without the assessment machinery that the 2013 Act built for the purpose, asks the weakest machinery in the field to perform its hardest task. The gap between the entitlement and its delivery, which the implementation literature on highway and coal acquisitions documents repeatedly, is the live frontier of the field and the strongest factual support the displacement framing retains after 2015.

## The price of land: how the formulas construct market value

Compensation across the regimes turns on a shared fiction that the map should expose, because the fiction explains the formulas. Every regime begins from market value, and market value in Indian land transactions is a constructed number, not an observed one. Consideration in registered sale deeds is systematically understated because stamp duty and capital gains tax ride on the stated price, so the documentary record from which the Collector must construct value understates the real market by a margin every practitioner acknowledges and no statute admits. The 1894 Act compounded the problem by freezing value at the Section 4 notification date and excluding, through Section 24, the very appreciation the project itself would create, so the owner was paid the stale, understated price of the land as farmland on the day before the highway was announced. The thirty per cent solatium was the system's rough acknowledgement that the number was not the whole loss: it compensated, in the statute's own words, for the compulsory nature of the acquisition, though not for livelihood, disturbance or the cost of re-establishing a life elsewhere.

The 2013 Act attacked the construction at three points, and the design is more intelligent than the public argument usually credits. Section 26 directs the Collector to adopt the highest of three measures: the value specified for stamp duty purposes in the area, the average sale price of similar land in the vicinity as shown by the higher half of the sale deeds of the preceding three years, and, where land is acquired for a private company or a partnership, the consented amount agreed with the proponent. The stamp duty or circle rate measure supplies a floor the deed record cannot drag below the state's own fiscal valuation, and the higher-half rule corrects the average for the understatement pattern. The First Schedule multiplier then applies a policy judgment on top of the corrected value: the further a rural parcel lies from urban influence, the higher the factor, up to two, on the theory that recorded rural values are the most understated and rural livelihoods the least replaceable. Solatium at one hundred per cent doubles the result again, and the additional amount of twelve per cent per annum from notification to award compensates the interval during which the owner is frozen out of the market while the process runs. Interest provisions police delay in payment thereafter. The sectoral statutes, before 2015, used none of this: their competent authorities applied 1894-style principles on the deed record, which is why two families on opposite sides of a district boundary, losing identical land to the same corridor project under different instruments, could be awarded sums differing several-fold, and why the 2015 extension, whatever its procedural limits, was received in the acquiring ministries as a budgetary event of the first order. Price in this field is not discovered. It is legislated, regime by regime, and the legislated price is the most honest single indicator of what each regime thinks a taking is.

## Overlaps, gaps and the consolidation question

A field built by sediment has characteristic defects, and three deserve naming because each generates real disputes. The first is overlap at the project boundary: a single industrial project may draw its plant land from a state industrial Act, its water pipeline corridor from the general law, its rail siding from the Railways Act and its approach road from the highways code, so that four regimes run concurrently over one footprint and neighbouring families displaced by the same project receive different procedures and, before 2015, different money. The second is the gap at the definition of the project itself: acquisition codes attach to purposes and authorities, not to projects as experienced, so ancillary takings, a labour colony, a dumping yard, a compensatory afforestation parcel, may be routed through whichever instrument is administratively nearest rather than the regime the main taking used. The third is the intertemporal gap: acquisitions are long, regimes change mid-course, and the transition provisions, Section 24 of the 2013 Act for the general law, the savings clauses of the special statutes, and the effective date of the 2015 Order for entitlements, decide which generation of law governs a taking that began under another, a question that has produced a disproportionate share of the field's appellate litigation precisely because the money at stake in the answer is, as the worked comparison showed, of the order of three to one.

These defects power the recurring proposal to consolidate: to repeal the sectoral acquisition codes and subject every taking in India to one statute with one procedure and one entitlement structure. The proposal has an obvious administrative elegance and the support of most of the academic commentary on the field. Its weakness is the one the system's history exposes. The sectoral codes persist because the executing ministries want process control over their own projects and have the legislative access to keep it, and a consolidation bill would have to defeat, in one vote, every ministry that the 2013 exemption defeated separately. The settlement the field has actually reached is consolidation of entitlements without consolidation of procedure: one schedule of money and resettlement, many codes of process. Whether that settlement is a stable resting point or a way station depends on whether the entitlement side continues to be enforced upward, through courts insisting that sectoral authorities apply the 2013 schedules in full, or erodes through administrative practice. The direction of that enforcement is the single most consequential open question on the map, and it is a question about administrators and courts, not about the text of any statute.

## The settlement since 2015: a failed bill, a surviving order and the states

The years after the ordinance lapsed settled the field into the shape the map describes, through three movements that are worth separating because commentators habitually merge them. The first movement is the fate of the amendment bill. The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement (Amendment) Bill, 2015, which embodied the ordinance's five-category exemption from consent and assessment, was referred in 2015 to a Joint Committee of Parliament chaired by S. S. Ahluwalia. The committee's deliberations ran through repeated extensions while state elections and the politics of the land question shifted around it, the government progressively signalled that it would not force the measure through, and the bill was never enacted, lapsing with the dissolution of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha in 2019. The central settlement of 2013 therefore survives in its enacted form, consent chapters, assessment chapters and all, not because a Parliament voted to keep it after full debate but because the attempt to amend it exhausted itself, which is a common enough mode of legislative survival in India and one the reader of any amendment trail should learn to recognise.

The second movement is the Union's redirection of the reform impulse to the states. With the central bill stalled, the Union government took the public position, articulated at the NITI Aayog's Governing Council meeting in July 2015, that states wishing to depart from the central settlement for projects within their territories could do so through their own amendments reserved for presidential assent under Article 254(2). The Gujarat amendment of 2016, the Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra schedule amendments, and the other state measures described in the state layer above are the products of that redirection. Its constitutional significance is that the 2013 settlement became, in operation, a default rather than a uniform law: states with the political will and the assent route available recalibrated the consent and assessment chapters for their listed categories, while states without them retained the enacted text, so that the law of acquisition for a private industrial project came to depend materially on the state in which the project sits. Federalism, which the Concurrent List entry made possible, the politics of 2015 made actual.

The third movement is administrative and judicial consolidation of the extension. The 2015 Order's three paragraphs had to be translated into the working instructions of highways, railways and coal authorities whose manuals predated it, and the translation was uneven enough that, as the litigation map below records, courts were repeatedly asked to compel what the Order had already commanded. Alongside that friction, the Section 24 transition litigation worked itself out through the conflicting two-judge and three-judge readings of the lapse conditions to the five-judge restatement of 2020 in Indore Development Authority v. Manoharlal, which settled that proceedings lapse only where the twin conditions are satisfied in the strict sense the majority gave them, and that compensation deposited in the government treasury counts as paid for the purposes of the clause. The cumulative position is a field whose centre of gravity has shifted from enactment to administration and adjudication: no major acquisition statute has been enacted or repealed in the field's recent history, while orders, state amendments, manuals and benches have continuously redrawn its operative boundaries. That is the deepest reason the map, rather than any single statute, is the correct unit of understanding for this subject.

## The litigation map: where acquisition disputes actually go

A field's real constitution is visible in its litigation, and acquisition litigation sorts into channels that the regime map predicts. The first channel is the statutory dispute route, which concerns money: the reference to the district court under the old general law, the appeal to the Authority and onward to the High Court under the 2013 Act, the arbitration under the National Highways Act, the tribunal under the coal Act. These channels are narrow, expert over time, and concerned with valuation evidence, comparable sales, potentiality and the apportionment of the award among claimants, and they absorb the great bulk of acquisition litigation by volume. The second channel is the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226, which concerns legality: challenges to the notification, to the dispensing of the hearing, to colourable exercise of the public purpose power, to discrimination between similarly placed owners, and, since the Forty-fourth Amendment, to deprivations said to violate Article 300A itself. The writ channel is where the doctrine lives, because the statutory channels cannot question the taking, only its price. The third channel is the enforcement litigation generated by the field's transitions and extensions, and it is the channel that best reveals the system's working: after the 2015 Order, landowners under the scheduled statutes repeatedly had to move the High Courts to compel competent authorities to apply the First, Second and Third Schedules to their acquisitions, and the courts directed that the extension be given effect, in several instances against administrative guidelines that had narrowed it. A reform's real content is what a claimant can enforce, and the extension passed that test only through litigation, which is a fact about Indian administration that generalises well beyond this field.

Two structural features of the litigation deserve the map's attention. The first is the asymmetry of time. Acquisition litigation runs against a project that continues to be built: possession is rarely restored even where the owner ultimately wins on quantum, and the practical remedy for most victorious landowners across all regimes has been enhanced compensation with interest, not restitution, the Singur outcome, where the Court ordered the land returned after the project had collapsed and possession had never been productively used, being the famous exception that proves the remedial rule. Interest therefore functions as the system's rough instrument of justice for delay, and the 1894 Act's enhanced interest rates after the 1984 amendment and the 2013 Act's additional amount provisions are best read as legislated acknowledgements that the state holds the land while the argument about its price proceeds at the state's pace. The second feature is the role of interim orders: because the land vests and the work proceeds, the decisive litigation moment in a contested acquisition is usually the stay application at the threshold, and the jurisprudence of public purpose deference described earlier does much of its work there, at the interim stage, before any final hearing. A practitioner reading the map should therefore understand that the doctrines are not distributed evenly across time: deference operates early, when the taking can still be stopped, and generosity operates late, when only the price remains to be argued.

## Land acquisition law at work: three readers, three routes

For the landowner or the family served with a notice, the map converts into a sequence of identifications, each of which changes what can be done next. The first identification is the instrument named in the notification's recital, which fixes the procedure: the objection that lies, the officer who hears it, and the clock it runs on. The second is the proponent, which under the general law fixes whether consent and assessment protections attach. The third is the character of the family's interest: titleholder, recorded tenant, livelihood dependent without title, or forest rights holder, because each character is recognised by different regimes to different degrees, and the livelihood register of the 2013 Act, carried into the scheduled statutes by the 2015 extension, is the difference between an award that sees the whole family and an award that sees only the name in the revenue record. The fourth is timing: whether the acquisition began under the 1894 Act and survives through the transition rules, whether it falls before or after the 2015 extension for entitlement purposes, and whether a state amendment has altered the central settlement in that territory. A family that can answer those four questions knows its regime, its forum and its realistic entitlements; a family that cannot is negotiating in the dark against an authority that knows all four.

For the researcher, the map's analytical yield is the exemption structure itself. The field demonstrates a generalisable pattern of Indian legislation: a reform statute of high ambition enacted with a schedule of exemptions for the sectors where the reform would bite hardest, an executive duty to close the gap by notification, and a political economy in which the notification, not the statute, becomes the real legislative event. The pattern invites comparison across fields, with environmental and labour legislation supplying their own schedules and their own deferred extensions, and it supplies a research question with genuine explanatory power: in any Indian regulatory field, what fraction of the activity the reform claims to govern actually sits inside the reform statute, and what fraction sits in the schedule? The land field's answer, that the largest displacing sectors sat outside the general law for the whole of the period from 1956 to 2015 and sit inside its entitlements but outside its procedures thereafter, is the kind of finding that reorganises how the field's history is written.

For the aspirant, the map is the revision structure for one of the most reliably examined areas of the polity and law syllabus, and the confusions that cost marks are precisely the ones this article has targeted. Property is not a fundamental right, and the answer that calls it one fails on the Forty-fourth Amendment. The 2013 Act does not apply to all acquisitions, and the answer that assumes it does fails on Section 105. Compensation under the scheduled statutes is not the old sectoral compensation, and the answer that stops at the exemption fails on the Order of 28 August 2015. Requisition is not a species of permanent taking, and the answer that treats the two as interchangeable fails on the obligation to release. Acquisition is concurrent, land is a state subject, and the answer that assigns acquisition law to either level alone fails on Entry 42 and Entry 18 read together. An aspirant who can state those five positions, and can attach to each the instrument that establishes it, holds the doctrinal skeleton of the field; the flesh, the assessment chapters, the multiplier mechanics, the transition litigation, is supplied by the statute articles to which this map links, and it is there that the depth marks are earned.

## Testing the two framings against the map

Argument about land acquisition in India is dominated by two framings, and the stance of this article is that the map vindicates each in part and refutes each in part, which is why both survive. The development framing, stated at its strongest by the governments that defended the 2015 amendment ordinance, holds that a country building highways, power, irrigation and industry at India's scale cannot assemble land by voluntary purchase alone, that holdouts and speculative fragmentation would make linear infrastructure practically impossible, and that the correct policy is generous compensation with a lean process. Arun Jaitley, defending the ordinance as Finance Minister, put the balance in exactly those terms: the higher compensation of the 2013 Act would continue, while the procedural rigours were loosened for five defined categories of project. Industry associations made the companion claim that the consent and assessment chapters, as enacted, had stalled project pipelines and that land had become the binding constraint on investment. The factual core of this framing is sound. Eminent domain exists in every functioning legal system because the alternative fails for network infrastructure, and the pre-2013 record shows that India's problem was never the existence of the power but its price and its process.

The displacement framing, stated at its strongest by the movements that grew around the great dam and industrial conflicts and articulated over decades by Medha Patkar and the Narmada Bachao Andolan, holds that acquisition in India has operated as a mechanism by which the cost of growth is loaded onto those least able to bear it: that the displaced have been disproportionately cultivators, forest dwellers and Scheduled Tribes, that compensation at recorded market value systematically understated what was taken, that the landless were excluded from the award altogether, and that rehabilitation was promised in project brochures and defaulted in fact. On the pre-2013 record this framing is not polemic but description, and official rehabilitation policy effectively conceded it by existing at all. Where the framing overreaches is in treating the power itself as the injustice: the same eminent domain that submerged the Narmada valley also runs the water supply of the cities its critics live in, and a legal order that abolished compulsory acquisition would not return power to the displaced; it would return it to whoever could wait out or outbid the fragmented owners, which is rarely the poorest among them.

Two evidentiary cautions discipline both framings, and the map is the right place to record them. The first is that India keeps no comprehensive official count of the persons displaced by development projects, so the aggregate numbers that circulate in the argument are estimates, the most cited of them associated with the researcher Walter Fernandes, whose work put the displaced of the post-independence decades in the tens of millions and the Scheduled Tribe share of them far above the community's share of population. The direction of those findings, a very large displaced population bearing a disproportionate tribal burden, is not seriously contested even by the development framing's defenders; the precise magnitudes are, and honest writing in this field attributes the figures as estimates and argues from the structure where the figures run out. The second caution runs the other way: the claim, heard from industry after 2013, that acquisition had become impossible is not supported by the record either. Acquisition continued under every regime through the period of the supposed impossibility, the national highway programme's construction pace rose markedly in the years following the 2015 extension on the government's own reporting, and the sectoral and state channels described in this article were never closed at all. What changed after 2013 was the price of land to projects and the distribution of procedural power, not the availability of the power to take, and arguments that conflate a higher price with an abolished power misdescribe the map in a way this article's structure exists to correct.

The honest verdict the map supports is that both framings are true about different variables. The development framing is right about the necessity of the power and wrong about the dispensability of the process, because the process, the assessment, the hearing, the independent award, is the only place where the price and the siting can be tested before the fact. The displacement framing is right about the historical incidence of the burden and wrong when it implies the injustice is inherent in acquisition rather than in the specific settlement, proprietary entitlement unit, suppressed market value, absent rehabilitation, that the 1894 Act bequeathed and the special statutes preserved. The real question, as the politics of 2013 to 2015 demonstrated, is what the state owes the person it displaces, and the field's trajectory since, multiplied compensation, a livelihood register of entitlement, extension of entitlements across the schedule, and a continuing fight over whether the testing process survives, is that question being answered in instalments. For the aspirant, the examinable core of the whole field is exactly this: property is a constitutional and not a fundamental right after the Forty-fourth Amendment, acquisition is a Concurrent List subject, the 2013 Act governs the general run of takings, thirteen scheduled statutes stand outside its procedure but inside its compensation and rehabilitation schedules since 2015, and requisition is a temporary taking of possession that must end in release or in compensated acquisition. A reader preparing notes and practice material from this cluster can [open spreadsheets, documents and slide decks in the browser with the ReportMedic office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which suits the comparison tables and award computations this subject generates, before moving to the statute articles that follow.

Between the two framings stands a third position that the map makes visible and that deserves statement in its own voice, because it is where much of the serious policy work in the field has migrated. The value sharing position holds that the core defect of classical acquisition is that it converts the landowner from a stakeholder in the project's success into its first casualty: the owner is paid the pre-project value of the land while the entire uplift the project creates accrues to the proponent and the state. The remedies this position generates are visible across the modern field: the multiplier and the hundred per cent solatium as crude uplift sharing inside the price, the annuity option inside the Second Schedule's rehabilitation entitlements, land pooling and readjustment as structural sharing of the uplift itself, and negotiated purchase at a premium as the market's version of the same idea. The position does not deny the necessity of compulsion and does not romanticise it; it asks that the displaced be made participants in the gain their displacement enables, and it explains why the field's instruments have been evolving, across regimes and without any single directing statute, away from price alone toward price plus a share in what follows. A reader who grasps the three positions, development, displacement and value sharing, and can say which instrument on the map embodies which, holds the argument of this field in a form that will organise everything else they read about it.

Lawyers who work in the field organise their knowledge exactly this way, by regime and by route, and the examination answers that score are the ones that reproduce that organisation rather than reciting a single Act. That is the sense in which the series thesis for this cluster should be understood: a field with a general statute and a schedule of exemptions cannot be understood from the general statute, and the exemptions, together with the unglamorous instrument that partially closed them, are where the operative law has always lived.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws govern land acquisition in India?

Land acquisition in India is governed by a layered set of laws rather than one code. The general law is the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, which replaced the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. Thirteen sectoral statutes listed in the Fourth Schedule to the 2013 Act, including the National Highways Act, 1956, the Railways Act, 1989 and the Coal Bearing Areas Acquisition and Development Act, 1957, run their own acquisition procedures, although the 2013 Act's compensation and rehabilitation schedules apply to them since 2015. State acquisition statutes, industrial development Acts and state amendments to the central law form a third layer, and requisition and negotiated purchase operate as further routes.

### Q: What is eminent domain and where does it come from?

Eminent domain is the inherent power of a sovereign state to take private property for public use on payment of compensation. The doctrine developed in European legal thought and reached India through colonial legislation, beginning with instruments such as Bengal Regulation I of 1824 and passing through the general acquisition Acts of 1857 and 1870 to the consolidating Act of 1894. The Indian Constitution does not grant the power in terms. It assumes the power as an attribute of sovereignty and regulates its exercise, first through Article 31 and, after the Forty-fourth Amendment removed property from the fundamental rights, through Article 300A, which requires authority of law for any deprivation of property.

### Q: What is the current constitutional status of the right to property?

The right to property is a constitutional right but not a fundamental right. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 omitted Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31 from Part III and inserted Article 300A in Part XII, which provides that no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law. A person deprived of property cannot move the Supreme Court directly under Article 32 for the property right itself, but may approach the High Court under Article 226. The Supreme Court has held, including in K. T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka, that a taking must still rest on a valid law, serve a public purpose and provide compensation that is not illusory.

### Q: Can a state make its own land acquisition law?

Yes. Acquisition and requisitioning of property is Entry 42 of the Concurrent List in the Seventh Schedule, so state legislatures may legislate on the subject alongside Parliament. States have used the power to enact acquisition machinery inside industrial development Acts, highways Acts and housing statutes, and to amend the central 2013 Act for their own territories. Where a state law conflicts with the central law, Article 254 ordinarily gives the central law primacy, unless the state law was reserved for the President's consideration and received assent, in which case the state law prevails in that state. Land itself, as distinct from its acquisition, is a State List subject under Entry 18.

### Q: Which acquisitions fall outside the main land acquisition law?

Acquisitions under the thirteen enactments listed in the Fourth Schedule to the 2013 Act fall outside the Act's own procedure by force of Section 105: highways, railways, metro railways, coal bearing areas, atomic energy, electricity, petroleum and minerals pipelines, ancient monuments, the Damodar Valley Corporation, mines, tramways, resettlement of displaced persons and requisitioning and acquisition of immovable property. Their compensation and rehabilitation entitlements were nevertheless aligned with the 2013 Act by the extension of 2015. Acquisitions under state statutes such as industrial development Acts also proceed outside the central Act where those statutes govern, and takings of possession by requisition, negotiated purchases and land pooling schemes are not acquisitions under the main law at all.

### Q: What is the difference between acquisition and requisition of property?

Acquisition permanently extinguishes the owner's title and vests the property in the acquiring authority, against payment of the value of what is taken. Requisition takes only possession and use for a limited period: title remains with the owner, compensation is assessed as payment for the period of deprivation rather than as a price, and the property must be released when the purpose ends. The principal central instrument is the Requisitioning and Acquisition of Immovable Property Act, 1952, which contains both powers. A requisition maintained indefinitely, or used to avoid paying acquisition compensation, is treated by the courts as a colourable exercise, because temporariness is the condition that justifies requisition's lighter procedure and lower payment.

### Q: Who decides what counts as a public purpose?

Primarily the acquiring government. Every acquisition statute conditions the power on a public purpose, and the courts have consistently declined to substitute their own assessment of the purpose for the government's, treating the statutory declaration as near-conclusive. Judicial review intervenes where the power is exercised in bad faith or as a colourable exercise, taking land ostensibly for a public purpose for what is in substance a private or collateral purpose. The 2013 Act narrowed the discretion by enumerating public purposes in Section 2(1) and by attaching consent thresholds where the beneficiary is a private company or a partnership, while the special statutes continue to operate with the older, more deferential doctrine.

### Q: Can land acquisition be done for a private company?

Yes, through routes that carry additional safeguards because the beneficiary is private. Under the 1894 Act, acquisition for a company proceeded under a separate Part, requiring an inquiry into whether the work would prove useful to the public and an agreement by the company to bear the cost; the Singur acquisition was set aside by the Supreme Court in Kedar Nath Yadav v. State of West Bengal in 2016 in a dispute over that route. Under the 2013 Act, acquisition for a private company requires the prior consent of at least eighty per cent of affected families, and the company bears the cost of acquisition and rehabilitation. What the state may not do is dress a private taking in public purpose language to bypass the company route.

### Q: How can a landowner tell which law their land is being acquired under?

Read the preliminary notification. Every compulsory acquisition begins with a notification that recites, in its opening words, the statute under which it is issued, names the acquiring authority and the purpose, and describes the land by survey number. A notification under the 2013 Act points to the assessment, hearing and consent machinery of the general law. One under Section 3A of the National Highways Act or the corresponding provision of another Fourth Schedule statute points to a sectoral procedure with the 2013 Act's compensation and rehabilitation schedules applied through the 2015 extension. One under a state industrial or highways Act points to that state code. The recital answers the routing question before anything else is read.

### Q: What is the Fourth Schedule of the 2013 land acquisition Act?

The Fourth Schedule is the list of thirteen central enactments that Section 105 of the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 excludes from the Act's own operation. It covers the Ancient Monuments Act, 1958, the Atomic Energy Act, 1962, the Damodar Valley Corporation Act, 1948, the Indian Tramways Act, 1886, the Land Acquisition (Mines) Act, 1885, the Metro Railways Act, 1978, the National Highways Act, 1956, the Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines Act, 1962, the Requisitioning and Acquisition of Immovable Property Act, 1952, the Resettlement of Displaced Persons Act, 1948, the Coal Bearing Areas Act, 1957, the Electricity Act, 2003 and the Railways Act, 1989. Section 105(3) required their compensation and rehabilitation provisions to be aligned with the 2013 Act by notification within one year.

### Q: How were the compensation rules of the 2013 Act extended to the special acquisition Acts?

Section 105(3) of the 2013 Act required the Central Government, within one year of commencement, to extend the Act's provisions on determination of compensation under the First Schedule and rehabilitation and resettlement under the Second and Third Schedules to acquisitions under the thirteen Fourth Schedule enactments. The extension was first carried by the amendment ordinance promulgated on 31 December 2014, which declared the provisions applicable from 1 January 2015. When the amendment bill failed and the ordinance lapsed on 31 August 2015, the extension was preserved by the Removal of Difficulties Order, 2015, published as S.O. 2368(E) on 28 August 2015 under Section 113 and brought into force on 1 September 2015.

### Q: Why does India have so many separate land acquisition laws?

Because the field was built by accumulation rather than design. The general law of 1894 was supplemented sector by sector as the state took up new functions: a highways code in 1956, a coal code in 1957, a metro code in 1978, a special railway projects chapter in 2008, each written by the executing ministry to fit its own engineering sequence and to avoid the general law's hearings and court references. The states added their own industrial, highways and housing acquisition statutes under their Concurrent List competence. No consolidation ever repealed the accumulating layers, and the 2013 Act, instead of absorbing the special statutes, listed them in its Fourth Schedule and exempted them, leaving plurality as the field's permanent structure.

### Q: How do the special acquisition statutes differ from the general law in safeguards?

The differences are procedural and institutional. Under the general law, objections were heard through a structured stage, the award was made by the Collector, and dissatisfaction carried the dispute to a court on reference under the 1894 Act or to the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority under the 2013 Act. Under the special statutes, the objection is typically confined to the use of the land for the notified purpose, the award is made by a competent authority of the acquiring project, and the further route runs to an arbitrator appointed by the Central Government under the National Highways Act or to a tribunal under the coal legislation. The special statutes also contain no social impact assessment and no consent requirement, gaps the 2015 extension of entitlements did not close.

### Q: Which law applies when land is acquired for a special economic zone?

No special economic zone acquisition code exists. The Special Economic Zones Act, 2005 creates the zone framework, developer approvals and fiscal concessions, but contains no machinery for compulsorily acquiring land. Zone land has in practice been assembled by state governments and their industrial development authorities, either by acquisition under the general land acquisition law in force, by acquisition under a state industrial statute, or by purchase and transfer of government land. The governing acquisition regime is therefore whichever instrument the state actually uses, with its own compensation and safeguards, and controversies over zone land, including the Nandigram conflict in West Bengal, were fought legally as ordinary acquisition disputes under those instruments rather than under the zones legislation.

### Q: Can a state keep its own acquisition statutes outside the 2013 Act?

Yes, through the mechanism the 2013 Act itself supplies. Section 105A, inserted into the Act by state amendment with presidential assent under Article 254(2), allows a state to place its own enactments in a Fifth Schedule and exempt acquisitions under them from the central Act. Tamil Nadu used the route for three enactments, the Tamil Nadu Acquisition of Land for Harijan Welfare Schemes Act, 1978, the Tamil Nadu Acquisition of Land for Industrial Purposes Act, 1997 and the Tamil Nadu Highways Act, 2001, with effect from the central Act's commencement, and Maharashtra used it for the Maharashtra Highways Act. State statutes that operate without colliding with the central Act also continue alongside it without needing the schedule route.

### Q: What happens when a state acquisition law conflicts with the central law?

Article 254 of the Constitution governs. Where a state law on a Concurrent List subject such as acquisition is repugnant to a central law, the central law prevails and the state law is void to the extent of the repugnancy. The exception is Article 254(2): if the state law was reserved for the President's consideration and received assent, it prevails in that state notwithstanding the conflict, until Parliament legislates again on the subject. This is the route by which state amendments to the 2013 Act, including exemption of listed project categories from the assessment and consent chapters, operate lawfully within a state. The analysis is provision-specific: only the colliding provisions yield, not the whole state statute.

### Q: Can the government buy land by negotiation instead of acquiring it?

Yes. Compulsory acquisition is a power, not a duty, and a government or authority may purchase land by agreement at a negotiated price, taking title by registered conveyance without opening the acquisition code at all. Several governments have adopted direct purchase as policy for projects where acquisition litigation would cost more time than the price premium costs money, negotiating through district-level committees at prices above the statutory formula. For the landowner the encounter changes character: there is no notification, objection or award, but also no compulsion, and the owner's remedy for an inadequate offer is to refuse and leave the state to choose between improving the offer and invoking acquisition, with its statutory entitlements and delays.

### Q: Can a state industrial authority acquire land under its own statute?

Yes, and a large share of industrial land in India has been acquired exactly that way. State industrial development legislation, of which the Maharashtra Industrial Development Act, 1961 is the prototype and the Karnataka Industrial Areas Development Act, 1966 a close relative, empowers the state to declare an industrial area, hear objections through its own officers, vest the land in the state on payment of compensation computed on principles modelled on the general law, and transfer the estate to the development corporation for development and allotment. Tamil Nadu's Acquisition of Land for Industrial Purposes Act, 1997 supplies an expedited version of the same pattern. These acquisitions proceed under the state code, outside the central Act's procedure, and land banks of pre-acquired plots are their continuing product.

### Q: What must be settled under the Forest Rights Act before forest land is taken?

The rights recognised by the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 must be recognised and settled before forest land is diverted or acquired for a project: individual rights of habitation and cultivation, community rights of use and access, and the community right to protect, regenerate and manage community forest resources. In the Niyamgiri decision, Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment and Forests in 2013, the Supreme Court required the Gram Sabhas of the affected villages to determine the presence of such rights, including religious and cultural rights, before the proposed bauxite mining diversion could proceed, and the Gram Sabhas' rejection ended the proposal. The Forest Rights Act is not an acquisition statute, but on forest land it operates as a condition precedent over every acquisition regime.

### Q: Who pays the cost of an acquisition made for a company or an authority?

The beneficiary pays. Acquisition law treats the exercise of eminent domain for an identified proponent as a public act performed at the proponent's expense: under the 1894 Act, the agreement required in company acquisitions obliged the company to bear the cost of the acquisition, and under the 2013 Act the cost of acquisition, including the social impact assessment, the compensation and the rehabilitation and resettlement entitlements, is borne by the requiring body for which the land is acquired, whether a company, an authority or a partnership vehicle. The state contributes its coercive machinery, its officers and its forums, not the price. This allocation is one reason the routing of a project matters commercially: the proponent's budget carries whatever regime's entitlements the routing produces.
