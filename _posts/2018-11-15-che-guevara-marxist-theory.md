Almost everyone alive can recognise Che Guevara, and almost nobody can say what he thought. The face on the poster, the beret, the gaze directed past the camera toward some horizon of struggle: that image has circulated so widely, and been emptied so thoroughly in the circulating, that it now functions as a free-floating sign of rebellion in general, detached from any doctrine in particular. Behind the image stands a writer. Ernesto Guevara produced two bodies of theoretical work during a short life, and they are not equal in weight. The first, a theory of insurrection built around the armed foco, made him famous and was taken up, with catastrophic results, by armed groups across a continent. The second, an argument about how a socialist economy should actually be managed, conducted in Cuban journals against named opponents over questions of accounting, incentives, and the law of value, is more substantial, more original, and almost entirely unknown outside specialist circles. This profile treats both bodies of work at the length they deserve, which means reversing the usual proportions. The guerrilla theory gets its full hearing, including its record. The economic argument gets the centre of the article, because it is the centre of his claim to be a theorist at all.

![Ernesto Guevara in uniform, the theorist behind the familiar poster, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The claim that organises everything below can be stated in one sentence. The unknown Guevara, the finance minister arguing that socialist enterprises must not behave like capitalist firms and that a transition built on material incentives would reproduce the consciousness it claimed to abolish, wrote the most defensible pages of his career in a debate that the reading public has never heard of, while the celebrated Guevara of the manual and the photograph advanced a theory of revolution whose practical record is close to uniformly negative and whose author died applying it. Recovering the first figure does not launder the second, and this article will not attempt the laundering in either direction. His role in the revolutionary tribunals of the new Cuban state, under which people were executed after summary proceedings, is stated plainly in the profile that follows, not deferred to a footnote of the assessment. His coercive positions on labour discipline are stated with the same plainness. A thinker profile that hides the record to protect the theory, or waves the record to avoid reading the theory, fails its subject twice.

## Che Guevara as a theorist: two bodies of work, one reputation

Begin with the shape of the output, because the shape is the puzzle. Guevara wrote as a participant, never as an academic. His texts are a manual of guerrilla warfare distilled from a campaign he had just fought, speeches delivered in office, journal articles written in the middle of an administrative war over the Cuban economy's operating rules, a long essay on the individual under socialism composed as a letter to a Uruguayan editor, a diary of a failed African expedition, and a set of private critical notes on Soviet political economy that he did not publish and that surfaced only four decades after his death. Nothing in the corpus was written for a university. Everything in it was written to settle a practical question that its author faced in a specific post: how a small armed band survives and grows, how a ministry allocates steel and sugar, how a worker can be induced to care about a plan he did not write, how a poor country trades with rich socialist ones without being subordinated by the exchange.

That practical origin cuts both ways, and a fair assessment has to hold both edges. On one side, the writing has a concreteness that seminar Marxism often lacks. When Guevara argues against enterprise self-financing, he is arguing about enterprises he administered, workers whose absenteeism he had measured in his own ministry's reports, sugar harvests whose shortfalls landed on his desk. When he argues that an insurrectional nucleus can conjure into being the very preconditions of its triumph, he is generalising from a landing that began with a few dozen survivors and ended, two years later, with the collapse of a dictatorship. On the other side, the writing generalises recklessly from single cases, treats the Cuban exception as a continental rule, and in the economic work builds an entire transition strategy on a psychology of motivation that his own decade in office kept contradicting. The two bodies of work share this structure: a real experience, honestly reported, extended into a universal claim that the experience cannot carry.

The reputation, meanwhile, was assigned by the image and by the manner of his death, and it selected the weaker corpus for immortality. The manual is short, translatable, and dramatic. The economic debate is long, technical, mostly untranslated, and conducted in the vocabulary of accounting systems. A reader who wants to know what Guevara thought about interest rates inside a planned economy, about whether a socialist firm should hold its own bank account, about the moral status of overtime pay, will find positions argued at a level of detail that most heads of government never reach. A reader who wants the poster will find the poster everywhere and the positions nowhere. The imbalance is not an accident of taste. It is the predictable result of what each corpus demands from its audience, and one section below is devoted to the Spanish-language archive and the translation gap that keeps the economics buried.

A word on method before the life. This profile separates three questions that polemic habitually fuses. What did he claim? What happened when the claims were acted on? And what, if anything, survives the collision between the two? The foco theory will be stated in its own three propositions before its record is examined. The budgetary finance system will be set out in the accounting vocabulary its author used, before its critics are heard. The opposing position in the economic debate, enterprise self-financing, will be given in the words and reasoning of its named proponents, at full strength, because Guevara's side of that argument is unintelligible without the strength of what it opposed. The strongest objections to the whole legacy, from the destroyed generation of foquismo to the charge that his economics was voluntarism dressed as theory, are stated by name and answered or conceded as the evidence requires. Where the record is contested in its numbers, as with the executions at the tribunals he supervised, this article follows the protocol its brief imposes: the fact is stated, the political affiliations of the competing estimates are noted, and no figure is asserted.

## The making of a revolutionary: Argentina, the road, Guatemala

Ernesto Guevara was born in Rosario, Argentina, in 1928, into a family of comfortable means and bohemian politics, and he carried through life two inheritances from that household: a ferocious asthma that structured his childhood around reading and chess, and a familiarity with radical argument as ordinary table talk. He trained as a physician in Buenos Aires, qualifying in 1953, and the medical degree matters to the biography less as a profession than as a travelling credential. Twice during his student years he crossed South America on long journeys, once by motorcycle with a friend, and the diaries of those journeys record a young doctor moving through mining camps, leper colonies, and indigenous villages, registering the continent's poverty with a clinician's eye and a deepening conviction that its causes were structural rather than accidental. The leper colony work in Peru, where he lived among the patients and bridged the river that separated them from the staff, fixed in him a lifelong pattern: an attraction to the excluded, and an impatience with charity as an answer to exclusion.

The journeys made him a Latin American before they made him a Marxist, and the order matters. His radicalism began as continental and humanitarian, a doctor's anger at treatable misery, and acquired its theoretical frame gradually, through reading undertaken in the same autodidact spirit as his medicine. By the time he reached Guatemala in late 1953 he was a convinced if unsystematic leftist, drawn to that country because its elected government under Jacobo Arbenz was attempting the most ambitious reform programme on the continent: land redistribution against the great estates, labour rights, a challenge to the foreign fruit company whose holdings dominated the republic's economy. He attached himself to the edges of that experiment, worked among its supporters, and watched it destroyed. In 1954 a coup organised and armed from Washington overthrew Arbenz, installed a military regime, and reversed the reforms, while the reformist camp fragmented, hesitated, and in the decisive days failed to arm its own supporters.

### Why did Guatemala matter so much in his formation?

Because it supplied the negative lesson around which his later theory organised itself. An elected reform government with popular support declined to arm the population, trusted constitutional forms against an enemy that observed none, and was swept away by a modest expedition backed by a foreign power.

Guevara concluded that peaceful reform in the Americas would be met with force, and that conclusion, drawn from a single searing case, hardened into the premise of everything he afterward wrote about how revolutions begin and why they cannot wait for permission from history.

He fled to Mexico as the coup consolidated, carrying the Guatemalan lesson and very little else. In Mexico City he worked at odd medical jobs, moved in the circle of Cuban exiles gathered around the survivors of the Moncada barracks attack, and in the summer of 1955 met Fidel Castro, who was assembling an expedition to return to Cuba and open a guerrilla front against the Batista dictatorship. The meeting is usually told as destiny; in the record it looks more like recognition. Castro offered a concrete instrument for the conclusion Guatemala had produced, an armed nucleus that would not wait for conditions, and Guevara signed on as the expedition's doctor. He trained with the group in Mexico, was briefly imprisoned with them by Mexican police, and sailed on the yacht Granma in November 1956 as ship's physician, the only non-Cuban in a command role and the only member of the expedition whose route to it ran through the theory of a continent rather than the politics of an island.

## The diaries: a continent read from the road

The two journeys of his student years deserve more than the biographical paragraph they usually receive, because they are the first texts of the corpus and they set its method. The young Guevara travelled as a clinician with a notebook, and the notebook's discipline, observe, record, generalise reluctantly and then all at once, never left him. In the mining settlements of the north he registered the conditions of the copper and nitrate workers and the wealth that flowed past them to foreign owners. In the leper colonies of Peru he crossed, deliberately and repeatedly, the river that separated the patients' settlement from the staff quarters, eating with the sick and playing football with them, and drew from the experience a conclusion he would later generalise into a theory of the vanguard: that the excluded know their exclusion precisely, and that solidarity is a practice before it is a doctrine. Among the indigenous communities of the Andean highlands he encountered a poverty that no charity reached and no market described, and his notes from those encounters already contain, in embryo, the continental frame within which all his later thinking moved: Latin America as a single historical unit, divided by flags but unified by its subordination.

What the diaries do not contain is equally diagnostic. There is no party in them, no programme, no strategy, barely a system. The politics is a doctor's politics: the conviction that misery on this scale is made by arrangements and can be unmade by arrangements, joined to a growing contempt for the gradualism of those who administered the misery humanely. Readers who come to the diaries from the later work often search them for the theorist and find only the witness. That is the correct finding, and it explains the shape of the mature work. Guevara's theory was never built downward from a system; it was built upward from witnessed cases, Guatemala, the Sierra, the ministry's ledgers, and its characteristic strength and weakness both derive from that origin. The cases are real and the reporting is honest. The generalisations outrun them, because a mind formed by witnessing treats the witnessed case as the truth of the whole, and the whole continent, the whole transition, the whole socialist camp, keep being summoned to answer for what was seen in one valley, one ministry, one fortress.

## The Sierra administered: the rebel zone as a small state

The foco's political function, asserted in the manual, was practised in the Sierra before it was theorised, and the practice is worth reconstructing because it is the strongest evidence for the doctrine's careful form. In the zones the rebel army controlled or contested, it did not merely fight. It governed. It operated a rough judiciary that peasants used in preference to the dictatorship's courts, because its judgements were swift, unbought, and enforced. It ran workshops that repaired weapons and made uniforms and shoes, a tannery, a bakery, a hospital service staffed by the expedition's doctors and by recruits trained in the mountains. It broadcast from a clandestine radio station that carried the movement's account of the war into the cities and abroad. Late in the campaign it promulgated an agrarian reform law for the liberated territory, distributing land and thereby converting the peasantry's sympathy into a material interest in the rebellion's survival. Guevara, who commanded one of the fronts and administered one of these zones, learned statecraft in this miniature: taxation in kind, discipline of his own troops, the management of informers and collaborators, the arbitration of disputes among the population whose allegiance was the army's supply line.

The miniature matters for the assessment because it shows what the foco actually was when it worked. It was not a detonator. It was an embryo state that grew by offering a better order than the incumbent's in the spaces the incumbent had abandoned, and its military expansion followed its administrative credibility rather than preceding it. Read against this record, the doctrine's second proposition, the claim that the nucleus creates the conditions, appears as a misdescription of its author's own achievement: the Sierra foco succeeded by painstakingly building conditions, village by village, judgement by judgement, clinic by clinic. The tragedy of the codification is that it exported the misdescription and left the practice behind, since the practice, patience, embedding, governance, could not be compressed into a slogan and the misdescription could. A generation learned that the foco creates conditions. The Sierra's actual lesson was that conditions are created by governing, slowly, in the places where the old power does not reach, and that lesson, unglamorous and unexportable, died of its author's fame.

## The other war: the urban underground and the llano

A further correction to the foco's mythology belongs in the record, because the Cuban victory itself refutes the doctrine later drawn from it. The insurrection against Batista was never only, or even mainly, a rural war. In the cities of the island, an urban underground, the llano, raised money, bought and smuggled weapons, organised strikes and sabotage, published clandestine material, and maintained the movement's presence among students, workers, and professionals. Its cadres died at rates comparable to the Sierra's, in safe houses and street actions rather than in mountain ambushes, and its most gifted organiser, Frank País, was murdered by the dictatorship's police in Santiago while still in his early twenties. The general strike attempted in the spring of 1958 failed, and the failure taught the movement's leadership hard lessons about the relation between the armed struggle and mass action; but the urban network's contribution to the dictatorship's isolation, and to the rebel army's supply, was continuous and indispensable.

The doctrine that emerged from the victory quietly erased this half of it. In the manual, and far more in the codification, the city appears as a subordinate theatre, useful for support but incapable of decision, and the countryside is enthroned as the basic terrain of the struggle. The erasure had a logic: the rural theory was the exportable one, the one that distinguished the Cuban experience from the orthodox parties' urban strategies and gave the new doctrine its polemical edge. But as history it is false to the victory it claims to summarise, and as strategy it was ruinous, because it taught the continental imitators to despise the patient urban organisation that had in fact sustained the Sierra, and to stake everything on the rural nucleus that the Sierra, in truth, had never been. When later movements perished in the countryside for want of supply, intelligence, and political cover, they were paying for a falsified account of how the one successful foco had actually lived.

## Mexico, the landing, and the Sierra: the combatant formed

The Granma landing in December 1956 was a military disaster that the subsequent mythology converted into a founding miracle, and the manual Guevara later wrote is best read as an attempt to extract transferable method from the disaster's narrow escape. The expeditionaries were ambushed within days, scattered, and reduced to a remnant hiding in the Sierra Maestra mountains, short of weapons, food, medicine, and contact with the urban movement that was supposed to support them. What followed over two years was the slow construction of a rebel army from that remnant: the recruitment of peasants, the building of supply networks, the administration of liberated zones with schools, workshops, a radio station, and a rough justice, and a sequence of engagements that grew from ambushes of patrols to the defeat of a government summer offensive and finally to columns descending from the mountains as the dictatorship's authority dissolved. Guevara rose through it from doctor to combatant to commander of a column, earning in the campaign the nickname by which the world knows him, Che being the Argentine interjection he could not stop using.

Two features of the Sierra experience deserve emphasis because they feed directly into the theory. The first is that the rebel army's survival depended on peasant support that had to be earned by conduct: the guerrillas paid for food, punished theft by their own men severely, provided medical care to villagers and to captured enemy soldiers, and presented themselves as a more reliable order than the dictatorship's rural guards. The foco, in its original Cuban practice, was embedded in a population and disciplined by that embedding. The second feature is that the campaign's decisive political conditions were supplied by the enemy: Batista's regime was corrupt, isolated, increasingly abandoned by its American patron, and incapable of reforming itself, so that the guerrilla nucleus grew inside a general crisis of the old order rather than creating that crisis from nothing. Both features would be quietly dropped when the experience was codified into a portable doctrine, and the dropping is the hinge on which the theory's later record turns.

When Batista fled at the start of 1959 and the rebel columns entered Havana, Guevara was among the most prominent commanders of the victorious movement and among the least Cuban, an Argentine whose authority rested entirely on the campaign. The new government distributed its inheritances quickly. He was declared a Cuban citizen by birth in recognition of his services, a legal fiction that made him eligible for the highest offices, and he was assigned a sequence of posts that moved him from the revolution's coercive apparatus to its economic direction within a single year. Those posts, and what he did in the first of them, belong to the record and are stated next without varnish.

## The victor's appointments: fortress, bank, and ministry

In January 1959 Guevara was given command of La Cabaña, the fortress on the eastern side of Havana harbour, and with the command came responsibility for the revolutionary tribunals that tried officials and agents of the fallen dictatorship accused of crimes under the old regime. His role must be stated plainly, here in the profile and not deferred. He presided over the apparatus of those trials at La Cabaña, reviewed cases, and confirmed sentences. The proceedings were summary by the standards of any developed jurisprudence: defence rights were restricted, appeals ran to the same revolutionary authority that prosecuted, and numbers of the condemned were executed by firing squad after conviction. How many died under the tribunals he supervised is a question on which the sources divide along lines of unmistakable political affiliation, with exile and opposition accounts giving figures far above those of the Cuban state and its historians, and this article asserts no figure of its own. What is not in dispute is the existence of the executions, his supervisory responsibility for them, or his public defence of revolutionary justice as a necessary instrument against the old regime's criminals. Supporters frame the trials as the settling of accounts with torturers and informers of a dictatorship that had murdered its opponents for years. Critics frame them as the founding terror of a new one. The profile's obligation is narrower and harder: to record that the theorist of the new man began his career in power presiding over a fortress where men were shot after brief trials, and to let that fact stand in the assessment beside the economic writings, neither used to cancel the other.

### How should the tribunal record be stated?

As a fact of the profile, in the same register as his offices and campaigns. He commanded La Cabaña during the months when its tribunals sat, held review authority over their sentences, defended the proceedings in public, and bears supervisory responsibility for executions carried out under them.

Estimates of the number executed diverge sharply between sources of opposed political affiliation, and any honest account reports the divergence rather than adopting a side's figure.

From the fortress he moved to the economy, and the speed of the move tells something about the revolution's personnel famine and about his own restlessness. In November 1959 he was appointed president of the National Bank, an appointment he is said to have accepted after a meeting at which he misheard an appeal for an economist as an appeal for a communist; the anecdote may be embroidered, but the appointment was real, and he signed the new banknotes with his nickname, a gesture his critics read as contempt for money itself and his defenders as a joke about the office. In February 1961 he became minister of industries, charged with the industrial sector of an economy undergoing rapid nationalisation, and it was in that post, confronting the daily accounting of factories, imports, wages, and shortfalls, that the economic theory for which this profile chiefly remembers him was formed. The ministry was his university. Its problems were brutally concrete: how to run nationalised enterprises whose managers had fled, how to allocate imported inputs without a functioning price system, how to keep workers producing when the old incentives of fear and hunger had been abolished in principle and the new ones existed mainly in speeches.

### What did he actually administer at the ministry of industries?

A large share of Cuba's industrial plant during its conversion from private to state ownership: sugar mills' engineering works, mines, factories, and the institutes that grouped them. The work meant setting production norms, allocating scarce foreign exchange among competing plants, appointing and removing managers, and answering for output.

It was an economy losing its technicians to exile and its spare parts to embargo, and the administrative experience, recorded in ministry documents and speeches, is the raw material from which the budgetary finance argument was assembled.

Alongside the administration ran the writing of the manual. In 1960 he published Guerrilla Warfare, the codification of the Sierra experience, and its propositions, examined next, travelled further than anything else he wrote. The sequence is worth pausing on. In the same years, the same man was constructing, in parallel, a theory of how revolutions are made and a theory of how the society they make should be run, and the two theories share a deep assumption: that consciousness, will, and organisation can substitute for missing material conditions, in the mountains by refusing to wait for a revolutionary situation, in the economy by refusing to wait for abundance before abolishing market incentives. Voluntarism is the thread, and whether it is a heroic thread or a fatal one is the question the rest of this article is organised to answer, once for each corpus, on each corpus's own evidence.

## Agrarian reform: the apprenticeship at the land institute

Between the fortress and the bank, Guevara passed through the institution that taught him the Cuban economy's real structure: the agrarian reform institute created to execute the land reform of 1959, where he directed the department charged with industrialising the agricultural sector. The posting is usually treated as a way station. It was, in fact, his first sustained encounter with the problem that would dominate his economic thought: how a state that has seized the commanding heights actually runs what it has seized. The reform expropriated the great estates, above all the sugar latifundia and the cattle ranches of the foreign companies, and created in their place a vast state agricultural sector alongside cooperatives and small private farmers. Administering that transformation meant confronting, daily, the questions the Great Debate would later formalise. Should the new state farms buy their supplies and sell their cane as autonomous units, keeping accounts and answering for surpluses? Or were they limbs of a single national enterprise, to be supplied and directed from the centre against a plan? The institute practised, in agriculture, the centralised model he would later defend for industry, and the experience convinced him that the centralised model was not merely an administrative option but the form corresponding to the revolution's nature.

The agrarian apprenticeship also fixed his sense of where Cuba's wealth and vulnerability lay. Sugar dominated everything: the harvest calendar organised the nation's labour year, the export earnings paid for the imports of food and machinery, and the dependence on a single crop sold into a single market structured the island's subordination first to the United States and then, after the rupture, to the Soviet Union. His later insistence, in the Algiers speech, that trade between socialist states reproduced imperialist patterns was not an abstraction for the man who had administered the sector whose product was traded on those terms. The economics of the unknown Guevara begins here, in the land institute's ledgers, with the discovery that a revolution can own everything and still be governed by the prices at which its sugar is bought.

## The bank and the signature: money distrusted in office

The presidency of the National Bank, which he assumed in November 1959, is remembered for a gesture: he signed the new currency with his nom de guerre, Che, and the signature became a parable in both literatures, proof to admirers of his contempt for money's majesty and proof to critics of his unseriousness in office. The gesture deserves a slower reading. A revolutionary who believed that money under socialism was destined to wither into an accounting token was, in the signature, performing his doctrine: the bank's authority did not descend from the mystique of the signature but from the state behind it, and a nickname scrawled where engravers expected a statesman's flourish announced that the institution had changed its nature. His critics' reading is not wrong either; the gesture announced, to a financial community deciding whether to flee, exactly the cavalier relation to monetary convention that accelerated the flight. Both readings capture the same trait that runs through the whole corpus: a preference for the symbolic act that declares a principle over the prudent act that preserves a mechanism, visible in the mountains, in the ministry, and finally in Bolivia.

The bank presidency itself was brief and mostly custodial: the reserves were haemorrhaging as capital fled, the old banking system was being absorbed, and the real decisions of economic direction were migrating to the planning organs and to the ministries. What the posting gave him was a vantage over the island's monetary skeleton at the moment of its transformation, and a confirmation of the instinct he carried into the Great Debate: that money is never merely a technique, that whoever designs a society's monetary arrangements is designing the motives of its members, and that a revolution careless of that design will be redesigned by it.

## The manual's craft: what the book teaches besides strategy

Guerrilla Warfare repays reading as a craftsman's notebook even by readers who reject its strategy outright, and separating the craft from the doctrine is part of grading the corpus fairly. The bulk of the book is given to the unglamorous logistics of irregular war: the selection of terrain that favours the weak, the organisation of supply through the population, the care of the wounded with improvised means, the manufacture of necessities in the field, the discipline of the column on the march, the treatment of prisoners as a political instrument, the education of the fighter, the relations between the armed band and the peasants among whom it lives, whose support the book treats, correctly, as the single resource on which everything else depends. Woven through the logistics is an ethic: the guerrilla as social reformer, the prohibition of theft from the population, the obligation to pay for what is taken, the insistence that the band's conduct is its propaganda. As a record of how a small force survived in the Sierra and of the standards its commanders enforced, the book retains documentary value that its strategic refutation does not touch.

The separation matters because the book's afterlife fused what the assessment must divide. The imitators took the strategy and skipped the craft, above all the ethic of embedding and the patience of supply, which could not be hurried and therefore contradicted the licence they wanted. The book's defenders, conversely, point to the craft's wisdom as though it redeemed the strategy. Neither move is available to a careful reader. A manual can be excellent on the care of the wounded and wrong on the creation of conditions, and this one is. Its craft sections belong to the literature of irregular war, where they are read by professionals of several allegiances. Its strategic propositions belong to the history of an error, where they are read as a warning. The same binding holds both, and the binding is the only thing about the book that misleads.

## The foco theory: a manual and its three propositions

Guerrilla Warfare is a short book with a double character. Most of it is craft: how to choose terrain, organise supply, treat the wounded, conduct propaganda, punish informers, and behave toward the peasantry among whom the band lives. Woven through the craft is a strategic doctrine, stated most compactly in the book's opening chapter, which detaches itself from the Cuban case and offers itself as general theory. The doctrine rests on three propositions. First, that popular forces can defeat a regular army: a small, mobile, politically conscious armed nucleus can, given favourable terrain and popular support, grow into an army capable of destroying the military apparatus of a dictatorship. Second, that it is not necessary to wait for all the conditions of revolution to exist before beginning: the insurrectional foco can itself create those conditions through its action, so that the subjective decision to begin substitutes for the objective ripeness that orthodox strategy demanded. Third, that in the underdeveloped Americas the countryside, rather than the city, is the basic terrain of the armed struggle, since the repressive apparatus is weakest there, the peasantry is the majority, and the urban working class is small and penetrated by the state.

Each proposition had a polemical target, and naming the targets clarifies what was actually being claimed. The first answered the military establishments and the sceptics who held that modern armies were invulnerable to irregulars. The third answered the orthodox communist parties of the continent, whose strategy centred on urban organisation, electoral alliances, and the long accumulation of forces under legal or semi-legal conditions. The second proposition answered the deepest orthodoxy of all, the inherited Marxist sequence in which objective conditions, economic crisis, mass radicalisation, the decomposition of the ruling order, must mature before revolutionary action becomes anything other than adventurism. Guevara's claim was that the sequence could be run backward: action first, conditions after, the foco as a small motor that sets the big motor of the masses turning. It is this proposition, the reversal of the classical view of revolutionary conditions, on which the theory stands or falls, because the other two are claims about tactics and terrain while this one is a claim about history.

### What did the foco theory claim a small armed nucleus could do?

Create the conditions of its own success. Where orthodox strategy required a matured revolutionary situation before armed action could be more than a gesture, the foco doctrine held that a disciplined nucleus, correctly placed in rural terrain and correctly behaved toward the population, could generate the crisis it needed.

It would provoke repression that radicalised the uncommitted, demonstrate the regime's vulnerability, and build, through its own growth, the army and the political authority that a revolution requires.

The manual itself contains qualifications that its later readers, and arguably its codifiers, stripped away. Guevara wrote that where a government had come to power through some form of popular vote and maintained constitutional appearances, the guerrilla outbreak could not be promoted, because the possibilities of peaceful struggle had not been exhausted. He insisted that the guerrilla fighter must be an armed reformer embedded in the peasantry, that the struggle required the population's support as its oxygen, that the enemy's political decomposition was a precondition of final victory, and that the foco was the embryo of a broader movement rather than its substitute. These qualifications matter for the fairness of the assessment. The theory as written is a theory of a specific configuration: a dictatorship without legitimacy or reform capacity, a countryside with grievances and weak state presence, a nucleus disciplined enough to earn support, and time for the nucleus to grow. The theory as practised by its imitators was often a theory of the armed act alone, and the distance between the two is the subject of a later section. But the qualifications do not dissolve the core claim, and the core claim is the second proposition. Even in its careful form, the doctrine asserted that the subjective factor could lead and the objective factor would follow, and it asserted this as a generalisable lesson of the Cuban experience rather than as a description of one island's luck.

### What separates a foco from an ordinary guerrilla band?

Its political function. A band fights; a foco, in the doctrine, is the embryonic form of a revolutionary army and a revolutionary power, carrying within itself the programme, the discipline, and the claim to legitimacy of the future state, so that its military and political growth are one process.

The guerrilla unit was therefore required to administer justice in its zone, to teach, to heal, to embody the new order in miniature, rather than merely to raid and withdraw.

## The standing opponent: the orthodox parties and their case

The foco theory was written against an opponent, and the opponent's case deserves the same full-strength treatment this profile gives the economic debate's losing side, because the parties Guevara argued against were not the cowardly bureaucracies of the codification's caricature. The orthodox communist parties of Latin America had histories written in persecution: they had been banned, raided, and driven underground by dictatorships for decades; they had built unions in the teeth of employers and police; their leaders had served prison terms and their militants had been murdered in numbers that gave them a standing to discuss sacrifice with anyone. Their strategy, the patient accumulation of forces, the united front, the combination of legal and illegal work, the subordination of armed action to a mature political crisis, was not invented to excuse inaction. It was the deposit of their own defeats, above all the defeats of premature insurrections in earlier decades, whose costs they had paid and remembered in their institutional bones.

Their case against the foco ran as follows. A revolution is not an event but a relation of forces, built over years in workplaces, villages, universities, and barracks, and no armed shortcut exists because the shortcut's product, a band without a movement, faces the state alone. The peasantry, on whom the doctrine staked everything, is the most dispersed and politically heterogeneous class on the continent, capable of rebellion and of fierce localism, of loyalty to a caudillo and of informing on strangers, and it follows organisation that serves its interests over time rather than proclamation that arrives with rifles. The imperial power to the north, finally, would not permit a second Cuba, and any strategy that ignored the certainty of its intervention, its advisers, its counterinsurgency doctrines then being written, was a strategy for producing martyrs. Every element of this case was vindicated by the continental record. The parties were wrong about many things in those decades, about the Soviet Union above all, and their caution decayed, in places, into a conservatism that abandoned the field of rebellion to the very adventurism they condemned. But on the question the foco posed, the question of whether the nucleus creates the conditions, the standing opponent was right and the famous theory was wrong, and the assessment of Guevara's guerrilla thought is incomplete until the men he dismissed as bureaucrats are given their due as the better strategists of their continent's reality.

## The reversal examined: conditions, will, and the Cuban exception

The second proposition deserves a slower examination, because it is the point at which Guevara's guerrilla writing touches a genuine theoretical nerve and the point at which it does its damage. The classical position he reversed was not a bureaucratic excuse, though it had often decayed into one in the hands of parties that used the absence of conditions as a reason for permanent postponement. In its serious form, the doctrine of conditions held that revolutions are made by masses in motion, that masses move when the old order's contradictions become unliveable, and that a vanguard's task is to organise, educate, and lead a movement whose energy it does not manufacture. The voluntarist reversal asserts that the energy can be manufactured by example: that the spectacle of a nucleus fighting, surviving, and winning recruits the masses, discredits the regime, and polarises the middle strata until the revolutionary situation exists because the foco has summoned it.

Cuba fitted the reversal suspiciously well, and the fit should have been a warning. The conditions the foco supposedly created were, on inspection, largely present in advance: a dictatorship that had seized power by coup and forfeited constitutional legitimacy, a rural population with sharp grievances over land and the seasonal brutality of the sugar economy, an urban opposition already in motion, a regime whose American sponsor withdrew support at the critical hour, and an army whose morale and cohesion were rotting from corruption. The foco did not create these conditions; it exploited them with exemplary skill, and supplied the one thing they lacked, a credible armed alternative around which the regime's collapse could organise itself. Generalised honestly, the Cuban lesson is that a disciplined nucleus can detonate a crisis that already exists. Generalised as Guevara stated it, the lesson became that the nucleus could substitute for the crisis, and on that stronger claim the continental record would render its verdict.

There is a further asymmetry worth naming. The orthodox doctrine of conditions, whatever its abuses, contained a built-in protection for the movement's own people: it told militants not to spend their lives where the ground was unprepared. The reversal removed the protection while keeping the moral prestige of sacrifice. A theory that instructs small groups that their action creates its own justification, and that waiting is a form of betrayal, places the burden of its errors on the bodies of its believers. When the doctrine's own author carried it to Bolivia, he carried it into a country whose government had been installed by a revolution that had already distributed land, whose peasantry was therefore bound to the state by the very reform the foco promised, and whose communist party declined to support the venture. The conditions were not merely absent; several of them were inverted. The theory, applied by its author with full knowledge of the manual's qualifications, could not save him from its own central proposition, because the proposition taught that absence of conditions was the normal starting point rather than a reason to stop.

## Codification: Debray and the hardening of a doctrine

The theory's transmission to the continent ran through a codifier. The French philosopher Régis Debray, who had travelled to Cuba and interviewed its leaders, published in 1967 a short, brilliant, and fateful systematisation, Revolution in the Revolution?, which converted the Cuban experience into a portable strategic doctrine for Latin America. Debray's version sharpened everything. The guerrilla foco was declared the party in embryo, superior in principle to the political apparatus of the orthodox parties, which were portrayed as bureaucratic brakes on the struggle. The distinction between the military and political leadership of the movement was collapsed into the armed nucleus itself. The long work of mass organisation before the outbreak, which the orthodox strategy prescribed and which even the Cuban practice had partially honoured through its urban networks, was demoted to a distraction. Where Guevara's manual had been a practitioner's notes with qualifications, Debray produced a doctrine with the qualifications footnoted and the claims promoted to laws.

The distinction between the manual and the codification is not pedantry, because the continental generation that took up arms in the following years read the doctrine largely in its codified form, and because Guevara himself, whatever his private reservations, lent the codification his authority by association and by silence. Foquismo, as the doctrine came to be called, acquired the character of a licence: if the foco creates the conditions, then the absence of a mass movement, of a party, of a crisis, is no argument against beginning; indeed the beginning is the argument. Groups across the continent, many of them composed of the bravest and most serious young militants of their countries, acted on the licence in conditions wildly unlike the Sierra Maestra: against elected governments, in countries with organised working classes and legal lefts, in countrysides where the peasantry was bound to the state by recent reform or divided by language and region from the urban students who arrived to liberate it. The results constitute the record examined below.

### Why did Debray's codification change the theory's meaning?

Because systematisation is selection. The manual had been a practitioner's account in which tactical craft, political embedding, and strategic claim were inseparably mixed, and its hedges were load-bearing parts of the argument. The codification extracted the strategic claim, demoted the hedges, and elevated the armed nucleus over the political movement.

What circulated as the theory was therefore harder, purer, and far more permissive than the text it claimed to summarise.

Before the continental record, one episode from Guevara's own practice belongs in the file, because it is the theory's first failure under its author's hand and he knew it. In 1965 he disappeared from Cuban public life and led a Cuban expedition to the Congo, intending to support a rebellion in that country's east and to demonstrate that the foco method could be exported beyond Latin America. The expedition failed completely: the local rebellion was fragmented and unserious, the Cuban force could neither train it into coherence nor fight in its place, disease and demoralisation consumed the column, and after some months Guevara withdrew his men across Lake Tanganyika in defeat. His diary of the episode, unsparing toward the Congolese leadership, toward his own illusions, and toward the gap between the theory's premises and the terrain's realities, is among the most honest documents he wrote. The Congo matters here for one reason. It showed its author, two years before Bolivia, that a foco inserted into a struggle it did not grow from could not manufacture the conditions the doctrine promised, and it showed him this in the hardest currency. That he nonetheless proceeded to Bolivia is the biographical fact on which any charitable reading of the theory must eventually break: the doctrine had ceased, for its author, to be a hypothesis and had become a commitment that contrary experience could inform but not revoke.

## The Tricontinental message: two, three, many Vietnams

The last major text of the life was written in hiding and published in Havana in the spring of 1967, while its author was already in Bolivia: the message to the Tricontinental, the gathering of the revolutionary movements of Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Its argument is the foco doctrine universalised and its rhetoric is the most uncompromising he ever signed. The enemy is one, the imperial system headed by the United States; its strength is finite and its commitments are multiplying; the duty of the revolutionary world is to multiply them further, to open fronts until the system's resources are dispersed beyond its capacity, in the message's famous arithmetic of creating two, three, many Vietnams. The text dismisses the peaceful road where the system can strangle it, dismisses the disputes of the socialist camp as a luxury the dying cannot afford, and closes with the affirmation, quoted wherever his words are quoted, that the revolutionary's lot is to die or to triumph, and that the struggle itself is the only certain good.

As theory the message is the doctrine at its most exposed, since it generalises from the Cuban exception to a planetary strategy in a single step, and the objections assembled in this profile apply to it with compound interest: fronts are not created by proclamation, the system's resources were not finite in the way the arithmetic required, and the movements that answered the call paid for the generalisation in the currency this article has already counted. As a document of its author it is indispensable, because it shows the will that the assessment must finally judge: a man who had seen the Congo fail and the camp fracture, who knew the parties' objections and the economists' objections and had answered neither to his own satisfaction, and who concluded not that the premise needed revision but that the hour needed more of the premise, applied by more hands, at more points, beginning with his own. The message and the Bolivian diary were being written in the same months. Posterity reads them together, the trumpet and the autopsy, and the tension between them is the truest portrait of the theory's author that the corpus affords.

## The Congo diary: failure examined by its author

The Congo expedition's diary, which its author kept with the intention of eventual publication and which appeared decades later, is the most self-critical document in the corpus, and its testimony belongs in the record of the theory because it shows the doctrine failing in its author's own hands and being honestly described in the failing. The expedition's premise had been the foco premise exported: a seasoned nucleus, inserted beside a local rebellion, would stiffen it, train it, and by its example create the army the rebellion lacked. What the diary records is the premise's inversion. The local movement's leaders were absent for long stretches, negotiating abroad or avoiding the front; its fighters would not submit to the training regime the Cubans imposed; the population, ground between the rebellion's exactions and the government's reprisals, wanted quiet more than liberation; and the Cuban force, unable to fight in the population's place without becoming an occupying army, consumed itself in garrison duties, disease, and the corrosion of waiting. Guevara's judgements in the diary fall on everyone: on the Congolese leadership's frivolity, on his own men's declining morale, on the African revolution's unreadiness, and, between the lines, on the theory that had told him a nucleus could substitute for all of it.

The diary's final assessment, that the episode was a story of failure from which the lessons must be extracted without consolation, is the sentence of a man whose honesty survived his doctrine. It did not, however, revise the doctrine. The lessons he extracted were lessons about personnel, preparation, and the choice of allies, the secondary variables, while the primary variable, the premise that the nucleus creates its conditions, was carried intact out of Africa and into Bolivia. The pattern is familiar in the history of doctrine and worth naming plainly: a theory whose failures are always attributed to its execution becomes unfalsifiable in practice, since every refutation is reclassified as a misuse. Guevara's greatness as a witness and his weakness as a theorist meet in the Congo diary, on the same pages, in the same hand. He saw everything except the conclusion, and he wrote down everything he saw, which is why the document remains valuable to readers who draw the conclusion for him.

## The continental record: foquismo attempted

The record of the foco strategy outside Cuba can be summarised without cruelty and without evasion. In country after country during the 1960s and after, rural focos were established by militants acting on the doctrine, and in country after country they were isolated, hunted, and destroyed by security forces, usually within a short span, usually without igniting the mass rising the theory predicted. Venezuela's armed struggle, among the most substantial, wound down in defeat and a negotiated return to politics by its surviving organisations. Guatemala's guerrilla movements, born in a country whose reform government Guevara had watched fall, persisted for decades at enormous cost to the population among whom they operated without approaching power. In Peru, in Colombia's varied insurgent landscape, in Argentina, in Brazil, in Bolivia itself, the pattern repeated with local variations: the nucleus did not create the conditions; the conditions, where they existed, were not of the kind the nucleus could use; and the states against which the focos were launched, learning quickly and often assisted from Washington, proved far more capable of isolating a rural band than Batista's rotting dictatorship had been.

Two qualifications keep the summary honest. The first is that armed struggle in Latin America was not one thing, and movements that survived for long periods generally did so by ceasing to be focos in the doctrinal sense: building mass organisations, embedding in real social conflicts, negotiating, adapting, becoming political armies rather than detonating nuclei. Survival, where it occurred, was purchased by abandoning the theory's distinctive claim. The second qualification is that the failure was not primarily one of courage or even of craft. The militants of the focos were often formidably brave and sometimes skilled. The failure was theoretical in the strict sense: a false proposition about the relation between armed action and revolutionary conditions, acted on with complete sincerity by people who paid for its falsity themselves. That is what makes the record an argument rather than merely a tragedy, and it is why the objection stated in the next section carries the weight it does.

### What went wrong in the Congo expedition?

Nearly everything the doctrine assumed. The rebellion the Cubans arrived to assist was fragmented among rival leaders, its fighters lacked the discipline the method presupposed, and the expedition could neither substitute itself for a local movement nor conjure one into existence, so after months of futility Guevara withdrew the force.

Disease and demoralisation had consumed the column in the interval, the population's grievances never organised themselves around the inserted nucleus, and the episode demonstrated in practice that a foco cannot be airlifted into conditions it has not grown from.

## Bolivia: the author's own application

In late 1966 Guevara entered Bolivia in disguise, with false papers and shaved head, to establish the foco that was supposed to open a continental revolution: Bolivia's central position, its borders with five countries, its impoverished mining proletariat, and its history of upheaval made it, in the doctrine's geography, the weak link from which the struggle would spread. A small force of Cubans, Bolivians, and a few others assembled at a farm at Ñancahuazú in the country's southeast and began operations in early 1967. Almost every condition the manual itself had specified was absent or inverted. The Bolivian Communist Party, whose support the plan assumed, refused it; its leader Mario Monje met Guevara, demanded command of the venture as the price of participation, and withdrew when the demand was refused, leaving the foco without the party's networks, safe houses, or recruits. The peasantry of the region, beneficiaries of the land reform that had followed Bolivia's own revolution of 1952, regarded the foreign-led column with suspicion and in instances informed on it; the foco's promise of land redistribution addressed a grievance the state had already, however imperfectly, answered. The government in La Paz was not a Batista-style dictatorship in decomposition but a military regime with sufficient cohesion to respond, and it responded with an army trained and advised by the United States, which hunted the column through the ravines of the southeast as the foco shrank, split, and lost its communications, its supplies, and finally its cohesion.

Guevara's Bolivian diary, published after his death, records the disintegration month by month with a clinician's honesty: the desertions, the ambushes, the failure to recruit a single peasant in the zone of operations across long stretches, the asthma attacks that left him riding a mule while commanding a column that no longer believed in its own geography. In October 1967 the remnant was cornered in the Quebrada del Yuro. Guevara was captured, wounded, and held overnight in the schoolhouse of the village of La Higuera, where, on orders from the Bolivian high command, he was executed the following day. The body was displayed to the press, flown to Vallegrande, and buried in secret; the grave was located three decades later and the remains transferred to a memorial at Santa Clara in Cuba. The foco theory's author died at thirty-nine, applying his doctrine in conditions his own manual should have told him to refuse, and the manner of the death completed the myth that the record of the life cannot sustain.

### What conditions did the manual itself require before a foco was legitimate?

On the manual's own pages, several: a regime that had closed the possibilities of peaceful struggle, a peasantry whose support could be won and held, an enemy whose political decomposition made final victory conceivable, and time, the slow time of embedding and building the zone before the enemy concentrated against the nucleus.

Governments resting on a popular vote and maintaining constitutional forms were declared improper targets on those same pages. Bolivia offered the foco none of these in usable form, which is why the campaign reads as the doctrine overriding its own safeguards.

## The hidden year: disguises, false papers, and the road to the ravine

Between the Congo withdrawal and the Bolivian landing stretches the strangest year of the life, a year of concealment in which the most famous face in the revolutionary world was hidden by surgery, disguise, and false documents, first in Africa, then in Europe, while its owner read, wrote the critical notes, and prepared the campaign that would kill him. The episode is usually told as adventure. It belongs in a theoretical profile for what it reveals about the doctrine's hold. A man in his late thirties, with a family, a continent's admiration, and a secure immortality already banked, chose to erase himself, to become a balding businessman with a passport in another name, for the sake of re-entering the mountains as an anonymous combatant in a country not his own. The choice was not madness and was not, in his own accounting, even a sacrifice: it was the doctrine's syllogism completed in a biography. If the nucleus creates the conditions, then the experienced nucleus is the scarcest resource of the world revolution, and its owner's comfort is an embezzlement. He had written the premise; the premise, in the hidden year, collected its author's life as its due, a year before the ravine collected the remainder.

The preparations also displayed the theory's characteristic blindness at the level of logistics. The Bolivian venture was assembled with the support of the Cuban state he had resigned from, through networks of couriers, safe houses, and funds that constituted exactly the patient organisational infrastructure the doctrine taught its believers to despise as secondary. The foco that would create its own conditions arrived in Bolivia carried by the most elaborate support apparatus any Latin American insurgency had ever enjoyed, and the apparatus's existence refuted, in advance and in practice, the self-sufficiency the doctrine claimed. When the apparatus was rolled up by the Bolivian security forces in the campaign's first months, the foco stood revealed in its actual condition: a few dozen foreigners and locals in a ravine, without supply, without intelligence, without the population, exactly as naked as the theory had always promised it could afford to be. The hidden year is thus the doctrine's perfect parable, complete in advance: total commitment, superb tradecraft, a premise false at the centre, and the bill presented to the bravest first.

## The objection at full strength: the destroyed generation

The strongest objection to the foco theory is not that it failed. Many honourable theories fail. The objection, stated at full strength by its named critics, is that the theory's central proposition was a licence for the destruction of the very people a revolutionary movement could least afford to lose, and that this outcome was not an accident of misuse but the predictable consequence of the proposition itself. The Bolivian party's Mario Monje made the objection from within the movement at the time, refusing to subordinate his organisation to a venture he judged adventurist, whatever the personal and political cost of the refusal. Régis Debray, the doctrine's own codifier, arrived at the objection by the hardest road: imprisoned in Bolivia after visiting the foco, he spent his subsequent career retreating from the codification, and his later writings on the Latin American armed struggle amount to a long self-indictment of the doctrine that had told a generation that the gun could substitute for the party, the mass organisation, and the patient construction of political power. Historians of the continental left, surveying the wreckage across two decades, have put the objection in its coldest form: foquismo licensed small armed groups to act without the mass organisation that alone could have protected them, drew the bravest militants of a generation out of the unions, universities, and parties where their work compounded, and delivered them, isolated and outnumbered, to security forces that the doctrine had taught them to despise as paper armies.

The objection extends beyond the dead. The focos, by presenting the state with armed challenges it could crush in isolation, repeatedly supplied regimes with the justification and the theatre for repression that fell on the unarmed movement as well: on unions dissolved, parties banned, universities occupied, and communities caught between an insurgency that taxed them and a counterinsurgency that terrorised them. The theory had promised that repression would radicalise the masses on the foco's behalf. In the event, repression more often atomised the masses, and the foco, too small to protect anyone, could not convert the atomisation into recruitment. A doctrine is answerable for its foreseeable applications, and the application of the second proposition by sincere believers, in country after country, was foreseeable in 1967 in a way it perhaps was not in 1960. That is the objection, and it is not answered by pointing to the manual's qualifications, since a theory whose qualifications are invariably discarded by its readers, including finally by its author in Bolivia, has its permissiveness built into its central claim rather than into its reception.

## The manual abroad: translations and the seminaries of the foco

The manual's career as a physical object is part of the doctrine's history. Printed in Havana in cheap editions, translated within a few years into the languages of every continent where insurgency was contemplated, carried in rucksacks and quoted in safe houses, Guerrilla Warfare achieved the circulation of a classic while its author was still alive to be embarrassed by the use made of it. Seminars were held on it in the training camps of movements he never met; passages were memorised like catechism by young men and women who would die applying propositions whose qualifying conditions they had been taught to skip, since the codification described earlier in this profile travelled with the book and usually arrived first. The phenomenon has a name in the history of texts: the manual became a relic that instructed, and relics are not read whole. They are kissed, quoted, and carried into battle opened at the page that blesses the battle already chosen.

The responsibility question, which the assessment must face squarely, is how much of the misreading the text invited. A fair collation shows the conditions present in the manual and prominent in its argument: the insistence on the population's support, on the exhaustion of legal roads, on the war's political direction. It also shows the reversal stated without its counterweight, the famous propositions numbered like theorems, in a genre, the handbook, whose whole rhetoric promises that the world can be reduced to instructions. An author who writes a handbook for other men's wars accepts a share of the handbook's fate, and Guevara, to his credit, seems to have known it: the later essays qualify, the Bolivian diary confesses, the message to the Tricontinental universalises anyway. The manual abroad is therefore the doctrine's parable in print. The conditions were in the book. The licence was on the cover. Posterity, which received both, chose the cover, and the choice was made easier by everything in the book that gleamed.

## Conditions stated and conditions absent: the answer and the concession

What can be said in the theory's defence should be said precisely, because precision is what separates an assessment from a verdict by slogan. The theory claimed, in its careful form, that a foco could create conditions where a specific configuration existed: a dictatorship that had exhausted peaceful recourse, a countryside in which the state's presence was thin and its legitimacy thinner, a peasantry with unmet grievances that an embedded and disciplined nucleus could organise, and an enemy in political decomposition. Where those conditions were absent, the theory's own text withheld its licence, most explicitly in the passage refusing the guerrilla outbreak against governments resting on popular vote and constitutional forms. The continental disaster was therefore, in part, a disaster of reading: movements applied the licence where the stated conditions did not hold, against elected governments, among peasants already bound to the state by reform, in the absence of any decomposition of the enemy. The theory claimed about its conditions something its imitators ignored, and the answer to the objection begins there.

It cannot end there, and this article concedes the remainder plainly. The record of the foco is close to uniformly negative. Outside the single Cuban case whose conditions the theory misdescribed, no foco created the revolutionary situation it promised; the movements that endured did so by abandoning the doctrine's core; and the theory's author, given a final opportunity to apply his own stated conditions as a test, proceeded in their absence and did not survive the application. A doctrine whose central proposition fails wherever it is tried, whose safeguards fail to restrain even its author, and whose costs are paid in the lives of its most committed adherents, has been refuted in the only court it recognised, the court of practice. What survives of the guerrilla writing is its craft and its testimony: a superb account of how one particular band survived in one particular set of mountains, and a permanent exhibit in the history of an idea, the idea that will can substitute for conditions, whose refutation the continent purchased at a price this profile has no intention of discounting.

## The body and the will: asthma as a formation

No account of the voluntarism is complete without the body that practised it first on itself. Guevara's asthma was severe from childhood: attacks that confined him to bed for days, a adolescence organised around the management of breath, sports pursued against the condition's advice, swimming and rugby and mountain climbing undertaken as deliberate provocations of a body that had announced its limits early. The biographers agree on the fact and divide on its weight, but the profile of a theorist of the will cannot treat it as decoration. A boy who cannot rely on his lungs learns one of two lessons, resignation or defiance, and the whole subsequent life is the second lesson elaborated into doctrine. The guerrilla who carried his medicines in his pack and fought through attacks in the mountains, the minister who cut cane until he collapsed, the diarist who records his own suffocation in the Bolivian ravine with a clinician's detachment, are the same pupil at later examinations.

The theoretical significance is double, and the assessment should hold both sides. On one side, the formation explains the doctrine's moral authority: the man who preached the conquest of circumstance by will had conquered, daily, the circumstance nearest to him, and his audiences knew it. On the other, it explains the doctrine's characteristic blindness, the projection of a private discipline onto peoples and economies. What a singular will can do against a body's rebellion, by terror of surrender and by the concentration of a lifetime, is not evidence for what a ministry can do against a society's habits, because the will in the first case commands the whole territory it governs and in the second commands almost none of it. The asthma made him, and it misled him, and the two propositions together are worth more than either alone: they are the personal form of the profile's central finding, that his strengths and his errors were not two lists but one quality, the refusal of limits, entered in different ledgers.

## The diplomat abroad: the rostrum as a second front

Between the ministry's ledgers, Guevara served his government as its most abrasive public voice abroad, and the diplomatic speeches belong to the corpus because they carry the economic argument onto the world's stages. At Punta del Este in 1961, before the hemisphere's finance ministers gathered to launch a programme of American-sponsored development aid, the Cuban delegate dissected the offer as an instrument of control and predicted, to the room, that the alliance being celebrated would fail on its own terms. At the United Nations trade and development conference in Geneva in 1964, he spoke for the developing world's grievances against the structure of world prices with a technician's command of the commodity detail. At the United Nations General Assembly later that year, he delivered the speech remembered for its defiance, answering the charges of the great powers with a catalogue of their own interventions and declaring Cuba's alignment with the oppressed of every continent. The performances were consistent with the doctrine: a refusal to conduct diplomacy as the management of appearances, a preference for stating the structural case at maximum clarity regardless of the audience's comfort.

The diplomatic record matters to the assessment for a reason beyond colour. It shows the economic thought operating at its proper scale. The man who argued in Havana that a state firm must not sell to its sister firm argued in Geneva and Algiers that a socialist state must not sell to its brother state on the world's terms either; the unit of analysis changed, the principle did not. Forms are not neutral; exchange on unequal terms forms unequal relations; a camp or a country that accepts the forms accepts the relations. His diplomacy was the budgetary finance system with a flag, and its reception abroad, admiration among the poor countries' delegations, fury among the great powers, a certain embarrassment among his Soviet allies, prefigured the Algiers explosion, where the principle was finally turned on the allies themselves. A profile that omitted the rostrum years would miss the bridge between the ministry and the speech that ended his career in government.

## The farewell letter and the vanishing

In the spring of 1965, Guevara disappeared. He had returned from a long tour of Africa and Asia, delivered the Algiers speech on the way, published the essay on socialism and man, and then withdrawn from public view so completely that rumour placed him dead, imprisoned, or purged. In October of that year, at a ceremony in Havana, Fidel Castro read aloud a farewell letter in which Guevara resigned his offices, his rank, and his Cuban citizenship, declaring that other lands required his modest efforts and that he left behind nothing material for his family and a clear conscience for himself. The letter's most quoted line renounces everything; its least quoted feature is its structure, which is that of a will. He was settling accounts before an enterprise he expected might kill him, and disposing of his reputation in advance by assigning the credit for the revolution to its collective authorship.

The vanishing was the foco doctrine applied to a biography. Cuba's revolution, in his judgement, had entered its administrative phase, in which his talents were surplus and his presence, after Algiers, a diplomatic liability; the doctrine he had written taught that a revolutionary's place is where the nucleus is smallest and the conditions hardest; and so the minister of industries became, successively, an anonymous adviser in the Congo and an anonymous patient in Bolivia. The farewell letter should be read alongside the critical notes he was composing in the same period, because the two documents are the private and public faces of one conclusion: that the socialist camp as constituted was travelling in a direction he could neither endorse nor, from inside its governments, arrest. He chose the mountains over the ministry not from romantic preference alone, though the preference was real, but because the doctrine of his whole life left a man who believed what he believed with no honest post to hold. That the choice destroyed him is the record. That the choice was, on his premises, nearly compulsory is the biography's bleakest insight into those premises.

## The doctor in the administrator: diagnosis as a habit of mind

The medical training is usually treated as a biographical prelude, the profession abandoned for the revolution. It is better treated as a permanent habit of mind that the administrator carried into every office he held. The doctor of the motorcycle journey had learned to read a continent as a clinician reads a patient: the leper colony's separations, the mine's silicosis, the peasant's diet, presented themselves to him not as misfortunes but as symptoms, and a symptom, in the clinical habit, is an accusation directed at a cause. The Marxism he adopted in those years fitted the habit exactly, since it offered a pathology of the social body with the disease named and the therapy implied. His later writings keep the clinical signature. Economies have fevers and gangrenes in his metaphors; the revolution is a surgery; the new man is a convalescent; the enemy's system is a corpse that does not know it has died. Even the tribunals' terrible season has its clinical shadow in the record, the language of excision applied to the social body's infected parts, a metaphor whose human cost this profile has already entered in the plainest terms available to it.

The habit explains a virtue and a defect that run together through the work. The virtue is the diagnostic eye itself: the refusal to treat arrangements as natural, the insistence on asking what a form produces in the people inside it, which is the question that makes the Great Debate still worth reading when its systems are dust. The defect is the clinician's confidence transposed: the doctor who has named the disease may feel licensed to prescribe without the patient's consent and to interpret the patient's resistance as a symptom of the disease, and that transposition, from the clinic to the ministry and the mountain, is the precise mechanism by which the voluntarism's premises protected themselves from correction. A peasantry that declines the foco is, in the clinical frame, not a constituency with a judgement but a case with a prognosis. The profile has met that mechanism at every stage of the argument. It began, as so much in this life began, in a strength: in the young doctor's discovery that suffering has causes and that causes can be fought. The whole work is that discovery's expansion, and its limit is the discovery's shadow, the moment when the fighter of causes stops hearing the sufferers.

## The economist in office: from the Sierra to the ledger

Turn now to the corpus that this profile exists to recover. Between 1963 and 1965, in the journals and internal publications of revolutionary Cuba, a sustained technical argument took place over a question that sounds like bookkeeping and is in fact one of the deepest questions a socialist transition can face: by what rules should state enterprises relate to one another and to the state that owns them? The question arrived with the nationalisations. Once the commanding heights of the Cuban economy had passed into state hands, the new ministries inherited enterprises that had kept capitalist accounts, bought from one another at prices, borrowed from banks at interest, and measured their managers by surplus. The revolutionary state could run its firms as firms, preserving money, prices, credit, and enterprise autonomy inside public ownership. Or it could run the whole economy as a single enterprise, in which transfers between units are internal movements of a single balance sheet, money is an accounting token rather than a power, and the plan allocates directly. The choice was not administrative. It determined what socialism would mean in daily practice: whether a worker's effort would be summoned by wages and bonuses or by consciousness and exhortation, whether a factory's success would be counted in profit or in fulfilled assignments, whether the transition would use the law of value as its instrument or treat that law as the enemy to be extinguished.

Guevara, as minister of industries, took the second side and built it into a system. His antagonist was not a straw man but the other half of the Cuban economic leadership, together with the accumulated practice of the Soviet bloc, where enterprises operated under what was called economic calculus: self-financing units, holding their own funds, buying and selling among themselves, judged by profitability, paying interest on capital, and incentivising their workers materially. The argument ran in the open, in print, across two years, with contributions solicited from European Marxist economists of the first rank. It is known in the literature as the Great Debate, and its documentary record, minutes, articles, speeches, and rejoinders, is the most intellectually serious product of the Cuban revolution's first decade. That it remains unknown to the public that wears its protagonist's face is the standing irony this article is written against.

### What was the Great Debate on economic management?

an argument in Cuba between 1963 and 1965 over how a socialist economy should be run. Guevara held that enterprises should not be autonomous profit centres and that moral incentives should predominate; his opponents argued for enterprise self-financing on the Soviet model then in use.

The debate's stakes were disguised by its vocabulary, and the disguise has lasted. Words like budgetary financing and economic calculus sound like rival accounting conventions, and in one sense they are. But each accounting convention encoded a theory of the transition. The self-financing model held that the transition would be long, that scarcity would persist through it, that human beings would respond throughout it to material incentives as they always had, and that the economy should therefore harness self-interest inside public ownership rather than pretend to have abolished it, letting market categories wither gradually as abundance approached. The budgetary model held that the transition's whole point was the transformation of human beings and their relations, that every use of market mechanisms reproduced the consciousness the transition existed to overcome, and that the economy should therefore be organised from the start on the principles of the society it was travelling toward, with the plan, the single budget, and moral incentives doing the work that prices and profits did elsewhere. Between these two theories of time, one gradualist and one voluntarist, the same fault line runs that divided the foco from the orthodox parties. In economics as in guerrilla war, Guevara refused to wait.

### Who took part in the Great Debate, and where was it conducted?

In print, chiefly in Cuban journals devoted to foreign trade, planning, and economic theory, between 1963 and 1965. On one side stood Guevara and the officials of his ministry; on the other, Carlos Rafael Rodríguez and Alberto Mora, defending versions of the self-financing system then practised in the Soviet bloc.

Rodríguez was the revolution's most experienced economic administrator and Mora its minister of foreign trade. The editors solicited contributions from abroad, and two European Marxist economists of standing, Ernest Mandel and Charles Bettelheim, entered the argument in print, joining the Cuban administrators to the wider argument about markets and planning then running through the socialist world.

## The reader: an intellectual formation in the margins

Behind the minister and the guerrilla stands a reader, and the formation deserves a section because the corpus's character, its reach and its blind spots alike, is the character of his reading. Guevara read the way he did everything else, in campaigns: the youthful journeys were also reading tours, the asthma enforced long sedentary stretches that he filled with books, and by the Sierra years his notebooks show a mind stocking itself deliberately across poetry, philosophy, history, and the Marxist canon in several languages. In Mexico he studied with exiles; in the mountains he ran the column's school and his own; in office he annotated the Soviet manual with the fury recorded in the critical notes; in the hidden year he worked through the philosophers again, filling notebooks on the young Marx and the humanist tradition that his new man presupposed. The corpus's erudition is real, and so is its asymmetry. He read the tradition he served exhaustively and the traditions arrayed against it selectively: the economists of the rival schools appear in his pages mostly as the manual's vulgarisers, the strategists of his own continent's parties mostly as foils. A reader forms his questions from his shelf, and the questions this shelf never posed, about the centre's information, about the administrators' formation, about the enemy's capacity to learn, are precisely the questions on which the work founders.

The marginalia habit also explains the corpus's survival value. Because he read with a pen, arguing in the margins of the camp's own scripture, the critical notes exist: a minister's running quarrel with the doctrine he administered, preserved by the habit of a lifelong annotator. The same habit stocked the diaries with judgements intended for eventual readers and preserved, in the Bolivian diary's bleak entries, the evidence against his own strategy in his own hand. Posterity's advantage over his contemporaries is that it can read the margins. The assessment of the unknown Guevara should therefore end its survey of the work where the work itself began, at the desk of a reader whose notes outargued his conclusions, and whose conclusions the world preferred to his notes because conclusions fit on a poster and notes do not.

## The ministry in detail: institutes, norms, and the war on waste

The system was not a pamphlet; it was an administration, and its texture is recoverable from the ministry's practice. Industry was organised into consolidated enterprises, groupings of factories by branch under single managements, which were themselves the budgetary system's units: they received their assignments, their materials, and their wage funds from the centre, and reported their costs upward in the uniform accounting the system prescribed. Within the ministry, a permanent campaign was waged on costs, on the inventories of idle machinery and hoarded materials that the old economy's habits had scattered through the factories, on the quality failures that a sellers' market of scarcity concealed. Guevara's speeches to his own apparatus, preserved in the ministry's records, are remarkable for their tone: impatient, pedagogical, contemptuous of excuses, and fixated on the moral meaning of administrative detail. A wasted tonne of imported steel was not, in his rhetoric to the managers, an accounting variance. It was a theft from the people's accumulation, committed by the very stratum entrusted with preventing it, and he told them so in terms that made the ministry's internal life a continuous examination of conscience.

The texture explains both the system's achievements and its ceiling. Where the minister's attention fell, things moved: costs were hunted, hoards were surfaced, a certain austere honesty in reporting was enforced by the knowledge that the man at the top read the figures and despised flattery. But attention does not scale. A ministry of thousands of units cannot be examined into efficiency by one man's conscience, however sleepless, and the system's design offered no substitute mechanism that operated when the attention wandered, as it visibly did after his departure for the diplomatic tours and then for good. The administrators of the rival model drew the moral in the debate: that an economy needs disciplines which function on the minister's holidays. The defenders of the budgetary construction replied that the rival disciplines function, on the minister's holidays and on everyone's, by teaching the whole apparatus to serve its own indicators rather than the society. Both morals are true, and the ministry's daily life under its extraordinary chief is where a reader can watch them being true at once.

## The budgetary finance system: the economy as a single enterprise

Guevara's positive construction was called the budgetary finance system, and its logic is best grasped by following a single enterprise through it. Under budgetary finance, a state factory does not own its funds, does not hold a bank account it can treat as its own, does not borrow at interest, and does not sell its output to other state enterprises in any sense that enriches it. Its receipts flow to the state budget; its expenditures are drawn from the state budget against the plan; the transfers of goods between state units are recorded in money terms for accounting purposes, but the money is a unit of account, a measuring rod, not a fund that any manager commands. The enterprise's task is to fulfil its plan assignments at the lowest possible cost in real resources, and its success is judged by that fulfilment and by cost reduction, never by a surplus it retains, because it retains nothing. The economy, in the system's organising image, is one great enterprise, and the ministries are its workshops.

The construction was deliberate at every point, and each feature answered a feature of the rival model. Where self-financing gave the enterprise its own funds, budgetary finance abolished the funds, so that no unit could accumulate a separate interest. Where self-financing let prices between state units carry real consequences, budgetary finance reduced the internal price to a bookkeeping entry, so that allocation followed the plan's physical priorities rather than the accidents of inherited price structures. Where self-financing paid interest on capital to discipline its use, budgetary finance refused interest inside the state sector as a fiction, the state charging itself for the use of its own resources. Where self-financing measured the manager by profit, budgetary finance measured him by plan fulfilment and cost, and sought to motivate the worker not by the bonus but by the consciousness of building the new society, supplemented by collective and moral incentives: emulation, honours, voluntary labour, and the social recognition of the exemplary workplace.

Underneath the accounting sat a philosophical claim, and Guevara stated it without embarrassment. The categories of capitalism, money, price, profit, interest, material incentive, are not neutral instruments that a transition can borrow indefinitely without consequence. They are, in his argument, the forms in which a particular kind of human being, the self-interested calculator of bourgeois society, reproduces himself daily. A transition that organises its economy through those forms will produce people fitted to those forms, and will therefore never arrive at the society whose members work from consciousness of social duty. The budgetary system was thus not an accounting preference but a wager about human formation: that the economy's operating rules are a school, and that a state serious about the new man must choose the curriculum deliberately, even at the cost of running its factories with blunter instruments than the market supplies.

### How did the budgetary finance system handle an enterprise's surplus?

It abolished the category at the level of the unit. A factory's receipts belonged to the state budget, not to the factory; its outlays were budgetary assignments against the plan; and the difference between what it produced and what it consumed never appeared as a fund at its own disposal.

Success was redefined accordingly, away from retained surplus and toward plan fulfilment at falling real cost, so that no manager could build a local empire on the unit's earnings and no unit could develop an interest distinct from the whole.

The system's difficulties were as structural as its virtues, and its author, to his credit, administered it long enough to meet them personally. A single-enterprise economy must decide, somewhere, what to produce and in what proportions, and without internal prices that carry information about scarcity, the decisions fall to the plan's constructors, who must somehow aggregate the needs of a whole society through administrative channels. Costs can be recorded without being felt; a workshop that overspends draws more from the budget rather than facing the discipline a fund of its own would impose, so the system's softness toward the wasteful unit mirrors, in administrative form, the softness it condemned in the market's treatment of the failing firm. Quality, assortment, and the thousand small adjustments by which an economy fits itself to its users all depend, in the budgetary model, on the vigilance of inspectors and the consciousness of producers, and consciousness proved to be a variable input, abundant in campaigns and scarce in the long routine of ordinary production. These difficulties are the territory of the opposing case, which deserves, and now receives, its full strength.

## Why Cuba argued: the open season of the journals

A puzzle precedes the debate's content: why it happened at all. Socialist states have usually settled their economic doctrines by appointment and announcement, not by open journal controversy among serving ministers, and the Cuban exception requires explanation because it conditions how the documents should be read. The revolution's first years were institutionally fluid in a way later decades were not: the governing coalition still contained several revolutionary currents, the party that would unify them was still being assembled, and the leadership practised, in those years, a genuine if bounded pluralism of counsel, in which a minister could lose an argument in print without losing his post. The journals themselves, new foundations of a revolution that prized literacy and polemic, had editors willing to host a quarrel whose outcome the leadership had not predetermined. And the subject forced the opening: the island had to build an economic administration from nothing, under blockade, with two models on offer and cadres trained in neither, so that argument was not a luxury but a method of government in a state improvising its own machinery.

The season closed as the institutions hardened. The debate's later instalments appeared under increasing constraint, the controversy was settled by the alignments described earlier in this profile rather than by a concluding article, and no Cuban journal would again host a dispute of that candour among men of that rank. The documents therefore preserve something rarer than a doctrine: a moment when a revolutionary state thought in public, with its premises exposed and its ablest administrators divided in the open. Readers who approach the exchange as a museum of a dead system miss its documentary value. It is the record of the transition question debated at the only level at which it has ever been honestly debated, by the people who would have to live with the answer, before the answer was imposed by the necessities that ended the season. That the season was brief, and that its brevity was itself one of the necessities, is the melancholy frame in which the whole exchange now sits.

## The inheritance: the textbook tradition the debaters shared

Both sides of the Great Debate argued inside a textual inheritance, and naming it clarifies why the argument took the shape it did. The Soviet Union had codified its economic doctrine in an official manual of political economy, the textbook whose successive editions taught the bloc's administrators that the law of value operated under socialism in a transformed manner, that enterprises required economic accountability, and that material incentives were a legitimate and necessary instrument of the transition. That manual was the self-financing side's scripture: Rodríguez and his colleagues were not improvising a Cuban deviation but applying the received doctrine of the socialist world, with its authority and its experience behind them. Guevara's side, by contrast, argued from the older texts against the manual: from the Critique of the Gotha Programme, where Marx's account of the first phase of communism, with its defects and its bourgeois right in distribution, could be read either as a warrant for the manual's patience or, as Guevara read it, as a warning against entrenching those defects into a system; and from the writings on the withering of commodity relations that the manual's tradition had learned to quote and ignore.

The inheritance explains the debate's peculiar courtesy and its peculiar ferocity. The courtesy came from the shared canon: both sides cited the same founders, acknowledged the same authorities, and conducted the dispute as an exegesis. The ferocity came from the same source, because in a tradition where legitimacy descends from texts, to lose the exegesis is to lose the right to govern, and every participant knew that the argument over interest rates was also an argument over who understood the founders. Guevara's critical notes, written later against the manual itself, were the debate's logical terminus: the moment when the exegete concluded that the scripture of the camp he served had codified the error, and that fidelity to the founders required war on their official interpreters. A reader who grasps the inheritance grasps why a dispute about factory accounting could carry, for its participants, the emotional charge of a schism.

## How the argument was conducted: journals, deadlines, and rejoinders

The Great Debate's form deserves attention, because the form is part of its distinction. This was not a conference or a purge. Nobody was arrested for an accounting position. The argument ran through the ordinary machinery of a revolutionary state's intellectual life: articles in the foreign trade journal, in the planning organs, in the theoretical reviews, circulated drafts, written rejoinders, and the solicited interventions of foreign specialists whose prestige both sides courted. Positions were attributed, signed, and answered by name. Guevara published his system's exposition as a considered article, on the budgetary finance system, laying out its principles with an administrator's specificity. His opponents replied in kind. The exchange lasted roughly two years, and it ended not with a verdict but with a dissolution: the reorganisation of the Cuban economy's institutions and the deepening of the Soviet alliance settled administratively what the journals had left open intellectually.

Compare the conduct of this argument with the conduct of doctrinal disputes elsewhere in the socialist world of the same decades, where the losing position in an economic controversy could cost its holder his post, his freedom, or his life, and the Cuban debate's civility becomes a datum in its own right. For two years, on the most sensitive question a planned economy faces, the revolutionary state argued with itself in public, at a high level, without the police. Defenders of the revolutionary record are entitled to the point. Critics are entitled to the sequel: that the argument was never concluded, that the side which lost the institutional settlement lost it to geopolitics rather than to refutation, and that the habit of public doctrinal argument did not survive the decade in which it flowered. Both entitlements belong in the profile, and the debate's documentary remains, minutes, articles, and all, are the proof that the unknown Guevara worked in a milieu that, briefly, let a minister lose an argument in print without losing his head.

## A worked example: two factories under two systems

The debate's abstractions become intelligible if a single transaction is followed through both models. Imagine a state steelworks supplying a state machine factory, in a country where both have passed into public ownership. Under self-financing, the steelworks sells its steel to the machine factory at a price. The price matters to both: it swells or starves the steelworks' own funds, from which it pays its workers' bonuses and finances its improvements; it enters the machine factory's costs, against which that factory's surplus, and its workers' rewards, are computed. Each management therefore watches the price, haggles over quality and delivery, economises on its inputs, and experiences the other factory as a counterparty whose interests partly oppose its own. The system's defenders called this realism: the opposition of interests exists in scarcity whether it is acknowledged or not, and pricing it makes it visible and governable. Its critics called it the reproduction, inside the revolution's own house, of the market's psychology: two groups of workers set against each other by an accounting fiction, each learning to flourish at the other's expense.

Under budgetary finance, the same steel travels without a sale. The plan assigns the tonnage; the transfer is recorded at an accounting price so that costs can be compared across periods and plants; no fund changes hands in any sense that either factory commands. The steelworks is judged on whether it delivered the assigned tonnage at or below the planned cost in fuel, ore, and labour. The machine factory is judged likewise on its assignments. Neither can enrich itself from the transaction, and neither is disciplined by it either: if the steelworks wastes fuel, the budget absorbs the waste; if its steel is poor, the machine factory's complaint travels upward through the ministry rather than sideways through a refused payment. Guevara's wager was that the discipline lost could be replaced by the plan's norms, by inspection, and by the producers' consciousness. His opponents' wager was that it could not, and that the attempt would cost more in waste than the market's psychology cost in solidarity. Every finance ministry in the socialist world was, in those years, placing one of these two bets. Cuba's journals are where the bets were argued most honestly, and the worked example is why: because at the level of two factories, both systems show their real faces, and neither face is a caricature.

## The opposing case: self-financing in its proponents' own terms

The self-financing position in the Great Debate was not a capitulation to capitalism wearing a party card, and it must not be rendered as one. Its principal Cuban proponent, Carlos Rafael Rodríguez, was the revolution's most seasoned economic thinker, a man who had administered agriculture and planning and who understood scarcity not as a slogan but as the daily condition of a poor island. His argument, and the argument of Alberto Mora at foreign trade, ran as follows. The transition to socialism is a long historical period in which the productive forces remain inadequate to abundance. During that period, whether anyone wills it or not, goods are scarce, labour must be allocated and motivated, enterprises must economise on resources they did not create, and the social product must be measured and distributed by rules that function on bad days as well as good ones. The law of value, on this view, is not a capitalist conspiracy but the form taken by an unavoidable fact: that in scarcity, the labour embodied in things must be counted, compared, and economised, and any system that refuses the counting will do the counting badly, in queues, shortages, and arbitrary privilege, rather than openly in prices.

From that premise the institutional conclusions followed with the force of engineering. Enterprises should be self-financing: holding their own funds, buying their inputs, selling their outputs, covering their costs from their revenue, and retaining a share of their surplus for investment and for the funds from which workers are rewarded. Profit, cleansed of private appropriation, becomes in this scheme a measuring instrument, the single figure in which an enterprise's economy or waste announces itself to the centre without an army of inspectors. Interest on capital is the price that teaches managers that resources are not free. Material incentives are not a moral failure but the honest recognition that the worker of the transition is the worker history has actually produced, formed by the old society, responsive to his family's needs, and entitled to see his effort connected to his livelihood while consciousness is being educated by slower means. To demand that he work for moral incentives alone, the proponents argued, is to build the economy on the scarcest resource of all, heroic virtue, and to guarantee that when virtue tires, the economy will be disciplined by nothing at all.

The European reinforcement of this position carried its own weight. Charles Bettelheim, the French economist whose studies of planning were read across the socialist world, intervened to insist that the law of value could not be abolished by decree or by accounting convention, because it expressed real relations of scarcity and separated labour processes that no proclamation could fuse; enterprises under socialism remained, in his analysis, distinct centres of activity whose relations had to be mediated, and money with them. Ernest Mandel, from a different corner of the Marxist tradition, occupied a mediating position, sympathetic to Guevara's horror of market restoration yet insistent that the withering of commodity relations was a process of the whole transition and could not be jumped by administrative will. The proponents of self-financing could thus present their model not as the Soviet habit defended by inertia, though Soviet practice was its working example, but as the position consistent with the materialist premise both sides claimed: that consciousness follows being, slowly, and that an economy designed for human beings as they are will outperform, in output and in honesty, an economy designed for human beings as the orator requires them to be.

### What did Rodríguez mean by economic calculus?

The discipline of counting. Under economic calculus, the enterprise keeps its own accounts in money, confronts the real cost of its inputs including interest on the capital it uses, and is judged by whether its revenue covers its outgoings with a surplus for the social fund.

The calculus was defended not as a capitalist survival but as the only known instrument by which a planned economy can discover, continuously and without inspectors in every workshop, where resources are being wasted and where they are being well used.

Stated at that strength, the opposing case exposes the genuine wager in Guevara's position. He was not ignorant of the counting problem; he had run a ministry inside it. His claim was that the instruments of counting carry a pedagogy, that a transition which counts like capitalism will breed calculators, and that the shortfall in efficiency was a price worth paying for a population being formed for a different society. The proponents replied that the price would not be paid by the theorists but by the population itself, in shortages that no amount of consciousness could eat. Both sides were arguing about the same island, the same scarcity, and the same human material, and the argument has never been settled in principle, which is why the debate of those years remains the most valuable document either side produced. It is also why the debate cannot be scored by Cuba's later history alone: the island's eventual adoption of self-financing after Guevara's departure was a political settlement as much as an economic verdict, entangled with the Soviet alliance and its subsidies, and the assessment below keeps the two questions separate.

## Investment: who decides what gets built

The debate's least discussed question may be its most consequential: under each system, who decides what new capacity gets built, and by what criterion? Under self-financing, the answer is divided. Enterprises accumulate a share of their surplus and propose investments from it, ministries and banks appraise the proposals against profitability and the plan's priorities, and credit, carrying interest, rations the claims. The criterion is partly decentralised and partly commercial: a project must promise to cover its costs in the eyes of those who appraise it, which biases the system toward the quick return, the proven product, the region already developed. Under budgetary finance, the answer is unitary. All investment funds sit in the state budget; the plan allocates them according to the leadership's priorities; no project needs to promise a return in money, because the return is defined in the plan's own terms, a region developed, a dependency broken, a capacity created whose value will appear in other sectors' ledgers or in the nation's strategic position.

Guevara's defence of the unitary answer followed his system's logic to its root. Investment is where an economy declares its future, and a transition that lets profitability govern the declaration will reproduce the geography and the structure of the old economy, since profit is found where the old economy left its markets, its skills, and its ports. Only a centre armed with the whole surplus can build against the market's grain: the steelworks where no steel was bought, the factory in the neglected province, the industry whose product the nation imported and whose absence was its subordination. The critics' reply also followed their logic. A centre that allocates everything will allocate by the lights it has, which are political lights: the prestigious project, the minister's pet, the region with advocates, while the unglamorous replacement of worn machinery in a hundred obscure plants, which no ceremony attends, is deferred until the machinery fails. Profitability, for all its bias, is at least a question that must be answered with numbers; priority, in a budgetary system, is a question answered with access. The island's later history supplied evidence to both: grand projects that embodied the leadership's will and rotted, and a capital stock whose quiet decay no plan admitted until it presented itself as breakdown. The investment question is the debate in its purest form, because it asks what a society trusts: the aggregated judgements of its accountants, or the declared purposes of its leaders. Guevara trusted purposes, and built the system that purposes require. The record of purposes, his own included, is the standing cross-examination of that trust.

## Prices between sister enterprises: a market that was not one

One corner of the debate, apparently the driest, repays attention because it concentrates the whole dispute: the question of what price a state enterprise should notionally charge another. In the budgetary system, internal prices were accounting devices, set by the centre, useful for comparing the efficiency of plants and periods, but carrying no power to reward or punish. Guevara defended the arrangement by an analogy his opponents found revealing: within a single capitalist corporation, transfers between divisions are not sales, and no one imagines that a conglomerate's left hand profits from its right. The socialist economy, being one enterprise, should stop pretending otherwise. The opponents answered that the analogy concealed the problem. A conglomerate's divisions are disciplined from outside, by the corporation's confrontation with a market in which it can fail. Remove the external discipline and the internal accounting price becomes the only signal left, and a signal that nobody is rewarded for heeding or punished for ignoring is not a signal but a decoration.

The exchange exposes the deepest difficulty of the budgetary construction, which its author never fully answered: the problem of information. A planned economy must learn, continuously, where its resources are scarce, where its products are unwanted, where its processes are wasteful. Markets, whatever their injustices, generate this information as a by-product of exchange, in the movement of prices and the accumulation of unsold stock. The budgetary system proposed to generate it administratively, through norms, reports, inspections, and the plan's own revisions, and the history of every command economy testifies to how imperfectly administrative channels carry bad news upward, since every reporter in the chain is judged by the news he reports. Guevara's answer, that consciousness and revolutionary vigilance would keep the channels honest, is the voluntarist premise wearing an accountant's suit. It is also, in fairness, an answer that identifies a real disease of the rival model, whose price signals in a monopolised state sector were themselves fictions, set by the same centre that claimed to obey them. Neither side possessed a working information system. They possessed rival theories of which fiction would corrupt the economy more slowly, and the debate's honesty consists in both sides knowing it.

## Wages and norms: the pay packet under the system

The worker met the budgetary system in his pay packet, and the meeting deserves description because it is where the theory of incentives became a household fact. The ministry's wage policy under Guevara moved toward a national salary scale, in which pay attached to the post and its norm rather than to the enterprise's fortunes, severing the link between a factory's surplus and its workers' incomes that self-financing preserved. Output norms were set centrally and revised in campaigns; fulfilment was the worker's duty, overfulfilment a matter for collective honour rather than for an individual bonus calculated on the excess. Around the wage stood the apparatus of moral reward: the emulation movement between workplaces, the titles and ceremonies for exemplary workers, the voluntary labour mobilisations in which ministers and machinists cut cane side by side for the cameras and, in the minister's case, in fact. The social wage, free services, subsidised food, education, and health, was meanwhile expanding, so that the money wage formed a shrinking share of the worker's real claim on the social product, a development Guevara welcomed as the money form's practical withering.

The system's defenders could point to a genuine egalitarianism in the design: the compression of differentials, the refusal to let scarce skills auction themselves, the insistence that the administrator and the porter were building the same society. Its critics, inside the debate and after, pointed to the design's friction with the workforce it governed. A norm experienced as arbitrary, a bonus abolished while prices and shortages persisted, an emulation title distributed by political criteria: each taught the worker a lesson opposite to the curriculum's intention, and the lesson accumulated in the absenteeism and low intensity that the ministry's own reports lamented. The pay packet is the tribunal in which incentive theories are actually judged, and the verdict there was mixed in a way neither side's pamphlets admitted: the egalitarian design commanded real loyalty in the revolution's early enthusiasm, and consumed that loyalty as a fuel, without building the mechanism that would replace it when the enthusiasm thinned.

## The law of value: guide, instrument, or residue

At the debate's theoretical centre sat a single concept, the law of value, and the two sides' handling of it shows how far the argument reached beyond Cuba. In Marxist political economy the law of value names the regulation of production by socially necessary labour time, operating through exchange: commodities trade in proportion to the labour embodied in them, and producers who ignore the proportion are disciplined by loss. Under capitalism the law rules blindly, behind the producers' backs. The question for a planned economy is what becomes of it. The self-financing school held that the law persists through the transition because its material basis persists: labour remains scarce, products remain distinct, and the social accounting of labour must therefore continue to operate through value forms, which the plan can use consciously, bending prices and profits to social purposes while respecting the underlying proportions. Guevara's position was more radical and more interesting. He held that the law of value, as a regulator, belongs to the society of generalised exchange and cannot be imported into relations between state enterprises without importing its logic; that within the state sector, where the owner is one, talk of buying and selling is a fiction concealing administrative transfer; and that the transition's task is not to use the law but to restrict its domain consciously and progressively, replacing allocation by exchange with allocation by plan and need, beginning immediately rather than at abundance's distant arrival.

The difference sounds scholastic and is not. If the law of value is a guide, then prices should be set to reflect real costs, enterprises should respond to them, and a ministry that fixes prices by decree is falsifying the economy's signals and will be punished in shortages and surpluses it cannot read. If the law is a residue, then the same ministry is performing the transition's essential act, withdrawing domain after domain from the empire of exchange, and the shortages that follow are the growing pains of a new principle rather than the refutation of an old one. Every concrete dispute in the debate, over interest, over enterprise funds, over bonuses, over whether a state farm should sell to a state factory or merely deliver to it, was this single dispute wearing overalls. Later generations of reformers across the socialist world would fight the same battle under other flags, market socialism against plan, decentralisation against command, and the Cuban journals of those years contain, in embryo and at a high level, the arguments all of them would use. This is the sense in which the unknown Guevara anticipated the entire later argument about market socialism: not by predicting its outcomes, but by stating, earlier than almost anyone in office, the strongest version of the anti-market case from inside a government that was actually trying to run an economy without one.

### What separates the budgetary finance system from Soviet-style planning of the same years?

The treatment of the enterprise. In the Soviet practice of the period, the state firm remained an accounting person: it held funds, bought and sold, paid interest, and answered for its profit, so that market forms operated inside public ownership and disciplined managers through their balances.

Under budgetary finance the firm was dissolved as an accounting person into the single enterprise of the whole economy, its money reduced to a measuring unit, its success redefined as plan fulfilment at falling cost, and its workers addressed through moral rather than material incentives.

## The young Marx in the ministry: the anthropology behind the system

The new man had a philosophical pedigree, and tracing it explains why an Argentine doctor administered a ministry as though it were a seminar on human nature. The Marx who presides over Guevara's economics is not primarily the analyst of capital but the young philosopher of the Paris manuscripts, the thinker for whom labour under alienation deforms the labourer, species being is realised in conscious collective activity, and the transformation of circumstances and the transformation of men are one process or neither. That text, recovered and published only in the twentieth century, had become the scripture of the Marxist humanism that flourished after the war in opposition to the Soviet orthodoxy's mechanical materialism, and Guevara's formation, in the Mexico years and in his ceaseless reading, placed him squarely in that current. The budgetary finance system is the young Marx administered: an institutional design whose every feature, the abolition of the enterprise as a separate accounting person, the subordination of the money wage, the honours, the voluntary Sundays, is a device for de-alienating labour by redesigning the relations in which labour occurs.

The pedigree also marks the work's distance from the tradition's other pole. Against the humanist reading stood the structural reading then emerging in Paris, for which the young Marx was a pre-Marxist and consciousness a derivative of structures that operate behind men's backs. Guevara's whole construction is a wager against that reading before it was fully formulated: a wager that the transition is precisely the epoch in which consciousness, organised by a vanguard and embodied in institutions, can lead the structure rather than trail it. The wager's terms explain the work's emotional register, its impatience with every counsel of delay, and its characteristic error, the systematic underrating of the structure's revenge: of prices that reassert themselves as black markets when they are abolished as signals, of hierarchies that re-form inside the institutions built to dissolve them, of the old man's persistence inside the new man's overalls. The anthropology was profound and the economics built on it was serious. Their conjunction in one ministry is what makes the episode, alone among the century's administrative experiments, worth a student's whole attention: it is the purest case on record of a philosophy of man attempted as a system of accounts.

## Moral incentives, material incentives, and the new man

The debate's most quoted element, and its most misunderstood, is the argument over incentives, because it is usually received as a piece of moralising when it is in fact a claim about the mechanics of transition. Guevara's position was not that workers should be exhorted to virtue while their material needs were ignored; his ministry worried obsessively over supplies, norms, and the real wage. His position was that the predominance among incentives matters: that a system in which the material bonus is the chief lever teaches the worker, daily and institutionally, that his labour is a commodity he sells to society as he once sold it to an employer, and that the consciousness so formed is the old society reproducing itself inside the new one's factories. Moral incentives, collective recognition, emulation between workplaces, voluntary labour, the honouring of exemplary workers, were proposed not as decorations on the wage but as the embryonic forms of a different relation between the individual and the social product, in which work is performed as a social duty and a source of standing rather than as a sale.

The new man was the name for the human product of that arrangement, and the phrase has suffered the fate of slogans. In its author's usage it named no superman and no saint. It named the transitional individual in whom the motivations of the old order, accumulation, competition, the reduction of others to instruments, were being displaced, incompletely and against resistance, by motivations of solidarity and duty; and Guevara was explicit that the displacement was the work of the whole transition, conducted simultaneously through education, through the example of the leadership, and above all through economic arrangements that did not contradict the lesson in the act of teaching it. The argument's logical skeleton deserves respect even from its opponents. If institutions form consciousness, as every materialist claims, then a transition that retains the institutions of self-interest while preaching solidarity is incoherent, and the incoherence will be resolved by the institutions. The new man was Guevara's answer to the question of which side of that contradiction a serious transition should choose.

### Why did he distrust material incentives as the transition's main lever?

Because of what he believed they taught. A bonus system instructs the worker, in the most concrete pedagogy there is, the pedagogy of his own pay packet, that his relation to society is an exchange in which he gives effort and receives goods, exactly the relation the transition existed to supersede.

Guevara argued that an economy could not lecture solidarity in its schools and practise exchange in its factories without the factories winning, and that material incentives, once installed as the predominant lever, would entrench the consciousness that made them necessary.

The counterargument, pressed by his opponents then and by economists since, is empirical and severe. Moral incentives displayed a marked tendency to decay: emulation campaigns produced ritual compliance, voluntary labour produced spectacle, and the worker whose consciousness was to be the economy's engine continued, in his great majority, to calculate his family's interest with the persistence his critics predicted. Absenteeism in the ministry's own factories was a standing problem its minister acknowledged in speeches of unusual candour. The assessment that this article eventually reaches does not require the reader to choose between the two psychologies in the abstract. It requires noticing that Guevara identified a real mechanism, the formative power of incentive structures, and then wagered a national economy on the speed at which the mechanism could be run in reverse. The identification was theory. The wager was voluntarism again, the foco's second proposition translated into political economy: the refusal to wait for conditions, applied now to the interior of the worker.

## Voluntary labour: the minister with a machete

Voluntary labour was the incentive system's theatre, and its leading actor was the minister himself. On Sundays and in the harvest mobilisations, Guevara worked in the cane fields, on the docks, and on construction sites, performing the manual labour he asked of others and accumulating the hours that earned him, pointedly, the same certificates of voluntary work awarded to any citizen. The performance was not faked; his asthma made the cane work a genuine ordeal, and the photographs of the minister with a machete record a man spending his body on his doctrine. As propaganda the institution was superb. As economics it was the doctrine in miniature, with the doctrine's virtues and its flaw in a single image: the mobilisation of enthusiasm for tasks the price system performs routinely, at a cost in organisation and fatigue that enthusiasm must continually repay.

The institution's career after his departure is its verdict. Voluntary labour survived as ritual long after it ceased to signify as economics, and the later harvest mobilisations, conducted at vast scale with conscripted enthusiasm, became bywords for the waste of human energy on symbolic production. The trajectory illustrates the general law this profile keeps encountering in his work: a practice whose meaning depends on the sincerity of its participants cannot be institutionalised without the sincerity becoming a quota, and a quota of sincerity is a contradiction that expresses itself, in time, as cynicism. Guevara saw the danger in principle; his writings warn against the empty campaign and the manufactured enthusiasm. What he did not supply was a mechanism by which a state could tell its own genuine mobilisations from its manufactured ones, and in that absence the institution decayed along the path of least resistance, from example to expectation to obligation. The minister with the machete remains a moving image. It is also a warning about every politics that proposes to run an economy on the fuel of its citizens' devotion.

## The new man in the classroom: education and the long formation

The formation argument had a second theatre besides the factory: the school. The revolutionary government's literacy campaign, which sent brigades of young teachers into the countryside in the revolution's third year, belonged to the same project as the budgetary finance system, and Guevara's writings treat them as one undertaking: the manufacture, by deliberate institutional design, of the human material of a new society. Education in his conception was not a service the transition provided but the transition itself conducted by other means, since the new man could no more be decreed in the classroom than in the ministry; he had to be formed by participation, by work joined to study, by the polytechnic ideal in which the student labours and the labourer studies. The schools in the Sierra's liberated zones, where guerrillas taught peasants' children between engagements, were the embryo; the literacy brigades were the embryo grown national.

The assessment of this theatre follows the pattern of the others. The literacy campaign's achievement, a dramatic reduction of illiteracy in a single year, was real, and its method, mobilised youth teaching in the homes of the taught, formed the teachers as much as the pupils, exactly as the theory prescribed. The longer project of formation through the school encountered the same decay as formation through the factory: curricula hardened into catechism, participation into attendance, and the distinction between educating a consciousness and certifying a loyalty blurred in the hands of the apparatus. The pattern is not a refutation of the formative ambition, which every society pursues through its schools whether it admits it or not. It is a refutation of the timetable and of the innocence: the belief that a single generation, formed by a single doctrine, under a single party's tutelage, would emerge as the new man rather than as the old man's most adaptable descendant. On the evidence of his own century, Guevara's question, who forms the formers, and by what forms, remains standing after his answer has fallen.

## Socialism and man: the essay on consciousness

In March 1965, weeks after the Algiers speech that announced his break with the socialist camp's complacency, Guevara published the text by which his economic thought is usually, and misleadingly, represented: a long essay in the form of a letter to the editor of a Uruguayan weekly, known in English as Socialism and Man in Cuba. The essay gathers the whole position into a single argument about the individual in the transition. Its claims can be followed in order. The transition to socialism is not completed by the expropriation of the expropriators, because the old society survives inside the new one's members, in their habits of competition and accumulation, and the struggle against that survival is the transition's real content. The instruments of the old order, commodity production, the market, material interest as the lever of effort, cannot build the new consciousness, since a society is formed by the means it uses and not by the ends it proclaims. The vanguard's role is therefore exemplary before it is administrative: those who lead must embody the renunciation they demand, accepting sacrifice as the normal condition of leadership, because a privileged vanguard preaching equality corrupts the lesson at its source. And the individual, far from being dissolved in the mass, is completed in it, finding in collective work and collective purpose the full development that the old order's competition stunted.

The essay contains the sentence by which its author is most often quoted in the sympathetic literature, his acknowledgment that what he is about to say risks seeming ridiculous, followed by the claim that "the true revolutionary is guided by great feelings of love". The sentence is usually deployed as a devotional caption under the photograph. In its argumentative place it does harder work: it is the emotional corollary of the incentive theory, the assertion that the motive force of the new society is not a technique but a transformed affect, and that a revolution whose leaders are not moved by love of the people they claim to serve will reproduce domination under new names. A reader is free to find the claim naive. What the reader may not fairly do is detach it from the system it belongs to, in which love names the subjective pole of an objective argument about how economies form the people who run them.

### What did he mean by consciousness as a productive force?

That the transition's scarcest resource was a state of mind, and that the state of mind could be built or squandered by economic policy itself. If workers experience production as a social contribution recognised by their community, effort, care, and inventiveness follow in quantities no bonus schedule can purchase.

If they experience production as a sale, the economy receives exactly what it pays for and nothing beyond. Consciousness, in this usage, is not morale; it is an input into production as real as fuel, and the budgetary system was designed as the machinery for manufacturing it.

The essay's limits are the system's limits, stated in a higher register. It assumes that the vanguard's example can be institutionalised without becoming theatre; that sacrifice, demanded continuously, does not curdle into resentment or hypocrisy; that the distinction between the consciousness the state wishes to form and the obedience it finds convenient can be maintained by the very apparatus that benefits from confusing them. Guevara himself supplied, in his administrative practice, the evidence for the prosecution on the last point, and the next section takes up that evidence, because a profile that quotes the essay's love while omitting the ministry's discipline would be performing the hagiography it exists to refuse.

## Emulation and the honours system: recognition as a currency

Between the wage and the exhortation stood an institution the budgetary system took seriously as economics: socialist emulation, the organised competition of workplaces and workers for collective and individual honours. Its mechanics were elaborate. Work centres pledged fulfilments, compared results, and received, in ceremonies of genuine public weight, the banners, titles, and diplomas that constituted the currency of moral reward. The exemplary worker was decorated, photographed, held up to his fellows; the exemplary factory flew its distinction where a capitalist firm would have posted its profits. Guevara's theory of the institution was precise: in a society where the product belongs to all, recognition by all is the natural form of reward, and the desire for the community's esteem is a motive as real, and far more cultivable, than the desire for a larger packet. The honours were meant to be the transition's mint, issuing the coin in which the new man's economy would trade.

The institution's pathology was as predictable as its theory was elegant. Honours distributed by committees become currency in a second sense: they are sought, traded, and debased. Emulation results were massaged by the same reporting chains that massaged production figures; titles proliferated until their scarcity, and with it their value, eroded; and the worker who watched a banner awarded for a fiction learned the system's real lesson faster than its intended one. The parallel with money is exact and was available to the system's author, whose whole argument rested on the claim that forms corrupt their contents. A currency of recognition requires the same defence against inflation as a currency of paper: an honest mint, a limited issue, and punishments for counterfeiting that fall on the powerful as readily as on the weak. The ministry could no more guarantee those conditions for its honours than the old bank could for its notes, and the honours inflated accordingly. The episode is a small, complete model of the larger argument: the formative power of institutions is real, it operates on the institutions of virtue as surely as on the institutions of exchange, and a transition that understood the first half of that truth and not the second built its school of consciousness with a debased coin.

## Cadres: the selection of the men who would run the factories

A ministry is its middle, and Guevara's cadre policy was the budgetary system's personnel counterpart: the attempt to staff the apparatus with men formed as the doctrine required. His criteria were stated often and applied personally. The administrator of the new economy was to be technically competent, since incompetence is not a revolutionary virtue; politically reliable, since the apparatus disposed of the society's surplus; and austere in his own consumption, since the stratum's privileges were, in the minister's diagnosis, the seed of the restoration he feared. Training schools attached to the ministry taught accounting and Marxism in the same curriculum, a pairing that summarised the whole project: the ledger and the doctrine as one discipline, each certifying the other. Promotion was to follow the combined standard, and the minister's own inspection tours, arriving unannounced at factories whose managers learned to dread and to respect them, enforced the standard by fear and example in roughly equal measure.

The policy's fate rehearsed the system's. A combined standard administered by men selects, in practice, for the element that can be displayed, and political reliability displays more readily than competence; the schools' certificates multiplied while the plants' accounting, as the shadow ledgers in the drawers attested, quietly seceded. After the minister's departure the cadre system settled into the ordinary pattern of a governing party's personnel machine, in which the doctrine's vocabulary survived as the language of advancement and the austerity requirement survived as its rhetoric. The episode generalises beyond Cuba, and the generalisation is one of the profile's recurring findings: every element of the construction that depended on the quality of persons decayed at the rate at which persons are actually supplied, while every element that depended on the design of incentives persisted at the rate the design dictated. Guevara's economics inverted that relationship in theory, staking the design on the persons. The cadres are where the inversion met its personnel department, and lost.

## Labour discipline and the coercive edge

The theorist of moral incentives governed, in practice, through sanctions as well as exhortation, and the record of his ministry and his stated positions includes a coercive edge that belongs in the centre of the profile rather than at its margins. He defended the subordination of individual consumption to accumulation with a rigour that fell hardest on the workers in whose name it was exercised. He supported the militarisation of labour discipline in moments of mobilisation, treated absenteeism and indiscipline as political failings rather than as signals about the system's own design, and held positions on the correction of deviant conduct, through labour, that placed him on the severe side of his government's internal spectrum. His conception of the vanguard's sacrifice, applied downward, became a demand that ordinary people live at the level of renunciation appropriate to guerrilla fighters in a war economy, indefinitely, in peacetime, as the normal price of the transition. The voluntary labour he championed and personally performed, cutting cane and unloading ships, was genuinely voluntary in his own case and genuinely exemplary; as an institution generalised across a workforce, voluntariness administered by the state has a way of becoming a quota with a flag on it.

This is the point at which the two halves of the profile must be held together with both hands. The man who wrote of the revolutionary guided by great feelings of love also presided over tribunals that sent men to firing squads, administered an economy through campaigns of exhortation backed by discipline, and constructed a theory of human motivation whose practical expression, in the hands of any state including his own, trends toward the moralisation of obedience. None of this refutes the economic argument, which stands or falls on its analysis of incentives and value. All of it disciplines the reader's response to the argument's author, and it forecloses the sentimental reading in which the economist of consciousness was simply a gentler figure than the guerrilla of the photograph. He was the same figure: a man of extraordinary consistency, whose consistency ran from the Sierra to the ministry to the tribunal without changing its character, and whose virtues and severities were a single trait, the subordination of every present good to a future he could describe in detail and would not wait for.

## State farms or cooperatives: the countryside's form

The agrarian question returned to the ministry's agenda in the revolution's fifth year, when a second agrarian reform expropriated the remaining medium holdings and the government had to decide the form in which the socialised land would be worked. The choice, between state farms administered as enterprises of the ministry and cooperatives owned and governed by their members, was the Great Debate conducted in furrows, and Guevara's position was the budgetary system's logic applied to land: the state farm, the higher form, in which the peasant becomes a worker of the whole society, his product and his wage alike social, while the cooperative, retaining group property and the market relations it implies, perpetuates the particularism the transition exists to dissolve. His opponents in the countryside's administration made the countryside's reply: that the peasant's attachment runs to land he can call his own in some form he recognises, that the state farm's wage labour reproduced, under a red management, the plantation relation the revolution had promised to end, and that a form imposed against the cultivator's sense of justice would be worked at the cultivator's pace, which is the pace of a man labouring on another's account.

The island's subsequent agricultural history, with its chronic shortfalls and its repeated reorganisations, supplied both sides with evidence and neither with victory, and the episode's value for this profile is diagnostic rather than verdictive. It shows the system's signature move at its clearest: the preference, wherever a lower and a higher form contend, for the higher form immediately, on the ground that the transition's purpose is to ascend and that lingering on the lower rungs entrenches them. The move has a logic, and the logic has a cost, and the cost is paid by the people standing on the rungs. In industry the cost appeared as the shadow ledger and the withdrawn effort; in agriculture it appeared as the peasant's withheld care for land that was everyone's and therefore, in the daily sense that decides harvests, no one's. The cooperative question is the budgetary debate's rural chapter, and it ends, as the urban chapters end, with the form's education running in both directions: the state teaching the peasant the doctrine of the higher form, and the peasant teaching the state, harvest by harvest, the doctrine of his own unconquered particularity.

## Diversification: the argument about sugar and steel

The ministry's first economic battle was not about accounting but about the island's shape. The inherited economy grew one crop, and the revolutionary government's earliest economic instinct, which Guevara championed, was to break the monoculture: to diversify agriculture, to plant what the island imported, and to industrialise at forced pace, so that the nation's dependence on a single buyer's market for a single harvest would end. The policy's logic was the logic of the whole revolution's self-understanding. A country that sells sugar and buys everything else is not sovereign in any sense that survives a quarrel with its customer, and the quarrel had already happened. Industry, in this view, was not a sector but a declaration of independence with chimneys.

The policy's costs arrived on schedule and were severe. Diversification diverted land and labour from the crop that paid the island's bills before the new plantings and factories could pay anything, sugar output fell in the years when the reorientation eastward most needed it to hold, and the industrial programme, launched at a pace set by politics, accumulated half-built capacity whose completion awaited the very imports the programme was meant to replace. By the middle of the decade the government had quietly reversed course, returning to sugar as the earner and the foundation, and the reversal was administered by the same leadership that had proclaimed the diversification, with the minister of industries already departing for other fronts. The episode is the budgetary debate's material preface: it taught the government the cost of willing an economic structure into being faster than the structure's own gestation allowed, and it taught Guevara's critics in the apparatus the argument they would use against his system entire, that the economy punishes the confusion of a proclamation with a harvest. His defenders reply, with justice, that the monoculture he attacked was itself the island's standing catastrophe, that the dependence he tried to break reasserted itself for decades and strangled every later model in turn, and that a minister who had merely administered the sugar economy efficiently would have administered its subordination. The diversification failed as policy and stands as diagnosis, which is the verdict this profile has reached, in different vocabularies, about most of the economic work.

## The European echo: the argument beyond the island

The Great Debate's significance is measurable by the company it kept. When the Cuban journals opened their pages to foreign contributions, the argument ceased to be a ministry quarrel and became the most visible site of a controversy running through the entire socialist world: the controversy over the reforms that, in those same years, were decentralising the Soviet and East European economies toward enterprise autonomy, profit indicators, and market levers. Guevara's budgetary finance system was, in effect, the anti-reform: a demonstration, from a government actually attempting it, that the opposite road existed and could be argued at the level of principle. Reform economists in Europe read the Cuban exchange as ammunition for their opponents; anti-reformers read it as proof that the market road was a choice rather than a necessity. Ernest Mandel's participation carried the argument into the Trotskyist tradition's own long dispute over the nature of the Soviet economy, where the question of whether value forms persisted under planning had been contested for decades. Charles Bettelheim's intervention carried it into the French debates that would soon, in the upheavals of the late sixties, radicalise a generation of economists against the Soviet model from the left.

The echo matters for the grading of the unknown Guevara, because it establishes the audience his economics actually reached. The manual reached millions and persuaded, in the end, almost no one who tested it. The debate reached thousands, in journals and in the specialist literature, and it persuaded a durable minority of the century's most serious Marxist economists that the anti-market case had been made from inside a government with a force it had never been made from the academy. Influence of that kind does not appear in the iconography. It appears in citations, in syllabuses of political economy, in the footnotes of the market socialism literature, where the Cuban exchange is treated as the classical statement of the road not taken. A thinker's stature is partly a function of who must answer him. By that measure the economist outranks the guerrilla, since the guerrilla's propositions are answered by a chronicle, while the economist's are still being answered by arguments.

## Sugar, oil, and the subsidy: the economy he actually governed

No assessment of the economic work is honest that floats free of the island's material situation, and the situation can be stated without the figures this article's protocol forbids itself in the contested domains. Cuba's economy rested on sugar: on the harvest that employed the countryside's labour for a season and left it idle for the rest, on the mills and the railways built to serve them, on the export earnings that paid for imported food, fuel, and machinery. The revolution inherited that monoculture along with its markets, and the rupture with the United States, which had absorbed the crop and supplied the imports, forced a wholesale reorientation toward the socialist camp in a matter of months. Soviet oil arrived in Soviet tankers; Cuban sugar travelled east; the island's survival arithmetic was rewritten in Moscow's ledgers. Every economic system debated in the journals operated inside that dependence, and the dependence is the answer to any assessment that treats the debate as a free choice among models. Cuba could choose its accounting. It could not choose its patron, its crop, or its geography, ninety miles from a hostile superpower that organised an invasion, an embargo, and a long campaign of sabotage against it.

Guevara understood the dependence as a theorist, which is why his economics kept escaping the island's borders into the theory of the camp. The Algiers speech's charge, that socialist trade reproduced imperialist exchange, was the complaint of a sugar minister who had watched his country's crop priced by the market his allies claimed to have abolished among themselves. The critical notes' prediction of restoration was the same complaint generalised: a camp held together by subsidised exchange on world-market terms had not transcended the world market, and would in time be reabsorbed by it. The subsidy objection to his system, pressed by his critics, is therefore double-edged in a way its pressers rarely notice. Yes, the budgetary experiment floated on Soviet support. So did the self-financing system that succeeded it, for a quarter century, until the support vanished and the island's economy contracted catastrophically, revealing how much of every Cuban model had been the subsidy all along. The debate's real subject, visible only from that later vantage, was never which system could stand without the patron. It was which system a society should build while the patron lasted, and what would remain when he left.

## Punta del Este: the minister faces the hemisphere

Before Algiers there was an earlier rostrum, and the contrast between the two speeches measures the arc of the disillusion. In August 1961, at the inter-American economic conference at Punta del Este, Guevara led the Cuban delegation into the assembly where the United States was launching its Alliance for Progress, the great programme of aid and reform by which Washington proposed to answer the Cuban example with development rather than invasion alone. His speech there, delivered to the hemisphere's finance ministers in their own chamber, was the revolution's economic manifesto addressed to its enemies: a refusal of the aid on the terms offered, an indictment of the structure that made the aid necessary, and a prediction, delivered with a minister's command of the figures of underdevelopment, that the continent's peoples would settle the argument the conference was convened to avoid. The performance made him, for the hemisphere's establishments, the revolution's most articulate and therefore most dangerous representative, the doctor who could out-argue the economists in their own vocabulary before returning to the mountains in his other one.

Set the two speeches side by side and the education of their author is legible. At Punta del Este the enemy is external and the camp is a refuge; the speech's anger is directed outward, at the empire and its clients, and its confidence rests on the solidarity of the socialist world behind the speaker. At Algiers, less than four years later, the anger has acquired a second target, the camp itself, accused in its own forum of reproducing the enemy's relations. Between the speeches lie the trade negotiations at the world market's prices, the missile crisis's lesson in hierarchy, and the ministry's daily experience of dependence. The diplomat abroad sections of this profile have treated the rostrums as episodes of a career. They are better treated as the public stations of a single argument, the argument the critical notes would finish in private: that the revolution's enemy was a system of relations, that the system had franchises inside the camp that claimed to have abolished it, and that a minister who had administered the franchises was obliged, by the honesty his whole persona proclaimed, to say so where it cost the most.

## Algiers: the charge against the socialist camp

In February 1965 Guevara addressed an Afro-Asian economic seminar in Algiers, and the speech detonated the diplomatic fiction on which Cuba's alliances rested. His argument was an extension of the economic debate to the scale of the world. The socialist countries, he charged, traded with the developing world on terms that reproduced the structure of imperialist exchange: buying the poor countries' raw materials at the depressed prices of the world market and selling them manufactures at that market's inflated ones, so that the socialist camp profited from the same unequal exchange it denounced when capitalists practised it. A socialist country that accepts those terms becomes, in the phrase the speech made famous in substance, an accomplice of imperialist exploitation. The remedy he proposed followed from his whole system: trade among the socialist and developing worlds should be reorganised on the basis of need and solidarity rather than world prices, with the more developed socialist states accepting deliberate sacrifice as the price of a genuine camp. The speech named no names and needed none; its target was the Soviet Union's commercial practice, and every delegation in the hall understood the address.

### What did the Algiers speech accuse the socialist countries of doing?

Of complicity through commerce. By trading with the developing world at world market prices, buying its raw materials cheap and selling manufactures dear, the socialist states reproduced the unequal exchange that defined imperialism and drew profit from the underdevelopment they claimed to oppose, becoming in that respect their enemy's partner.

The charge applied his domestic argument at world scale, since forms are not neutral and a camp that trades like its adversary cannot claim exemption from the analysis it applies to that adversary.

The speech's consequences were personal and historical. Personally, it marked the point beyond which Guevara's position inside the Cuban leadership became untenable in its existing form: the island's survival depended on the Soviet relationship his speech had indicted, and within weeks he had published the essay on socialism and man, left public life, and departed for the Congo. Historically, the speech stands as the clearest statement of the third position he was constructing, neither Washington's nor Moscow's, in which the socialist camp itself stood accused by the standards it proclaimed. The accusation has outlived the camp it addressed. Every later argument about fair trade, about the terms on which rich states of any ideology exchange with poor ones, replays the Algiers structure, usually without knowing its source.

## The missile crisis: the patron's lesson

In October 1962 the world learned that Soviet missiles had been installed in Cuba, and then that they would be withdrawn under an arrangement negotiated between Moscow and Washington from which Havana was excluded. Guevara's part in the crisis, as a member of the leadership that had accepted the deployment, and his reaction to its settlement, belong to the intellectual biography because the episode completed his education in the character of the camp he served. The Cuban leadership had accepted the missiles as the island's shield and as a contribution to the camp's strategic balance; the withdrawal, announced over the radio to a Havana that had mobilised for invasion, taught the smaller ally that its territory was a counter in a game whose players sat elsewhere. His statements in the crisis's aftermath, to a British journalist among others, carried the defiance for which he became famous in the West: that had the missiles remained under Cuban control, they would have been used. The sentence is usually quoted as bloodthirstiness. In the profile's frame it is better read as the obverse of the lesson: a determination that the island's fate would not again be settled in its absence, by a patron whose solidarity stopped at the water's edge of its own security.

The crisis's doctrinal consequence ran underground and surfaced at Algiers. If the camp's leading power would trade the ally's shield for its own safety, then the camp was not a camp but a hierarchy, and its economic arrangements, the subsidised trade, the administered prices, the fraternal credits, were the hierarchy's bookkeeping rather than socialism's international form. Guevara's economics had always insisted that forms express relations. The missile crisis taught him which relations the camp's forms expressed, and the Algiers speech, two years later, said so in the language of trade because trade was the language his office had given him. The road from the October crisis to the February speech is the road from a minister's disillusion to a doctrine's indictment, and it is the last stage of the education that the critical notes would complete in private.

## The tour of the camp: Moscow, Beijing, and the widening crack

In the winter of 1960 Guevara led a Cuban economic delegation on a tour of the socialist world, negotiating the trade agreements on which the island's survival would depend, and the tour's itinerary reads, in retrospect, as the map of his disillusion. In Moscow he signed the agreements and studied the model's homeland with a minister's eye for its machinery. In Beijing he met the Chinese leadership and encountered a revolution whose style, austere, voluntarist, contemptuous of the Soviet gradualism he was coming to question, resembled his own instincts far more than the host's in Moscow had. The Sino-Soviet split, then widening into open polemic, presented every smaller socialist state with a choice of patrons and of models, and Guevara's sympathies in that quarrel were an open secret of the period: the Chinese critique of Soviet revisionism, its insistence that the transition could be reversed and that consciousness remained a battlefield, was his own critique in another accent. Cuba's official neutrality in the split was a diplomatic necessity. His private alignment was a theoretical fact, and it deepened the isolation that the Algiers speech would make public.

The tour also taught him the camp's economics at the counter where they were practised. The agreements he negotiated priced Cuba's sugar and Cuba's imports by formulas tied to the world market, softened by the patron's generosity in the early years and never escaping the structure his later speech would indict. A minister who has signed such agreements knows exactly what his Algiers sentences meant, and knows it in the first person: that the camp's solidarity was administered through the enemy's price list, that the subsidy and the subordination were the same fact viewed from opposite ends, and that a revolution which could not feed itself from its own soil would argue about socialism in the vocabulary of its creditors. The critical notes of the following years are the tour's delayed report, written when the minister had ceased to be one and could afford, in private, the conclusions his office had taught him and his office had forbidden him to print.

## The letters: a workshop in correspondence

The corpus's most unguarded stratum is its correspondence, and a reader who wants the theorist without the rostrum should spend time there. The letters run from the youthful travel notes home, through the Sierra's communications, to the famous farewells, and they show the workshop in which the public texts were forged: a man thinking on paper to particular persons, trying out the judgements that the speeches would later proclaim, and confessing, to his mother above all, the doubts and the reading that the persona of certainty concealed. The letters to his parents from the road are an intellectual autobiography in instalments, the conversion recorded as it happened, book by book and corpse by corpse, in the leper colonies and the mines and the capitals of a continent he was learning to read as one country. The letters of the ministry years, fewer and briefer, carry the administrator's fatigue and the reader's appetite in the same sentences, requests for books interleaved with the business of the state.

The farewell letters stand apart and are treated elsewhere in this profile as acts. The ordinary correspondence matters here for a methodological reason. Every judgement in this article that rests on the published treatises and speeches can be checked against the letters, and the check is reassuring in a way that distinguishes Guevara from many of the century's revolutionary writers: the private man is not better than the public one, as the letters of the tribunals' season grimly attest, but he is the same man, arguing the same premises with the same ferocity and the same blind spots, to his mother and to the United Nations. Posterity is accustomed to correspondence that dissolves a legend, revealing the calculator behind the prophet or the coward behind the hero. This correspondence does the rarer thing. It confirms the legend's central claim, the unity of the life and the doctrine, while documenting, in the doubts confessed and the books requested, the cost at which the unity was maintained. The unknown Guevara is not hiding in the letters. He is working there, in the open, at the desk the icon never shows.

## The critical notes: reading the Soviet manual against itself

After the Congo failure and before Bolivia, during months of concealment in which he prepared the final campaign, Guevara filled notebooks with a systematic critique of the Soviet manual of political economy, the standard textbook through which the bloc's doctrine was taught. The notes were not published in his lifetime. They appeared in Spanish four decades later, issued by the Havana study centre that bears his name, and they are the most startling documents in his corpus, because they show the minister of industries, in private, arriving at the conclusion toward which his public arguments had been travelling: that the Soviet model was not a defective socialism but a system in motion back toward capitalism. His reasoning followed his debate positions to their end. A system that organises its enterprises through the law of value, judges them by profit, motivates its workers materially, and stratifies its managers into a privileged administrative layer has retained the essential mechanisms of the old society, and mechanisms, in his materialism, have consequences that intentions cannot veto. The restoration of capitalism in the Soviet Union would not require a counterrevolution, on this analysis; it would require only time, the gradual assertion of the forms the system had never abolished.

The prediction deserves a careful verdict. As prophecy it has an obvious retrospective glamour, since the Soviet Union did, a quarter century later, dissolve into capitalist restoration under the management of its own administrative layer, much as the notes' mechanism suggests. As analysis it shares the strength and weakness of his whole method: the mechanism is genuinely identified, the formative power of economic forms over the people who operate them, and the timeline and necessity are asserted rather than demonstrated, since the notes cannot show that no other outcome was available to a system running on value forms. What the notes settle beyond dispute is the character of his Soviet criticism. It was not factional, not a Cuban grievance dressed as doctrine, and not the resentment of a client toward a patron. It was an economic argument, continuous with the budgetary finance system and the Algiers speech, made in private with no audience to perform for, and it cost him, in the end, the only alliance his country's survival strategy had. A thinker is often most himself in the texts he does not publish. The unknown Guevara is nowhere more unknown, or more himself, than in these notebooks.

### What did the critical notes predict about the Soviet Union?

That it would return to capitalism by the operation of its own retained mechanisms. Enterprises governed by profit and the law of value, workers governed by material incentives, and managers consolidated as a privileged stratum would assert the logic of those forms over time, making restoration the system's maturation rather than its overthrow.

The notebooks were written in the mid nineteen sixties and published in Spanish four decades after his death.

## After the minister left: the system's fate in other hands

The budgetary finance system's institutional biography after 1965 is brief and instructive. Its author departed; his ministry was reorganised; and the Cuban economy entered a period of radicalisation in which his voluntarism was first extended beyond anything he had administered, in the great mobilisations and the abolition of remaining private commerce that culminated in the drive for a record harvest at the decade's end, and then repudiated in the reaction that followed that drive's failure. Through the nineteen seventies the island standardised its institutions on the Soviet pattern: a planning system of the bloc's design, enterprise self-financing under economic calculus, material incentives restored to predominance, the full apparatus against which the Great Debate's losing side had argued. The settlement was presented as maturity, the revolution grown administrative and realistic. Read against the debate's documents, it was also a verdict delivered by alignment rather than by argument: the model adopted was the patron's model, adopted with the patron's credits, advisers, and institutional templates, and the journals' open argument of the early sixties was not resumed to justify it.

The fate carries a lesson about doctrinal history that this series has cause to repeat. Arguments in a revolutionary state are settled by the state's necessities, and the record of the settlement is then written as though the argument had been concluded on its merits. The budgetary system was not refuted in 1965 or after; it was discontinued, first by its author's departure and then by the logic of the alliance that kept the island fed. Its documents remained in the journals, available for the later generations who would reopen the file when the adopted model itself collapsed with its patron. That reopening, in the specialist literature and in Cuba's own later reform debates, is the system's second life, and it confirms the distinction this profile has insisted on throughout: between a theory that practice destroyed and a theory that politics shelved. The foco belongs to the first category. The budgetary finance system belongs to the second, and confusing the categories is the commonest error in the literature on both sides.

## The great harvest drive: voluntarism extended past its author

Three years after Guevara's death, the Cuban leadership staked the island's economy on the largest voluntarist mobilisation of the era: a sugar harvest of unprecedented size, proclaimed as the nation's honour and pursued by diverting labour, transport, and investment from every other sector into the cane. The drive failed of its target, and the failure's cost, in dislocated production, neglected industries, and exhausted people, forced the leadership's public self-criticism and opened the road to the Soviet-model standardisation of the following decade. The episode belongs in this profile because it is the controlled experiment his theory never received in his lifetime: the mobilisation of consciousness as an economic instrument, conducted by his own comrades, at national scale, with his posthumous authority blessing the method. The result was the refutation the economists had predicted in the journals. Enthusiasm harvested less than the plan required and consumed more than the economy could spare, and the leadership that had inherited his mantle drew the conclusion he had refused: that the island would be run thereafter by the calculus he had spent his ministry opposing.

The harvest drive also clarifies his personal position in the sequence, which the assessment should state fairly. The voluntarism of the late sixties exceeded his own practice in office, where mobilisation had been an instrument among others, bounded by the ministry's accounting and by his own ferocious attention to costs. The drive's architects removed the bounds and kept the exhortation, producing the caricature his critics had always described. A doctrine is not answerable for every use its author's successors make of it, and the distinction between the minister's bounded campaigns and the later unbounded one is real. But the distinction cuts less deeply than his defenders wish, because the theory itself supplied no principle by which the bounds could be defended: if consciousness is a productive force and the leadership embodies it, then the demand for more consciousness, more mobilisation, more sacrifice has no internal stopping point, and the stopping point must be imported from the very calculus the theory despised. The harvest drive found the stopping point empirically, in exhaustion. The theory had no other to offer.

## Doubts among comrades: the internal critics

The objections to Guevara's economics and strategy did not come only from enemies and later scholars. They were voiced, in his lifetime, from inside the revolutionary camp, and the internal critics deserve their place in the assessment because they cannot be dismissed as hostile witnesses. Within the Cuban leadership, the administrators who had to make the economy function week by week regarded the budgetary system's abolition of enterprise accounting as a luxury purchased with their own exhaustion, and said so in the debate's pages with a concreteness the theorists could not match: a manager who cannot know his costs cannot be blamed for his waste, and a ministry that must approve everything will approve anything, badly, late. Within the continental left, the orthodox parties' leaders, men who had built mass organisations over decades and buried their own militants from earlier defeats, received the foco doctrine as an insult compounded by a death sentence: the insult of being pronounced obsolete by a visitor, the sentence of watching their youngest and bravest members drawn away into ventures the parties judged, correctly, to be suicidal. The Bolivian party's refusal at Ñancahuazú was the famous instance, but the judgement was general, and the parties' subsequent vindication by the record has never quite repaired the bitterness, because the dead were theirs as well as his.

There were also the doubts of the practitioners who served under him. The ministry's own technicians, engineers and accountants formed in the old economy's methods, worked the budgetary system with a loyalty the record acknowledges and a scepticism it also records: they kept, in drawers and in private, the shadow accounts the system had abolished, because without them the plants could not actually be run. That detail, small and devastating, belongs in any honest account of the construction. A system that its own operators must secretly supplement with the instruments it forbids has failed a test more intimate than any in the journals. Guevara, who prized honesty in reporting above almost every administrative virtue, would have recognised the drawer's shadow ledger as evidence. The assessment does him no favour by omitting it, and this profile enters it as the internal criticism that weighs most, precisely because it was delivered not in polemic but in practice, by the people who tried hardest to make his system work.

## The critics of the economics: voluntarism and the subsidy

The economic work must now face its strongest objection, stated at full strength and attributed, as the foco objection was, because an assessment that steelmans only the dead theory and not the live one would be rigged. The objection, pressed by economists who studied the Cuban experiment from outside and by critics within the Marxist tradition itself, holds that the budgetary finance system was never a serious alternative model of a socialist economy. It was, on this charge, an unsustainable voluntarism: a set of arrangements that functioned, to the extent it functioned at all, only while an external subsidy carried the economy it was applied to. The Soviet Union's purchases of Cuban sugar at above-market prices and sales of oil at below-market ones underwrote the island's experiment, and the experiment's accounting never had to survive contact with its own costs. Worse, the system's foundation was a theory of human motivation, the predominance of moral incentives and the formation of the new man, which the record of the decade refuted in the ministry's own reports: absenteeism persisted, productivity stagnated, emulation decayed into ritual, and the worker of the transition obstinately remained the worker of the old society, calculating his family's advantage. Carmelo Mesa-Lago, the economist whose studies of the Cuban economy remain the standard external account, documented the system's inefficiencies and its dependence on campaign mobilisation in place of routine administration. Critics in the tradition of the self-financing school added the theoretical edge: a model that cannot count cannot plan, and an economy that refuses the discipline of value will discover scarcity in the form of shortage, queue, and arbitrary privilege, which are merely prices paid in time and favour instead of money.

The objection's strongest form adds a historical observation. When Guevara left, the system he built was dismantled by his own comrades, not by counterrevolutionaries: Cuba moved, through the late nineteen sixties' radicalisation and reaction, toward the Soviet model of economic calculus that he had spent two years arguing against, adopting enterprise self-financing and the full apparatus of the bloc's planning system. If the budgetary system had been the workable alternative its author claimed, the objection runs, its survival would not have depended on one minister's presence. Institutions that require their inventor in the room are not institutions but performances.

## The evidence of the shop floor: the workers' reply

One constituency was never formally seated in the Great Debate, and its evidence arrived by other channels: the workers in whose name both systems were designed. Their reply to the budgetary construction can be reconstructed from the ministry's own preoccupations, since a minister does not harp for years on absenteeism, on low intensity, on the ritual fulfilment of norms, unless the shop floor is telling him something his theory has not budgeted for. What it was telling him, in the judgement of the debate's later historians, was not that Cuban workers were selfish, the explanation his rhetoric sometimes reached for, but that they were rational in a situation his system had made opaque: where effort and reward are disconnected by design, where the norm arrives from a centre that cannot see the machine's condition or the material's quality, where the honours go to the visible and the connected, the rational worker conserves himself. The system's theory of the worker was of a being in formation, ascending toward the new man. The shop floor's theory of the system was of a being in place, the administrator, whose formation no one discussed. The asymmetry is the deepest criticism the episode affords, deeper than the economists' counting objection: the doctrine of formation was applied downward, to those who laboured, and never upward, to those who planned, and a doctrine of universal human transformation that exempts its administrators from transformation reproduces, in the transition's own house, the division between the formers and the formed that it existed to abolish.

Guevara was not wholly blind to the asymmetry, and the fairness this profile owes him requires the admission. His insistence that the vanguard must live at the level it demanded, his own voluntary labour, his ministry's austerity, his contempt for the emerging privileges of the administrative stratum, were attempts to apply the formation upward, to make the formers the first formed. The attempts were personal where they needed to be institutional. A minister's example can shame a stratum; it cannot redesign its incentives, and the stratum's incentives, in a budgetary system that concentrated every decision at the centre, ran toward the accumulation of exactly the discretionary power his doctrine condemned. The workers' reply, delivered in the only currency available to them, their effort's quiet withdrawal, was thus also a verdict on the system's unexamined premise: that the centre which designs the formation stands outside it. No part of the corpus answers that verdict, and the assessment below counts the silence among the economic work's genuine limits, alongside its genuine insights.

## The question of the party: the vanguard organised

Every doctrine in this profile presupposes an organisation, and the organisation's construction occupied the years that the economics did. The Cuban revolution had been made by a movement, not by a party of the classical type, and the task of the early sixties was to fuse the movement, the old communists, and the student revolutionaries into a single ruling party on the Leninist pattern. Guevara took his part in the construction, in the schools of revolutionary instruction and in the debates over the party's character, and his position carried the signature of everything else he touched: the party was to be a vanguard of the formed, an order of the exemplary, selective in admission, austere in standard, whose members' lives would be the doctrine's evidence. The construction's first crisis, the sectarianism affair of 1962, in which the old communists' organisational secretary was removed for stacking the new apparatus with his own current, showed the alternative the vanguard idea was meant to refuse: the party as a machine for placing cadres, in which membership is a career and doctrine a password.

The question outlived the affair and shadows the whole corpus, because the foco and the budgetary system both require, at their centre, a minority that can be trusted with the formation of everyone else, and the party is that minority's institutional form. The record of the construction, in Cuba as in every state that attempted it, is that the vanguard's selectivity decays into the machine's self-perpetuation on a timetable measured in years, and that the exemplary standard survives as rhetoric precisely as it lapses as practice. Guevara saw the decay beginning, in the privileges and the careerism he denounced from the ministry, and his remedy was the one his whole work prescribes: exhortation, example, the moralisation of the institution by the quality of its members. The remedy's inadequacy is the corpus's most general lesson, since it is the same remedy, and the same inadequacy, in the factory, in the countryside, and in the mountain. An institution that can be saved only by the virtue of its members has not been designed; it has been hoped for, and the distinction between design and hope is the line on which the assessment of this entire body of work finally rests.

## The rectification: the successor state's second thoughts

Two decades after the Great Debate was closed by alignment, the Cuban leadership reopened its file from the other side. The standardised system of the nineteen seventies, with its enterprise calculus, its material incentives, and its managerial autonomy, had produced by the middle eighties a harvest of its own: enterprises serving their indicators, managers enriching themselves within the rules, a petty market in favours and goods, and a stratum whose manners the leadership now denounced in the vocabulary of the budgetary system's author. The rectification campaign that followed curtailed the market mechanisms, recentralised decisions, revived voluntary labour and moral exhortation, and rehabilitated, in the official discourse, the positions that had lost in 1965. Guevara's economic writings, long shelved, returned to print and to honour. The wheel had come full circle, driven not by the debate's arguments but by the successor system's decay along exactly the path his side had predicted for it: the forms educating their operators in the service of the forms.

The rectification does not vindicate the budgetary finance system, and the assessment must resist the symmetry's seduction. It vindicates a diagnosis, that a socialism administered through market levers will be colonised by them, while leaving the prescription as unproven as before, since rectification itself governed by exhortation and campaign, consumed the leadership's authority in mobilisations, and was overtaken within a few years by the patron's collapse and the emergency economics of survival. The sequence as a whole, budgetary experiment, standardised calculus, rectification, emergency, is the real data set for the transition question, and it supports a conclusion neither side of the Great Debate would have welcomed: that both systems, administered by the same state under the same siege, decayed toward the pathologies their opponents had predicted, the one toward exhortation without information, the other toward information without allegiance. The debate's enduring value is that its documents state both pathologies in advance, in the words of the men who would administer them. A student who reads the exchange whole possesses the transition's dilemma in its classical form, which is more than the century's later literatures, for all their econometrics, have always managed to give him.

## The answer: motivation, politics, and what the objection proves

The answer begins by separating the position's two claims, which the objection fuses. The first claim was about motivation: that incentive structures form consciousness, that material predominance reproduces the old society's subject, and that a transition indifferent to this mechanism will be shaped by it. The second claim was about the politics of the transition: that the choice of economic forms is a choice about what kind of society is being built, made in the present and not deferrable to abundance, and that a leadership which chooses market forms for efficiency's sake has, by that choice, selected its destination. Against the first claim the objection's evidence is genuinely damaging, and this article concedes what it proves: moral incentives decayed as a routine motivator, the new man did not arrive on schedule, and the speed of human transformation was asserted by the theory rather than established by it. Against the second claim the objection proves much less. That an economy run on value forms will be efficient at producing calculators is not a refutation of Guevara's politics but a restatement of his premise, and the subsequent history of the socialist world, in which market mechanisms installed as temporary instruments repeatedly became permanent destinations, is evidence for the premise's seriousness rather than against it. The subsidy charge, finally, cuts at every Cuban model equally: the self-financing system that replaced his own ran on the same Soviet subsidy for two further decades, so dependence on the patron explains the island's economics in general and cannot, without more, convict one accounting system rather than the other.

What the objection does establish, and what a fair assessment must carry into its verdict, is the difference between a critique and a construction. As a critique of market socialism's complacencies, of the assumption that forms are neutral and incentives innocent, Guevara's economic work remains formidable, and its central mechanism, the formative power of economic arrangements over the people who inhabit them, has been absorbed into the common sense of several disciplines that do not cite him. As a construction, a working system for running an economy, the budgetary finance system failed the test its author most respected: it did not survive practice, it could not count what it consumed, and it rested its routine operation on the scarcest of inputs, sustained collective enthusiasm. The unknown Guevara is therefore not a suppressed genius whose system would have worked. He is something rarer and more useful: a government minister who stated, at the highest level of technical seriousness, the strongest case against the path his whole world took, and whose case remains on the record, unanswered in its central point, after the path itself collapsed.

## The adversaries' assessment: how the other side graded him

One further witness class deserves a hearing before the assessment closes: the adversaries who studied him professionally while he lived. The intelligence assessments of the United States, declassified in later decades, tracked Guevara with a respect their public rhetoric never admitted, grading him the most capable and dangerous of the revolution's leaders after its chief, the one whose abilities, in the analysts' repeated judgement, would make him formidable in any post to which he was assigned. The counterinsurgency establishments of the hemisphere read the manual as an operational document, built their training programmes around its refutation, and thereby conceded, in the only currency that finally counts in such matters, that the theory had to be answered by armies because it could not be answered by neglect. The Bolivian campaign itself, with its foreign-trained rangers and its intelligence architecture, is the adversaries' assessment rendered in institutions: a small state's whole security apparatus, reinforced by a superpower, mobilised for eleven months against a few dozen men, because the few dozen were led by the author of the doctrine the apparatus existed to disprove.

The adversaries' grading cuts against both cults. Against the denunciatory literature, it establishes that the man was not the incompetent fanatic of the caricature: the professionals charged with defeating him treated his competence as the central fact of their problem. Against the hagiography, it establishes the limit with equal clarity: the same professionals, having graded the competence, defeated the doctrine, in the Congo's aftermath and in the ravine, by the methods the doctrine said could not work, mass, intelligence, logistics, and the population's withheld allegiance. An assessment that has weighed his own side's testimony, the internal critics, the shop floor, the successor regimes, should end its evidence with the enemy's, because the enemy had the strongest incentive to grade him exactly. They graded the theorist high and the theory beatable, and the half century since has not overturned either grade.

## The name itself: how an interjection became a flag

The world knows him by a nickname that is not a name, and the accident is worth a paragraph in a profile concerned with the difference between the man and the emblem. Ernesto Guevara de la Serna acquired the syllable in Guatemala and Mexico, from the Argentine habit of punctuating speech with the interjection che, a vocative as ordinary in the River Plate as hey in English, which his Central American and Cuban comrades fastened on the foreigner who used it in every sentence. He adopted it, signed with it, and in time the syllable swallowed the surname entirely, so that the minister and the comandante were known on every continent by a word that means, roughly, you there. The reduction had consequences its bearer would have appreciated in another man's case. A surname belongs to a family and a record; it can be looked up, argued with, held responsible. A monosyllable belongs to whoever shouts it. The passage from Guevara, the Argentine doctor with a bibliography and a tribunal record, to Che, the universal vocative, is the passage from the historical figure to the icon compressed into a single word, and it happened in his lifetime, with his collaboration, since the signature on the famous letters is the nickname and not the name.

The profile has used both forms deliberately, and the usage can stand as its method in miniature. Where the record is in question, the tribunals, the ministry, the ravine, the man is Guevara, answerable under his full name to the evidence. Where the afterlife is in question, the poster, the pledge, the seminar and the shrine, the figure is Che, the syllable the world actually received. Confusing the two registers is the founding error of both cults: the hagiography that signs the tribunals with the nickname's glamour, and the denunciation that hangs the poster's crimes around the neck of a reader of the young Marx. Keeping them apart will not reconcile the camps. It will at least locate the argument where the evidence lives, in the space between a name that can be graded and a syllable that can only be waved.

## The biographers' war: hagiography, denunciation, and the scholarly middle

The literature on Guevara divides into camps whose boundaries the assessment must cross in both directions. The hagiographic tradition, produced in the first decades after the death and sustained by the Cuban state's publishing apparatus, gives the saint of the iconography his literary form: the life as a sequence of exempla, the writings as scripture, the failures as betrayals by lesser men, the tribunals passed over in the vocabulary of revolutionary justice. The denunciatory tradition, produced by the exile community and by the revolution's enemies, gives the icon's mirror image: the life as a sequence of crimes, the writings as rationalisations, the tribunals as the essence, the economics as a vandalism refuted by its results. Between the camps, a scholarly literature has grown up slowly, working from the archives as they opened: the debate's documents published and studied, the diaries edited with their self-criticisms intact, the critical notes printed at last, the ministry's record examined by economists without a poster to defend or to burn. It is from that middle literature, and from the primary texts, that this profile has drawn its judgements, and its conclusions will satisfy neither camp, which is some evidence, though not proof, that they are pointed at the record rather than at the icon.

The camps' symmetry is itself instructive. Hagiography and denunciation agree on the essential premise, that the man was the doctrine and the doctrine was the man, and they divide only on the sign of the equation. The scholarly middle denies the premise. It finds a theorist whose two theories met opposite fates, a minister whose system was shelved rather than refuted, a guerrilla whose manual misdescribed the war its author had won, an executioner of a season whose record neither the economics nor the strategy can be made to carry, and a reader whose margins outlasted his manifestos. That composite figure is less useful to every army that marches under his face, and more useful to the student, whose business is not to march but to understand how a first-rate intelligence, total sincerity, and a false premise can occupy the same life, and what that occupation cost the people who lived within range of it.

## An assessment that holds both halves

The two debates table at this article's centre was built to force a single habit on the reader: to grade the icon by the economist and the economist by the record, and to refuse the two lazy verdicts between which the literature oscillates. The hagiographic verdict, in which the guerrilla's courage sanctifies the theory and the photograph certifies the thought, cannot survive contact with Bolivia, with La Cabaña, or with the ministry's own absenteeism reports. The denunciatory verdict, in which the tribunals and the failed foco exhaust the subject and the writings are dismissed as the rationalisations of a violent romantic, cannot survive contact with the Great Debate's documents, with the Algiers speech's analysis of unequal exchange inside the socialist camp, or with the critical notes' private and prescient anatomy of the Soviet model. Between those verdicts stands the assessment the evidence supports. Guevara was a genuine theorist of the second rank and an uneven one: the author of one refuted strategy, one unfinished and partly unanswerable economic argument, and one prophetic critique he never published. He was also a man whose hands carry the record of the tribunals he supervised and whose doctrine of sacrifice, applied to others, licensed severities that his theory of love cannot be allowed to perfume.

The series pattern this profile instantiates is the recovery of an unknown substantial work behind a famous lesser one, and the recovery changes the assessment in a specific way. Without the economics, Guevara is a guerrilla romantic whose theory died with him and whose afterlife is a photograph. With the economics, he becomes a figure in the serious history of socialist thought: the minister who forced the question of whether the transition's means determine its end, and who answered it with a system that failed as practice and survives as the standing objection to every market socialism that believes its instruments are innocent. That is a smaller immortality than the poster's. It is also a real one, earned in the journals rather than in the mountains, and it is the one this profile exists to record.

## The diary's journey: from a captured rucksack to the world's front pages

The Bolivian diary's publication history is an episode of the Cold War fought with a manuscript. Captured material from the campaign, including documents the guerrillas left behind, had already shown the hunters their quarry's identity and method. The diary itself, kept to the eve of the final battle, fell into the victors' hands with its author's effects, and its disposition became an affair of state: the Bolivian authorities prepared an edition whose framing would serve the official account, while copies of the text travelled, by channels the literature still disputes in detail, to Havana, which published its own edition with a prologue by Castro presenting the document as the campaign's authentic voice. The two editions confronted each other across the propaganda war, and scholars spent years collating them, establishing the text's integrity against the suspicions each side entertained of the other's forgery. The consensus that emerged, that the diary is genuine in substance in both printings, gave the world a rarity: a defeated commander's uncensored account of his own defeat, published by his enemies and his friends simultaneously, each for their own purposes, and serving in the end a third purpose neither intended, the instruction of readers who came to it without an army to recruit or a verdict to defend.

The diary's content has been weighed earlier in this profile as evidence about the doctrine. Its journey adds a last item to the iconography's account. The manuscript's authenticity mattered to both camps because the martyr's relics include his words, and the words, uniquely among the relics, argue back. The diary does not read as a saint's testament. It reads as a staff officer's log of a failing operation, terse, tired, intermittently bitter about allies and about the population's refusal, and its very flatness is what makes it the corpus's most credible document. Movements that marched under the portrait rarely marched under the diary. The distinction between the two receptions is, once again, the distinction between the icon and the unknown Guevara, stated this time in the history of a single book.

## The hunt: rangers, advisers, and the closing of the net

The Bolivian campaign's military history is a study in the asymmetry the foco doctrine refused to price. Against the few dozen guerrillas stood a state that learned. The Bolivian army, humiliated in the campaign's first encounters, rebuilt its effort around a ranger battalion trained by American advisers for exactly this war, supported by an intelligence effort that penetrated the foco's urban network, rolled up its couriers and safe houses in the cities, and progressively severed the column from supply, information, and reinforcement. The guerrillas' captured documents, including a diary of the campaign's first phase left behind in a cave, were exploited with professional competence. Deserters were debriefed. The population of the zone, on whom the doctrine counted for the sea in which the fish would swim, provided the hunters with information instead, whether from loyalty to a state that had given them land, from fear of the army's reprisals, or from the simple judgement of peasants about which armed band in their valley was going to lose.

Set the campaign beside the Sierra and the doctrine's misdescription stands in full light. In Cuba the state's army had been the rotting instrument of a dictator whose own patron withdrew its support; in Bolivia the state's army was the improving instrument of a government whose patron increased its support, and the difference decided the outcome before the first ambush. The foco theory had treated the enemy as a constant, a regular army whose defeat the first proposition simply asserted to be possible, and treated the international environment as scenery. In the event, the enemy was a variable, the environment was the decisive theatre, and the foco, fixed in its ravine by its own doctrine's contempt for retreat into politics, was ground down by the application of resources it had no theory for answering. Military historians who study the campaign professionally draw the obvious conclusion: that it was lost in the capital and in the countryside's allegiance before it was lost in the Quebrada del Yuro. The doctrine's author, who had written that the guerrilla's first duty is to the population among whom he operates, died in a zone whose population had declined the relationship, and the diary he kept records his knowledge of the fact in entries of mounting bleakness. The hunt is therefore not an epilogue to the theory. It is the theory's examination, set by an enemy who had read the manual and prepared the answers the manual said could not exist.

## The final day: capture, sentence, and the witnesses

The record of the last day can be assembled from the witnesses, and it should be, since the martyrology begins by dissolving it. Wounded in the leg in the ravine fight and captured with his carbine damaged, Guevara was held overnight in the schoolhouse of the village of La Higuera, where the officers who interrogated him found a prisoner who declined to bargain and who spoke, by their own later accounts, with a composure that unsettled the room. The sentence came by telephone from the high command in the capital: the prisoner was not to survive to a trial, whose platform the government had reason to fear. The execution was carried out the following day by a sergeant who volunteered or was chosen, the accounts differing in the usual ways, and the body was flown by helicopter, lashed to the skid in view of the cameras, to Vallegrande and the laundry slab described in the next section. Agents of the American intelligence services were present in the campaign's headquarters and at the margins of these events, advising, photographing the captured documents, and reporting to Washington; the degree of their influence on the sentence itself is disputed in the literature and is stated here as presence, not as authorship, since the order's chain that the evidence supports is Bolivian.

The witnesses' testimony, gathered over decades by journalists who tracked down the participants, has the grain of the real: the sergeant's drinking before the act and his haunted afterlife, the officer who kept a memento, the schoolteacher of the village who spoke with the prisoner in his last morning and remembered a man asking after the school. None of it supports the poster. It supports a smaller and harder image: an asthmatic prisoner with a wounded leg, in a mud-floored classroom, at the end of a campaign whose diary he had kept honestly enough to know it was lost, facing the consequence his doctrine had always priced at zero for its author because he had priced it, in advance, at everything. The assessment of the theory does not require the death to be noble. It requires only that the death be faced as he faced it, and the witnesses agree that it was.

## Vallegrande: the displayed body and the manufacture of a martyr

The iconography begins not with the portrait but with a corpse. After the execution at La Higuera, the Bolivian authorities displayed Guevara's body in the laundry room of the hospital at Vallegrande, laid on a concrete slab, washed, with the eyes open, and admitted the press to photograph it. The intention was evidentiary: to prove the death, to end the myth of invulnerability, to discourage the continent's imitators with the fact of the hunter's success. The effect was the opposite, and the photographs explain why. The displayed body, bearded, wasted, serene in a way the living man in his last months had not been, resembled nothing so much as the deposition imagery of Christian art, the dead Christ taken down and laid out for the mourners; the comparison was made immediately, by journalists who were present and by the world's editors who received the wires, and it fixed the interpretation of the death before any doctrine could be attached to it. The Bolivian command had intended a police photograph. It produced a pietà.

The martyrology that followed was therefore built by his enemies' evidence, and its structure shaped the afterlife this article has been tracing. A martyr requires no programme; he requires a death, a body, and an image. The Vallegrande photographs supplied all three within days of the execution, and the Korda portrait, exhumed from its studio in the same season, supplied the living face to match the dead one. Between the two images, the corpus disappeared: the world received a saint of rebellion with a complete iconography and no bibliography, and the economic debate, the tribunal record, the Bolivian diary's own account of failure, all became footnotes to a passion. Guevara had written, in the farewell letter, that wherever death surprised him it would be welcome provided the battle cry had reached a receptive ear. The martyrology fulfilled the sentence with a literalness he might have appreciated and a content he would have despised, since the receptive ears received the image and let the argument go.

## The movements that refused the doctrine and prevailed

The doctrine's sharpest cross-examination comes not from its failures but from its contemporaries' successes by other roads. In Nicaragua, the Sandinista movement that eventually overthrew a dynasty in 1979 had begun, in the early sixties, as a faithful foco and had been destroyed as one, in the mountains, with its founders dead or scattered. The movement that won was the rebuilt one: a broad alliance strategy that combined rural and urban organisation, legal and clandestine work, an insurrectionary final phase resting on mass uprisings in the cities, and a political programme addressed to classes far beyond the peasantry, conducted over nearly two decades of accumulation. In El Salvador, the insurgency that fought the state to a negotiated settlement in the following decade likewise rested on mass organisations built patiently in unions, universities, and parishes, with the armed force as their shield rather than their substitute. The comparison is not exact, since no two countries repeat each other's conditions, and the successful movements themselves honoured Guevara's memory and used his vocabulary. But the structural lesson is the one the orthodox parties had stated in the sixties and the foco had been invented to refute: the victories, where they came, came to the movements that built the political conditions first and treated the armed nucleus as an instrument of a politics that already existed.

The comparison also disciplines the assessment in the opposite direction. The movements that refused the doctrine and won their insurrections then faced, in government, the second of Guevara's questions, the transition's economics, and answered it with versions of the market accommodation his side of the Great Debate had warned against, under pressures, blockade, war, and inherited ruin, that make judgement easy only for the uninvolved. The foco's author lost the argument about how revolutions are made and posed, more sharply than any of the victors, the argument about what they are for. A profile that has been severe on the first failure owes the record that acknowledgment, since severity without it becomes the denunciatory camp's mirror. The doctrine's refusers won countries. The doctrine's author, dead in a ravine, left the questions that the winners' governments have spent their tenures failing to answer to their own satisfaction.

## Foquismo's second life: the doctrine after its author

The doctrine did not die at La Higuera, and its second life is part of its record. Through the following decade, armed organisations across the continent continued to invoke the foco's authority, adapting it to conditions its author never faced. In the cities, urban guerrilla movements translated the nucleus into the commando, substituting the spectacular armed action, the kidnapping, the expropriation, for the rural foco's slow embedding, and discovering in a few years what the rural movements had discovered: that spectacle is not organisation, and that a state which cannot be detonated cannot be embarrassed into collapse either. In the countryside, the surviving movements that endured into later decades did so, as noted earlier in this profile, by ceasing to be focos, building the mass structures the doctrine had demoted. The doctrine's name survived its content, attached to organisations whose practice refuted it, because the name carried the martyr's authority and the content was never the point of the attachment.

The second life also ran through the academy and the memoir literature, where the argument about foquismo became an argument about a generation: about whether the young militants who died in the focos were betrayed by a theory, by their parties, by their states, or by the structure of a continent that offered its reformers the choice between irrelevance and the gun. That argument is not settled, and this profile does not pretend to settle it. What can be said is that the theory's author, alone among its prophets, submitted himself to its test without exemption, and that the movements of the second life generally did not: their leaderships survived their doctrines, issued communiqués about the fallen, and grew old in exile or in politics. The asymmetry does not rehabilitate the doctrine. It does complicate the moral accounting of its afterlife, in which the man most responsible for the licence was also the man who paid its price in full, while many who retailed the licence paid in instalments or not at all.

## The ritual calendar: anniversaries, anthems, and the pioneers' pledge

In Cuba the memory is not left to chance or to commerce; it is calendared. The anniversaries of the birth and of the death are state observances, the schools bear his name in their thousands across the island and beyond it, and generations of Cuban schoolchildren have begun their mornings with the pioneers' pledge to be like him, a formula whose very familiarity illustrates the distance between commemoration and comprehension. A child can recite the pledge for a decade without encountering the Great Debate, the critical notes, or the Bolivian diary's verdict on its author, and the state's pedagogy prefers it so, since the exemplar required by a school morning is a posture, not a bibliography. The ritual calendar thus performs, inside the revolution's own house, the same operation the poster performs outside it: the conversion of a writer into an emblem, serviceable precisely in proportion as he is unread.

The student of the phenomenon should resist the easy conclusion that ritual is merely falsification. Rituals preserve what arguments cannot: the emotional fact that a man once lived at that pitch of commitment, available to citizens who will never read a page of him, as a standard against which the bureaucrat's day is silently measured. Guevara himself, who designed ceremonies of emulation and understood the technology of example from inside, would have recognised the pioneers' pledge as his own instrument turned upon his own memory, with its characteristic decay already advanced: the honours system of the new man, applied to the new man's inventor, inflating its currency by daily issue. The observation is not a sneer at the schoolchildren or their teachers. It is the final turn of the profile's recurring screw. Every institution he built or inspired, the foco, the ministry, the honours, the cult, exhibits the same law: the form outlives the animating sincerity and must then be judged, as he taught his readers to judge all forms, by the relations it actually produces rather than by the devotion it proclaims.

## The icon: a photograph's career

On the fifth of March 1960, at a memorial service in Havana for the victims of an exploded freighter in the harbour, the photographer Alberto Korda, working for a Cuban newspaper, took two frames of Guevara as he stepped briefly into view on the speakers' platform: thirty-one years old, beret with a single star, hair to his shoulders, gaze fixed on something beyond the crowd. The newspaper did not use the photograph. It hung in the photographer's studio for years, one portrait among many, while its subject departed for the Congo, for Bolivia, and for the grave. After his death the image began its second life. An Italian publisher who had obtained prints in Havana reproduced it; the Irish artist Jim Fitzpatrick converted it into the high-contrast graphic that made it infinitely reproducible; and within a few years the face had become the most widely reproduced portrait of the twentieth century, detached from the photographer's name, from the memorial service, from Cuba, and eventually from any proposition its subject ever defended.

### How did the Korda photograph travel from a funeral to the world?

Slowly, and then completely. Unpublished by the newspaper that commissioned it, the portrait survived in its maker's studio until its subject's death in Bolivia made him a martyr in search of an image; then a publisher's edition and an artist's graphic simplification made the face infinitely reproducible across the world.

Each stage of the journey removed context: the crowd, the occasion, the photographer's authorship, until what circulated was not a document of a man at a funeral but a mask of rebellion available for any cause, including causes he would have shot at.

The photograph's career is the exact inverse of the economic writings' career, and the two careers together explain the reputation this article opened with. The image required nothing of its audience: no Spanish, no accounting, no assent to any proposition, only recognition. It could therefore travel everywhere, and it did, onto walls, banners, clothing, and commodities of every description, until the theorist of the new man had become the most successful brand image of the century that invented branding, a fate his own analysis of the culture industry, had he lived to apply it at home, would have savaged. The writings required everything of their audience and therefore travelled almost nowhere. Between the image that asks nothing and the argument that asks everything, the century chose as it usually chooses. The photograph is not a falsification of the man; Korda's frame is a true likeness of a real moment, and its subject's expression, grief and defiance at a funeral for sabotaged dockworkers, is documentary. The falsification happens in the circulation, where the likeness is retained and the life is discarded, and it is a falsification its subject's own theory predicts: a form, endlessly reproduced, whose content drains away until only the form's market remains.

No current product or brand is named in this discussion, deliberately. The phenomenon under analysis is not any single commodity but the mechanism, and the mechanism is more visible without the distraction of examples: an image of a man who argued that forms form people, converted into a form that forms nothing but recognition, sold back to the world as the sign of refusing to be sold. The culture industry argument developed elsewhere in this series, in the study of [the culture industry](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), supplies the framework; the Korda photograph supplies its most perfect case, a revolutionary portrait absorbed so completely by the apparatus it opposed that the absorption itself has become invisible.

## Editions and editors: how the corpus reached print

The corpus the world reads was assembled by editors, and the assembly is part of its history. In his lifetime Guevara published the manual, the war memoir in instalments and in book form, and the essays and speeches that the journals and the state's presses issued as they were delivered; the rest, the diaries, the critical notes, the Congo account, the correspondence, reached print posthumously, across half a century, in editions whose timing and framing served the purposes of their publishers. The Cuban state's editorial apparatus issued the works as scripture, in uniform editions with apparatus that guided the reading toward the exemplar. The exile and commercial presses issued them as sensation or as evidence. The scholarly editions, arriving last of all, restored contexts, dated the fragments, and printed the doubts alongside the proclamations, and it is on those editions that a profile like this one silently depends.

The editorial history explains a peculiarity of the reception that the student should understand. The corpus arrived out of order: the manual and the memoir first, the economics in fragments, the notes and the Congo diary last of all, decades after the judgements they would have complicated had hardened into the icon and the anti-icon. A thinker whose most self-critical and most heterodox texts are published two generations after his death is, in effect, two thinkers in the public record, and the second has been in print for too short a time to have displaced the first in any arena except the specialist literature. The editions are still appearing, the archive is still opening, and the assessment of the economic work in particular should be held with the humility that an incomplete corpus imposes. What can be said is that every newly published stratum so far, the notes, the diaries, the letters, has moved the figure in the same direction: away from the poster and toward the desk, away from the saint and the criminal of the rival cults and toward the minister arguing with his textbook in the margins. The editors, in the end, are grading the icon too, one volume at a time.

## Teaching the debate: the syllabus afterlife of the economics

The economic work's most durable institution is not a ministry but a syllabus. In university courses on the political economy of socialism, on Latin American development, and on the history of economic thought's heterodox branches, the Great Debate survives as a set text, excerpted in readers, assigned in the week devoted to the transition problem, and examined in the comparative frame this article has used throughout: the budgetary system against the calculus, the moral against the material incentive, the plan's consciousness against the market's information. The pedagogical survival is not accidental. The debate is almost uniquely teachable, because it is brief, because its protagonists write with a clarity that academic economics rarely permits itself, because both sides argue from shared premises to opposite conclusions, so that a student can watch the premises do the work, and because its outcome is known, which lets the classroom do what the participants could not: grade the arguments against the record they produced. Generations of students who could not find Bolivia on the icon's itinerary have nonetheless written essays on the budgetary finance system, and some of them, in the ministries and universities of several continents, have carried its questions into their own work on planning, on incentives, and on the design of institutions that must run on motives better than fear and worse than sainthood.

The syllabus afterlife also corrects the iconography's geography. The poster hangs in the dormitory; the debate is filed in the library, and the two publics overlap less than either supposes. When this profile claims that the unknown Guevara is the economist, the claim is not a paradox but a report on where the reading actually happens: in the seminar, in the reader's excerpts, in the footnotes of the transition literature, where the name attached to the argument is not the syllable on the flag but the minister of industries who held, against the whole weight of the socialist world's received doctrine, that a society gets the motives it pays for in whatever currency it chooses, and that choosing the currency is therefore the most political act an economy performs. That proposition, detached from the system built on it, is the economic work's surviving export, and it is quoted, usually without attribution, wherever incentives are debated as a question of morals rather than of mechanics.

## Sources in Spanish and English: the untranslated Guevara

A final structural fact governs everything this profile has claimed, and it belongs in the open. The documentary basis of the two reputations is asymmetrically available. The guerrilla manual was translated immediately and everywhere; it is a short book, and it made its author. The economic debate's documents exist principally in Spanish, in the Cuban journals where the argument ran and in later Cuban collections, and the English reader who wants to study the Great Debate at first hand will find only partial collections, selections, and scholarly summaries, assembled decades after the event, rather than the full run of articles and rejoinders in which the argument actually lives. The critical notes on the Soviet manual were published in Spanish, in Havana, four decades after they were written, and their circulation in English has been fragmentary. The Bolivian and Congo diaries, by contrast, are available in polished editions in every language of the reading world. The archive, as delivered to an English reader, is thus pre-selected for the romantic Guevara and against the economist, and the selection is not a conspiracy but a market: publishers translate what the image sells.

The consequence for the reader is practical. An English-language assessment of Guevara's economics necessarily rests on the scholarly literature that has worked the Spanish archive, on the translated selections, and on the summaries of participants, and this profile has been written under that discipline, attributing positions to their holders and treating quotations with the suspicion that second-hand transmission deserves. The imbalance in availability is itself part of the explanation the brief for this article demanded: the economics is unknown in part because it is unread, and it is unread in part because it is untranslated, and it is untranslated in part because the photograph does not need it. Breaking that circle is beyond one article's power. Naming it is not.

## The remains at Santa Clara: the state's custody of the relics

For three decades the location of Guevara's remains was unknown, and the unknowing was itself a part of the cult: a hero without a grave belongs everywhere, and movements on four continents could claim a patron who had no address. In the late nineteen nineties a Bolivian exhumation, guided by testimony from the soldiers who had buried the guerrillas in unmarked ground near the Vallegrande airstrip, recovered remains identified as his and his companions', and Cuba received them with the full ceremonial of a state funeral, interring them in a memorial at Santa Clara, the city of his most famous victory. The relics' homecoming completed the iconography's institutional form: the martyr acquired a shrine, a perpetual flame, a museum of his effects, and a place in the state's calendar of pilgrimage, where schoolchildren and foreign visitors are conducted past the bronze statue that raises his rifle over the city he took.

The state's custody of the memory is a political fact the assessment should record without cynicism and without piety. A revolution that governs for decades requires ancestors, and it selects and shapes them: the Guevara of the memorial is the guerrilla and the internationalist, the minister of austerity whose example rebukes the bureaucrat, while the economist of the Great Debate, whose doctrines the governing system set aside, and the critic of the socialist camp, whose notes slept unpublished for forty years, are present at the shrine in the way that ancestors are present, honoured and unconsulted. The pattern is not Cuban and not new; it is the standard fate of founders in the custody of their successors. What distinguishes this case is only the global scale of the counter-cult outside the state's control, which keeps its own Guevara, the face without the corpus, in a custody no ministry administers. Between the shrine and the poster, the reader with the corpus open is the memory's only uncaptured custodian, which is the practical ground of this article's insistence that the texts, all of them, are the subject.

## A note on method: how this profile has used its sources

The reader is owed a word about the handling of evidence in what precedes, since the subject's literature is a battlefield and this article has taken positions on it throughout. Where the record is documentary, the debate's published exchanges, the manual, the speeches, the diaries in their scholarly editions, the profile has argued from the texts and quoted sparingly and within the limits its series observes. Where the record is testimonial, the tribunals' season, the final day at La Higuera, the ministry's daily life, it has preferred the convergence of hostile and friendly witnesses to the colour of either, and has said so at the points where the convergence thins. Where the brief governing this article forbade figures, in the matter of the tribunals and the campaigns, it has quantified nothing and has directed the reader to the literatures where the rival quantifications, and the reasons for their rivalry, can be inspected. And where judgement was unavoidable, the profile has followed the rule its subject's better self exemplified and his worse self violated: that the strongest form of the opposing case is the only form worth answering. The result is not neutrality, which on this record would be a posture rather than a finding. It is an assessment with its working shown, which is the most that a profile in a series of this kind should promise, and the least that its subject, who showed his own working in diaries he knew would be read against him, is entitled to demand.

## What survived of the ministry: institutions and their half-lives

Institutions outlive doctrines at different rates, and a brief inventory of the ministry's survivals sharpens the assessment. The consolidated enterprise, the branch grouping through which the budgetary system administered industry, dissolved with the system itself into the standardised structures of the following decade. The central planning board, created in the revolution's first years to stand above the ministries, outlasted every system it successively administered, calculus and budgetary alike, a reminder that the plan as an institution is older and tougher than any doctrine of the plan. The training schools' pairing of the ledger and the doctrine persisted in the party's educational apparatus long after the ledger's half of the pairing had been quietly dropped. The voluntary labour mobilisations survived as ritual into the era of the rectification and beyond, their meaning inverted from the minister's example into the state's levy. And the wage scale's egalitarian compression, the pay packet's severance from the enterprise's fortunes, persisted as a social fact through every subsequent reform, defended by the workforce itself whenever later governments proposed to differentiate earnings by results, so that the most durable element of the budgetary construction turned out to be the one its author regarded as transitional: the egalitarian wage, which the workers, formed by the system in a sense he did not intend, declined to surrender.

The inventory teaches the profile's final institutional lesson. What survives of a system is not its centre but its conveniences: the arrangements that some constituency finds useful enough to defend when the doctrine that built them is gone. The budgetary system's centre, the moral economy of the whole, had no constituency but its author's conviction, and it departed with him. Its conveniences, the compressed wage, the socialised services, the security of the state payroll, acquired constituencies of millions and became the social contract whose terms every successor government has had to renegotiate rather than repeal. A theorist is not responsible for the selection his posterity makes among his works, but a profile is responsible for recording it, since the selection is the truest referendum the work will ever receive. By that referendum the unknown Guevara's most successful policy was the one he valued least as theory, and the doctrine he valued most was the one no constituency would keep. The irony is structural, not personal, and it awaits every builder of systems who confuses the permanence of his convictions with the permanence of his constructions.

## The questions left open: the agenda the corpus bequeaths

A body of work should finally be graded by the questions it leaves in working order, and this corpus bequeaths four that no subsequent literature has closed. The first is the transition's information problem, posed by the budgetary system's failure to answer it: by what signals does a society that has abolished the market learn where its resources are scarce and its products unwanted, and can those signals be generated without recreating, in the reporting chain, the very interests the abolition was meant to dissolve? The second is the formation problem, posed by the new man's career: by what institutions can a society deliberately shape the motives of its members without the shaping apparatus becoming a caste whose own motives escape the shaping? The third is the strategy problem, posed by the foco's refutation: under what conditions, specifiable in advance rather than narrated afterward, does armed action by a minority accelerate a revolution rather than substitute for one, and who is authorised to judge that the conditions hold? The fourth is the camp problem, posed at Algiers and in the critical notes: what relations between socialist states would not reproduce, in fraternal dress, the hierarchy of the market they claim to have superseded, and what institution could embody them?

The list has a property worth remarking. Each question was posed by its author's failure at least as much as by his insight: the information problem by the ministry's shadow ledgers, the formation problem by the honours' inflation, the strategy problem by the ravine, the camp problem by the patron's prices and the patron's telephone diplomacy. A corpus whose questions are the scar tissue of its defeats has a claim on students that a more successful body of work might not, since success conceals its premises in its results while failure exposes them for inspection. The questions are bequeathed open, and the openness is not a defect of the bequest. It is the bequest, the part of the unknown Guevara that remains in circulation among people who have never worn his face, working in seminars and ministries and movements on problems he did not solve and could not evade. The icon asks for imitation. The corpus, read whole, asks for answers, and grades its readers by the same standard its author applied to himself: that the answer be paid for in the attempt.

## Reading the corpus whole: the shape of the work

A final survey of the work as a whole, before the assessment closes, repays the student who has followed its parts. The corpus is not large by the standards of the century's major Marxists, and its shape is unusual: no single systematic treatise, but a ring of genres around two centres. Around the guerrilla centre stand the manual, the war memoir, the continental essays, the campaign diaries, and the final message. Around the economic centre stand the debate articles, the ministry speeches, the essay on consciousness, the Algiers address, and the posthumous notes. The genres are occasional, every one of them written for an audience and a deadline, and the occasionality is the corpus's strength and its limit in one. Strength, because the writing is everywhere in contact with practice, tested against ministries and mountains rather than against other books. Limit, because the systematic questions, the theory of the transition as a whole, the account of the state, of law, of the party's form, are everywhere approached and nowhere completed, so that the corpus reads as the record of a mind perpetually interrupted by the events it was trying to theorise, first by office and then by war and then by death.

Read whole, the two centres illuminate each other in a way the iconography never permits. The foco and the budgetary system are the same thought in two elements: in war, the nucleus whose consciousness creates the army; in peace, the plan whose consciousness creates the economy; in both, the formed minority as the efficient cause of the unformed majority's transformation, and example as the mechanism of formation. The thought is noble, coherent, and, on the record assembled in this profile, mistaken in both elements for the same reason: formation is real, but it is slower, more reciprocal, and more dependent on material feedback than the thought allows, and the minorities who embody it are themselves formed, deformed, and finally judged by the processes they claimed to conduct. That sentence is the corpus's epitaph and its continuing use. No subsequent theory of revolution or of transition can responsibly ignore the questions this body of work posed at the point of a gun and a ledger. None can responsibly adopt its answers unexamined. The distance between those two obligations is exactly the width of the unknown Guevara, and measuring it has been this article's business.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination problem with Guevara is that the syllabus figure and the historical figure diverge, and marks are awarded for knowing both and confusing neither. Prepare the two debates as a pair: for the foco, learn the three propositions cold, state the second proposition's reversal of the classical sequence in one sentence, and hold the record ready, Cuba's exceptional conditions against the continental failures and Bolivia. For the economics, learn the budgetary finance system's features as a list you can unpack, self-financing as its rival in its proponents' terms, and the incentives argument as a claim about formation rather than a slogan about virtue. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach Guevara as a paired text and the pairing does the pedagogy. Set the manual's opening chapter beside a chapter of the economic debate and let the seminar discover the shared structure for itself: the refusal to wait, the substitution of will for conditions, the insistence that forms form people. The economic debate is the finest classroom instrument in this cluster, because it gives students a real argument, between named administrators, at a technical level they can actually follow, over questions, enterprise autonomy, interest, bonuses, that connect directly to the incentives problem treated in the companion study of [the incentive problem in socialism](/2019/11/01/incentive-problem-socialism/). Run the debate as a structured controversy: half the room briefed as Rodríguez, half as Guevara, each side required to state the other's case before attacking it. Keep the tribunal record in the first session, not the last, so that no student builds an admiration the record then ambushes. The revolution's own narrative belongs to the companion study of [the Cuban revolution](/2018/11/01/cuban-revolution-marxist-analysis/); your seminar's distinctive work is the thinker, and the thinker is most teachable where he is most wrong and most serious in the same paragraph.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on Guevara is archival and Spanish. The Great Debate's full documentary run, the ministry's internal records, and the critical notes on the Soviet manual reward work in the original journals and collections, and the English-language literature's dependence on selections should be treated as a finding about the field rather than as a convenience. Three questions repay attention. First, the debate's European dimension, the Mandel and Bettelheim interventions, situates Cuba inside a socialist-world argument about value that the standard island-centred narratives flatten. Second, the notes' prediction of Soviet restoration invites a rigorous rather than a celebratory reading: what exactly was the mechanism claimed, and how much of the later collapse does it explain? The companion study of [whether the USSR was socialist](/2019/05/15/was-the-ussr-socialist/) frames that argument. Third, the debate's afterlife in later market-socialist reform programmes, traced in the study of [whether planning can beat the market](/2019/10/15/can-planning-beat-the-market/), remains underworked. Journalists, meanwhile, might retire the photograph from stories to which its subject's economics is the actual relevance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Che Guevara?

Ernesto Guevara was an Argentine physician who joined the Cuban expedition of 1956, rose to command a column in the Sierra Maestra, and after the victory of 1959 directed the new state's economy from the national bank and the ministry of industries. He wrote a manual of guerrilla warfare, argued a distinctive system of socialist economic management in a public debate, criticised the Soviet model in print and in private notes, and died in 1967 leading a guerrilla foco in Bolivia. His face, from a 1960 photograph, became the most reproduced revolutionary image in the world.

### Q: What is the foco theory?

The foco theory holds that a small, disciplined armed nucleus, inserted into favourable rural terrain, can itself create the conditions for a revolution instead of waiting for those conditions to mature. Its three propositions are that a people's forces can beat a standing army, that the insurrectional foco generates the revolutionary situation through its own action, and that the countryside, rather than the city, forms the basic terrain of struggle across the underdeveloped Americas. The doctrine was set out in Guevara's manual of 1960 and codified, in a harder form, by Régis Debray in 1967.

### Q: Did Che reverse the Marxist view of revolutionary conditions?

Yes, and the reversal is the theory's centre. The classical position required objective conditions, a crisis of the old order and masses in motion, to ripen before revolutionary action could succeed. Guevara's second proposition ran the sequence backward: the foco's action would provoke repression, expose the regime's vulnerability, recruit the population, and thereby manufacture the situation it needed. Cuba appeared to vindicate the reversal, though the island's crisis largely pre-existed the landing. Applied across the continent, where the conditions were absent, the reversal failed wherever it was tried.

### Q: What was the Great Debate on economic management?

Between 1963 and 1965, Cuban administrators and invited European economists argued in print over the operating rules of the nationalised economy. Guevara defended a budgetary finance system in which enterprises held no funds of their own, internal transfers were accounting entries, and moral incentives predominated. Carlos Rafael Rodríguez, Alberto Mora, and others defended the self-financing alternative modelled on Soviet practice, with profit as a measure, interest on capital, and material incentives. The argument joined questions of accounting to the deepest question of the transition: whether market forms could be used without reproducing the society they came from.

### Q: What is the budgetary finance system?

It is the system Guevara built as minister of industries, in which the whole state economy is administered as a single enterprise. A factory's receipts flow to the state budget and its spending is assigned from that budget against the plan; it keeps no bank account of its own, pays no interest to the state, and is judged by plan fulfilment at falling real cost rather than by any surplus, since it retains none. Money between state units becomes a unit of account rather than a fund of power, and allocation follows the plan's priorities directly.

### Q: What did Che mean by the new man?

The new man was his name for the human product the transition was supposed to form: a person in whom solidarity and social duty progressively displace the competitive self-interest formed by the old order. The phrase named no finished saint. It named a direction of formation, driven by education, by the vanguard's example, and above all by economic arrangements that reinforced rather than contradicted the teaching, since a worker summoned chiefly by bonuses learns that his labour is a commodity. Moral incentives were the proposed machinery of the formation, and their decay in practice is the standing objection to the whole conception.

### Q: Did Che criticise the Soviet Union?

Publicly, in code that every listener understood, and privately, without code at all. In his Algiers speech of February 1965 he charged the socialist countries with complicity in imperialist exploitation through the terms of their trade with the developing world. In notebooks written afterward and published in Spanish four decades later, he criticised the Soviet manual of political economy directly and argued that a system retaining the law of value, profit criteria, and material incentives was travelling back toward capitalism. The criticism was economic and structural, grounded in his side of the Cuban debate, not a factional quarrel between clients and patrons.

### Q: What was Che's record at the revolutionary tribunals?

In the first months of 1959 he commanded La Cabaña fortress in Havana, where revolutionary tribunals tried accused agents of the fallen dictatorship. The apparatus answered to him: cases passed across his desk, death sentences required his confirmation, and the hearings moved with a speed that left the accused little room for defence before men were shot. How many died remains unresolvable from the literature, since the exile accounts and the official Cuban accounts were written by enemies and share no common arithmetic. The executions themselves, his authority over them, and his willingness to justify the trials in public as justice against the dictatorship's torturers are not in dispute.

### Q: Why did foquismo fail?

Because its central proposition was false in the conditions where it was applied. The foco was supposed to create the revolutionary situation; instead, inserted among peasants bound to the state by reform, against governments with cohesive armies and foreign support, and without the mass organisations that alone could protect it, the nucleus was isolated and destroyed, repeatedly, across the continent. Movements that endured survived by shedding the doctrine's central claim and remaking themselves as political armies embedded in real social conflicts. The theory's author, applying it in Bolivia in the absence of his own manual's stated conditions, was captured and executed in 1967.

### Q: Why did the Korda photograph become so famous?

Alberto Korda photographed Guevara at a Havana memorial service in March 1960, and the newspaper that employed him left the frame unused; it rested in a studio drawer for years. His death in Bolivia then created a martyr in need of an image, a publisher carried the portrait abroad, and an artist reduced it to a stark graphic that any press could reproduce, at the exact moment when movements across the world wanted a universal sign of rebellion. The image asked nothing of its audience, no language, no doctrine, no assent, and so it travelled everywhere, shedding context at every stage until the likeness of a man remained and the life, the economics, and the record had all been discarded.

### Q: How does the unknown Guevara grade the familiar icon?

Harshly, and in the icon's disfavour. The familiar image certifies a romantic guerrilla whose strategic theory failed everywhere it was tried and whose author died of the failure. The unknown figure, the minister arguing over accounting systems and incentives, produced the work that specialists still read with respect: the anti-market case in the Great Debate, the Algiers analysis of unequal exchange within the socialist camp, and the private prediction of Soviet restoration. Grading the icon by the economist reverses the usual proportions. The photograph is the least substantial thing about him that the world has chosen to keep.

### Q: What should a student separate between the economic writings and the guerrilla manual?

Their evidentiary status. The manual generalises one campaign into a strategy and was refuted by the continental record, including its author's final campaign; it survives as testimony and craft. The economic writings state an argument, about incentives, value, and the formative power of institutions, that was displaced politically rather than refuted analytically, and that recurs in every later debate over market socialism. A student who treats both texts as equally authoritative, or equally dismissible, has missed the distinction on which any serious assessment of their author depends. Separate the refuted strategy from the unfinished argument.

### Q: Why did the foco theory fail where its conditions were absent?

On its own pages the doctrine withheld permission from situations lacking its preconditions: a dictatorship that had closed peaceful recourse, a peasantry with grievances the state had not answered, an enemy in decomposition. Where movements began anyway, in defiance of elected governments, among peasants whose land hunger a reform had already answered, or facing armies that were cohesive and foreign-supplied, the nucleus could not generate the missing conditions by example. Repression atomised rather than radicalised the population, the foco could protect no one, and recruitment starved. The failure in absent conditions is therefore not a puzzle about the theory's application but the predictable working of its central error.

### Q: How does the two debates table order his legacy?

By refusing to grade the legacy as one thing. The table sets his two great arguments side by side, the foco debate on how revolutions begin and the economic debate on how the transition is run, with the opposing position, the record, and the residue of each. Read down its columns, the foco's claim ends refuted and the economic claim ends unfinished, displaced rather than answered. The ordering matters because the public legacy inverts it: the refuted argument made the reputation, through the manual and the photograph, while the unfinished one, which specialists respect, remains largely unknown and substantially untranslated.

### Q: What separates the theory's stated conditions from the licence later groups took from it?

The stated conditions were restrictive: the manual refused its method to countries where peaceful struggle remained possible, demanded a peasantry won by conduct, and presupposed an enemy in decomposition. The licence later groups took was the second proposition detached from those restrictions, the claim that the armed act creates its own conditions, read as permission to begin anywhere, against any government, without prior mass organisation. Debray's codification performed much of the detachment by demoting the hedges and elevating the nucleus over the party. The distance between the restricted text and the general licence is where a generation of militants was lost.
