The most instructive Romeo and Juliet site in Verona is the one that refuses to open. On Via Arche Scaligere, a few metres from the Gothic tombs of the Scaliger lords and a short walk from the most visited courtyard in Italian literary tourism, stands the Casa di Romeo: a fortified medieval palace of brick and white tuff, three wings around a courtyard, a round-arched gate in a crenellated wall, and beside the gate two plaques. The gate stays shut. The building is a private residence, documented from 1339, owned across its recorded life by the Scaliger, the Nogarola and the Bevilacqua-Lazise, and attached to Romeo by a tradition that cannot produce a single document placing his family inside it. Verona built a ticketed shrine industry on the heroine's side of the story, complete with a balcony assembled in the twentieth century, and on the hero's side it maintains two plaques that cleaners periodically scrub free of graffiti. This article argues a verdict and defends it across the whole map: Verona's Romeo and Juliet geography is a single-protagonist geography, and the asymmetry is not an accident of neglect but the shape of the myth itself, because the play gives Juliet architecture and gives Romeo weather. Juliet receives a window, a chamber, a tomb, rooms that a city can furnish and sell. Romeo receives a sycamore grove outside the walls, a street brawl, the outside of another family's orchard wall, a friar's borrowed cell and a road to Mantua. Tourism can only build where the text builds rooms, and the text built Romeo none. The closed gate on Via Arche Scaligere is therefore the most honest object on the entire itinerary: the one site that admits, in brick, that the fiction is a fiction.

## Where Is Romeo's House in Verona? The Fortified Palazzo on Via Arche Scaligere

The house stands at Via Arche Scaligere 2, in the oldest quarter of the historic centre, with the Scaliger tombs at one shoulder and the small square of the Peschiera vecchia, the old fish market, at the other. Its position deserves a moment of attention before its legend does, because the position is real and the legend is not. The Arche Scaligere, the raised Gothic sarcophagi of Cangrande I, Mastino II and Cansignorio, enclosed behind their wrought-iron fence beside the church of Santa Maria Antica, commemorate the dynasty under whose ancestors the story is set: Luigi da Porto placed the lovers in the Verona of Bartolomeo della Scala, who ruled from 1301 to 1304. A visitor standing at Romeo's gate is therefore standing between the two poles of the Veronese myth, the documented tombs of the ruling house on one side and an undocumented lover's house on the other, and the tombs are the ones with the evidence. The walk from the gate to Juliet's courtyard on Via Cappello takes under ten minutes. The two houses of the feud, as the tourist map arranges them, are close enough to visit in a single morning and far enough apart in treatment to measure the whole argument of this article in footsteps.

The building itself is the best object on the Romeo and Juliet itinerary, which is part of the indictment. The architectural historian Giovanni Luigi Lugoboni, in his survey of Veronese residences published in 2017, ranks the palace among the finest and best-preserved surviving examples of a late medieval civil dwelling in Verona, a judgment the standard catalogue of Scaliger-era buildings supports. The complex develops on three sides around a courtyard, in the U-shaped plan of a fortified urban house of the fourteenth century, and presents itself to the street through a high wall crowned with swallowtail battlements, the Ghibelline merlons that announced a household's allegiance in stone. Behind that wall the three wings were each fronted at ground level by a loggia; the wing facing the entrance portal keeps its arched loggia, while the loggias of the flanking wings were walled up and absorbed into the fabric across the centuries. The elevations mix brick with white tuff stone and carry windows of three periods, Romanesque, Gothic and Renaissance, the sediment of a working house repeatedly adapted rather than a monument preserved in amber. The right-hand wing still wears its original crenellation. An external stair rises beside the gate. The whole composition reads, as the German reference literature puts it, as a festes Haus, a strong house, built by people who expected trouble: massive outer walls, few openings, a defensible courtyard, a gate of dressed stone that could be barred. Whatever else is doubtful about the Casa di Romeo, its fabric is a genuine document of the fortified domestic architecture of factional Verona, the built environment of the world the play imagines. Verona owns no more authentic piece of the feud's material setting, and Verona cannot sell a ticket to it.

### Can visitors go inside Romeo's house?

No. The Casa di Romeo is a private residence, and its interior, courtyard and loggia are closed to the public in every form: no ticket, no tour, no museum hours exist for it. The visit is the street frontage alone, wall, gate and two plaques, taken in from the pavement of Via Arche Scaligere in minutes.

The contrast with the building's twin could hardly be sharper, and the companion account of the Casa di Giulietta and its manufactured balcony sets out the other side of the ledger in full: [Casa di Giulietta: The Balcony That Lies](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/). There, the city bought the palace in 1905, restored it into romance, assembled a balcony in the 1930s, and admits the world for a fee. Here, the city maintains two plaques. Between those two treatments lies the entire history of how a literary myth becomes a municipal industry on one side of a story and remains a rumour on the other, and the chapters of that history are recoverable in unusual detail: a documentary record that names the house's real owners, a family tradition that attaches it to the wrong family at the level of a city district, an official restoration programme that tried to buy it and failed, and a play whose own geography explains why the failure was permanent. Each of those chapters deserves its evidence in full.

The approach to the gate already teaches the lesson, because the walk is a walk through the documented city that ends at the undocumented house. The standard itinerary reaches Via Arche Scaligere from Piazza dei Signori, the seat of Veronese government, where a statue of Dante, raised in 1865, commemorates the seven years of exile the poet spent under Scaliger protection in the city his verse made answerable for the Montecchi and Cappelletti. From the square the route passes the Palazzo della Ragione and the Torre dei Lamberti, instruments of the commune's real history, and arrives at Santa Maria Antica, the small church the Scaliger adopted as their own, with the family cemetery at its door. Every object on that approach can be footnoted: the commune, the poet, the dynasty, the church. The tombs then supply the approach's climax, Cangrande on his horse above his sarcophagus, Mastino and Cansignorio under their canopies, the most concentrated display of signorial self-fashioning in the Veneto, and beside them, sharing their street and borrowing their authority by adjacency, stands the gate of a house whose famous resident is documented nowhere at all. Geography performs the argument before any plaque is read. The hero's house derives what credibility it has from its neighbours, the way a weak currency circulates on the strength of the reserves held next door, and the neighbours are entirely real. Strip the Arche Scaligere away and the Casa di Romeo would be a handsome fortified palace in a quiet lane, admired by architectural historians and photographed by nobody. Set beside them, it becomes the tomb-side stop on a pilgrimage, and pilgrims arriving from the tombs bring the tombs' authenticity with them in their eyes. The map's first mechanism is therefore contiguity: the fiction is moored to the finest documented monuments in the quarter, and the mooring is never examined because the monuments hold the attention the examination would require.

The street itself completes the arrangement. Via Arche Scaligere is narrow, shaded, and short; it does not open into a square or widen into a forecourt; the palace wall runs along it at the height of a fortification, and the pedestrian's experience is of passing a fortified frontage rather than arriving at a destination. Juliet's house, by contrast, is approached through a vaulted entrance passage that delivers the visitor into an enclosed courtyard, a room outdoors, where the visit can be staged, prolonged, photographed and monetized. The two approaches are the two fates in embryo. One site receives its public in a space; the other receives its public on a pavement, in transit, at the speed of walking. Urbanistically the difference is an accident of medieval building lines; commercially it is the whole story, since a pavement cannot hold a queue, a ritual, or a till.

## Whose House Was It Really? The Documented Owners of the Casa di Romeo

The documentary life of the palace begins in 1339, when the building enters the written record in the form catalogued by Lorella Dalle Piane in her study of the house for the repertoire of Scaliger presences in the Veronese territory. That date matters more than any other fact in this article, so its weight should be felt precisely. Da Porto set the lovers' story in the years of Bartolomeo della Scala, 1301 to 1304. The house that tourism calls Romeo's is first documented thirty-five years after the story's supposed events, and its documented owners belong to the generation of Cangrande's successors, not to the Verona of the legend. A building can of course stand before it is mentioned, and the fabric of the palace is consistent with a fourteenth-century origin; the gap between the legend's date and the document's date is not a disproof of anything by itself. What the 1339 date establishes is the shape of the evidence: for this house, unlike a cathedral or a castle, the archive begins after the myth's moment and names families the myth never mentions.

The chain, as the Veronese scholarship reconstructs it, runs as follows. The property appears first in the Scaliger orbit and passed to the Nogarola, a powerful Veronese family of French descent bound to the ruling house by marriage: Bailardino Nogarola had married Caterina della Scala, a sister of Cangrande I. The residence is associated in the record above all with Cagnolo Nogarola, the member of that family whose name the palace still carries in its formal designations, Palazzo Nogarola or Palazzo Cagnolo Nogarola. At an undocumented point the property reverted to the Scaliger, and from 1416 it belonged to the Bevilacqua-Lazise, another of the city's prominent houses, whose name it also retains in the compound form Palazzo Nogarola, Bevilacqua-Lazise. In the fifteenth century the Nogarola connection ended in sale and the complex was divided among several owners, the fate of most great urban houses, and its later centuries are a history of subdivision, adaptation and mixed use: the Italian reference entry for the building lists its present uses as a private dwelling, a restaurant and a tourist attraction, in that order, which is a fair summary of its actual standing. A restaurant, the Osteria al Duca, trades at the same address on the ground floor, profiting from the street's footfall and the house's name while the residence above and behind it stays closed. Nothing in the documented chain, at any link, names a Montecchi as owner, tenant or resident of this building.

One more feature of the documented building deserves emphasis, because it quietly disqualifies the palace from the role the myth assigns it. A fourteenth-century Veronese house of this rank was not a residence in the separable modern sense; as the catalogue literature notes of this palace, it fulfilled the double function standard for its class and period, dwelling and business premises under one fortification, with storage, trade and household life stacked behind the same wall. The Casa di Romeo was a working asset of a courtier family, a strongbox with rooms, adapted across centuries as its owners' fortunes and subdivisions required: loggias walled in when space was wanted, windows recut in the taste of successive centuries, the whole fabric repeatedly negotiated between defence, display and rent. Nothing in that biography is a criticism of the building, which survives precisely because it stayed useful; but it is a biography of the wrong genre for a shrine. The myth requires a house arrested at the moment of the story, a chamber where a boy slept before the feast, a stair he climbed. What the documents offer instead is a premises, continuously renegotiated by Scaliger clients, Nogarola heirs and Bevilacqua-Lazise purchasers, in a city where even the ruling family's palace complex was a working seat of government rather than a stage set. Avena's design, had it been funded and the owners persuaded, would have had to freeze this working house into a tableau against the whole grain of its history, evicting not a legend but a going concern. The owners' refusal, read in that light, was not philistinism; it was the building's biography defending itself, and the city, presented with the bill for the defence's defeat, declined to pay it. The shrine industry got its tableau elsewhere, in a palace whose family had died out of it and which the market had already reduced to an inn, a building whose working life was over and whose tableau life could therefore begin. The distinction between the two houses is finally that simple: hers was available because it was finished, his was refused because it was still in use.

### Did the Montecchi family own the Casa di Romeo?

No document places the Montecchi in ownership of the palace. The documented chain runs from the Scaliger to the Nogarola, back to the Scaliger, and from 1416 to the Bevilacqua-Lazise, the house being associated above all with Cagnolo Nogarola. The Montecchi connection works at district level: the family lived in this quarter, and proximity did the rest.

Set that chain beside the chain that supports Juliet's house and the difference in kind becomes visible. The Cappello identification rests on a family that demonstrably held one specific palace on Via Cappello across centuries, a name that echoes the fictional Cappelletti, and a carved hat, the cappello, on the courtyard keystone that gave the echo a visible pun. None of that makes the Via Cappello palace the home of a fictional girl, and the scholarship is united that it does not; but it is a chain with links, each link attaching the fiction to a document, a name, a stone. The Romeo identification has no equivalent links. It has a district. The standard Italian account of the building states the basis of the identification with a candour the tourist literature rarely matches: the palace is counted among Verona's Shakespearean sites because the Veronese family of the Montecchi really did live in that contrada, that quarter of the old town, while the building itself was in reality the residence of Cagnolo Nogarola. A contrada is not an address. The claim, stated at its strongest honest pitch, is that Romeo's supposed family lived somewhere in the neighbourhood of a house that belonged to somebody else, and that the house, being handsome, fortified and conveniently placed near the Scaliger tombs, was elected to stand for the family's unlocated home. Guide literature commonly improves on this by promoting the district into a deed of ownership, presenting the palace straightforwardly as the Montecchi family home until the early fourteenth century; the improvement is precisely the point at which marketing parts company with the record, and the record, as this article's map grades it, should be graded on the documents and not on the improvement.

## The Montecchi Claim: Dante's Line, Da Porto's Names, and a District Stretched into a Deed

The remoteness of the claim deserves one more level of scrutiny, because the Montecchi themselves stand at two removes from the story before any house enters the question. The earliest literary notice of the family in connection with factional sorrow is a single line of Dante. In the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, the poet turns on Italy and calls on the emperor to come and see the evidence of a land abandoned to feud: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti," come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, paired in one verse with the Monaldi and the Filippeschi as exhibits of a country tearing itself apart. Dante's Montecchi are Veronese; his Cappelletti are generally placed at Cremona; the verse mourns the Montecchi as already brought low and looks at the Cappelletti with suspicion of worse to come. No lovers appear, no feud between the two named families is described, no Verona romance is hinted at. The line is a political lament, and it belongs to the years around 1310, when the Montecchi's power in Verona was already spent. The play's premise requires the opposite condition: two households at the height of their strength, "both alike in dignity," filling the streets with armed retainers. Whatever Dante is witnessing, it is not the world of the tragedy, and the one contemporary witness to the families describes a decline where the fiction requires a zenith.

It was Luigi da Porto who converted Dante's pair of names into the cast of a love story. Writing at his villa at Montorso Vicentino, where the twin castles of Montecchio Maggiore stood in his view, da Porto composed his novella of two noble lovers before June 1524; it was published in Venice after his death, around 1530 and 1531, as the Historia novellamente ritrovata di due nobili amanti, and reissued in 1535 under the title La Giulietta. Da Porto set his tale explicitly in the Verona of Bartolomeo della Scala, presented it as a story he had heard from an archer of his company named Peregrino, and gave the lovers the names and families that the tradition has carried since: Romeo Montecchi and Giulietta Cappelletti. Verona's own historian Girolamo della Corte then admitted the tale into his history of the city as fact, the decisive laundering by which a novella became a chronicle, and Matteo Bandello's retelling of 1554 carried the newly historical lovers across Europe toward the French and English versions that reached Shakespeare. Every subsequent stage of the tradition, the play included, inherits da Porto's geography as an article of faith. The point for the map is simple: the story's Verona address system was invented by a Vicentine soldier writing two centuries after the supposed events, drawing his family names from a line of Dante and his setting from the most famous signorial court of the region's past. When Verona's tourist industry went looking for the lovers' houses in the twentieth century, it was searching a real city for the furniture of that invention, and it found exactly what the invention could support: a plausible palace with a punning name for her, and a handsome fortress with the right postcode for him.

The guidebook version of the house's past, repeated on tour after tour at the gate, adds one more layer of invention, and it should be separated from the record with care because it sounds so much like history. In that version, the Montecchi held the palace until the 1320s, joined a conspiracy against Cangrande della Scala, were banished from Verona, and saw their confiscated palace handed to the loyal Nogarola, a narrative that even supplies the banished family's flight to Udine and a named conspirator. The elements are not all fantasy in the abstract: the Montecchi were indeed broken as a Veronese power, their descendants were indeed found at Udine, where da Porto's own novella locates the family's survivors under the name Monticoli, and confiscations were the ordinary wages of failed plots in Scaliger Verona. What the record does not supply is the deed. No document produced in the scholarship connects this palace to that confiscation, and the catalogued history of the building begins in 1339 with Scaliger and Nogarola hands already on it. The conspiracy story performs the same service for Romeo's house that the balcony performs for Juliet's: it converts an absence of evidence into a piece of theatre, a fall-of-the-house drama that explains the fortress, the exile theme and the Nogarola occupancy in one satisfying arc. The honest version is less satisfying and more interesting. A great family lived in the district, declined, and vanished from the city; two centuries later a novella made its name immortal; five centuries later a city cast about for the family's house, found no document, and chose the finest fortified palace in the quarter where the family had once lived. That is not a deed of ownership. It is an act of casting.

Two further details of the chain deserve their place because each tightens the same conclusion. The first is that da Porto's novella itself exports the Montecchi from Verona. In the tale's framing, the family's descendants are no longer Veronese at all: they are found residing at Udine, under the diminished name of Monticoli, a detail the novella offers as the sad residue of the great house. The inventor of Romeo's surname therefore testifies, inside his own fiction, that the Montecchi ended elsewhere; the tradition that later fixed their palace in Verona was contradicting its own founding text, which had already emptied the city of them. The second detail concerns Dante's line, so often quoted at the gate as though it were a Veronese feud gazetteer. The verse belongs to a catalogue of Italy's civic diseases: Montecchi and Cappelletti stand in a list with the Monaldi and Filippeschi of Umbria, families summoned as symptoms of a peninsula at war with itself, and the Cappelletti of the line belong to Cremona's history rather than Verona's streets. Dante is not reporting a feud between two Veronese households; he is arraigning a civilization, with the Montecchi produced as Exhibit A of a great family ruined by the factional game. A pair of names drawn from that arraignment, by way of a Vicentine novella that relocated them for a love story, is the entire documentary inheritance of Romeo's house. The palace on Via Arche Scaligere was conscripted to embody an inheritance of two removes and a misreading, and it bears the conscription with dignity, because a building cannot object. The objection has to be supplied by the record, and the record, as the Veronese catalogues assembled it, supplies the objection in full.

## The Pilgrims Before the Plaques: A Century of Readers at the Wrong Addresses

The municipal programme of the 1930s did not invent the pilgrimage; it inherited one, and the terms of the inheritance already favoured the heroine. Literary tourism to Verona runs back to the first decades of the nineteenth century, when the Romantic generation of readers began converting the books they loved into journeys. Watson's research on the period recovers the pattern at the Cappello palace: Samuel Rogers sought the house out in 1822 and fixed it in his poem Italy, describing the old palace of the Cappelletti with its uncouth balcony and irregular windows in a lane near the market place, and asking what Englishman could behold it with indifference. A generation later the Baroness Blaze de Bury and her party were performing the characteristic rite of the early literary tourist at the same address, insisting on the story's truth as an act of imaginative occupation, fancying the household voices within calling for dates and quinces in the pantry and the masquers filing past the gateway to Capulet's feast. The tomb in the monastery ground at San Francesco al Corso drew the complementary devotion of the period, the graveyard pilgrimage, fed by the same sentimental current that made a relic of an empty sarcophagus and set visitors carrying away impressions, sketches and small thefts of stone. What matters for the map is the distribution of this early devotion among the sites. The nineteenth century, which invented the practice and fixed its stations, developed a cult of the heroine's house and of her tomb, and developed no cult of the hero's house at all. The gate on Via Arche Scaligere enters the pilgrimage late, as an antiquarian identification trailed by guidebooks, not as a station sanctified by a century of tears.

The reasons lie in the emotional economy the early tourists brought with them. The Romantic pilgrimage sought objects that could anchor a feeling, and the feelings the story offered for anchoring were asymmetrically housed. Grief had the tomb, an actual stone coffin, whether or not it had ever held the right body. Longing had the house and its window, the architecture of the night scene, where the visitor could stand in the courtyard as Romeo stood in the orchard and look up. What object could anchor Romeo's portion of the story? His portion is yearning in motion, the dawn walk in the grove, the climb, the ride into exile, states of feeling that occur outdoors and in transit and that no relic had been invented to hold. The period that furnished Byron's generation with a Juliet to mourn furnished Romeo with nothing portable, and the consequence hardened into habit: guide succeeded guide, the heroine's stations accumulated their liturgy of anecdotes, and the hero's house, when it was finally named, had to compete for the pilgrim's hour against sites already saturated with a century of performed emotion. Avena's failure in the 1920s was thus overdetermined. He was not merely refused a building; he was proposing to spend a poor city's money manufacturing, from nothing, a devotion that a hundred years of readers had declined to manufacture for themselves.

There is a further lesson in the comparison, and it belongs to the psychology of the invented site. The Cappello palace attracted pilgrims before it had a balcony, before it had a museum, before it had municipal blessing, in the years when it was merely an old inn-yard palace whose name sounded right; the devotion generated the fabric, not the reverse, the balcony and the rose window arriving in the 1930s as answers to a demand the readers had been voicing since Rogers. Romeo's house never generated the first term of that sequence. No Rogers versified it; no Blaze de Bury heard voices in its courtyard; no generation of travellers demanded its interior and was refused. When the demand was finally hypothesized, it was hypothesized by an administrator with a programme, and the hypothesized demand proved, at the negotiation table, to have no constituency willing to pay for it, neither the owners in kind nor the city in cash. Literary tourism is often described as a species of credulity, but its Verona history shows a colder discipline at work: readers endow the sites their reading actually needs, and the reading needs the window and the tomb. The hero's address was surplus to the reading, and surplus addresses do not become shrines, however fine their battlements.

## The Museum That Never Opened: Antonio Avena and the Romeo Project That Failed

The neglect of Romeo's house is often described as though the city simply forgot the hero while lavishing attention on the heroine. The record shows something more pointed: Verona tried to build Romeo a shrine, with the same official, the same programme and the same decade that produced Juliet's, and was stopped by the two most ordinary obstacles in municipal history, an owner who would not sell and a budget that would not stretch. The official was Antonio Avena, historian and director of Verona's civic museums, the man who more than any other individual manufactured Shakespearean Verona. Avena's campaign took the city's scattered literary rumours, the Cappello palace, the empty sarcophagus at San Francesco al Corso, and gave them architecture, signage and opening dates. Juliet's house was fitted out and opened in 1937; her tomb, rearranged in a purpose-built crypt in the former monastery, followed in 1940. The Romeo project ran in parallel and shared the ambition. Through the 1920s Avena repeatedly proposed to the city administration that the Nogarola palace be bought and prepared as the house of Romeo for the rising tourist traffic, and he worked the proposal up into an actual design. Lugoboni's account of the palace records the outcome in a single sentence of accumulated frustration: the design stayed in the drawer, because the owners would not part with the building and because the city lacked the money. Among all of Avena's projects for the reinvention of Verona, the house of Romeo is counted as one of the few he never saw realized.

The early 1930s added a spur that makes the failure more instructive still. George Cukor travelled to Verona to gather material for the Hollywood Romeo and Juliet then in preparation at MGM, the film released in 1936 with Leslie Howard and Norma Shearer, and the director was astonished, on the standard Veronese account preserved by Lugoboni, to find none of the locations the story had led him to expect: no balcony, no staged tomb, no hero's house dressed for pilgrimage. The astonishment of a film director carrying a studio's expectations is a useful measure of the gap between the myth's international circulation and the city's physical provision at that date, and it stiffened Avena's determination to close the gap. The gap was closed, on Juliet's side, with a thoroughness that even recruited the cinema as a template: the tomb's 1938 rearrangement made the crypt resemble the tomb as the new screen realism had taught audiences to picture it, and the house acquired its balcony, its rose window, its Gothic doorway, the whole apparatus of a stage set made permanent. On Romeo's side nothing could be recruited, because the raw material was behind a gate that the city did not own. Avena could fabricate a balcony for a house in municipal hands; he could not fabricate an interior for a house whose owners declined to sell it. The asymmetry of the modern map was therefore locked in during a single decade, by a single administrator, working a single programme, and diverging at exactly one point: title deeds. Everything that followed, the millions at one gate and the graffiti at the other, is the long echo of that divergence.

### What did George Cukor find when he came to Verona looking for the play's locations?

He found their absence. In Verona in the early 1930s to prepare his Hollywood Romeo and Juliet, Cukor was astonished, on the account preserved in the Veronese scholarship, to find none of the sites the story had taught him to expect: no dressed balcony, no staged tomb, no house of Romeo.

A weaker version of the Romeo house's twentieth century circulates in guide literature and on the web, and accuracy requires naming it as weaker. In that version the palace passed in the 1930s to a Veronese literary society, which intended a Shakespeare museum on the premises until the Second World War intervened. The story has the shape of the Avena episode but not its documentation: the Veronese scholarship that catalogues the building, Dalle Piane's study for the Scaliger repertoire and Lugoboni's history of the city's residences, records proposals, a design and a refusal, not a transfer and a museum aborted by war. The two accounts cannot both be true in their particulars, since a house ceded to a literary society is not a house whose owners refuse to sell it, and the version carried here is the one with page references behind it. The distinction matters beyond pedantry. The museum-that-war-stopped story flatters the city, recasting a failed purchase as a casualty of history; the documented version assigns the failure where it belongs, to a negotiation that never closed and a treasury that never found the sum. Verona did not lose Romeo's house to the twentieth century. Verona declined, or failed, to buy it, and then built the myth everywhere else instead.

The cinema, which completed the shrine's furnishing on the heroine's side, had nothing to offer the hero's, and the contrast closes the Avena chapter. Film arrived in the story as a template supplier: the tomb's crypt was rearranged to match the screen's idea of a tomb, and the heroine's museum in time acquired the very bed from the celebrated 1968 film, exhibited as a relic of the fiction's most famous visualization, an object whose authenticity is real and entirely cinematic. For Romeo's house the cinema supplied no template because the cinema, following the play, had never imagined his interior either: the screen's Romeos are met in streets, gardens, ballrooms and tombs, other people's rooms, and the famous film Veronas were themselves assembled from studio sets and borrowed towns, the 1968 production shooting its Verona largely in Tuscania, Gubbio and Pienza. The medium that taught the twentieth century what Juliet's chamber looked like left Romeo's chamber as blank as the dramatist had. A museum-maker of the 1930s working on the heroine could thus copy, from stage and screen, a room the public already half recognized; a museum-maker working on the hero would have had to invent in a vacuum, against the grain of every visualization his audience carried. Avena, a practical man, built where the templates were. The templates were hers. They still are: the itinerary's visitors arrive at Via Cappello with a century of images to verify against the stones, and arrive at Via Arche Scaligere with nothing to verify, which is why the stop feels empty even to visitors who know the whole story by heart. Emptiness, at this gate, is not a failure of interpretation. It is the accurate registration of an absence that runs from the play's scene list through the cinema's storyboards to the city's title deeds, unbroken.

## Romeo Has No Address: The Play's Own Geography of Thresholds

The deepest cause of the asymmetry lies neither in deeds nor in budgets but in the text, and it is visible in a simple census of the play's spaces. Quotations here follow the Folger Shakespeare text. Juliet's Verona is a geography of interiors. The Capulet house contains the play's great set pieces in sequence: the feast in the hall where the lovers meet (1.5), the chamber above the orchard where the night's dialogue and the morning's parting unfold (2.2, 3.5), the chamber where the potion is drunk in solitude (4.3), the chamber where the body is found (4.5). Above these rooms hangs the window, the one piece of domestic architecture the whole world can draw, from which Juliet speaks and beneath which Romeo stands in the orchard. The tomb completes her itinerary: the Capulet monument in the churchyard, where her body is laid and found and where the action ends. House, window, chamber, tomb: the heroine's story can be walked as a series of rooms, and a tourist industry is nothing but a series of rooms with tickets. Romeo's Verona, by contrast, contains no room of his own at all. No scene of the play is set inside the Montague house. His father and mother appear in the street in the first scene, anxious about a son they cannot reach, and in the churchyard in the last, beside his body. The house that the tourist map calls his is a location the drama never uses, an address the text never visits. The companion study of the play's setting in Verona traces the same distribution from the other side, examining [where and when Romeo and Juliet is set in Verona](/2022/03/04/romeo-and-juliet-setting-verona-explained/) and the slender relation between the play's city and the real one; the census of rooms belongs to the map, and the map is lopsided because the play is.

Where the play does place Romeo, it places him in transit and in the open air. His first appearance is reported before it is seen, and the report is a piece of anti-domestic geography: "Underneath the grove of sycamore / That westward rooteth from the city side, / So early walking did I see your son" (1.1). The sycamore grove stands outside the walls, to the west; Romeo at dawn is beyond the city's boundary, walking in a wood, augmenting the morning dew with tears, and when his father approaches he steals "into the covert of the wood" and measures the audience of his grief by his own sighs. The play's first coordinates for its hero are a direction, a tree and the outside of a wall. From there his itinerary runs through thresholds: the street of the brawls (1.1, 3.1), the exterior of Capulet's orchard, which he enters by climbing, "He ran this way, and leapt this orchard wall" (2.1), the orchard itself, where he stands beneath a window that is not his, hearing Juliet reason about his name, "The orchard walls are high and hard to climb" (2.2), and his answer, "With love's light wings did I o'erperch these walls; / For stony limits cannot hold love out" (2.2). The friar's cell receives him repeatedly (2.3, 2.6, 3.3), but the cell is Laurence's room, a workplace of herbs and counsel belonging to the one adult who serves both houses. Then exile converts his placelessness into the plot's engine: a street in Mantua (5.1), an apothecary's shop that is another man's miserable premises, a churchyard entered by force at night with a crowbar and a letter of explanation. Romeo's spaces are borrowed, climbed into, broken into or passed through. He is the tragedy's great trespasser, and trespass leaves no address behind it. A city cannot put a ticket desk in a grove, a wall's outer face, or a road.

The census can be carried through the whole scene list, and the pattern survives the audit. The play's locations divide into four classes. First, the public places: the opening brawl in a street or square, the Prince's audiences, the fatal encounter of Mercutio and Tybalt, scenes the early texts head with the rubric of a public place in Verona, the city's commons where both households appear as factions and where Romeo is repeatedly found as a citizen among citizens. Second, the Capulet interior system: the hall, the orchard, the gallery, the chambers, the site of the feast, the marriage's aftermath, the potion, the discovery, a connected domestic machine in which Juliet lives every decisive hour. Third, the friar's cell, the play's one workplace, visited by Romeo for counsel and marriage and by Juliet for the potion, a threshold space belonging to neither household and to the Church, where the plot's machinery is assembled by hand. Fourth, the elsewhere: Mantua's street and apothecary's shop, the churchyard and the monument. Romeo's personal distribution across these classes is the census's payload. He is present throughout the first class, a visitor in the second, a client in the third, and the sole protagonist of the fourth. The Montague house, which ought by symmetry to constitute a fifth class answering the Capulet system, appears in no heading and in no stage direction: it is the play's great unbuilt set. Directors have noticed the fact in practice for centuries without remarking on it, because a production, like a city, furnishes the rooms the action enters and leaves the unentered house as a painted flat or nothing. Verona's map is in this respect only a very durable production design, executed in real estate: it built what the play stages and omitted what the play omits, and then, honesty failing at the last step, affixed the omitted building's name to a palace the play's author could not have pointed to on any street.

The name problem compounds the space problem, and it deserves its own sentence in the census. Juliet's spaces come with her name attached by the action: the window is hers because she appears at it, the chamber hers because she suffers in it, the tomb hers because she is laid in it, and each can be labelled with the genitive the tourist trade loves, Juliet's. Romeo's spaces resist the genitive by their nature. The orchard is Capulet's; the cell is Laurence's; the street is the Prince's; the apothecary's shop is the apothecary's; the grove belongs to no one, which is why a lovesick boy can weep in it at dawn. Even the churchyard monument is the Capulet monument, into which Romeo breaks as an intruder and in which he is finally a guest corpse in his enemies' vault. A heritage industry runs on genitives, on the capacity to say whose place a place is, and the play denies its hero a single ownable interior from the first scene to the last. The city that wished to honour him with a house was therefore not correcting the play's geography but reversing it, supplying offstage the one thing the dramatist had withheld onstage, and the attempt failed, this article argues, because reversal of a text's deep distribution is a heavier work than municipal budgets or private refusals: the constituency for it does not exist, since no reader's memory contains a Romeo interior to be homesick for.

### Where does the play first place Romeo at dawn?

Under a sycamore grove outside the walls. Benvolio reports to the boy's father that he saw Romeo at early morning beneath the grove "that westward rooteth from the city side" (1.1), adding tears to the dew; approached, Romeo stole into the wood's covert. No itinerary has ever marked the position.

His one piece of domestic equipment tells the same story in miniature. For the wedding night Romeo does not use a stair, a door or any architecture of his own house; he commissions tackle. He charges the Nurse to procure cords, "a ladder, by the which your love / Must climb a bird's nest soon when it is dark" (2.4), and the Nurse duly delivers the rope ladder, the "tackled stair" by which the bridegroom ascends to the chamber and descends at dawn (3.2, 3.5). The contrast with the heroine's equipment could not be more diagrammatic. Juliet's side of the night is architectural and permanent in the imagination: window, balcony in the tradition, chamber, bed. Romeo's side is nautical and disposable: a rope, purchased for one ascent, carried by a servant, drawn up after use. The tourist industry, which merchandises durable versions of the story's furniture, has predictably canonized the architecture and forgotten the tackle; no gift shop sells the cord ladder, no courtyard ritual commemorates the climb down. Yet the ladder is the hero's truest furnishing, the object that expresses his relation to every interior in the play: admitted temporarily, by arrangement, from outside, and gone before the watch changes. A house museum for such a hero would have to be furnished around an object whose essence is that it leaves no trace, and the impossibility is not a failure of the museum-makers. It is the play's design, faithfully resisting conversion into premises.

The point sharpens when the play's own closing promise is set against the modern map. In the churchyard, over the bodies, the fathers compete in grief with building projects. Montague pledges a monument: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That whiles Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3). Capulet answers in kind: "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie; / Poor sacrifices of our enmity!" (5.3). The text, in other words, commissions a pair: two golden figures, hers funded by his father, his by hers, standing together as the feud's memorial. Verona eventually cast the commission in bronze and cast half of it. The statue of Juliet by Nereo Costantini, installed in her courtyard in 1972 and touched by generations of visitors until the worn original was withdrawn indoors and a copy took its place, fulfils Montague's pledge in the wrong metal and without its pair. No statue of Romeo stands beside it; no Veronese founder has ever been commissioned to complete the set. The companion article devoted to the statue examines that bronze and its strange career on its own terms; here the statue matters only as the census's final entry, the place where the play's explicit symmetry, a statue for a statue, meets the map's settled habit of building for one lover and pointing at a wall for the other.

## Two Plaques and a Locked Gate: The Smallest Shrine in Verona

What the city provides at Romeo's address can be inventoried without a paragraph break in prose if the inventory is kept to objects: a wall, a gate, two plaques. Beside the entrance portal, as the architectural literature records with a dryness that deserves quoting in paraphrase, two tablets indicate the supposed house of Romeo, and the tablets have to be freed from graffiti at regular intervals. That last detail is the most eloquent sentence in the house's whole documentation. At Juliet's courtyard, writing on the fabric became so institutional that rules were devised for the love notes left in the entrance passage and the walls are periodically cleared under management; the heroine's shrine metabolizes its visitors' inscriptions into ritual. At Romeo's gate the same human impulse arrives as vandalism. The plaques are scrubbed, not celebrated, because no institution has framed the writing as devotion. A shrine is, among other things, a machine for deciding which marks on a wall are offerings and which are damage, and at Via Arche Scaligere the machine was never installed.

The text the plaques carry completes the irony with a precision no satirist would dare invent. The quotation mounted at the house is drawn from Romeo's first scene, from the sycamore-grove melancholy of the play's opening movement, and it is the speech in which Romeo denies being present at all. Benvolio asks after him; the exchange runs toward Romeo's confession of a self mislaid in unrequited love for Rosaline: "Tut, I have lost myself; I am not here; / This is not Romeo, he's some other where" (1.1). Consider what the city has therefore mounted, in permanent material, at the one address it assigns its hero: his own declaration that he is not there, that the person being sought is elsewhere. Every other Romeo and Juliet site in Verona asserts a presence the record cannot support; the tomb asserts a burial that never happened, the balcony a conversation at a window assembled in 1936. The plaques at Romeo's house assert, accurately if accidentally, an absence. The quotation was presumably chosen as a wistful lover's paradox, suitable for a romantic wall. Read against the building's history it functions as the site's own catalogue entry, the only label in Shakespearean Verona whose text accurately describes its object. He is not here. He is some other where. The wall says so, and for once the wall is right.

### What does the plaque on Romeo's house quote?

It quotes Romeo's disclaimer from the first act, the sycamore-grove confession "I have lost myself; I am not here; / This is not Romeo, he's some other where" (1.1). Chosen, apparently, as an emblem of the lover's distraction, the lines fixed to an unoccupied gate read as the site's accurate self-description: a permanent notice of absence.

The official language at the gate hedges in a way the shrine's language never does, and the hedge is worth reading as evidence. Where the courtyard on Via Cappello is presented by its custodians in the grammar of fact, furnished with dates, restorations and admission arrangements, the house on Via Arche Scaligere is presented, in the reference literature and the city's own tourist materials alike, under a permanent qualifier: the so-called house of Romeo, the supposed house, the house said to be his. The German architectural entry's formulation, that two tablets beside the gate indicate the vermeintliche house, the presumed or putative house, captures the register exactly. A qualifier of that kind is the residue of a negotiation between departments: the custodians of the record will not sign the claim, the custodians of the itinerary will not surrender the stop, and the compromise is a plaque that asserts with one word and doubts with another. No other major site in the lovers' Verona carries its doubt on its face in this way. The balcony's custodians do not label it the so-called balcony; the sarcophagus is not exhibited as the supposed tomb, though its claim is weaker in evidence than the palace's, an empty coffin of uncertain date against a documented palace in a documented family quarter. The doubt is distributed, like everything else on the map, by institutional confidence rather than by evidence: the sites the city owns are asserted, the site it failed to buy is supposed. Scholarship could hardly ask for a cleaner demonstration that the map's certainties are conveyancing all the way down.

The commercial life of the address tells the same story in a lower register. A restaurant occupies the ground floor of the complex at Via Arche Scaligere 2, the Osteria al Duca, which presents itself as heir to one of the city's old tavern traditions and serves the traditional Veronese kitchen, bigoli, pastissada and the rest, to a clientele of locals and visitors. Tour companies walking the Shakespeare itinerary pause at the gate for the photograph and the story, then move on; the house figures in their programmes explicitly as an exterior, an optional stop, a paragraph spoken on a pavement. Nothing about this is scandalous, and this article's verdict does not require scandal. The restaurant is honest commerce at an honest address; the guides are selling a walk, not a relic. But the shape of the commerce completes the census. At the heroine's address the myth sustains a museum, a ticketing system, a courtyard ritual, a statue, a secretarial bureau for the world's love letters. At the hero's address it sustains lunch. The myth's earning power, measured at street level, is the female interior's earning power: rooms, furnishings, a bed exhibited from a film, a balcony to stand on for a photograph. A wall with a gate in it can sell a meal to people who stop to look at it. It cannot sell admission to itself, and so the hero's house remains what the documents always said it was, somebody else's palace with two plaques on it.

Read as a table, the map exposes its own geometry. Column by column, the sites divide into the furnished and the pointed-at. The furnished sites are all hers or nobody's: the house, the tomb, the statue. The pointed-at sites are his or the city's: his palace gate, the rulers' tombs, the walls, the unmarked grove, the unclaimed cell, the absent second city. Even the table's neutral rows conspire in the pattern, because the itinerary's genuinely historical furniture, the Scaliger monuments and the Roman gate, belongs to the political Verona of Dante and the signoria, the Verona of record, while the lovers' Verona of ticket and ritual belongs to one lover. The companion anatomy of the largest of these industries, the [tomb of Juliet in Verona](/2029/07/13/tomb-of-juliet-verona/), shows the furnished end of the map at full stretch: an empty coffin in a built crypt, venerated for two centuries on the strength of a story. Set beside that empty sarcophagus, the locked gate needs no rhetoric. One site is a fabrication that admits thousands; the other is a fortress that admits none; the fabrication is hers, the fortress his, and between them the whole tourist geography of the play declares which lover it knows how to sell.

The map's omissions deserve a reading of their own, since a map is known by what it leaves blank. No itinerary marks the Prince's palace, though Escalus is the play's Verona made personal, the authority whose edicts open the action and whose judgment closes it; the city's real seat of government stands in Piazza dei Signori, visited for Dante and the commune, its Shakespearean tenancy unplaqued. No itinerary marks a site for the brawls, though the play begins and turns on street fighting in the public place, and though the city's market square, Piazza delle Erbe, is exactly the kind of ground the scenes require; the Erbe is sold as Rome's forum and the merchants' square, its fictional bloodshed unmentioned. No itinerary marks a house for Mercutio, the play's most beloved casualty, or lodgings for the Nurse's errands, or the herb garden behind the friar's cell, though Laurence's workplace is the plot's engine room. The pattern in the omissions is consistent with the pattern in the commissions: the map marks what can be furnished and feminized into devotion, and leaves unmarked the masculine, civic and violent Verona of the play, the Verona of edicts, factions and funerals in the street. Romeo's house is usually described as the map's great omission. On a full accounting it is the map's great exception: the one masculine site that received a marker at all, admitted to the itinerary as a wall and two plaques, a token male address in a geography that otherwise belongs to the heroine, the rulers and the Church.

## Walking the Map: How the Itinerary Spends Its Hours at Each Address

The map's asymmetry is finally a fact about time, and it can be measured with a watch. Consider the distribution of the visitor's hour across the two houses, as the commercial itinerary arranges it. At Via Cappello the visit is an occupation: entry through the passage, the courtyard, the statue and its ritual touch, the ascent into the furnished rooms, the balcony photograph, the exhibition cases, the shop, a sequence engineered to hold the visitor in place for the better part of an hour amid ticketing, queuing and crowd management on a scale that has repeatedly forced the municipality to redesign access, most elaborately when the courtyard's spontaneous crowds, which press accounts describe as reaching several thousand on peak days, were converted into a booked and channelled flow. At Via Arche Scaligere the visit is an interruption: the group halts, the guide speaks the paragraph the stop always receives, the wall, the family that was not there, the project that failed, cameras rise for a single frame, and the walk resumes, typically inside five minutes. Between the two houses the itinerary spends its substance at one end and its punctuation at the other. Operators' own programmes state the hierarchy without embarrassment: the better class of Verona walking tour lists Romeo's house precisely as an exterior, and frequently as an optional exterior, a stop to be included if the route and the clock allow, while the heroine's courtyard anchors itineraries as their reason for existing. A site that a tour can drop without breaking its product is a site the market has already graded, whatever the plaques claim.

The disappointment literature of the stop deserves a place in the record, because it is the sound a failed attraction makes. Visitor reviews of the Casa di Romeo, in the places where travellers report to one another, return to a narrow vocabulary: a wall, a gate, some plaques, nothing to see, not worth the detour, the advice to glance and move on. Read ungenerously, the reviews indict the tourists, who expected a shrine and found a residence. Read as evidence, they indict the category: a named house in a tourist city carries a contractual promise of entry, of interiority, of the furnished past, and the Casa di Romeo is the one address on the itinerary that breaks the contract simply by being what it is. The same travellers, transported to the courtyard on Via Cappello, accept furnishing assembled in living memory and a balcony younger than the cinema without demur, because the contract there is honoured in form: rooms exist, a body may enter them, the photograph can be taken from inside the fiction. The reviews at the gate and the queues at the courtyard are one phenomenon photographed from two sides, the visitor's demand for an inside, and they explain why no improvement in signage, interpretation panels or guided storytelling has ever lifted the stop's standing. The deficiency is not informational. It is architectural, and it is incurable at any price the site could command, because the cure is the one thing the owners have withheld since the 1920s: the door.

Verona's wider day puts the R&J circuit in proportion and delivers the verdict's last piece of context. The city is a UNESCO-listed palimpsest whose headline monuments, the Roman Arena in Piazza Bra, the forum-square of the Erbe, the Castelvecchio and its bridge, the Roman theatre across the Adige, absorb the bulk of most visits, and the lovers' itinerary is a themed walk threaded through a much larger inheritance. Within that thread, the distribution repeats the pattern at smaller scale: the heroine's complex is a destination capable of anchoring a half-day, while the hero's gate is a connective, something passed between the tombs and the square, enriching a walk whose destinations lie elsewhere. Even the commerce obeys the thread's geometry. Around the courtyard the myth supports dedicated retail, weddings, a letters bureau answering the world's correspondence from an office the shrine's fame sustains; around the gate it supports one restaurant's dining room. The itinerary, walked honestly from end to end, is therefore the most persuasive critic the map has: it demonstrates with feet and wallets what the documents demonstrate with deeds and dates, that Verona's Romeo and Juliet geography has one protagonist with premises and one with a plaque, and that the difference was settled by the interaction of a text that furnished her, a city that bought her house, and a pair of owners who kept his.

## Beyond the Walls: Mantua, the Exile City the Itinerary Forgets

Romeo's banishment speech is the play's one great aria of urban geography, and it is addressed to the boundary the itinerary never crosses: "There is no world without Verona walls, / But purgatory, torture, hell itself. / Hence banish'd is banish'd from the world, / And world's exile is death" (3.3). The friar answers with logistics, a horse, a servant, a timetable: "Either be gone before the watch be set, / Or by the break of day disguised from hence" (3.3). Exile in the play is not a voyage; it is a short ride to the next jurisdiction, some forty kilometres south-west to Mantua, near enough for a servant to ride back with news within the day, far enough for Verona's law to stop at the border. The tragedy's mechanism depends on that exact distance. Balthasar can reach Mantua with the false report of Juliet's death faster than Friar John's quarantined letter can travel the same road the other way (5.1, 5.2), and the poison can be bought, carried and used inside a single night's ride. Verona and Mantua in the play form one system, a city and its shadow, joined by a road along which every disaster is couriered. The tourist map keeps half the system. Verona's itinerary internalizes the banishment speech completely, building its whole circuit inside the walls Romeo could not bear to leave, and drops the destination he could not bear to reach.

The walls themselves warrant their sentence in the record, because they are the one element of Romeo's geography that a visitor can still walk entire. Verona's medieval circuit, raised to its mature form under Cangrande I in the 1320s and refashioned under later rulers, survives in long stretches, particularly on the hills east of the Adige and along the southern quarters, and the itinerary's streets pass its gates without pausing for the play. The circuit is not a ruin delivered to tourism; it is working urban fabric, carrying roads, houses and gardens on its back, and its very intactness explains its absence from the lovers' map: a wall that never fell down never became a monument, and a boundary still performing its office of dividing inside from outside has no need of a plaque. Yet the wall is the play's load-bearing structure in the most literal sense. Every decisive movement in the tragedy is a crossing of it or of its domestic miniature, the orchard wall: Romeo into the garden, Romeo out of the city before the watch is set, the friar's letter stopped on the road, Balthasar riding in with death in his mouth, Romeo returning by night with crowbar and poison to force the last door of all, the monument's. The itinerary that ignores the circuit while queueing for the balcony has inverted the play's own engineering, in which the balcony is a moment and the boundary is the machine. A walker who follows the walls southward, out of the gate the Mantua road uses, retraces the tragedy's true axis more faithfully than any courtyard visit, and does so unaccompanied, because the map has nothing to sell along the way.

Mantua's side of the story is documented absence. The city possesses a heritage of the first rank and markets it accurately: the Gonzaga palaces, the Palazzo Ducale with Mantegna's Camera degli Sposi, Palazzo Te, the UNESCO listing of 2008 earned with Sabbioneta for Renaissance urban achievement, the fame of Virgil, born in its territory. In that portfolio the town's Shakespearean episode figures, where it figures at all, as a line of garnish: an interesting fact, an opera connection, a stop on literary itineraries that begin and end elsewhere. No apothecary's shop is dressed for pilgrims on any Mantuan street; no Romeo lodging is plaqued; the exile's address is as unlocated there as the lover's is here, but without even the compensation of a plaque and a gate, because Mantua never appointed an Avena and never needed one. The asymmetry thus completes itself at the regional scale. The play gives its hero a city of desire and a city of exile, and the heritage industry developed one address in the first city, imperfectly, and no address in the second at all. Romeo's map, in the end, is a single wall in Verona and a blank in Lombardy, while Juliet's is a house, a tomb and a statue inside one walkable square mile. The itinerary does not merely favour her. It reproduces, with documentary fidelity it nowhere intends, the exact distribution of space the play itself decreed: the heroine installed in rooms, the hero at large in the roads between them.

The one Mantuan interior the play does describe repays a look, because it completes the hero's furnishing census with a kind of grim symmetry. The apothecary's shop is rendered in inventory: Romeo recalls "a beggarly account of empty boxes," green earthen pots, bladders and musty seeds, remnants of packthread and old cakes of roses, and above the stock the trophies of a starving trade, "in his needy shop a tortoise hung, / An alligator stuffed, and other skins / Of ill-shaped fishes" (5.1). It is the most densely furnished interior in the play's second half, and it belongs to a nameless tradesman in the wrong city, furnished by poverty for the purpose of a single illegal sale. Set the inventory beside the Gonzaga interiors Mantua actually possesses, the frescoed bridal chamber, the studiolo, the halls of Palazzo Te, and the play's Mantua stands revealed as a deliberate anti-palace: the exile city imagined from the road, all back street and needy shop, by a dramatist whose Verona had at least been given halls and orchards. Heritage cannot use such a room either; a reconstruction of the apothecary's shop would be a monument to a poison sale, the one transaction in the story no city wishes to commemorate at street level. Mantua's silence about its scene is therefore as overdetermined as Verona's silence about the grove. The play gave the second city exactly one interior, and made it unexhibitable.

Mantua's own literary afterlife sharpens the point, because the city has practice at being misused by dramatists and has declined to memorialize any of it. Verdi's Rigoletto, driven by Austrian censorship to relocate its licentious court from France to a Mantua whose Gonzaga line had been extinct for a century and a half, gave the city a second immortality as the seat of a fictional duke's crimes; Mantua absorbed the compliment without building a Rigoletto industry, exactly as it absorbed the exile of Romeo without building an apothecary shrine. The city's marketed soul remains its real one, the Gonzaga centuries, Mantegna's bridal chamber, Giulio Romano's Palazzo Te, the lakes engineered in the twelfth century that still ring it like a moat in travel photographs. A visitor crossing from Verona learns the contrast in a single day trip: the city of the lovers has organized itself around a story that never happened there, while the city of the exile has organized itself around the dynasty that demonstrably ruled it. Romeo is the hinge of both arrangements and the proprietor of neither. The road between the cities, the old corridor south-west across the plain, carries the day-trippers in both directions, and no marker on it records that the plot of the world's most famous love story was decided along its length, in the forty kilometres between a letter that stopped and a rumour that did not.

### How far is Mantua from Verona, and why did that distance decide the plot?

Mantua lies some forty kilometres south-west of Verona, across a jurisdictional border, beyond the reach of Verona's law. The plot turns on that gap: news, letters and poison travel the road between the cities, and the tragedy is the record of which messages arrive. Friar John's letter stops in plague quarantine; Balthasar's false report does not.

## Staged Authenticity or Knowing Pilgrimage? MacCannell and Watson at the Gate

Two theories of the tourist compete for this gate, and the gate adjudicates between them. The first belongs to Dean MacCannell, whose The Tourist: A New Theory of the Leisure Class (1976) remains the founding sociology of the subject. MacCannell argued that a sight becomes an attraction through a process he called sight sacralization, conducted in five stages: naming, in which a candidate is singled out and marked as worth preserving; framing and elevation, in which the sight is bounded, put on display and opened under protection; enshrinement, in which the framing apparatus itself becomes venerated; mechanical reproduction, in which prints, photographs and models carry the sight around the world; and social reproduction, in which groups, towns and regions name themselves after the attraction. Around the stages stands his bleaker doctrine of staged authenticity: the tourist, exiled from the real by modern life, searches for authentic experience and is sold a stage set dressed as a back region, a kitchen with the cooking performed for the audience. Measured on MacCannell's scale, Shakespearean Verona is a completed sacralization and an interrupted one side by side. Juliet's house passed through all five stages: named by tradition, framed and elevated by municipal purchase and restoration, enshrined in its courtyard ritual, mechanically reproduced on ten million postcards and feeds, and socially reproduced in Verona's self-naming as the city of love. Romeo's house completed the first stage and stalled in the second. It was named, emphatically and repeatedly, and framed, in the minimal sense that two plaques bound it as a sight; but elevation, the raising of the sight onto display, never occurred, because display requires an interior and the interior was never the city's to open. On MacCannell's account the reviews that dismiss the stop as a waste of a walk are the predictable acoustics of a sacralization arrested between stages: a marker promising a sight that cannot be delivered.

The second theory belongs to Nicola J. Watson, the literary scholar whose The Literary Tourist: Readers and Places in Romantic and Victorian Britain (2006) founded the modern study of readerly pilgrimage and whose writings on Shakespeare's Verona make her the indispensable witness for this article. Watson's taxonomy divides literary tourism into the placing of the author and the locating of the fictive, the pursuit of writers' graves and birthplaces on one side and of fiction's settings on the other, and her governing insight cuts against the condescension in MacCannell: literary place is produced by writing as mediated by acts of readerly tourism. The tourist at a fictive site is not primarily a dupe of staging. The tourist is a reader performing a material act of reading, seeking a tangible anchor for a world held in the mind, and the emotion at the site is genuine even when, especially when, the site's documentary credentials are known to be nil. Her Verona evidence runs from Samuel Rogers, who sought out the old palace of the Cappelletti in 1822 and asked what Englishman could behold it with indifference, through the nineteenth century's sentimental travellers, who insisted on the house's authenticity precisely as an act of willed reading, hearing the household call for dates and quinces within its walls. On Watson's account, nobody at Juliet's balcony has ever been deceived in the strong sense; the courtyard works because the visitors collaborate, supplying from the text everything the stones cannot.

John Urry, whose The Tourist Gaze (1990) supplied the third indispensable term, sides in method with neither and explains the street-level outcome better than either. For Urry the tourist gaze is organized by signs, and places are consumed as collections of signs anticipated from media: the visitor arrives to collect the image already possessed. A site that cannot be converted into the anticipated image, that offers no balcony to stand beneath and no courtyard to fill, simply fails to register as a sight at all; the gaze slides off a blank wall toward the nearest reproducible view. Urry's tourists at Via Arche Scaligere behave exactly as the theory predicts: a photograph of the gate, a glance at the plaques, and the walk resumes toward the Erbe and the courtyard, where the signs are dense, framed and lit. The photograph economy sharpens Urry's point into a mechanism. The courtyard offers the visitor a position inside the image: stand beneath the balcony, or on it, and the picture composes itself around a body, which is why the site's reproduction runs on visitor photography as much as on postcards. The gate offers no position. A photograph at Via Arche Scaligere can contain the wall or the visitor, not the visitor within the fiction, since the fiction's local habitation is on the other side of a locked door; the resulting images are interchangeable records of a frontage, and interchangeable images do not circulate. Mechanical reproduction, MacCannell's fourth stage, is thus not merely absent at the hero's address by institutional failure; it is refused by the site's geometry, and the refusal compounds annually, as the heroine's image multiplies through the feeds of everyone who ever queued, while the hero's wall appears only in the feeds of the completists. In seminar teaching the two façades are often set side by side as slides, and a reading surface such as the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) makes the comparison conveniently stark: on one side a courtyard composed for the camera across ninety years, on the other a wall that has refused composition since the fourteenth century.

The disagreement, then, is genuine and must be settled rather than surveyed. MacCannell's tourist is a seeker after authenticity betrayed by staging; Watson's is a reader whose pilgrimage authenticates the text rather than the stones; the two accounts predict different behaviour at a site known to be fabricated, betrayal or collaboration, and Verona's evidence is not ambiguous. The crowds at Juliet's house know about the balcony's date, are told it by guides, by plaques, by the press, and queue anyway; the collaboration Watson describes is the observed fact, and on the question of motive Watson wins outright. But motive is not mechanism. Why the collaboration crystallized at her address and never at his is a question Watson's reader-centred account answers weakly and MacCannell's institutional one answers exactly: sacralization is a supply-side process as much as a demand-side one, and it requires a proprietor. Readers supplied identical belief to both houses across two centuries of pilgrimage; the belief took architectural form only where a municipality could take possession, restore, enshrine and reproduce. At the gate of the Casa di Romeo, demand stood on the pavement and supply never arrived, and the sight stalled between naming and elevation in full view of the crowds walking past it to the completed shrine. The verdict of this article therefore divides the honours by function: Watson explains why Verona's fiction could be believed, MacCannell explains why only half of it could be built, and the play explains why the buildable half was always going to be hers. Belief is readerly, buildings are institutional, and the raw material both worked on was a text that furnished one lover with rooms and the other with roads.

The stages can be walked across the rest of the map as a control experiment, and the control confirms the reading. Juliet's tomb ran the same course as her house with a generation's delay: named by early nineteenth-century devotion, framed by the Austrian administration's tidying of the site in 1868 and by the municipality thereafter, enshrined in the purpose-built crypt of the Avena years, reproduced in every medium that has carried the story, and folded into the city's self-naming. The statue in the courtyard illustrates the fourth stage in its purest form: a bronze of 1972, an object younger than the cinema's classic versions, which mechanical and digital reproduction elevated into an icon so potent that the original fabric had to be withdrawn from the ritual it had created. The walls and gates never entered the sequence in Romeo's name at all, because the naming stage itself failed there: the itinerary names the walls as Verona's, the gates as Rome's or the Scaligers', and the banishment speech that might have baptized them as the hero's boundary is quoted at the gate of his house instead, displaced onto the one marked object he has. The sycamore grove, the map's blank, is the limiting case, a candidate sight that never achieved even naming, though its text is as famous as any in the play; no proprietor, no marker, no candidate fabric exists for it, and MacCannell's sequence explains its invisibility as the default condition from which attractions must be manufactured. Against that default, the gate's two plaques show exactly how far manufacture got in the hero's case: one stage deep, plus a frame, and then a century of standing still.

Watson's taxonomy returns a final verdict on the whole arrangement when its two halves are applied to the map. Verona contains no authorial site of Shakespeare's at all: the dramatist never stood in the city, no birthplace, desk or grave anchors the cult, and the entire apparatus belongs to her second category, the locating of the fictive, pursued without the ballast of biography. A pure fictive geography might be expected to distribute its honours by the fiction's own proportions, and in a sense the expectation is met, since the play's proportions are themselves the heroine's. But Watson's deeper point, that literary place is produced by writing mediated by readerly tourism, identifies the true authorship of the map: it was written jointly by da Porto, who supplied the city, by Shakespeare, who supplied the rooms, by two centuries of readers, who supplied the devotion, and by one administrator, who supplied what masonry he could buy. Romeo's house is the map's single unwritten page, the place where all four authors stopped at once. That is why it fascinates beyond its size, and why this article has argued it as the itinerary's most instructive object: it is the control case that reveals the authorship of everything around it.

### What is sight sacralization, and at which stage did Romeo's house stop?

Sight sacralization is Dean MacCannell's term for the making of an attraction: naming, framing and elevation, enshrinement, mechanical reproduction, social reproduction. Romeo's house was named and minimally framed by its plaques, then stopped. Elevation onto display never happened, because display needed an interior the city never acquired, and the later stages had nothing to reproduce.

## Verdict: The One Honest Wall in Verona

The evidence permits the verdict to be stated without remainder. Verona's Romeo and Juliet geography is not a map of the play; it is a map of the heroine, with the hero retained as signage. The documented record gives the city every excuse for a richer map and the play gives it none: a superb fortified palace stands ready at the hero's address, better preserved and more genuinely medieval than the shrine across the quarter, and across six centuries nobody, not the owners, not Avena with his drawings, not the crowds with their cameras, succeeded in making it a place to enter. The wider accounting of what the two lovers earn their city, set out in the companion study of [Verona's economy built on two fictional lovers](/2029/07/27/verona-economy-fictional-lovers/), prices the industry as a whole; the distribution of that industry between its two supposed beneficiaries is the present article's subject, and the distribution is total. She has the house museum, the balcony, the tomb, the statue, the letters bureau, the wedding bookings, the ticket system. He has two plaques, a gate, a restaurant tenancy and a quotation in which he says he is not there.

The counterfactual is worth a paragraph, because it tests whether the failure was contingent or necessary. Suppose the negotiation of the 1920s had closed: the city buys the palace, Avena's design is executed, and Verona opens a house of Romeo beside the Scaliger tombs in the same years as the house and tomb of Juliet. What would the museum have contained? For the heroine's house the fabricators had a brief supplied by the text and the stage: a hall for the feast, a chamber, a bed, a window to be balconied, costumes and portraits to be assembled around the known scenes. For the hero's house the text supplies no brief at all. His chamber is mentioned once, by his parents, as the place he pens himself by day, and never described, never entered, never furnished with a single object by any line of the play; the museum would have had to invent an interior ex nihilo, without even the guidance of a stage tradition, since productions too leave the Montague house unbuilt. What Avena would have opened, at best, is a handsome medieval palace with Romeo's name on it and generic period furniture inside, a writer's-house museum without a writer, a character's-house museum without a scene. The likeliest fate of such an institution, judged by the itinerary's treatment of sites that cannot stage their claim, is the fate the actual house already suffers in miniature: a pause, a paragraph, a photograph, a walk resumed. The refusal and the empty treasury therefore did not rob Verona of a second shrine. They saved it the expense of learning, in real estate, what the reviews at the gate teach for free: the hero's cult has no interior to sell, because his author never built him one.

That distribution should not be read as the city's failure of imagination, and still less as a conspiracy of the tourist board. It is the myth's own structure made municipal. From da Porto forward, the story lodges its meaning in the heroine's spaces: the feast in her father's hall, the window of her chamber, the bed, the tomb. The hero's function is motion between those spaces, over walls, along roads, into exile and back to the churchyard, and motion cannot be enshrined. Where a determined administrator tried to correct the imbalance by main force, the attempt died of conveyancing; where readers tried, at the gate, with their pens and markers, their devotion was classified as graffiti and scrubbed away. Verona's map is therefore best read as an unintended work of criticism, the most expensive reading of the play ever produced, and an accurate one. It demonstrates, in ticket sales and title deeds, what the text demonstrates in scene headings: that the tragedy lives in Juliet's rooms and happens to Romeo in the spaces between them. The closed gate on Via Arche Scaligere is the map's one honest label, the single point at which the geography admits its own nature. Everything else in Shakespearean Verona asks the visitor to believe that a fiction had an address. The wall asks nothing. It stands in a quiet street beside the real tombs of real rulers, behind its battlements, and declines to open, and in that refusal it tells the truth about the whole enterprise more plainly than any balcony: the lovers were never in this city, and the proof is that only one of them could be given a house.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: On which street does the Casa di Romeo stand, and what famous tombs stand beside it?

The Casa di Romeo stands at Via Arche Scaligere 2 in Verona's historic centre, and the famous tombs are the Arche Scaligere themselves, the raised Gothic sarcophagi of the Scaliger lords of Verona, Cangrande I, Mastino II and Cansignorio, enclosed beside the church of Santa Maria Antica a few metres from the gate. The small square of the Peschiera vecchia borders the palace on its other side. The juxtaposition defines the visit: the documented monuments of the dynasty under which the legend is set stand open to view, while the lover's house beside them stays shut.

### Q: Who owns Romeo's house?

Private owners hold Romeo's house, and the documented record of the site does not trade in their names. The palace has remained in private hands through every episode of its modern history: subdivision among several owners in the fifteenth century, the failed municipal purchase campaign of the 1920s, and the present day, in which the building functions as a private residence with a restaurant trading at the same address. Ownership is the single fact that governs everything else about the site, since a house the city does not own is a house the city cannot open, furnish or ticket.

### Q: Who actually lived in the Casa di Romeo?

The Nogarola lived in the Casa di Romeo, a powerful Veronese family of French descent allied by marriage to the ruling Scaliger. The palace is associated above all with Cagnolo Nogarola, whose name it still carries in its formal designations, Palazzo Nogarola and Palazzo Cagnolo Nogarola. The property reached the family from the Scaliger orbit, Bailardino Nogarola having married Caterina della Scala, sister of Cangrande I, and after a return to Scaliger hands it passed in 1416 to the Bevilacqua-Lazise. No document places a Montecchi in residence at the address.

### Q: In what year is the building first mentioned in documents?

The building is first mentioned in documents in 1339, the date established in Lorella Dalle Piane's catalogue study of the house. The date carries an argument inside it. Luigi da Porto set the lovers' story in the Verona of Bartolomeo della Scala, who ruled from 1301 to 1304, so the documentary life of the palace begins some thirty-five years after the events it is supposed to have housed. Earlier fabric may well stand within the walls, and a first mention is not a construction date; but every claim for the house must begin from the honesty that its archive opens a generation late for the legend.

### Q: What do the swallowtail battlements of the house proclaim?

The swallowtail battlements proclaim a Ghibelline allegiance. In the factional architecture of medieval Italy, the forked merlon, cut like a swallow's tail, was the sign of the imperial party, while the square merlon marked the Guelf interest, and a great household carved its politics into its skyline as a warning and a boast. On the Casa di Romeo the original crenellation survives on the right-hand wing above a high street wall, part of a fortified composition of massive masonry and few openings that led the reference literature to class the palace as a strong house, built to be defended.

### Q: Which lines from the play do the plaques beside the gate quote?

The plaques quote Romeo's disclaimer from the first act: "I have lost myself; I am not here; / This is not Romeo, he's some other where" (1.1). The lines belong to the sycamore-grove melancholy of the opening movement, where Romeo, lovesick for Rosaline, describes a self dissolved by unreturned desire. Chosen, apparently, as an emblem of the lover's abstraction, the quotation fixed to the gate of a house its speaker never occupied reads as the site's accurate self-description: a permanent, municipally maintained notice that the person sought at the address is elsewhere.

### Q: Who proposed turning the house into a museum of Romeo, and in which decade?

Antonio Avena proposed it, in the 1920s. Avena, historian and director of Verona's civic museums, was the architect of Shakespearean Verona: under his direction Juliet's house was fitted out and opened in 1937 and her tomb rearranged and opened in 1940. For Romeo's house he pressed the city administration repeatedly through the 1920s to buy the Nogarola palace and prepare it for the rising tourist traffic, and he carried the proposal as far as a worked design. The Romeo project stands in the Veronese literature as one of the few Avena schemes that was never realized.

### Q: Why did the plan to buy Romeo's house fail?

The plan failed on refusal and on cost, the two plainest obstacles in municipal history. The owners of the palace would not sell it, and the city of Verona lacked the funds to compel the question by an irresistible offer. Avena's design for the house of Romeo therefore stayed in the drawer while his parallel projects for Juliet's house and tomb, both on property the city controlled, went forward to completion. The entire later asymmetry of Verona's Romeo and Juliet geography descends from that single stalled negotiation: one lover's house was purchasable, the other was not, and the myth was built where the deeds allowed.

### Q: Has the Casa di Romeo ever opened as a museum?

No opening as a museum has ever taken place at the Casa di Romeo. The palace has been a residence, a subdivided property, a stable in some accounts of its quieter centuries, and an address shared with a restaurant, but no museum of Romeo has operated within its walls at any date. A circulating story that the building passed to a literary society in the 1930s for a Shakespeare museum aborted by the Second World War is not supported by the Veronese scholarship that catalogues the house, which records instead Avena's purchase proposals, his design, the owners' refusal and the city's lack of funds.

### Q: How long does the walk from Romeo's house to Juliet's house take?

The walk takes under ten minutes. From the gate on Via Arche Scaligere the route crosses the few hundred metres of the old town to Via Cappello 23, passing through the streets around Piazza dei Signori and Piazza delle Erbe, the market square of Roman origin that anchors the historic centre. Tour itineraries routinely pair the two houses in a single morning for exactly this reason, and the pairing is the fastest way to feel the argument of the map in the feet: one house entered, photographed, ticketed and crowded, the other photographed from a pavement and left behind within minutes.

### Q: Which family gave Romeo his Italian surname, and what connects that family to this street?

The Montecchi gave Romeo his Italian surname, in Luigi da Porto's novella of about 1524, where the lovers first appear as Romeo Montecchi and Giulietta Cappelletti, the families' names lifted from a single line of Dante's Purgatorio. What connects the Montecchi to this street is residence in the district rather than in the building: the family genuinely lived in the contrada around Via Arche Scaligere in its years of power, before its decline, which Dante already registers as accomplished. The identification of the Nogarola palace as the family's home is tradition's rounding of that district-level fact into an address.

### Q: What did the historian Maria Cristina Zoppis suggest about Cagnolo Nogarola and Romeo?

Maria Cristina Zoppis recorded the suggestion that the literary figure of Romeo Montecchi himself leans on the person of Cagnolo Nogarola, the documented resident of the palace that bears Romeo's name. The proposal reverses the tourist claim with elegant cruelty. The house is marketed as a place the fictional Romeo occupied; the scholarly suggestion replies that the real occupant may have helped generate the fiction, a Veronese nobleman of the Scaliger court feeding, through chronicle and memory, the figure a Vicentine novella writer later christened Romeo. The evidence permits the suggestion to be aired, not proved, and it is aired here at exactly that strength.

### Q: Why is there no balcony at Romeo's house?

There is no balcony at Romeo's house because the balcony belongs to Juliet's side of the story and of the street. The remembered scene is a window scene at the Capulet house, Romeo below in the orchard and Juliet above at her chamber; the play gives the hero no equivalent feature at his own address, indeed no address in use at all. Verona's fabricators of the 1930s accordingly built the famous balcony where the fiction put the window, on the Cappello palace the city owned, and built nothing at the palace it did not own. A balcony on the Casa di Romeo would decorate a wall the myth never described, facing a street the hero never speaks in.

### Q: Does the play contain a single scene inside the Montague house?

No scene of the play is set inside the Montague house. Montague and Lady Montague appear in the street in the opening scene, reporting a son who walks at dawn in a sycamore grove outside the walls and pens himself in his chamber by day, a chamber the drama mentions and never shows; they appear again in the churchyard in the final scene beside the bodies. The Capulet house, by contrast, hosts the feast, two chamber scenes and the discovery of the body. The census is the textual root of the tourist map: one household is furnished by the play, the other is only ever outdoors.

### Q: How many of MacCannell's five stages of sight sacralization did Juliet's house complete?

Juliet's house completed all five. In Dean MacCannell's scheme the house was named by tradition as the heroine's residence; framed and elevated by municipal purchase in 1905 and Avena's restoration; enshrined as its courtyard ritual of notes, touches and photographs took hold; mechanically reproduced across postcards, films and feeds until the balcony became one of the most copied images in Italy; and socially reproduced when Verona named itself the city of love after its attraction. The completeness of the sequence is the institutional biography of the shrine, and the arrested two-stage career of Romeo's house is unintelligible except by contrast with it.

### Q: Does any business trade at the address of Romeo's house?

A restaurant trades at the address: the Osteria al Duca occupies the ground floor at Via Arche Scaligere 2 and presents itself as heir to one of Verona's old tavern traditions, serving the traditional Veronese kitchen. The Italian reference entry for the palace lists its uses, in order, as private dwelling, restaurant and tourist attraction, which correctly ranks the address's economies. The hero's house thus participates in the myth's commerce at one remove: it feeds the visitors the myth draws to its street, while the residence itself, the object the visitors came to see, remains closed above and behind the dining rooms.

### Q: Does Mantua keep a Romeo and Juliet site of its own?

Mantua keeps no Romeo and Juliet site of its own: no apothecary's shop dressed for pilgrims, no plaqued lodging for the exile, no house of Romeo in exile. The city markets a heritage of the first rank on other foundations, the Gonzaga palaces, Mantegna's frescoes in the Palazzo Ducale, Palazzo Te, the UNESCO listing shared with Sabbioneta, the fame of Virgil. Its Shakespearean episode survives as garnish in general accounts rather than as an address. The absence is structural rather than accidental: Mantua never appointed an Avena, and the exile city of the play remains the one major location in the story with no shrine at all.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Verona map to argue that Verona's tourist geography honours one lover and not the other?

An essay should use the map's three columns as its evidence chain. Set the claim column against the record column site by site, and the pattern assembles itself: every furnished, ticketed and reproduced site belongs to the heroine, every pointed-at, unmarked or absent site to the hero, and the grading of standing converts the pattern into institutional fact. The essay can then argue the cause on two levels, the conveyancing accident that left Romeo's house unsold in the 1920s and the textual distribution that gave Juliet rooms and Romeo roads, before closing on the play's own unfulfilled commission of two golden statues, of which the city cast one.

### Q: What does a visitor actually see at Romeo's house?

A visitor sees a wall, a gate, two plaques and one of the finest medieval house-fronts in Verona. The street elevation offers brick and white tuff under swallowtail battlements, a round-arched portal of dressed stone barring the courtyard, an external stair, and beyond the gate a glimpse of the courtyard and the surviving ground-floor loggia. The Scaliger tombs stand a few metres away, a restaurant trades at the same address, and the whole inspection takes minutes. What the visitor does not see is any interior, any furnishing, any Romeo: the site's entire provision is an exterior and a text, and the text says he is not there.
