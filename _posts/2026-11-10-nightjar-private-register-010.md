## Under the Drawer

The house stood at the upper end of a lane climbing away from the market square in Highstile, where stone walls leaned close and slate roofs held the night cold into morning. Hale Brannt came in by the back door, because the front step had iced and the seal on the front door was his own work, not to be broken without cause. Inside, the air smelled of cold ash, damp wool, and paper shut away in rooms. Thin light lay across kitchen flagstones worn smooth near the sink. Tape hung still across the study doorway, and everything else kept the tidiness of a man who had lived alone and expected each object to be found in its place.

He set his case on the kitchen table and opened it in the order he had taught younger men. Inventory book first, then a sharpened pencil, string, labels, spare seals. He wrote the heading the form required, the address, the name of the dead man, and the authority for the work. Corwen Ash. Brannt had known the name longer than the man, a common arrangement in Highstile. Ash had been a retired clerk from the Accord offices, home in the hill town on a pension, walking before breakfast, keeping little company. Ash had been civil, exact, and private, as if privacy were a form of good manners.

![A bound ledger in a constable's evidence box at Highstile, its cover plain and its page edges darkened by years of one man's hands - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-42.webp)

He began with the rooms that held the least. The kitchen took one box for the dresser and another for papers in a tin above the stove. Receipts, a household notebook, coal deliveries entered in a hand so regular that he paused over it despite himself. Ash had paid each bill and kept the stubs in order, week by week. He was there to list, wrap, and label, and to see that nothing left the house except under a signature.

The bedroom took longer. A narrow bed, made. Two blankets with corners softened by washing. A shirt laid over a chair back, as if its owner meant to return before supper. Brannt had boxed houses for the dead since he was a young constable with steadier knees, in rooms where widows waited in the yard and sons argued in low voices about a watch. He had learned that reverence, if it meant anything useful, meant accuracy. You wrote the chipped cup down as a chipped cup. You did not improve a poor room, and you did not make a quiet room sinister because questions had followed a man home.

In the parlour, books stood shelved by size more than by subject. A decanter held a finger of dark spirit, listed and left sealed in its cupboard because liquids travelled badly and the form allowed exceptions when they were witnessed. A clock had stopped at an hour that meant nothing beyond a winding forgotten. He wound nothing, touched only what the work required, and recorded the stopped hands anyway.

The study he saved for last, on purpose. Tape crossed its doorway, and men from the Accord had asked about that room by telephone before the seal had dried. He broke the tape, entered his name and the hour on the fresh form, and stood inside the threshold taking stock. A desk faced the window, its chair squared to it. Shelves held smaller ledgers, letter files tied with faded tape, a blotter, a pen rest, a lamp with a green shade. From the desk a man could see the lane, the roofs below, and the pale road leaving Highstile, long before a knock reached his door. Brannt noted that sight line on his form. He distrusted enquiries that began by deciding which details were beneath them.

He worked the shelves left to right, top to bottom, slowly, because no second pair of ears in the house could confirm him. Each file was entered by its label, or by its position when it had none, and went into the long box for ordinary study papers. Much of it was ordinary. Rates correspondence. A boundary ditch dispute pursued past two surveyors. Copies of letters to a sister in another town, brief and formal, tender only in their closings. Brannt read no further than identification required.

The desk had three drawers on the right and one long shallow drawer across the front, made for maps or instruments laid flat. The right hand drawers yielded pens, sealing wax, a broken spectacle frame, string sorted by length. He approved of the string and entered it without comment. The long drawer stuck, swollen at one corner in the manner of old wood in a cold house. He eased it open with the flat of his hand rather than force it, since forcing a drawer in a sealed house was how small damage became a later accusation. It showed an empty space lined with brown paper. Yet it dragged at the back as it moved, heavier than an empty drawer should feel on its runners.

He knelt, his knees protesting in their familiar way, and set lamplight on the floorboards at an angle. In the gap under the drawer box a dark edge showed where no edge should have been. He did not reach in at once. First he wrote: long drawer, front of desk, found to contain nothing, movement suggesting an object lodged below it. Then he lifted the drawer clear of its housing and set it upright against the desk. In the shallow hollow between the drawer bottom and the cross rail beneath it lay a book.

It lay flat, bound in dark cloth over boards, thick enough to explain the drag. Anyone opening that drawer in the ordinary way saw brown paper and emptiness. Anyone closing it could blame its weight on old wood. The book had not been stored in the drawer. It had been placed under it, in a space that existed because furniture was built with tolerances, and because a careful man might notice such a tolerance and use it. Brannt studied it for a count of ten before touching it. The spine bore no title. A paler band crossed the cloth where a hand had often gripped it, sliding it in and out. He recorded all of that before his fingers met the boards.

The boards felt cold through his thin station gloves, and the book weighed more than its size promised, as dense paper does in a binding made to last. He set it on the blotter and opened the inventory to a fresh line. A book found in a desk could be entered and boxed with the rest. A book found under a drawer had to be entered as found, in words a stranger could rely on in another town, without the room in front of him. Under the long drawer of the study desk, not within it. He wrote the sentence, read it back, and underlined the word under once.

He opened it no farther than the first leaf, and only to identify it. There, in the same regular hand as the coal receipts, stood a name and a heading in browned ink, with columned figures arranged beneath in patience. Ash's hand. Ash's book. Brannt closed it with care. What filled the remaining pages was business for whoever held the authority to ask, and he had a fair idea whose authority that would be.

While the book sat closed under his hand, other matters took their turn. The station had taken a call from the Accord offices, courteous and brief, saying officers concerned with Ash's former service might attend in due course. It had also taken a call from a man who gave no office and asked whether anything had been removed from the study. Brannt had asked for a name and an authority, and listened to silence before the line went dead. Two strangers had stayed at the lower inn on the night after Ash died, eating apart, asking the landlord nothing at all. In Highstile that was more remarkable than asking many questions. He had entered them in the day book without embroidery.

Beside the desk stood two boxes. One was marked for the Accord, half filled with files a telephone call had described, in careful language, as potentially pertinent. The other, nearly full, held ordinary study papers for the station. Put the book in the ordinary box, and it would be logged among household papers, its finding left to wait for a reader diligent enough to notice an underlined word. Put it in the Accord box, and he would be making a judgment beyond sorting. He knew that. He also knew sorting was judgment whether a man admitted it or not, and that pretending otherwise was how honest records acquired dishonest outcomes.

The Accord was who was coming. That was the plain shape of it. Ash had kept this book apart from coal and rates and boundary ditches, apart even from the drawer that seemed to hold it. The people who came up the lane for it would not come from the market square. They would come with authority from the city where Ash had served, and they would ask first for the box carrying their name, because institutions asked first for what had been labelled theirs. If the book belonged with them, it should lie where they would look, without tearing a station apart or accusing a constable of withholding. If it did not belong with them, his inventory would still say where it had been found and where he had placed it, signed and kept in books with no missing page.

He wrapped the book in station brown paper, loosely enough to spare the binding, and tied it once. On the label he wrote the position of finding again, then the words ledger, bound, manuscript hand, and his initials. He laid it in the Accord box on top of the files, where its weight settled with a sound louder than it should have been in a quiet room. In the inventory it took its own line and number, and in the margin he repeated the sentence about the drawer.

The rest of the study boxed quickly, and the plain work steadied him. Lamp, pen rest, letter files, each listed and wrapped. The blotter he rolled and tied, too large for any box without folding, and folding it to suit a box offended his sense of proportion. He walked the emptied room once, door to window and back, looking for a bare nail or a gap on a shelf that might mean something taken. He found none he could swear to. He sealed the Accord box at the desk, entered the seal number in two places, and carried it downstairs himself, one step at a time, free hand on the rail.

In the kitchen he set the boxes in rows by destination: station ordinary, station retained, and Accord, to be held for collection under signature. He checked every label against the inventory and every seal against its number. Then he locked the house and set fresh tape across the front door from outside, where wind worried at its lower corner and failed to lift it. Below in the square a cart passed with winter greens, and smoke rose in straight lines from the chimneys. Highstile went about its morning, as it should.

He walked down the lane with his case in one hand and the inventory book under his arm, keeping to the side where the stones were least slick. In his mind Ash sat again at that desk, able to see a visitor long before a knock, sliding a heavy book into a place that was no place at all in the furniture of the room. A man did that to keep a thing, and to keep the keeping invisible to an ordinary search. What the keeping meant was not Brannt's business. His business was an honest finding, an honest seal, and a handover in his station, under his eye, for exactly what his hand had written, no more and no less.

At the station he stamped the melt from his boots, hung his coat on its peg, and laid the inventory book on the duty desk to be copied into the day record before any other business took him. The Accord box waited while he logged its seal and set it on the evidence room shelf he had cleared that morning, before he ever climbed the lane. He had expected something of the kind, though he could not have said what kind. A careful man prepared a shelf without needing to name what would stand on it. He locked the evidence room and put the key back in its drawer, in it, where anyone checking would expect a key to be.

## Two Signatures

The Ninth Office occupied the upper floor of a stone building above the canal in Carrowmere, where the water carried the smell of rope, coal smoke, and cold iron up through windows that never quite shut against the weather. Mira Kovac had the desk nearest the inner wall, away from the draught. Its surface held a blotter scored by years of other hands, a telephone with a cord worn smooth, a tray for incoming paper, and a lamp that warmed one corner of the blotter and nothing else. Bells from the lower bridge marked the hour through the glass.

Her hands rested flat on the blotter, palms down, in the manner of someone keeping them where she could see them. The fear she had taken from Corwen Ash in Highstile had not left her with the journey home. It sat behind ordinary acts, patient and specific, the residue of eighteen years of a man's dread carried in one read and paid for in her own body. Eighteen years of Ash's fear. She had slept in pieces and woken each time with her jaw set. A crowded corridor the evening before had cost her more than she had admitted to the clerk who asked after her health. Field work, the kind that asked for speed and distance and a clear head in a strange town, was beyond what she honestly had. Desks, evidence rooms, telephones, paper. That was her measure for this case, and she knew it before anyone said it aloud.

Reni Vosk brought the tasking in at mid morning, carrying the folder with both hands as if its weight were literal, which in a sense it was. Reni was twenty one, junior in grade and careful in the way of the young who have learned that care is visible. She set the folder square with the edge of Mira's desk, checked its alignment once, and stepped back. The cover sheet bore Mira's name and callsign, a case designation tied to Highstile, and a routing mark she had not seen used on a recovery before. Beneath the routing mark, where a single authorising signature ordinarily stood alone, there were two.

Mira opened the folder and read the first page twice before she looked up. Commissioner Ilse Harrand had tasked the recovery personally. Not through the oversight secretariat, not under a printed authority delegated to a deputy, but under her own hand, in ink, with her full title written beneath the signature in a script that allowed no confusion about who had chosen to be named. Harrand oversaw the Accord bodies that the Office served and answered to, a figure Mira had seen at a distance in a corridor and once across a hearing room, a woman whose interventions usually arrived as minutes and findings long after field officers had finished their work. Commissioners did not task single recoveries.

The second signature stood below Harrand's, in the space reserved for a countersigning authority, and Mira knew the hand before she read the name under it. Arvo Leyden. Her controller, her handler, the man who had run her work since she came to the Office and who signed her taskings, her expenses, and once, after an incident in a bonded warehouse, a formal letter defending her judgment to people who had wanted it questioned. Leyden countersigned operational orders from other authorities on rare occasions, when jurisdiction overlapped. He had never countersigned a personal tasking from the Commissioner. Mira had served under him long enough, and had read enough of the Office archive in slow weeks, to use that word with care. Never. She could not find the precedent because, as far as she could establish, there was none.

She asked Reni to wait, because a witness to small reactions was sometimes worth having and because Reni's errand plainly had not ended at the desk. Reni admitted, with the air of someone delivering a message exactly as given, that Leyden wished to speak with Mira once she had read the whole folder, and that the Commissioner's office had telephoned twice to confirm the folder had reached her hands and no other hands first. Mira read on. The recovery concerned material boxed by the Highstile constabulary at Ash's house under its own authority. An inventory extract listed files, household papers, and one bound ledger in Ash's hand, found in a position the constable had described with unusual care. The box holding it had been marked for the Accord and sealed at the station. Her task was to recover it, intact, and deliver it under an unbroken chain of custody.

The cost stood on the second page, set out plainly, without the cushioning language that taskings used when they wished an officer not to notice what she was agreeing to. Whatever was recovered would go to oversight. Not to the Office. Not to the Office archive, not to Leyden's registry, not to any working file from which Mira or her colleagues might later draw on it. Custody, ownership of the record, and every decision about its future use would rest with Harrand's office from the moment of handover. The Office would retain the fact of the transfer and nothing of the thing transferred. Mira read that paragraph a third time, because plain language in an official document deserved the same suspicion as obscure language, and for the opposite reason. It hid nothing. It wanted her to know exactly what her work would not earn her own service.

Leyden arrived in person rather than use the telephone, which told her as much as the signature had. He closed the door of the small interview room off the main office, a courtesy rarely extended for a recovery briefing, and sat opposite her with the copy of the tasking open at the cost paragraph. He confirmed each element without ornament. Harrand had asked for Mira by name. The request had come to him as a countersignature, not as a consultation, and he had signed because the authority was lawful and because refusal would have been recorded as the Office declining an oversight direction concerning a dead officer's papers. He preferred it treated as a custody problem by someone who understood custody in her bones, from six years on bonded cargo at the Valtessa docks.

Mira asked what the Office received besides an empty line in its registry. Leyden said, a receipt. She asked whether oversight and the Office wanted the same thing from the recovery, since their signatures stood on the same page, and he gave her the honest answer under the careful one. Their signatures wanted a recovered box. What each institution wanted after recovery was a question the document deliberately left alone, and she should not mistake a shared signature line for a shared purpose. It was the sort of warning he gave rarely and only when he believed the paper in front of them was being read too hopefully by someone. She marked it, as she marked everything he said in that tone, in a private column of her attention reserved for instructions that would matter later.

Her own condition entered the conversation without drama. Leyden asked what she could do, and she told him the measure of it, spent, in the plain vocabulary they used between them for capacity after a heavy read. No pursuit work. No long days in the field. No reading undertaken for curiosity or for speed, and any reading the case required to be weighed beforehand like a charge against a small account. He accepted the account without argument. The case, he said, had been shaped accordingly, or had shaped itself. A sealed box in a constabulary evidence room. A constable with a reputation for exact records. Inventory, verification, transport, handover. It could be worked slowly, at desks and in evidence rooms, by telephone and by paper, with the journeys made by arrangement rather than by urgency. If it changed character, she was to stop and report rather than spend what she did not have.

Petra Dunmore joined them by telephone from her desk at the far end of the building, where the analysts kept the telephones busy and the windows shut against canal noise. Dunmore's part would begin once samples and copies could properly reach Carrowmere, and her voice on the line had the dry patience of a woman who trusted paper more than people and people more than telephones. She had already asked Highstile for a clean copy of Brannt's full inventory, not the extract in the tasking folder, because extracts, she said, reflected the judgment of whoever had cut them. She wanted the ledger's dimensions, its binding described, and any entry pattern the constable could report without opening what he had sealed. Dunmore also wanted it noted that dating a bound book from an inventory alone was guesswork dressed in uniform, and she would not be hurried into it.

The question of the second party occupied the last part of the briefing, and it stayed a question. In Highstile, on the night after Ash died in his house, two people had been seen where they had no settled reason to be, and Brannt's day book held their description without embellishment. No record placed them on the road out of the town. The innkeeper reported their room paid and empty, their departure unwitnessed, which in a hill town meant little and therefore meant everything worth worrying about. They might have gone over the fields before light. They might have taken rooms under other names two streets away. They might be sitting in a kitchen in Highstile drinking tea and waiting for a box to move. Leyden used no stronger word than unlocated, and Mira adopted it. Unlocated was a working condition, not a reassurance.

Reni returned with tea neither of them drank and with the acceptance copy for Mira's signature, and hovered in the doorway long enough for Mira to see her take in the room, the two senior signatures on the open folder, Leyden seated rather than standing, the whole unusual geometry of the thing. Reni would file the copy. Reni would learn, from the filing, how the Office recorded work whose product left the building for good. Whatever this kind of case became in the years ahead, it would be juniors like Reni who inherited its habits, and Mira found herself hoping the girl was noticing the right parts. She signed the acceptance with her ordinary signature, neither flourished nor shaky.

Alone at her desk again, Mira worked through the first slow steps in the order her customs years had taught her. She telephoned the Highstile constabulary and got Brannt himself, a voice with gravel in it and no hurry at all. He confirmed the box, its shelf, and its seal, and read the seal entry back to her from his day record when she asked, without resentment at being checked. The ledger entry he confirmed as written, under the drawer, not within it, his own underlining mentioned as matter of factly as a weather note. She wrote his words in her notebook in her own hand, because a summary made later from memory was a small corruption she could refuse at the start. She requested that nothing in the evidence room be moved on her account before arrangements were settled between the station, the Office, and oversight. Brannt said nothing there moved on anybody's account without a signature, and she believed him.

By early afternoon the case existed as paper in good order. A tasking folder with two signatures. An inventory extract and a promise of a fuller copy. Dunmore's requirements listed for the station in language Mira had checked twice for tone, since a constable who had done his work well deserved requests that sounded like requests. Her notebook held a page of questions in plain sequence. What of Ash's had survived the burning of his papers, and in what condition. Whether the box had stayed sealed since Brannt packed it. Whether the two unlocated people had gone home or were waiting for movement. Whether a spent officer could bring a heavy book down a hill by procedure alone, without spending herself into the ground on the way.

She closed the notebook and sat for a while with her hands flat on the blotter again, listening to the bridge bells and the knock of river traffic against timber. The fear that belonged to Ash kept its place at the edge of her attention, neither growing nor mercifully fading, a reminder priced into every decision she would make on his account. Harrand had tasked her personally. Leyden had countersigned, which had never happened before. The price was written where she could not pretend to have missed it. Whatever she recovered would go to oversight, and the Office, and Mira with it, would keep only the record of having handed it over. She reached for the telephone to begin the next call, slowly, in the manner of a woman pacing a long room she intended to cross entire.

## The Constable's Box

The evidence room at Highstile sat at the back of the constabulary station, past the duty desk and down a short passage where the stone changed from squared blocks to rougher work. Rain had left the yard dark and the passage smelled of wet wool, lamp oil and the iron tang that rose from the stove in the front room. Inside, the evidence room was smaller than Mira had expected. A barred window high in the far wall let in a thin wash of hill light. Shelves of blackened iron ran along two sides, each shelf crowded with tied parcels, sealed jars, folded cloth and boxes whose labels had been written by several different hands across several different years. The ceiling pressed low. A table stood in the middle, scarred along its edges where blades and box corners had bitten at the varnish, and a single lamp burned on it with a steady, unsparing flame.

Mira stood just inside the door and let the room settle around her before she moved. Her coat still held the damp from the walk across the yard. Her boots left a faint print on the boards. Behind her, Constable Hale Brannt closed the door with care, not because the sound would disturb anyone, but because he was a man who closed every door as if its hinges belonged to somebody else. He carried the key on a short chain at his waist. He carried a folded inventory in his left hand. He waited while she looked, and his waiting had patience in it rather than deference.

She was spent in a way that made ordinary tasks take on edges. Eighteen years of Corwen Ash's fear sat in her from the house read, not as a picture and not as knowledge, but as a heaviness that rose whenever she reached too quickly or turned her head before her eyes had finished their work. She had slept badly. She had eaten because Brannt's clerk had put food in front of her and because refusing would have made a small ceremony of weakness. Her hands were steady enough if she gave them time. Her thoughts were clear if she allowed one to finish before the next began. The case, if it could be worked at all by her, would be worked in this fashion, at a table, with paper, with seals and inventories and the slow comparison of one written line against another.

Brannt set the inventory down and aligned its corner with the table edge. "The box is the third on the lower shelf," he said. "I have not moved it since it was sealed."

"Who else has a key?" Mira asked.

"I do. The duty constable holds the spare in the front drawer, in my sight or in the sight of whoever sits the desk. The room is entered for cause and the cause is entered in the book by the door."

She looked at the book. Its cover had swelled from years of damp air. Names and dates and short reasons filled page after page in ink that had faded to brown at the older entries. The discipline of it pleased her. A room kept in this way did not guarantee honesty, but it gave dishonesty fewer places to stand.

"Bring it to the table yourself," she said. "I want your hands to be the last known hands before mine."

Brannt lifted the box from the shelf with both arms. It was made of pale wood reinforced with iron at the corners, the sort of box a station kept for documents that might have to travel. A paper seal crossed the lid and ran down one side. A second seal, wax this time, held the hasp. On the lid, in Brannt's upright hand, a label read that the contents came from the house of Corwen Ash, deceased, recovered under constabulary authority during the boxing of the premises after the breach. Below that, in smaller script, stood the words that had brought her up the hill road in her condition and kept her at the table despite it. For the Accord, when the Accord comes.

"You wrote that," she said.

"I did."

"Why the Accord and not the Office?"

Brannt rested his palms flat on either side of the box, not touching the seals. "Because the Accord is who was expected. Your people sent word through the valley office that oversight would want what the fire left. The Office was named in the same message, but the naming had a different weight to it. Forgive the word. It had a different place in the sentence."

Mira nodded. She had learned long ago, on bonded cargo work far from Highstile, that clerks and constables heard distinctions that senior men pretended not to make. A sentence could carry its true instruction in its ordering.

"Tell me about the ledger again," she said. "Not as you told the valley office. As you found it."

Brannt glanced toward the box, then back at her, as if deciding which kind of account she wanted. "The house had been gone through by the second party before our men arrived, though gone through is too loose for what they did. Drawers opened. Cupboards opened. Nothing smashed that we could find. Ash's study was the worst of it, if worst is the word for a room left tidy and wrong at the same time. My men boxed the study first. Books in one box, loose papers in another, instruments in a third with cloth between them. When the tall desk was emptied, Tomasin, who is my senior man, lifted the bottom drawer clear to check for fallen paper behind it. The ledger lay under the drawer. Not in it. Under it, flat against the desk's base, where a man would have to lift the whole drawer out to reach it."

"Wrapped?"

"In oilcloth. Tied once with twine. The twine had not been cut."

"Position?"

"Spine toward the back of the desk. Fore edge toward the front. If you slid the drawer in without looking, you would never know the book was there. If you pulled the drawer only halfway, you would never know either."

"And you concluded what?"

Brannt considered. "That Ash hid it. I did not conclude why. A man hides a book for more reasons than I have fingers, and half of them are shame that harms nobody."

That answer settled something in her regard for him. He had found the object that three separate interests wanted, and he had resisted the easiest pleasure available to a finder, which was to invent the reason for its hiding and then behave as if the invention were evidence.

"Your men read any of it?" she asked.

"No."

"You are certain."

"Tomasin opened the oilcloth at the desk because we had to know whether the thing under the drawer was a book or a packet or something that might bite. He saw a bound ledger and a first page with figures. He closed it. I saw the same when I checked his work. Neither of us turned a page. You may put that in whatever report you write, and you may put my name beside it in full."

"I may have to ask you to sign for it twice," she said.

"Then I will sign twice. Paper is cheap beside trouble."

She did not break the seals at once. First she walked the room.

Brannt watched without comment. When she paused by the window, he said, "You read places the way Tomasin reads a track in mud."

"Less reliably," she said.

"That is not how your reputation tells it."

"My reputation has never had to pay for being wrong."

She came back to the table and set her right hand, palm down, on the lid of the box, clear of both seals.

Weight needed an anchor. Flesh alone would not do, and air would not do, and a story told by another person would not do. Wood and iron would hold what hands had left, though wood held less faithfully than stone and far less faithfully than metal that had been handled with intent. The box had iron corners. The hasp was iron. The lid had taken the pressure of palms and fingers across its grain. If anything had been left here, it would be left there, thinned by the poor keeping quality of the wood, sharpened a little where the iron had caught and held it.

She breathed out and let the room narrow.

What came was not a scene. Weight never gave her a scene, however often people who had never carried the practice imagined otherwise. It gave her affect with the shape of handling pressed into it, dread or haste or care surviving in the anchor after the hands themselves had gone. The box gave her care first. That surprised her enough that she almost lifted her hand. Care, and patience, and a particular quality of attention that she knew from customs sheds and from strong rooms, the attention of somebody examining rather than taking. Under the care lay a second pressure, later or earlier she could not say with confidence, because the lid had been handled more than once and the impressions had settled over one another like prints in wet sand trodden by more than one boot. Brannt's handling she could account for. Tomasin's she could account for. The shelving of the box, the sealing of the box, the writing of the label, all of that she could account for.

What she could not account for sat beneath the seal line.

She moved her hand a fraction, until her fingertips rested where the lid met the body of the box, along the seam that the paper seal crossed. The care deepened there. It had a direction to it. Open. Pause. Look. Close again. The sequence sat in the wood and in the iron of the hasp as plainly as a footprint declares the direction of a step, and it sat below the level where Brannt's sealing ought to have been the final handling. Somebody had opened this box before the seals went on, or had opened it in a manner that left the seals as they stood, and the second of those was a thought she set aside because the seals showed no disturbance her eyes could find. Before, then. Before sealing, somebody had lifted the lid, had looked inside for long enough that patience had time to settle into the grain, and had closed the lid again with the same patience, neither hurrying nor fumbling.

Nothing in what she held spoke of theft. Theft left a different weather in an anchor when it left anything at all, a greed or a fear of discovery that quickened the handling. This had no quickness in it. This had the steadiness of an inventory performed by hands that did not own what they examined.

The cost arrived as it always arrived, without announcement and without mercy for her condition. The patience she had taken from the box did not come alone. It carried a thin bright thread of alertness, the watchfulness of somebody who had expected to be interrupted and had arranged not to be caught mid act. That alertness entered her chest and lodged there, and behind it came Ash's old fear, stirred up from wherever it had settled in her during the night, answering the newer feeling the way one struck bell answers another of similar tone. Her breathing shortened. The lamp flame doubled at its edge for a count of three. She lifted her hand from the lid and put it flat on the table instead, on bare scarred wood, until the room came back to its proper size.

"You are pale," Brannt said.

"I was pale when I arrived. You were too polite to remark on it."

"I remarked on it to my clerk. I asked him to feed you."

"Then your manners and your management are both in good order."

He did not smile, but something around his eyes eased. "You found something."

"Somebody has been through the box," she said. "Before it was sealed. Careful hands. They opened it, they looked, they closed it again. Nothing in the handling suggests taking. It suggests examination."

Brannt's face changed then, and she watched the change with respect, because it was the face of a man checking his own house for a fault he had not believed possible. "Through my box," he said. "In my station."

"Through the box before your seals. Where it happened I cannot say. It may have happened at the house, between the finding and the packing. It may have happened here, before the sealing was finished. The wood will not tell me where, and it will not tell me when with any precision worth writing down."

"Whose hands?"

"No."

He frowned. "No, you will not say, or no, you cannot?"

"No, I cannot. Weight records what a handling felt like from inside the person handling. It does not record a name. It does not record a face. It has never once, in eleven years of my reading, told me who. If I gave you a name from that lid, I would be inventing, and the invention would send you hunting through your own station on the strength of my invention. I will not do that to your men, and I will not do it to the case."

Brannt was silent for a while. The kettle in the front room had stopped singing. "Could it have been my hands you read? Tomasin's?"

"Then it may be nothing."

Brannt drew the folded inventory toward him and opened it on the table, smoothing it with the side of his hand. "Then we do the next work," he said, "and we do it where I can see it, if you have no objection."

"I have the opposite of an objection," Mira said. "Stay."

She broke the wax seal with the back of a station knife while he watched, and cut the paper seal at the lid line, and lifted the lid on hinges that complained softly. Inside, the contents lay in the order his inventory described. Oilcloth bundles. A packet of letters tied with tape. Ash's instruments in their cloth. And the ledger, still in its oilcloth, resting on top because Brannt had placed it uppermost for the Accord's convenience, a courtesy she noted and said nothing about. She lifted each item out in turn and set it on the table in a row, and Brannt named each item from his list as she lifted it, and she confirmed each name aloud before she set the item down. It took a long time. Her shoulders ached with the effort of doing a simple thing at a pace her body would accept. Twice she stopped and rested her hands flat and waited for the alertness she had borrowed from the box lid to fade back into the general burden she already carried.

At the end of the row there was nothing missing. Every line of the inventory had its object. Every object had its line. The letters were unopened, their seals whole. The instruments lay in their cloth in the order the cloth's creases remembered. The ledger's twine was uncut.

"Nothing taken," Brannt said, with an exhale he tried to hide.

"Nothing taken from the box as your list describes it," she said. "What the list cannot prove is whether anything was taken from the house before your men made the list. That door stays open as well."

"You keep a great many doors open, Officer Kovac."

"It is most of the work," she said. "Anyone can close them. Closing them early is how a case dies with its boots on."

The ledger, freed from its oilcloth, was heavier than its size promised. The binding was dark leather over boards, worn at the corners to a paler shade, and the spine had been repaired once with thread that did not match the original stitching. Ash's hand had titled nothing on the cover. No name, no subject, no warning. A book that had lived under a drawer had perhaps needed no title, or had needed the opposite of one.

Mira sat down to it because standing had begun to cost more than she wished to spend before noon. Brannt brought a second chair for himself and placed it at the table's far side, angled so that he could see the book's position and her hands without leaning over either. He brought also a clean sheet of station paper and a pencil, unasked, and set them at her elbow. She recorded the ledger's dimensions first, then its weight in her arms, then the state of its binding, then the twine and oilcloth set aside as items in their own right. Only when the describing was done did she open the cover.

The first page carried figures in columns, ruled by hand with a straight edge, the lines faintly uneven where the pen had pressed harder at the start of each stroke. Ash's writing was small and upright, the numerals formed with a clerk's consistency, each seven crossed, each zero closed. A date stood at the head of the page in story reckoning she recognised from the house papers, a session day and a month name from the hill calendar, and beneath it the entries began.

She read the first forty pages the way Brannt's clerk would have read them, and then she read them again the way a customs inspector reads a manifest, looking for the item that does not belong among its neighbours.

Nothing did not belong. That was the trouble, or it was the relief, and she could not yet tell which.

Coal, delivered by the sack, at intervals that matched the turning of cold weather. Lamp oil. Candles in the cheaper months, as if Ash had economised on light when the days lengthened and spent on it again when they shortened. Payments to a woman in the town for washing. Payments to a boy for carrying water during a dry spell when the house well ran low. Bread, salt, dried beans, tea of the coarse hill kind. A cobbler's bill for resoling. A glazier's bill for one pane in the study window. Postage, again and again, small sums for letters sent down to Carrowmere and smaller sums for letters sent no farther than the valley. Ink. Paper by the quire. A subscription to a journal she had seen in the study, paid in two instalments. A gift to the temple collection at the turning festival, modest, recorded without comment. A fee to a physician in a winter when, the entries implied, Ash had been ill for the better part of a month and had bought broth and physic and firewood in uneasy quantities.

The sums were small. The entries were regular. Week followed week with the faithfulness of a man whose life had narrowed to a house, a desk, a garden wall and a path into town, and who had kept his narrowing in columns because columns were the shape his mind trusted. There were memoranda in the margins, few and brief. A note that the roof leaked above the landing. A note that a neighbour's dog had died. A note that a certain letter had gone unanswered and that the writer did not intend to send another. Nothing coded that she could detect. Nothing swollen with significance. The hand did not hurry anywhere in forty pages. It did not linger anywhere either.

"Well?" Brannt said at last. He had held his peace through both readings, which she registered as another mark in his favour.

"If a man wanted to show me a retired life kept in a book," she said, "he would show me this."

"And if a man wanted to hide a different book inside this one?"

"Then he would start exactly this way, and so would an honest man, because this is how honest men start. The opening proves the opening. It does not prove the book."

She turned page forty one over and looked at page forty two without reading it closely, then closed the ledger and rested her hand on the cover without any attempt to read what the leather held. She was not ready to spend herself on the book itself. The box had cost her a thread of borrowed watchfulness that still sat under her ribs, and the memory of what a full read of Ash's leavings had done to her at the house stood in her mind like a sign at a cliff path. The ledger would be read in its turn, if a read was warranted, and with preparation. For the moment, paper and eyes would have to do the work that paper and eyes could do.

Brannt poured water from a jug into two cups and pushed one toward her. "The valley office asked me what I thought it was," he said. "I told them a dead man's accounts. I believed it when I said it."

"You may still be right."

"You do not sound as if you believe I am."

"I sound," she said, "like a woman who has read forty pages of a book that runs to several hundred, and who was trained by people who lost cargo and occasionally lost ships by admiring the top layer of a hold. Drink your water, Constable. We are going to think about this properly, which means slowly, which means all three ways at once until one of them falls down under its own weight."

"Three ways," he said.

"Three live possibilities. There are always more than three ways a thing can be, but only three of them are alive enough to spend a morning on. The first is that your first thought was the whole of it. The ledger is personal. A retired man's private accounts and memoranda, kept the way he kept everything, thoroughly and without audience, hidden under a drawer out of habit or out of a wish that his small economies not be read after his death by strangers. Half the old men in any hill town would hide a book of debts and gifts and small shames, not because the contents matter to anyone else, but because the contents are theirs. If that is what this is, then oversight will file it, the Office will lose interest, the second party has frightened a household and ransacked a study over a cobbler's bills, and Ash will have had the last word by being exactly as ordinary as he appeared. I can believe that reading. The first forty pages argue for it with every line they contain. Any honest person who opened this book where Tomasin opened it would reach that reading, and would be reasonable in reaching it."

Brannt turned his cup slowly on the table. "And the second way."

"The ledger is operational. Ash kept a working record of the case that produced the file from eighteen years ago, the case that ended his service and began his long quiet up here. He kept it against orders, if the Office's habits then were the Office's habits still, because a man who believes a file has been closed wrongly keeps his own copy of the truth as he saw it. Not the file itself. His record. What he did, what he was told, what he noticed and was instructed not to pursue. A book like that would need a cover of ordinariness if it lived in a house that might be entered, and Ash had eighteen years to give it one. Forty pages of coal and candles at the front of an operational record is not an argument against the record. It is what a careful man builds at the front of anything he does not want read by the first hands that open it. And the hiding argues this way too. Men hide account books from embarrassment. Men hide operational records because the contents could end them, or could end other people, or could reopen a thing that powerful interests paid to close."

"The eighteen years," Brannt said quietly. "You keep returning to them."

"The fear I took at the house had eighteen years in it. A man does not sustain that quality of fear over a glazier's bill."

"He might sustain it over being hunted."

"He might. Which is why the second way is alive and not proven. Fear tells me he was afraid. It does not read me his pages."

"The third way," she said, "is the least comfortable, so we will give it the same room as the others."

Brannt waited.

"The ledger is a list. Not an account of a thing. The thing itself. Names, or dates, or rooms, or some other set of entries whose value lies in the set being complete and in one hand. A list does not argue. A list does not explain itself, which is why lists frighten oversight more than narratives do. A narrative can be disputed. A list can only be used or buried. If Ash spent his retirement assembling the one set of facts that the official file was built to leave unassembled, then the book under the drawer is not a memoir and not a household record. It is an instrument. Whoever holds it holds the means to do something, though the book by itself will not tell us what, or whether the something is exposure or leverage or simple remembrance carried to a length that looks like intent."

The telephone instrument in the front room had a line down the valley that rain could interrupt and that the wind worked on like a string. Petra Dunmore came through it from Carrowmere with her voice thinned to a thread, reading from the photographed sample pages the valley office had transmitted and from a copy of Brannt's inventory that had travelled ahead of Mira on the previous day's coach. Mira held the receiver with her left hand and kept her right on her notes, and Brannt, unprompted, withdrew to the passage and pulled the evidence room door almost shut, leaving her the privacy of the call and keeping himself within call. She would remember that too.

"You have a dead man's shopping in forty pages," Dunmore said, after the greetings had been got through with the economy their acquaintance allowed. "I have twelve of those pages here in copy and I have a shopping list wearing a ledger's clothes. My desk chief asked me whether Carrowmere should send a carriage or a waste basket."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him waste baskets are how offices lose the one page that matters, usually somewhere past page forty. He disliked the answer. He disliked it accurately. What have you got that I have not?"

"A box that was opened by careful hands before Brannt sealed it. Nothing taken, by his inventory. Nothing provable as to whose hands, or where."

The line hummed. She could hear paper moving at the far end, and the scratch of Dunmore's pen, and under those sounds the larger murmur of the Carrowmere desk room, clerks and cabinets and the canal traffic beyond the windows there.

"Say that second part again," Dunmore said, "the way you would write it."

"Weight on the box showed examination, not removal. It does not identify the examiner. It does not date the examination usefully."

"Good. If you had given me a name down a telephone line I would have advised Leyden to take you off this and put you somewhere with soup. You sound like yourself. A slower version."

"I am a slower version. Work the possibilities with me, Petra. My constable has a steady mind but he has no file knowledge, and I want the three of them tested by somebody who can be rude about all three."

Dunmore took personal first, and gave it its full strength. "It fits the pages and nothing else needs to fit. Old men with a clerk's training keep stranger books than this for no audience at all. Operational next, since you will ask. The weather around the book argues for it. Harrand tasks this herself. Leyden countersigns. Eighteen years of fear sit behind it. But weather is not pages, Mira, and I distrust the way weather makes an object glow."

"List."

A pause came down the line that had paper stilled in it. "List is the reading nobody wants to say first at a desk, because a list changes what holding the object means. If it is a narrative, we are recovering a document. If it is a list, we are recovering a capability, and capabilities get fought over in yards and on roads. The second party behaved at that house the way people behave when they believe an object has a use. They did not behave as if they were hunting a memoir. Memoirs you can let the other side publish and then deny. Lists you have to hold or destroy."

"Then your order of belief?"

"My order of belief is that I refuse to order them on twelve photographed pages and a shopping list's worth of figures. Send me the dating work you do there. Send me the pattern of the accounts as far as you take them: intervals, gaps, where the regularity holds and where it strains. Accounts confess through their rhythm when they will not confess through their entries. Household books have a rhythm like a heartbeat, steady with small wanderings. Constructed books have a rhythm like a metronome, or they break stride where the real keeping begins. That is where I can help you, from this desk, without either of us pretending a sample is a book."

Mira wrote rhythm on her sheet and underlined it once. "I will work the dating here. Brannt's inventory goes back with my notes. The box does not travel until the transfer is arranged under Leyden's instruction, and until it travels it sleeps in Brannt's evidence room, in a room whose habits I have begun to respect."

"And the hands that were in it before you?"

"Open," Mira said. "Entirely open. I have care on a box lid and nothing else, and I will not build on it past what it holds." She thanked Dunmore, replaced the receiver, and sat a moment in the quieter room before she called Brannt back in.

Brannt came back in with fresh water and stood looking at the three headings on her sheet. "You have written the valley office's waste basket theory in better language," he said, "and then kept it alive beside the other two. Most investigators who come up this hill kill the dull reading in the first hour. It offends them."

"The dull reading has the most evidence," Mira said. "Forty pages of it. The other two have circumstances and fear and a hiding place. If I kill the reading that owns the evidence because it bores me, I am not investigating. I am entertaining myself at a dead man's expense."

"He would have liked you," Brannt said, and then seemed surprised at his own sentence. "Ash. He came into the station twice a year on parish business and once over a boundary stone. He had no patience for a man who reached a conclusion before his tea cooled. His words."

"Then the town lost a difficult neighbour and gained a complicated estate," she said. "Let us not disappoint him."

After Brannt had gone to speak with his clerk, Mira stayed at the table with the ledger closed in front of her and let her mind go, against its will, to the other matter the morning had stirred. The box had held too much care in one place and, in Ash's study, she had met the opposite condition in its purest form. [She had stood in a transit room at Carrowmere where a supervisor died and the room said nothing at all](/2026/11/01/nightjar-clean-room-001/). A line on a floor in Melora had divided something from nothing as cleanly as a door divides weather from shelter. A storeroom full of objects, each one old enough to have accumulated the handling of generations, had offered her silence arranged in shelves. None of those encounters had been written down by her in any place where another officer could set them side by side. She turned to a clean page of her own notebook and did that work herself, in pencil, in her slow hand, and thought of the page without planning the words as a private register.

Set down together in one place, the four entries settled the question that each encounter alone had left loose: an absence was not a failure of her attention, because a failure would not take the shape of the room it happened in, and every one of these had taken a shape, an edge, a volume that could be named.

| Place | Room | What was found | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carrowmere bonded wharf | transit room nine | no weight at all where a death had certainly happened | a room's full volume |
| a Melora court building | a room off the service stair | weight stopping and starting at a line on the floor | a volume with one straight edge |
| the Helden Collection in Melora | the north storeroom | no weight among two centuries of objects | a small room's volume |
| Highstile | Ash's study | no weight in the room where the phrase was kept | a study's volume |

She looked at the four lines for a long while. Paper held weight worst of all the anchors she knew, worse than cloth, far worse than stone or iron, and anything left in a place faded in any case, days clearing it, months leaving it faint, years reducing it to almost nothing. A written page was therefore the only kind of keeping that would not fade on that schedule, which was perhaps why Ash had kept a book, whatever kind of book his was, and why she had just started one of her own. The comparison pleased neither side of her mind. She closed the notebook and put it in her coat, and called Brannt back to seal the box again in her presence, with fresh wax and fresh paper, and to sign the inventory line beside her own cramped signature. He lifted the box back to its shelf himself. The ledger slept again in oilcloth and twine, its first forty pages as mild as a parish register and its remaining pages unjudged, personal or operational or a list, with the transfer still to be arranged and the question of a road to Carrowmere still open in front of them both.

## Four Years Apart

The evidence room at Highstile held the cold in its stone even when the small stove in the corner had been lit since before first light. Mira could feel it through the soles of her boots where she stood at the long table, and she could smell it too, damp wool from coats hung by the door, iron from the stove plate, paper dust from shelves that had not been aired in a long season, and the faint sour trace of ink left by many hands signing many forms in the same place. A narrow window high in the wall let in a flat grey light that made no shadows worth trusting. It fell across the ledger where Brannt had set it down inside its box lid, across the constabulary inventory sheets weighted with a chipped mug, and across the telephone that Dunmore would use from Carrowmere, its cord drawn tight to the wall and its receiver still warm from the duty desk. Outside, yard gravel shifted under a boot and then settled. Inside, the room kept its own quiet, the kind that belonged to a building used to waiting for people to finish looking at things they would rather not have found.

Brannt did not hover. He had brought the book in, set the box where she asked him to set it, and taken his place by the shelves with his hands loose at his sides, as if good custody were mostly a matter of not touching what did not need touching. Mira liked him for that. She liked him also for the way he had spoken about the house, without appetite and without apology, as a man speaks about work that has to be done in the right order because the wrong order creates trouble for strangers later. The ledger waited between them. Its binding was dark with handling, the corners softened, the page edges uneven where a thumb had found the same places again and again. It looked like a book that had lived in one room and had rarely been carried anywhere else.

She did not open it with expectation. Expectation had been spent elsewhere, and what she had left was method and a tired attention that she had to guard against drift. The damage she carried from the study still sat behind her thoughts, not as a picture and not as a voice, but as a heaviness that made ordinary patience cost more than it should. Sitting was easier than standing. Reading print was easier than reading weight. She had chosen the order of the morning accordingly, paper before anything else, and Brannt had understood without being told, which was another mark in his favour.

The telephone rang once, short and hard in the stone room, and Brannt answered it, gave his name and station, listened, and handed the receiver to her without comment.

Dunmore, in Carrowmere, had a voice made for such rooms. It carried no wasted warmth over distance and lost none of its precision for being heard through a poor line. She had the photographed sample spread before her, she said, and the constabulary inventory beside it, and a second copy of the inventory that Brannt's clerk had made before the box was sealed, and she had been working since the light came up over the canal with all three under her hands. Mira pictured her there without needing to picture much, a desk in a city that woke earlier than Highstile, windows giving onto water and warehouses, files stacked in the kind of order that looks like untidiness until a stranger tries to move one sheet and learns better.

"You have the book itself," Dunmore said. "I have what your constable thought worth photographing and what his inventory claims the book contains. Between us we should be able to tell whether they agree."

"They will agree in the small places," Mira said. "It is the large agreement I want tested."

"Then we start at the beginning and we do not skip," Dunmore said. "I have no patience left for readers who begin in the middle because the middle looks interesting. Interesting is how mistakes dress themselves."

Mira opened the cover. The first page held a name in a careful hand and nothing that announced purpose. No heading that explained the book to a stranger. No statement of intent. A private book, then, in its presentation at least, and the hand that had written the name had pressed hard enough to leave a shallow groove she could feel when she passed a fingertip lightly above the paper without quite touching the ink. She turned the page with the edge of her hand, the way she had been taught to handle paper that might still have something to say in its fibres if it was not bullied.

The accounts began as accounts were expected to begin. Small sums set down in order. Dates in a steady progression. Entries for coal and for lamp oil and for mending, for postage and for a pair of boots resoled, for tea bought in modest quantity and for a window latch replaced before winter. The hand varied in pressure across the years it covered, sometimes firmer, sometimes thinner, but the habits underneath the hand stayed constant. A man set down what he had spent and what he had received, and he did it soon after the fact, and he left little room in his margins for comment. Where a comment came, it was spare. A note that a price had risen. A note that a delivery had been short. Nothing that reached beyond the household that produced it.

"Fifty pages in the sample and they all read that way to me," Dunmore said when Mira described the opening stretch in low, plain terms. "Coal and cloth and a man counting carefully because counting carefully was the shape of his life. If I had only the sample, I would close the file and write personal on it and feel I had done honest work."

"So would I," Mira said. "Brannt's first clerk did. You can see it in the inventory language. He stops describing after a while because he thinks there is nothing left to describe."

"That is the trap in any long book," Dunmore said. "Sameness teaches the reader to skim. Skimming is where a keeper hides anything he wants hidden, not behind a lock, but behind boredom."

The book covered eighteen years. They established that first by the plain arithmetic of its own dates, first entry to last entry, with no missing span at either end large enough to suggest a second book begun or abandoned. Dunmore had reached the same span from the inventory alone and had not trusted it until Mira confirmed it from the pages themselves. Eighteen years of a solitary household, kept in one hand, in one place, with remarkably few gaps that ordinary life could not explain. Illness explained a short silence in one winter. Travel explained another. A death in the neighbourhood, unnamed and unadorned, explained a week where the sums stopped and then resumed with a purchase of black cloth.

"Hold there," Dunmore said, at a point well past the opening that the clerk had described and stopped describing. "Read that date again."

Mira read it again. Then she read the date before it, and the date after it, because a single date by itself said little and a sequence said more.

"Your sample ends just before that page," Dunmore said. "Your constable photographed a run of pages that were regular enough to put any reader to sleep. Listen to the inventory entry for the same place. He writes, and I will give you his words as he set them down, entries resume after interval. He does not say how long the interval was. He does not say what kind of interval. He had stopped counting the pattern and started summarising it, which tells me he had decided the book was dull."

"It is not dull here," Mira said.

The regularity had broken. For pages and pages the entries had come at intervals a household could predict, weekly reckonings and monthly settlements, the small pulse of coal and food and repair. At this place the pulse missed. Dates gathered where no gathering belonged, then thinned, then stopped for a stretch that no illness note explained, and when they resumed the hand was the same hand and the sums were the same modest kind of sums, yet the order underneath them had changed its step, as if the keeper had returned to his table after attending to something else that had taken precedence over coal.

"One break proves nothing," Dunmore said. "A man falls ill and writes nothing about it. A man goes to sit with a dying sister and keeps no accounts while he sits. A man simply loses the habit for a season and picks it up again out of shame. I have seen all three in papers less tidy than these. One break is life. Keep going."

Mira kept going. Her eyes ached in a way that had nothing to do with print size. She read dates aloud until the line from Carrowmere became the steadiest thing in the room, Dunmore marking and checking on her distant desk, Brannt motionless by the shelves except when the stove needed a nudge and he gave it one without looking away from the table for longer than the task required. The book gave up its length slowly. More coal. More oil. A new pen bought and noted as if the purchase itself had required justification. A roof tile replaced. A sum sent and a sum returned. Ordinary life insisted on its ordinariness page after page, and Mira respected the insistence, because ordinary life was real and a man was entitled to have lived most of his in peace.

The second break came further on, far enough from the first that she felt the distance in her own shoulders as she turned toward it. Again the dates stopped being regular. Again entries clustered and thinned and ceased for a stretch that carried no explanation in the margin. Again the hand resumed as the same hand, careful as ever, as if nothing had interrupted the keeping except the thing that had.

"How far from the first?" Dunmore asked. Her voice had changed in a small way. The change was not excitement. Dunmore did not perform excitement for any audience. It was attention narrowing to a point.

"Far," Mira said, and gave the interval as she had counted it, entry to entry, break to break, keeping her own voice level because level voices made counting more reliable. "Four years."

The line was quiet for a breath. Paper moved in Carrowmere, a sound so faint through the receiver that Mira might have imagined it if she had been inclined to imagine anything that morning.

"One break is life," Dunmore said again. "Two breaks at four years begin to look like a calendar that does not belong to a household."

"Keep it provisional," Mira said. "Two could still be chance."

"Provisional," Dunmore agreed. "I dislike chance that arrives wearing matching boots, but I will keep it provisional until you give me a third."

The third came. Mira knew it before she had finished counting, and the knowledge sat unpleasantly in her chest because she had wanted, in some unprofessional corner of herself, for the book to stay personal and harmless and fileable. The dates gathered in the same wrong way. The entries thinned in the same wrong way. The silence in the book lasted long enough to be a presence, and then the household sums resumed, modest and exact, as if a lid had been set back on a pot. The interval from the second break matched the interval from the first. Four years.

[At the Teshkar depot a chain had been taken apart around an empty room, and nobody had written that down anywhere either](/2026/11/03/nightjar-bonded-cargo-004/). The thought came to her uninvited while she held the page flat with the side of her hand, a memory of another absence handled as if absence were too slight a thing to record, and it sharpened her mistrust of every tidy summary on the inventory sheets. Things that mattered had a way of living in the spaces clerks summarised. Things that mattered also had a way of repeating at intervals too regular to be weather and too sparse to be habit.

"Third," she said into the receiver.

"Four years again," Dunmore said, and it was not a question.

"Four years again."

"Then I will do something I should have done when your constable sent the sample," Dunmore said. "I will stop reading his summaries as evidence and start reading them as a map of where he stopped looking. Give me the places again, in order, from the book itself. I will set them against the pages he photographed and the pages he only listed. If the pattern holds past a third instance, the sample is not a sample any longer. It is an evasion, even if the man who made it never intended evasion."

"My clerk is a good lad with figures," he said, when the pause invited him. "He is less good with boredom. Most of us are."

"Boredom has buried more evidence than malice ever managed," Dunmore said through the line, and Brannt gave a short nod as if she had been in the room to see it.

Across the street from the station, in the upper room of a lodging house whose landlady asked no questions of guests who paid in advance and kept their boots clean, the watcher sat where the window curtain could be drawn to a slit and the yard gate stayed visible without effort. The room smelled of other men's tobacco and of the oil the landlady used on her stairs. Rain from earlier in the week had left the street shining in patches, and every cart that passed threw up a fine spray that darkened the station wall a little further before it faded. He had a cold cup at his elbow and a notebook he did not write in. Writing was for men who expected to be asked to prove they had watched. He expected to report in person, in a room with no paper on the table, to people whose names he had never been given and did not want.

Whatever the Accord recovered would be filed where nothing followed from it. He believed that with the settled confidence of a man who had seen the filing happen. He had stood once, years of service behind him and no rank to show for it, in a corridor outside an oversight registry while a clerk with ink on her cuffs received a bundle concerning a granary fire that had never been a granary fire, and he had watched the bundle receive its label and its number and its place on a shelf behind a door that locked from the outside, and he had understood, without anyone explaining it, that the bundle had not been taken to be acted on. It had been taken so that nobody outside that room could act on it, and so that nobody inside any other room would ever be obliged to try. Oversight took custody precisely so that no working officer ever had to act on what custody contained. A live file in a working office was a risk. It invited questions in the wrong order. It sat on a desk and accused the man at the desk each morning until he either answered it or found a reason to send it away. A file in oversight custody accused nobody. It rested. It was preserved, which was the word they used, as if preservation were the same as honour, and the men and women who had gathered its pages were thanked for their diligence and sent to other work, and the matter ended not with a decision anyone had to sign but with a silence everyone could pretend was completion.

He had seen it happen with a harbour manifest that contradicted a ministerial account. He had seen it happen with testimony taken after a bridge failure, testimony that named a supplier and a payment and a date that matched nothing in the official finding. In each case the paper had been real, and in each case its reality had been the problem, and in each case oversight had solved the problem by receiving the paper into rooms where reality could be stored without consequence. He had no reason to expect a dead man's ledger to receive different treatment, whatever the ledger contained. If it contained nothing, it would be filed and forgotten, which was proper. If it contained something, it would be filed and forgotten, which was the design. The woman in the station could be as diligent as she liked. The constable could be as honest as his reputation suggested. Diligence and honesty ended at the transport door. Beyond that door lay shelves, and labels, and a quiet that had outlasted better arguments than any one officer could make.

He shifted the curtain a fraction with one finger and let it fall again. The yard stayed empty. Nothing in his posture suggested impatience, because patience was the whole of his trade, and he had been paid to learn what conclusions looked like long before they were announced. One conclusion, at least, he already held, and held correctly: the destination of the book mattered more than its contents, and its destination had been decided before anyone in Highstile had opened its cover.

Inside the station, Mira set the receiver down for a moment to rest her hand, and the watcher, who could not see her do it, went on watching the empty yard and expecting the book to be buried with honours.

She lifted the receiver again when Dunmore's voice returned, sharper with the small delays that a busy desk produces. They worked the last stretch of the book together, slower than before, because a pattern once seen creates its own temptation and neither of them intended to feed it. Mira read dates without emphasis. Dunmore repeated them back. Twice they stopped and recounted a month that contained a local fair and a run of small payments to carriers, because fair weeks distorted any household record and a fair distortion had to be ruled out before a break could be counted as a break. Once they set aside a thinning of entries that proved, on close reading, to be nothing more than a change of ink and a cramped hand in cold weather. The discipline steadied her. It also stripped away every remaining comfort.

The fourth break was there. It lay further on in the book, past accounts of a fence mended and a store of apples sold and a contribution given to a collection for a widow whose name was written once and never again. The dates stopped being regular in the same manner as before, neither a sudden cliff nor a gradual fade, but a gathering and a thinning and then a held silence in the entries, and the interval from the third break was four years, as the interval from the second had been four years, as the interval from the first had been four years. Four places. Four years apart. A rhythm laid under eighteen years of coal and cloth so quietly that a reader who wanted a household found a household, and a reader who counted found something else keeping time beneath it.

"Say it plainly," Dunmore said. "If we are going to kill a reading, we kill it in words, not in hints."

"A man's household accounts do not keep a four year rhythm," Mira said.

"No," Dunmore said. "They keep the rhythm of need and season and wages and weather. Four years is the rhythm of something that returns on a schedule set elsewhere, or of something a man checked, or fed, or paid, or met, at an interval he did not choose for domestic reasons."

"Personal dies on that," Mira said. "Whatever else this book is, it was kept against a calendar that is not a household calendar. The opening pages are real. The sums are real. The life is real. It is also a cover of a kind, not necessarily a lie, more like a coat worn every day because the weather requires a coat and because a coat has pockets."

"Operational or list," Dunmore said. "Those are the readings left standing. Operational means he worked from it and it records work, in his own fashion, against orders or beyond them. List means the book is the thing itself, names or dates or rooms gathered in one place, and the accounts are the binding he kept it in. I cannot separate those two from Carrowmere with a sample and an inventory. I doubt anyone could."

"Nor from here," Mira said. "Not by dates alone. The dates give us the rhythm. They do not give us the reason for the rhythm, and I will not invent a reason to make the morning feel finished."

"I will have my clerk write the inventory again," Brannt said. "Not the summaries. The dates, in full, in order, with the gaps left as gaps and no words put over them. If oversight wants prose, oversight can write its own."

"Leave the first inventory as it stands as well," Mira said. "Both. The first shows what a reasonable reader concluded. The second shows what counting finds. A file that hides its own mistakes invites worse ones."

Dunmore approved of that down the line with a sound that was almost amusement. "Send both by the transport," she said. "And mark the four places without marking the pages themselves. I want no ink of ours on his pages. Whatever his book is, it goes up as his book."

When the call ended, the room felt larger and less kind. Mira had the pattern and she had the limits of the pattern, and between them lay the part of the work she had been putting off since she entered the station that morning, not from fear of the book, but from a proper respect for her own depletion. She sat down at the table. Sitting changed the angle of the window light across the page edges, and she could see how uneven they were, how the book had been opened most often at certain places that did not correspond, in any simple way, to the four breaks they had counted. Use and significance were not the same thing. A man opened a book where his thumb found comfort. He kept his secrets where counting found them.

Brannt understood that a different kind of attention had arrived. He moved to the far side of the room without being asked and busied himself with the inventory sheets, giving her the privacy that procedure could offer without leaving her alone with evidence. It was a decent arrangement and she was grateful for its decency.

She set her hands on the closed book first. Binding, then boards, then the page edges with her fingertips resting lightly where another man's fingertips had rested for eighteen years. She did not reach for detail. She did not ask the book a question it could not answer. She let what had settled into it come at its own pace, the way she had learned to let a room speak before she judged whether it had anything worth hearing.

One man's hands and nothing else. That was the first and cleanest finding, and it held as she moved from binding to edges to the slight give of the spine under her palms. No procession of visitors had handled this book. No clerkly rotation, no family curiosity, no second keeper sharing the labour of its keeping. The weight in it belonged to a single sustained presence, repeated across years, returning to the same object in the same spirit, until the object had taken the shape of that returning the way stone takes the shape of water, slowly and without argument.

What that presence carried was dread. It came into her without words and without images, a tightening that belonged to anticipation rather than memory, the feeling of a man approaching a date he expected and did not welcome, year after year, entry after entry, his hand steady on the page and his dread steady underneath the steadiness. It had soaked into the binding where his hand gripped to open. It had settled into the page edges where his thumb waited while he decided how to set a sum down. It was not the sharp dread of a single night. It was the low, managed dread of a life organised around not being found out, or around not failing at a task no one had thanked him for, or around a knowledge that returned at intervals and had to be met each time with the same controlled face. Weight gave her the affect and refused her the rest. It always refused the rest, and she had known it would refuse here, and the refusal still landed as a kind of rebuke.

She turned a few pages under that attention, carefully, not reading print at all, and the dread did not change its character at the four places and did not lift at the household entries and did not gather into anything resembling a statement. It was uniform in the way deep water is uniform. It told her a man had been afraid, in a disciplined and enduring way, for eighteen years. It told her nothing about what had frightened him. It yielded not one word of information. No name rose from the paper. No room arranged itself behind her eyes. No date announced its meaning. The book would not read itself to her, and no practice she possessed could make it do so. She would have to do what Dunmore did with columns and what Brannt did with inventories and what any honest reader did with any difficult text. She would have to turn the pages and count and compare and accept the slowness of that work as the price of knowing anything at all.

The cost arrived as she lifted her hands away, a portion of that dread travelling with her the way such portions always travelled, lodging behind her ribs without permission and without drama. Her own tiredness met it and made room glumly. She breathed until the room was the room again, stove and stone and window light, and the ledger was an object on a table and not a climate she had to live inside. Brannt glanced up once, assessed her colour with a constable's practical eye, and said nothing, which was the correct response and she registered it as such.

"Well?" he said at last, when she had sat back and placed her hands flat on her own knees to keep them from returning to the cover out of habit.

"One man," she said. "Only his hands, over the whole life of the book. Afraid, most of the time, in a way he had learned to carry without spilling. Beyond that, the book keeps its counsel. Weight is affect. It is not information. If we want the rest, we earn it by reading."

Brannt considered that. "Then the four places are what we have."

"The four places are what we have," she said. "And the fact that two readings survive and one does not. I can work with that. I would rather work with a small true thing than a large comforting one."

She reached for the telephone again to give Leyden the shape of it before the transport arrangements hardened the day around them, operational or list, personal dead on the rhythm, the book heavy with dread and silent on everything that mattered most. As her hand closed on the receiver, she found she was reluctant to summarise, not because summarising was difficult, but because a summary sent to Carrowmere would become, somewhere along its journey, a filing, and a filing had a destination she had begun to think about with more care than the morning had first required. The thought did not stop her. It steadied her. A small true thing deserved to be carried carefully, by hands that knew what it weighed and what it refused to say.

## The Station Yard

The evidence room opened directly toward the station yard through a heavy door that Brannt had left ajar on its iron hinge, and through that gap Mira could see the yard in a strip whenever she lifted her head from the page: wet gravel darkened by days of rain, a low stone wall capped with moss, the pump with its handle chained, and beyond the gate the hill road climbing past shuttered houses toward cloud that sat low over Highstile and promised more rain without delivering it yet. She could hear the yard as well as see it, gravel under the boots of Brannt's people as they made their unhurried preparations, the clink of the transport harness being checked in the stable entry, a horse shifting and blowing, the duty clerk calling a name into the front room and receiving an answer she could not make out. Inside, the room kept its stone cold and its smell of paper dust and stove iron, and the table held only what the work allowed it to hold: the ledger, open where she had placed a clean strip of cloth to mark a place without marking a page, her notebook, a pencil worn short from other work, and the box waiting with its lid set square beside it. The transport would come through the gate, the box would go from this door to that door, and between the two doors there was only the yard, open to the street and to any eyes that had taken an interest in the station.

Mira sat because standing had become a negotiation she no longer trusted herself to win cheaply. Low was the honest word for her state, not spent in the grand sense and not broken, but depleted in the ordinary and humiliating way that turned each small task into a sum she had to do before she attempted it. Lifting the ledger had to be planned. Holding a pencil for a long stretch had to be planned. Keeping her attention in a straight line while her hand moved had to be planned most of all, because attention was where the depletion showed first, in a drift toward the window or toward the ache behind her eyes or toward the dread that still travelled with her from the book and from the study before it. She set her feet flat on the stone. She set her notebook where her left hand could steady its edge. She looked at the first of the four irregular entries until the print stopped swimming at the corners, and she began to copy.

She copied by hand because a hand copy was hers in a way no inventory summary could ever be, and because she understood, with a clarity that tiredness had stripped of decoration, what she was doing and what it would mean if anyone chose to name it later. The ledger was bound for oversight. That had been settled at the tasking, by signatures she had not seen placed together before, and she had accepted the settling. What went to oversight would rest where oversight kept such things, and working officers would turn to other work, and the rhythm in this book would become a label on a shelf unless a copy existed in hands that still had reason to count. She was not stealing the book. She was not altering a page or delaying the transport or deceiving the man who stood watching her do it. She was copying three pages, in the evidence room, in Brannt's sight, before the transfer, and the three pages in her notebook would hold the four irregular entries and the facing matter that gave those entries their place in the sequence, dates and sums and spare marginal words set down exactly as Ash had set them down, with the gaps left as gaps and her own observations kept out of the copy entirely.

The copying was slower than she had expected and she had expected slowness. Ash's hand in the irregular places was smaller than his household hand, as if he had written those entries at a different hour or in a different spirit, and she had to lean closer to separate figures that crowded one another without touching. She copied a date, then held it against the page again before she trusted it. She copied a sum, then counted its characters back in silence. Her pencil made a soft, dry sound that filled the room more completely than the noises from the yard, which reached her muffled by stone, and every few lines she stopped and flexed her fingers over the notebook, not shaking them, because shaking wasted what little steadiness she had hoarded, but opening and closing them until the cramp eased enough to continue. The cost of the morning sat in that hand as much as anywhere. It was not an error that made the letters want to wander. It was depletion, plain and measurable in the extra breath she needed at the end of each line.

[On the north cut a lock chamber had held an absence with a ceiling, and the Office had filed that as weather and water](/2026/11/06/nightjar-charge-that-failed-006/). The memory surfaced while she rested her hand at the turn of a page, unconnected to coal or sums except by the habit her mind had formed of filing what it could not explain under whatever heading lay nearest. She let the memory pass without following it. Filing was the danger in this room too, she thought, filing under a heading that closed a matter by naming it too small, and the notebook under her hand was her refusal to let the four places be closed that way before anyone with working responsibility had looked at them with the counting still fresh. She bent again to the page. The second irregular entry began with a date she had already checked twice with Dunmore, and she copied it a third time in her own hand as if her hand, having made the shape of it, would remember it independently of any shelf.

The third page of her copy took the longest. It held the last two of the four entries, close enough in the ledger that Ash had written the second of them with his page turned slightly, as a man turns a book when he writes at a table not his own or when weariness makes him careless of alignment, and she had to copy the facing matter first to be certain which sums belonged to which date. Her eyes kept trying to hurry ahead to the pattern, because a pattern recognised feels finished even when the work that proves it is not, and she pulled them back each time to the single line under her pencil. Line by line was the only honest speed available to her. A horse stamped in the stable entry and a chain rang once against stone, and Brannt's clerk came to the doorway with the transport word at last: the Accord carriage had been seen on the lower road, making the climb at a walking pace, with two attendants and a driver and no escort worth the name. Brannt acknowledged him without looking away from the table. The clerk understood the look and withdrew, and his boots receded across the yard gravel at a pace that suggested he had grasped that hurry, from this point onward, would be a form of carelessness.

Mira copied the final date. She copied its facing sum. She set a small, plain mark of her own at the foot of the third page, not on the ledger and not as a comment, only as a sign to herself that the copy ended where she had intended it to end. Then she read the three pages back against the book, silently, moving her lips only when a figure resisted her, and she found one transposed pair in a sum on the second page and corrected it by rewriting the whole sum beside it rather than scratching at the error, because a copy with a scratched correction invites a reader to wonder what else had been scratched. Satisfied at last, and satisfaction was a modest thing built from checks rather than feeling, she closed the notebook. She closed the ledger with both hands, slowly, as its binding deserved and as her own strength advised. She placed the book in its box exactly as it had rested when Brannt first set it before her, and she watched Brannt set the lid square and make the fastenings in the order his station used, each one checked by touch, each one named under his breath for the clerk who waited again at the door with the inventory in his hands.

The transport came into the yard as transports do in hill towns, with more noise than its size warranted and less ceremony than its errand deserved. Wheels ground over wet gravel. The driver called his horse to a stand before the stable entry rather than before the evidence room door, which was correct, because the box would travel the yard on foot and by hand, as it had arrived, and no wheels would shorten that passage or make it safer. The two attendants came down and presented themselves at the duty desk first, and their names were entered, and their authority was looked at by Brannt with the same unhurried attention he had given the inventory, while Mira stood in the evidence room doorway with the yard open before her and counted, without deciding to count, the places a person could enter it: the main gate still open after the carriage, the stable entry, the narrow passage beside the pump that led to the front of the building and the street. She disliked the openness with a professional dislike that cost her energy she could ill afford, and she spent the energy anyway, because the passage from door to door was the whole remaining task and a task disliked early was cheaper than a task regretted late.

Brannt lifted the box himself. That too was correct, and she understood his reasoning without needing it explained: the hands that had packed it would be the hands that carried it to the handover, and fewer hands meant a cleaner account if any account were ever demanded. She walked on the side toward the gate, not because she could have carried the box in her state, she could not have carried it far or well, but because her place was beside it and because a body beside a box is a fact other bodies must reckon with. One of Brannt's people walked on the other side, a broad young constable whose quiet she had noted at the duty desk, and the second station man fell in behind them with the signed inventory folded in an oilskin wrap against the rain that still threatened. The yard gravel gave under their boots in a rhythm that sounded, to her depleted ear, absurdly like the rhythm she had copied, and she dismissed the fancy as tiredness making music where no music had been offered.

They were halfway across the yard, with the station door behind them and the transport door ahead of them and the box between Brannt's hands as steady as a carried table, when the two people came through the main gate at a pace that announced purpose by trying to disguise itself as errand. One went, without hesitation, for the transport door and set a shoulder and a spread hand against it in the manner of someone claiming the right to be waited for. The other came directly at the box, hands open and visible and reaching not for Brannt but for the box itself, for its nearer handle and the purchase a handle gives a person who intends to turn a carried thing into a contested thing. There was no shout. There was, for a breath, only gravel and the horse blowing in alarm at the suddenness of movement and the driver's voice rising to calm it, and in that breath Mira understood that the attempt had been timed for the one passage in the whole recovery where the ledger belonged to no room, where it was neither shelved nor loaded, where hands were its only address.

She stepped into the reaching hands rather than away from them. She put her forearm across the line of the reach and her body between the stranger and the handle, and she took hold, with the hand that had copied three pages and had little left in it, of a wrist that was stronger than her grip and depended for its effect not on strength but on position. Position held where strength would have failed her. The stranger pulled once against the hold and found that pulling meant pulling the box toward Brannt rather than away from him, because she had set her weight, such as it was, on the side that made the geometry wrong for theft. Brannt, without setting the box down and without raising his voice, told his people what to do in the tone of a man reading a familiar order from a card, and the yard organised itself around his voice the way it had organised itself around his custody all morning.

The broad young constable took the second person at the transport door in a hold that used the door itself as its partner, an arm guided and kept, a body turned by leverage rather than force until its owner faced the gate again and had to choose between the dignity of standing still and the indignity of struggling against a grip that offered nothing to struggle profitably against. The station man from behind the box came around Mira's side and added his hands to hers, not prising and not striking, but completing the control she had begun, one hand over the reaching wrist and the other at the elbow, guiding the arm down and across until the stranger stood bent slightly and securely in a posture designed by generations of patient men for exactly this purpose: to make a person hold still without making the holding into anything larger than itself. Brannt kept the box. That was the centre of the yard and everyone in it, friend and stranger alike, understood that the centre had not moved. His hands stayed where custody had placed them, his stance widened by a fraction to receive any shift in the contest, and his voice continued at its ordinary level, naming the gate, naming the door, naming the next step of the transfer as if the transfer were proceeding in a yard where nothing unusual had been attempted, and the ordinariness of his voice did more to settle the horse and the attendants and the duty clerk at the front door than any command to be calm could have managed.

Brannt made the decision, as the yard was his and the tasking, when its words were recalled honestly, had never been about persons. He had been asked to keep a box and deliver a box. He had not been asked to fill his cells with unnamed strangers whose principals no paper named, and a constable who invents tasks beyond his tasking soon finds he has neglected the task he was given. He said, still at that ordinary level, that the gate was open and the road was the road, and his people understood him, because they had worked under him long enough to hear an instruction inside a remark. The control holds loosened in sequence, the door hold first, so that the person there could step away from the transport with hands free and dignity partly restored, then the hold beside the box, Mira's forearm lifting last of all as the station man's hands guided the stranger back by a full pace and then released the arm they had kept. Nobody pursued. Nobody raised a hand to detain. The two who had come through the gate went out through the gate, at a pace that had lost its disguise and made no pretence of errand any longer, and the road took them the way roads take people in hill towns, quickly around a bend and out of honest sight, while the yard stayed full of people who had work to finish and a horse that had decided, on its own authority, to be calm again.

The transfer resumed where it had been interrupted, which was to say it resumed exactly, step for named step, as if procedure were a rope that could be picked up at the precise point where hands had been forced to tighten on it. Brannt carried the box the remaining distance across the gravel. Mira walked beside it with her tired hand lifted clear and ready and no longer needed. The broad constable opened the transport door that had been claimed so briefly and held it with the air of a man reclaiming furniture. The attendants received the box at the door as Brannt delivered it, hands meeting hands in the overlap that custody requires, no gap in which the ledger belonged to nobody, no moment in which it rested unsupported between one responsibility and the next. The inventory in its oilskin wrap followed it. Signatures were made at the transport, on a board the clerk produced for the purpose, and Brannt signed at his end of the chain as he had signed at the station end, while Mira stood in the yard with the rain beginning at last in a fine, needling fall that darkened the gravel further and spotted the shoulders of her coat. Station door to transport door, the ledger had travelled unbroken, held by procedure and by hands, and the box was secured behind a fastened door before she allowed herself to look toward the gate again and find it empty except for rain.

## The Book Will Not Read Itself

The evidence room held its cold after the door shut. Stone walls kept the chill of the yard, and a small window high in the wall let in light that fell short of the table. The table was scarred pine, dark at the edges from years of inventory work, and it smelled of dust and lamp oil. Mira sat with her coat still on, the fear taken at Ash's house low behind her ribs.

Before her lay three things in a row she had made without thinking. The constable's box sat to her left, lid closed, seal marked in Brannt's careful hand. In the middle, on a single sheet, were the four irregular dates she had checked against the book itself, each separated from the next by the same long interval. To her right lay the three pages copied by hand, the irregular entries and their facing pages, ink dark where her pen had pressed harder.

The telephone stood at the far end of the table. She had not yet asked the desk to place the call to Leyden in Carrowmere, and she kept the silence longer, setting the case in order without defending any part of it aloud.

A household book would have kept a household time. For forty pages the ledger had worn that face well enough to fool any honest reader. Then the dates had loosened in four places, spaced as evenly as posts along a road, four years apart each time. A man did not mislay his accounts on a schedule. He kept something else on that schedule, hidden inside the accounts because they were the one book no one would question. The dread read off the binding told her how that keeping had felt for eighteen years. It told her no name, no room, no reason. For all its weight, the book would not read itself.

The box had its own smaller secret. Someone had been through the contents before her hands touched them, hands that opened, looked, and closed again before Brannt sealed the box. Nothing had been taken, and the care in that handling had been plain in the read. Weight kept the affect and discarded the identity, as it always did. Those hands might have belonged to the second party lingering in Highstile, or to someone within the Accord sent to be certain of what would be recovered. She had no way to choose between them.

Behind both secrets sat the tasking itself. Harrand had tasked the recovery in her own name, and Leyden had countersigned beneath her, which had never happened before on any order Mira had carried. The price had been stated without softening: whatever came out of Ash's house went to oversight custody, not to the Office. Mira would recover a thing she would not be allowed to keep, for people who had not said whether they wanted the same outcome.

Except that she had kept something. The three pages were hers in a way the ledger had never been. Brannt had watched her copy them and said nothing, because copying was forbidden in no instruction she had been given. When she spoke to Leyden she would report the box, the seal and the state of the book, and she would not name the copies in that first conversation.

She reached for the telephone and asked the duty desk to make the connection. While the line clicked in her ear, she folded the pages inside her coat, where they would travel with her and not with the box.

## Into Oversight Custody

Carrowmere received the transport in rain that had turned the canal the colour of slate. From the oversight building steps Mira watched water stipple the dark surface below the embankment wall, heard it tick against the stone coping and against the covered box as two attendants carried it between them from the carriage to the door. The building smelled inside of wet wool, heated dust and the iron tang of its radiators. A clerk had set a lamp burning in the custody room though the afternoon had not yet failed, and its light pooled on a long table wiped clean for the receipt. Nothing else had been left on that table. The emptiness was deliberate, and Mira approved of it before she approved of anything else Harrand had arranged.

The box came in with its cords uncut and its seals unbroken. Brannt had packed it at Highstile under his own authority, and his inventory, written in the same careful hand that had marked the seal, travelled in a sleeve fixed to the lid rather than loose inside with the contents. The senior attendant set the box down at the centre of the table and stepped back at once, as if distance after delivery were part of the procedure. The second attendant laid the carriage log beside it, open at the page where each handover on the road had been entered, time and place and initials in three different inks that still told a single unbroken story.

Mira stood where she could see both the seal and the door. She had ridden with the box from the station yard to this room by stages she could recite without looking at the log. It had left the evidence room in Brannt's hands and hers. It had crossed the yard under the eyes of the station's own people. It had been locked into the transport compartment before the horses were brought round, and that compartment had stayed locked through two halts she had insisted on watching personally, though her body had protested every cold minute of waiting with a weariness that went deeper than muscle. [At Highstile a man had died in his own kitchen while his study, the room where he kept the phrase, read as nothing at all](/2026/11/09/nightjar-last-interview-009/). That emptiness had followed her all the way to Carrowmere, not as a presence but as a refusal, a room shaped hole in everything the ledger was supposed to explain.

Commissioner Ilse Harrand entered without ceremony and without an escort of clerks. She was taller than Mira had remembered from briefings, grey hair drawn back with a severity that suited the office she held, and she wore no insignia that the room did not already confer. Leyden followed her in and took the place by the wall that a controller took when the matter had passed out of his hands and he intended to witness that it had. He nodded to Mira once. He did not speak. His countersignature on the tasking had said whatever he meant to say on paper, and both of them knew the rest would come later and in private.

Harrand read the carriage log before she touched the box. She read it the way Mira liked to see a document read, finger resting below each line, eyes moving once and not returning, and when she reached the final entry she compared its time against the clock above the custody room door and made a small mark of her own in the margin. Only then did she turn her attention to the seals. She examined the wax and the cord knots with a patience that did not perform diligence for the room. She asked the senior attendant one question about the second halt, received an answer that matched the log, and dismissed both attendants with a courtesy that closed the road portion of the chain as cleanly as a signature.

The inventory came out of its sleeve next. Brannt had signed it at the Highstile end, at the foot of the page where his list ended, his name written beneath a line that stated the box had been packed in his station and sealed in his presence. Harrand laid the sheet flat and read every item aloud, not loudly, in the level voice of a woman accustomed to making rooms listen by refusing to raise hers. One bound ledger, heavy, in a single hand, recovered from beneath a drawer in the house of Corwen Ash. One cloth wrapping. One station tag wired to the wrapping, bearing the constable's number for the house. Nothing else was listed, because nothing else had been placed in the box. Mira listened to the short list with her hands folded in front of her and felt the weight of everything the list correctly omitted settle and pass.

Her three copied pages rested inside her coat, folded, against her side where they had travelled from the evidence room. They were named in no inventory. Brannt's list did not name them because they had never been an item in his custody; they were copies made by an officer's hand from a book open on his table, and his inventory described the book, which was its business. The carriage log did not name them because they had not been freight. Harrand's receipt, when she wrote it, would not name them because oversight was receiving the ledger and the ledger alone. Mira stood through the reading with that knowledge held steady and separate, neither hidden in panic nor offered in confession. She had copied the four irregular entries and their facing pages before the handover, in plain sight, under a tasking that had never forbidden it, and the pages were the only portion of Ash's eighteen years that would leave this building with anyone who had actually read the book.

Harrand asked for the box to be opened. The senior clerk who had waited by the inner door came forward with a knife made for cord and did the work under her direction, cutting nothing that could not be accounted for and laying each severed length in a row on the table edge. Inside its wrapping the ledger looked smaller than Mira remembered and more ordinary, which she had learned to distrust in objects that had cost this much to move. The binding was dark and rubbed at the corners. The page edges held the uneven tone of paper handled across years by the same hands, at the same table, in the same poor light, a life narrowed to a ritual and then defended past the point where ritual served any living purpose.

Harrand did not open the book. Mira noted that and stored it with the other facts of the afternoon. The Commissioner satisfied herself with the station tag, with the wrapping, with the visible state of the binding, and with Mira's spoken confirmation that the book inside the box was the book she had examined in the Highstile evidence room, the same weight, the same hand, the same four places where the dates broke their pattern. Her questions were few and exact. She did not ask what the irregular entries meant. She did not ask whether Dunmore in her Carrowmere office, working from the sample and the accounts pattern, had formed a view about names or rooms. Meaning, in this room, was a later and lesser business than custody. Custody was the promise that meaning would still be available to whoever was eventually permitted to look for it.

The receipt took two pages. Harrand wrote the first herself, describing the object, the condition of its seals on arrival, the number of hands through which the official log showed it had passed, and the unbroken character of that passage. On the second page she signed at the Carrowmere end of Brannt's inventory, below his signature and on the same sheet, so that the inventory was signed at both ends and held the whole journey between two names. The station that had packed the box and the oversight office that had received it faced each other on one piece of paper, and nothing in between them lacked a witness. Leyden countersigned the receipt as the tasking officer, his pen moving without hesitation, and the sound of it was very small in the quiet room.

Mira signed where she was directed to sign, as the recovering officer, below them both. Her signature looked tired to her own eye, the letters formed with more care than grace, but it was legible and it was hers. She thought of Ash forming his entries year after year with a steadiness she could not have matched even before his fear had taken up residence in her. She thought of the watcher logic Dunmore had reported from the edges of the case, the calm expectation that oversight took custody of inconvenient paper precisely so that no working officer would ever have to act on it, that the book would be filed where nothing followed from it. The expectation might prove right. Mira had watched the Accord bury paper before, in drawers that were also a kind of grave. Yet a burial with a signed receipt at both ends was not the same act as a disappearance, and the difference mattered to her even if it never mattered to anyone else. What had been buried without a record could be denied. This book, whatever room it slept in, could be summoned by its chain.

The inner custody door was opened then, the one that led from the receipt room to the strong room where oversight kept the objects it did not intend to circulate. It was a plain door made heavy by its construction, and it moved on hinges that had been oiled by someone who understood that silence in such a mechanism was a form of respect. Harrand carried the ledger herself, rewrapped in its cloth, the station tag still wired in place, and Mira understood that this too was procedure of a personal kind. The woman who had tasked the recovery in her own name would complete it in her own hands. No clerk took the weight from her. No attendant followed her through.

Reni Vosk waited by the inner door with the strong room key on its cord, junior enough to be given the holding tasks and steady enough, Mira had observed on the road paperwork, to hold them without fidgeting. For a moment the girl's gaze moved from the wrapped book to Mira's face, and Mira saw her register the plain fact of the transfer and file it in the private ledger every young officer kept of how the world actually worked. Reni would remember this room. She would remember that a thing could be recovered at cost, carried under seal, and then surrendered whole to a locked door, and that the officer who recovered it walked away with empty hands that were not quite empty. Whatever the private register of this case became over time, in files or in memory or in the notebook habits of the people who had touched it, some portion of it would pass through Reni's keeping eventually. Institutions forgot through their juniors as often as they remembered through them, and Mira found she trusted this particular junior's attention more than she trusted the strong room door.

The ledger went onto a shelf that held few other objects, each separated from its neighbours by a width of empty wood that spoke of categories too sensitive for company. Harrand set it down squarely, binding outward, tag visible, and stood a moment with her hand resting flat on the cloth. The gesture was not reverence. It looked to Mira like verification by touch, a last confirmation that the thing was solid, present and accounted for, after a case in which the most important room had offered nothing to any sense she possessed. Then Harrand withdrew her hand, stepped back, and let Reni lock the strong room with a key turned twice.

Back in the custody room the paperwork closed over the event like water over a stone. The clerk sanded the receipt, made the copy for the Office that the tasking allowed, and blotted Brannt's twice signed inventory before filing the original where Leyden indicated. The empty box remained on the table, cords cut, seals preserved in an envelope Harrand had labelled herself, because even the evidence of an unbroken chain was part of the chain and would be kept as long as the object was kept. Mira answered the last formal questions in the order they were asked. The seals had been intact on departure. The seals had been intact on arrival. She had no addition to make to the inventory. Each answer was short, and each was complete within the limits of the form that asked it.

When it was finished, Harrand dismissed the room with a nod that included Mira and excluded any suggestion that gratitude formed part of oversight business. Leyden lingered by the door long enough to tell her, in the tone he used for matters that were not orders, that he would speak with her after she had rested. She accepted that in the same tone. The three pages inside her coat made no sound as she moved, and no one in the custody room looked at her coat at all.

Mira went down the oversight steps into rain that had eased to a fine mist over the canal. The transport carriage had already gone from the yard, its work complete, its log closed. Behind her the building held a bound book in a locked room, received, receipted and secured, its custody chain running unbroken from a constable's table in Highstile to a shelf in Carrowmere with every link signed. The case that had begun with a search for anything of Ash's that had survived the burning ended there, with the object secured and the recovery paid in full. She walked toward the Office through the wet streets with her hands free and the copied pages dry against her side, carrying the only part of the ledger that oversight had never been given and the only part she had ever intended, from the evidence room onward, to keep.

## Four Lines in a Notebook

Her room above the Office annexe was cold in the particular way of rooms that had stood empty through a working day. Rain had left the window glass beaded, and the canal below carried the lamps of the embankment in broken lines. Mira lit the small lamp on the desk and left her coat on the chair back, within reach, rather than hanging it by the door. The desk drawer stood open. It was empty except for string, a broken seal stamp and paper she had never found a use for.

The notebook had come from the Office stores that afternoon, issued without a question because notebooks were beneath questions. Reni Vosk had brought it up with the receipt copies, a slim volume bound in grey cloth, its pages unruled, and had set it on the desk without noticing anything else. Whatever this notebook became, its first witness had already carried it up the stairs, and Reni tended to remember such deliveries accurately.

She had not planned the register as an act of defiance. It had formed the way Ash's ledger must have formed, from the discovery that the official record had a shaped gap in it and that the gap would widen each time a case closed around a thing no form could hold. The Office files recorded a death in a Carrowmere transit room and said nothing about the room itself. The Melora file reduced a line walked in a building to an anomaly in a margin. The Helden report listed objects across two centuries and never mentioned that the storeroom had offered her nothing at all. The Highstile papers would record a study and a phrase, and the emptiness of that study would survive only as long as her memory carried it. Four absences, each witnessed by her body, each filed by the institution as nothing worth a heading.

She wrote them in her own hand, slowly, because the hand mattered as much as the words. Handwriting stayed personal, an admission that the keeper had chosen to keep. The first line named the Carrowmere transit room where a supervisor had died and the room had held no weight at all. The second named the room off the service stair in the Melora building, where weight had stopped and started again at a straight line on the floor. The third named the north storeroom of the Helden Collection, empty among objects that should have crowded any reading. The fourth named Ash's study at Highstile, the room where the phrase had been kept and where she had found the same refusal she had been meeting, case after case.

Leyden came after the lamps along the embankment had steadied. He knocked once and entered with the restraint of a man who had decided before climbing the stairs what he would say and what he would leave for another occasion. He saw the notebook open on the desk. To his credit, Mira thought, he read only enough of the page to understand its nature, and his gaze did not travel down the lines as if they were a report owed to him.

He told her to stop keeping it. The words were plain and they were not unkind. A register of her own, kept outside the files, would become a liability if oversight ever learned that she maintained a private account of anomalies her superiors had declined to record. He said Ash had kept a book against orders for eighteen years and the keeping had eaten the life it was meant to protect. He asked her to let the four rooms go the way the files had let them go.

Mira agreed to his face. She said she would stop keeping it, and she said it evenly, with her hands still, looking at him as she had through every debrief he had ever directed. The agreement was complete in the moment she made it and false in the same moment. She understood, with a clarity that hurt more than the fear she still carried from Ash's house, that it was the first time she had lied to him. Other silences between them had been professional, matters of tasking and timing. This was a simple lie offered to the person who had countersigned her work, and she offered it without flinching.

Leyden believed her, or he chose the belief as his own kind of mercy, and the conversation moved for a few minutes to rest, to food, to the hour she would report in the morning. When he left, the room seemed colder for the ordinary quality of their parting. She did not call him back. She did not open the desk drawer and place the notebook inside it as a surrendered thing, and she did not cross out the lines she had written while the lie was still warm in the room. The cost of it stayed where she had put it, carried quietly, neither confessed nor undone, a weight with an anchor she could name even if she would name it to no one else.

She read the four lines once more to be certain each room was given its due and no room was given more than she knew. Then she closed the notebook on the plain record of four places where the world had declined to leave its mark, in a hand that anyone who knew her work would recognise as hers alone. Four lines in a notebook, in her own hand, and the notebook going into a coat pocket rather than a drawer.

## The Case File

### Q: What does Accord evidence custody require of the officer who accepts a boxed item?

Accord evidence custody begins when a named officer accepts an item under written authority and signs for it before the person who surrenders it. From that signature the accepting officer answers for the item, for the condition of its seal, and for every movement entered against it until a further signature discharges the responsibility. Custody under the Accord is personal before it is institutional, so a gap in the record attaches to the last signer rather than to the service at large. The item is kept apart from working papers, is opened only under the conditions named in the authority, and is produced on demand to the body that imposed the custody. An officer who can produce neither the item nor a complete record of its movements has failed in custody, whatever explanation is offered afterward in mitigation.

### Q: What may oversight remove from Office custody, and by what instrument does a removal take effect?

Oversight may remove any paper, book, or object that the Office holds under Accord authority where the matter touches the conduct of the Office itself, the handling of a sensitive file, or the interests of more than one member service. Removal takes effect by a written direction signed by the Commissioner or by an officer acting under a recorded delegation, and the direction names the items, the reason in general terms, and the place to which custody passes. The Office may record an objection on the file, and the objection is preserved with the direction, but an objection does not delay the transfer. Once the direction is served and receipt is signed at the receiving end, Office custody ends in full, and any copy retained against the direction is treated as an unauthorised copy.

### Q: How does the Highstile constabulary keep its evidence room, and who may enter it while on duty?

The Highstile constabulary keeps its evidence room as a locked room within the station, separate from the duty desk and from the cells, with a single key held by the constable on duty and a duplicate held sealed by the senior constable of the station. Entry is limited to the constable on duty, the senior constable, and a person admitted in the company of one of those two for a recorded purpose connected with an item held there. Every entry is written in the room log at the time it occurs, with the item concerned named by its box mark or register reference. Nothing is placed in the room, and nothing is taken from it, without a corresponding entry. The arrangement is modest in scale, suited to a town station, but its discipline is treated as equal in standing to that of a larger store.

### Q: What must a field register contain before it will pass inspection, and how long is it retained afterward?

A field register will pass inspection only where each entry gives the time, the place, the persons present, the action taken, and the custody state of any item handled during that entry, all written in the order in which events occurred. Entries made later than the events they describe must be marked as late entries, with the reason for the delay stated in the margin and initialled by the officer concerned. Blank spaces are ruled through rather than left open, and no page may be removed from a bound register for any reason. After inspection the register passes to the registry, where it is retained for the full retention span set for case papers of its class. Destruction at the end of that span requires a registry certificate, which is kept after the register itself has gone.

### Q: What standing does a note written by an officer for private keeping hold under Office rules?

A note written by an officer for private keeping has no standing as a record, and the Office will neither cite it nor accept it as evidence of any fact in a proceeding. Its position is narrower than that simple statement suggests, because the rules treat such a note as the officer's own property only while its contents remain private in fact. Where a private note records matters learned in service, the Office may require the officer to declare that the note exists, though it may not compel production of the pages merely on suspicion. If the note is produced voluntarily, or is relied on by the officer in any statement, it enters the file at that moment and is thereafter handled as a file paper. An undeclared note discovered after a related case has closed is treated as a disciplinary matter in its own right.

### Q: What does a constabulary inventory record at the moment a box is packed?

A constabulary inventory records the box itself before it records anything placed inside it, giving the box mark, the material of the box, the fastening used, and the condition of any earlier marks upon it. Against each item packed the inventory gives a short plain description, the place within the premises where the item was found, and the name of the person who found it and the person who placed it in the box. Items found together are entered together, and an item found apart from the place its character would suggest is entered with that circumstance stated in words. The inventory closes with the time of packing and the names of all persons present while packing was done. Nothing is added to a closed inventory except by a further entry, separately signed and separately timed.

### Q: What is settled when an inventory is signed at packing and again at receipt?

A signature at packing settles that the items listed were present, in the condition described, when the box was closed, and it binds the signer to that account against any later dispute about what the box contained at the start of its journey. A signature at receipt settles the corresponding account at the end of the journey, confirming that the box arrived with its seal and fastening as described and that the contents, where they have been checked, answer to the packing list. Between the two signatures the inventory is the standing measure of the transfer, and any difference between the ends must be explained on the face of the document rather than by separate correspondence. Where both signatures stand without qualification, the transfer is treated as complete and regular, and a later challenge must attack the inventory itself.

### Q: In custody terms, what is a copy made by hand, and how far does its authority run?

In custody terms a copy made by hand is a working paper created by the person who made it, carrying no authority beyond that person's attestation that the copying was done faithfully from an original seen at a stated place and time. It is not a substitute for the original in any proceeding, and it cannot be produced to prove the contents of a document that the holder declines to produce or cannot produce. Its value lies in preservation and in reference, since a hand copy may guide an inquiry toward the original and may hold wording that would otherwise be lost. The copy must state on its face that it is a hand copy, name the original by its register reference or box mark, and give the span of pages copied. A hand copy presented without those particulars is given no weight at all.

### Q: What follows when a box seal is found opened before receipt has been signed?

When a box seal is found opened before receipt has been signed, the receiving officer must refuse the ordinary receipt and record the condition of the seal, the fastening, and the box itself in the presence of the person presenting the box. The box is not opened further, and its contents are not examined, until a senior officer of the receiving service or a representative of the sending service can attend. A qualified receipt may then be given, stating the defect in terms, so that custody can proceed without any party being taken to have accepted the box as sound. The sending service is notified by the fastest regular means, and the last signer in the chain is required to account for the interval during which the seal was broken. Until that account is accepted, the contents are held as compromised for evidentiary purposes.

### Q: Who may open an evidence room log, and what does an entry in such a log bind?

An evidence room log may be opened by the officer responsible for the room, by a senior officer of the same service on recorded business, and by an inspector acting under written authority that names the room and the span of the log to be examined. No other person may open the log, and a log left open on a desk or read aloud to an unauthorised person is treated as a breach of room discipline. An entry in the log binds the service that keeps the room, because the log is the service's own account of who entered, at what time, and for what item. Where the log and another record disagree, the disagreement is entered in both records and signed by the keepers of each. A missing entry is not supplied from memory at a later date, since a reconstructed entry would conceal the failure the log exists to expose.

### Q: How are the working books of a retired officer dealt with in the retirement record?

The retirement record deals with working books by class rather than by title, entering how many books were surrendered, how many were certified as destroyed under supervision before retirement took effect, and how many were declared as private books that had never held service matter. Each class entry is signed by the retiring officer and countersigned by the officer who received the surrender, so that the record answers the custody question without describing the contents of any book. A book that falls into dispute between classes is held by the Office pending a determination, and the holding is noted with the book identified by its binding and mark. Where a working book comes to light after the record has been closed, the record is reopened for that item alone, as a fresh proceeding rather than an alteration of the closed entries.

### Q: Once an object has entered oversight custody, may the Office recall it, and on what terms?

Once an object has entered oversight custody the Office has no right of recall, and a request from the Office for the return of the object is treated as an application rather than as an instruction. Oversight may grant the application in whole or in part, may lend the object back for a stated purpose under a fresh signature at each movement, or may refuse it without giving reasons beyond the fact of refusal. While the object remains in oversight custody the Office may not inspect it, copy it, or take a read of it except by permission recorded in the oversight register. The reason for the severity of the position is stated in the standing direction: custody was taken so that questions about the object would be settled by the body responsible for the Office itself. The Office retains only its inventory reference and the receipt given at transfer.

### Q: What is a personal notebook in Office terms, and how is it treated when a case turns on its pages?

In Office terms a personal notebook is a book kept by an officer outside the register system, in the officer's own hand, for the officer's own use, and never issued, numbered, or inspected by the Office in the ordinary course. The Office recognises the category because officers have always kept such books, and its rules govern the consequences rather than the keeping. When a case turns on pages of a personal notebook, the notebook changes character for those pages, and the officer may be required to produce them, to have them copied under supervision, and to answer for the interval between the events recorded and the production of the book. Pages outside the matter in issue remain private, and the file copy is limited accordingly. An officer who destroys such a notebook after learning that a case may turn on it commits a serious breach of discipline.

### Q: For how long does a town constabulary retain a sealed box that no service has claimed?

A town constabulary retains a sealed box that no service has claimed for the retention span fixed by the town authority for unclaimed custody, and during that span the box stays in the evidence room under its original seal with its inventory attached to the room log. The constabulary may not open the box to hasten a claim, and it may not lend the box, or permit it to be viewed, on the strength of an informal request from any service. At stated intervals the constabulary gives notice in the ordinary town form that unclaimed boxes are held, describing each box by its mark and the premises it came from, without describing the contents. When the span expires unclaimed, disposal follows the direction of the town authority and is recorded against the original inventory, which is retained after the box itself has gone.

### Q: What does a break in a chain of custody do to the standing of the item concerned?

A break in a chain of custody does not destroy the item, and it does not by itself prove that the item was altered, substituted, or read by unauthorised hands during the interval concerned. What a break destroys is certainty, and certainty is the quality on which the standing of the item depends in any later proceeding. From the point of the break onward, every assertion about the item must be supported by something outside the chain itself, such as an inventory made before the break or the agreement of the item with an independent account. Assertions that rest only on the chain fail with it. The service responsible for the interval in which the break occurred bears the burden of the failure in discipline terms, even where no harm to the item can be shown, because the chain exists to make such proof unnecessary.

### Q: What does the retirement record of Corwen Ash settle, and what does it leave open?

The retirement record of Corwen Ash settles the formal close of his service, entering the span he served, the surrender of Office papers and instruments made at his final posting, and the classes of books dealt with under the retirement proceeding. It also settles his entitlement position and the address at Highstile to which Office correspondence concerning his record was thereafter directed. What the record leaves open is everything that stood outside the proceeding itself. It does not certify that no paper remained in his hands, because no retirement record can prove a negative of that kind, and it does not describe any private book, account, or memorandum kept in a personal capacity. Where a later inquiry concerns material found at his house, the retirement record is a starting point for custody questions rather than an answer to them.

### Q: How does the duty desk at a station such as Highstile record the arrival of a visiting service?

The duty desk records the arrival of a visiting service in the station day book, giving the time of arrival, the names and service of the persons presenting themselves, the authority they produce, and the business they state in general terms. The constable on desk duty examines the authority, notes its form and any countersignature it carries, and enters the name of the station officer to whom the visitors are conducted. Departures are recorded in the same manner, so that the day book shows the whole span of the visit without relying on the recollection of any person present. A visitor who declines to produce authority is recorded as an unaccredited caller and is not admitted to the evidence room or to any sealed premises under station control. The day book is a town record, and a visiting service has no right to amend it.

### Q: Who inspects a field register after it is filed, and what may an inspector require to be amended?

A field register is inspected by the registry officer assigned to its class of case, and in sensitive matters by an inspector appointed by oversight, who examines the filed original rather than any copy made for working purposes. The inspector compares the register against the report, the custody entries for any item named in it, and the day records of any other service whose officers appear in its pages. An inspector may require a late entry to be marked as such, an unsigned correction to be signed by the officer who made it, and an omitted custody movement to be entered with its omission explained. An inspector may not require the substance of an entry to be rewritten, and a dispute about substance is settled by a further signed entry recording the disagreement. The inspection closes with a certificate on the register itself.

### Q: What conditions govern copying by hand inside an evidence room?

Copying by hand inside an evidence room is governed first by the authority under which the room was opened, since a visitor admitted to inspect an item is not thereby admitted to copy it. Where copying is permitted, it is done at the room table, in the sight of the officer responsible for the room, and within the span of the recorded entry that admitted the copier. The copier uses the copier's own book or loose sheets declared at entry, and the number of pages copied is entered in the room log before the copier leaves. Material copied is limited to the pages named in the permission, and copying beyond those pages is treated as unauthorised copying even where the additional pages lay open beside the permitted ones. The existence of the copy, and the occasion of its making, are therefore matters of record.

### Q: How is custody transferred when a constabulary hands a sealed box to an Accord transport?

Custody passes at the moment the transport officer signs the receipt against the constabulary inventory, and until that signature is given the box remains in constabulary custody even while it stands in the station yard or on the transport itself. Before signing, the transport officer examines the seal and fastening with the constable who packed the box, or with the constable holding the room key, and any defect is dealt with by qualified receipt rather than by delay at the yard. The constabulary entry recording the handover gives the time, the names of both signers, and the destination named in the transport authority. From the signature onward the constabulary answers only for the period of its own holding, and later questions are directed to the chain that begins with the transport receipt. A handover completed without the inventory present is treated as irregular.
