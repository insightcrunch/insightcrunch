The Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded at Delhi on 21 October 1951, was the first party to carry Hindutva into sustained electoral competition, and the twenty-six years between that founding and the Janata merger of 1977 constitute the first sustained test of whether a cadre-based Hindu nationalist movement could operate inside the democratic-electoral architecture of the Indian republic. The standard treatment of the phase reads it backwards from 1980: the Jana Sangh as the Bharatiya Janata Party's organisational antecedent, a preliminary body whose meaning lies in the successor it prepared. The record of the phase supports a different and stronger claim. The Bharatiya Jana Sangh phase produced four institutional patterns the post-1980 BJP inherited: cadre-electoral organisational separation, regional-base building in the Hindi belt, a coalition-engagement architecture for post-poll alliances, and an ideological-positioning balance between Hindutva commitment and electoral-democratic engagement. The phase was institutional-experimental, not merely preparatory, and the experiment, rather than the electoral arithmetic, is what the successor party needed and took.

## Two Readings of a Twenty-Six-Year Phase

The antecedent reading has distinguished sponsors. Bruce Graham's *Hindu Nationalism and Indian Politics*, published by Cambridge University Press in 1990, remains the canonical specialist treatment of the party, and its subtitle, *The Origins and Development of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh*, announces the frame: the Jana Sangh as an origin, studied for what it originated. Christophe Jaffrelot's *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics*, published in 1996, distributes the Jana Sangh decades inside a movement history whose narrative gravity pulls toward the movement's later electoral ascent. Craig Baxter's *The Jana Sangh: A Biography of an Indian Political Party*, published in 1969 while the party still existed, is exempt from the backward pull, and Baxter's contemporary account records a party arguing about its own direction with no knowledge of 1980. Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle, in *The Brotherhood in Saffron* of 1987, approached the phase from the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh side, as one panel in the Sangh Parivar's organisational expansion. Each treatment is valuable, and the differences among them mark the disagreement this article adjudicates. The antecedent reading asks what the Jana Sangh led to. The institutional-experimental reading asks what the Jana Sangh did, phase by phase, and what the doing deposited.

Baxter's position inside the disagreement deserves emphasis because it supplies the experimental reading with a contemporary witness who cannot be suspected of hindsight. Writing in 1969, with the party's future entirely open, Baxter organised his biography of the Jana Sangh around its organisation, its ideology and its leadership problems, the categories of a going concern, and he recorded the party's own internal arguments about coalition behaviour and RSS relations as live disputes among men who expected to go on disputing them. Graham, writing with 1980 in view, could treat those same disputes as waypoints in a development whose terminus he knew. Jaffrelot, writing in 1996 with the BJP's ascent under way, redistributed the weight again. The experimental reading's methodological claim is simple: the men of 1954, 1965 and 1967 were solving the problems in front of them, and an account that begins from their problems recovers deposits that an account beginning from 1980 prices at scrap value. The article therefore follows Baxter's categories while disputing, on the later evidence, his relative pessimism about the party's capacity to convert organisation into endurance.

The distinction matters because the two readings weigh the same evidence on different scales. On the antecedent scale, the phase's electoral record is the headline: five general elections between 1952 and 1971, a peak of 35 Lok Sabha seats in 1967 on 9.31 percent of the national vote, a party that never held office at the centre in its own name and dissolved its separate existence into the Janata Party in 1977. Weighed on that scale, the phase is a modest prelude. On the experimental scale, the headline is a sequence of institutional problems posed and answered: how a movement with a disciplined cadre and no electoral machine builds one; how a party survives the death of a founder of national stature twenty months after its founding; how a doctrinal identity is codified so that it can survive leadership churn; how a party of pronounced ideological identity enters governments with socialists and communists without dissolving into them; and how a party can merge into a larger party, serve in its government, and emerge with its cadre intact when the larger party breaks. The Jana Sangh answered each of those problems between 1951 and 1977, and the answers, codified as working patterns, passed to the BJP at the refounding of April 1980.

The evidence base for the experimental reading is documentary and dated. The founding documents of October 1951 record the design choices of the founding convention in Delhi. The Election Commission of India's statistical reports for the general elections of 1951-52, 1957, 1962, 1967 and 1971 record the party's vote and seat returns constituency by constituency. The state archives of Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar preserve the records of the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalition governments of 1967 to 1971, an under-cited primary source that documents the coalition experiments in operational detail. The Janata Party's founding documents of early 1977 record the terms on which the Jana Sangh dissolved itself into a four-party merger. A researcher working through these records in digital form, with the statistical reports open beside the party documents, will often read the scanned statistical tables through a general-purpose [office file viewer for spreadsheets, documents and presentations](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), because the Election Commission's returns reward exactly that kind of side-by-side tabular scrutiny. Read in sequence, the records show a party learning in public, at the cost of visible failures, and banking the lessons in organisational form.

The claim defended across the sections that follow is therefore precise. The Jana Sangh phase is misread when it is measured only as preparation, because preparation implies that the phase's value was exhausted by the successor's arrival. The four patterns were not preparations. They were solutions to standing problems of Indian electoral politics, produced under the pressure of specific dated episodes: the founding design of October 1951, the succession crisis opened by Syama Prasad Mukherjee's death in detention at Srinagar on 23 June 1953, the doctrinal codification completed under Deendayal Upadhyay by 1965, the coalition governments of 1967 to 1971, and the merger and dual-membership crisis of 1977 to 1979. The BJP of the 1980s did not invent its organisational separation from the RSS, its Hindi-belt base strategy, its post-poll coalition method or its calibrated ideological presentation. The BJP inherited all four from the Jana Sangh's experiment, and the inheritance is traceable episode by episode.

## The Bharatiya Jana Sangh Founding Design of October 1951

The founder requires exact statement. Syama Prasad Mukherjee, born in 1901, had been the youngest vice-chancellor of the University of Calcutta, president of the Hindu Mahasabha from 1944 to 1946, and Minister for Industry and Supply in Jawaharlal Nehru's first cabinet from August 1947. On 8 April 1950, Nehru and Pakistan's prime minister Liaquat Ali Khan signed the Delhi Pact on minority protection, and Mukherjee resigned from the cabinet in protest, holding that the pact failed the Hindu minority of East Pakistan and that the government had surrendered the only leverage, reciprocal pressure, that might have protected it. The resignation made Mukherjee the most senior political figure in India outside the Congress, a former cabinet minister with a national reputation, a parliamentary following and no party. The Jana Sangh was built to give that position an electoral vehicle.

The building proceeded through 1951 in stages that the founding documents preserve. A Bengal unit was organised under Mukherjee's own hand in April 1951. Balraj Madhok organised the Punjab and Delhi units in May 1951. State-level committees in the United Provinces, Madhya Bharat, Rajasthan and elsewhere preceded the national founding, so that the Delhi convention of 21 October 1951 ratified a federation of existing units rather than conjuring a party from a platform. The convention elected Mukherjee president and adopted the party's first programme, an eight-point platform whose planks joined national integration, a strong defence posture and the abrogation of Article 370 to economic swadeshi, land reform within ceilings, and the protection of refugees from Pakistan. The party's symbol, the deepak or earthen lamp, was chosen for the ballot. The first plenary session followed at Kanpur in December 1952.

### What did the founding design of October 1951 actually test?

The founding design tested whether a Hindu nationalist cadre movement could contest democratic elections through a formally separate party, neither dissolving into the parent movement nor severing the cadre supply that staffed every constituency. The Jana Sangh was constituted as an open electoral party; the RSS remained a non-electoral cadre body; deputed personnel were expected to hold the boundary.

The design's load-bearing element was the loan of RSS pracharaks, full-time organisers, to staff the new party's apparatus. Deendayal Upadhyay, an RSS pracharak from Uttar Pradesh, became the party's organising secretary and then its general secretary, the office he held for sixteen years. Atal Bihari Vajpayee, another pracharak, edited the party's journals and entered Parliament within the decade. The arrangement answered the question every new Indian party of the period faced: where do full-time workers come from. Parties built on notables, the Swatantra Party of 1959 being the clearest case, could recruit leaders faster than the Jana Sangh and could not match its constituency-level staffing. Craig Baxter, writing in 1969 from close observation, treated the pracharak supply as the party's defining organisational fact, the feature that separated the Jana Sangh from every other opposition party of the first two decades. Andersen and Damle, in *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987), documented the same arrangement from the Sangh side: [the RSS cadre architecture as institutional support](/2019/09/13/kb-hedgewar-founding-rss-1925/) was not an informal sympathy but a standing personnel system, in which the Sangh's sarsanghchalak, M. S. Golwalkar, approved the deputation of organisers to the political affiliate.

The separation half of the design was equally deliberate, and its terms were contested from the start. Mukherjee insisted that the Jana Sangh was an open party, that its membership was available to any Indian who accepted its programme, and that the RSS connection was a source of workers rather than a chain of command. Golwalkar's public position held the RSS apart from party politics as an organisation, even as individual swayamsevaks staffed the party. The friction latent in that double claim, a party autonomous in form and dependent in personnel, was the founding design's central tension, and the phase's institutional history is in large part the record of that tension being managed, tested and renegotiated: in the succession crisis of 1954, in the Madhok expulsion of 1973, and in the dual-membership crisis that broke the Janata Party in 1979. Graham's 1990 treatment judged the separation a fiction in the party's early years, pointing to the RSS provenance of the post-1953 leadership. Jaffrelot's 1996 account gave the separation more substance over time, as the party's electoral engagements generated interests of its own. The experimental reading sides with a version of Jaffrelot's position for a defined reason: whatever the separation's sincerity in 1951, the party's behaviour across the four phases repeatedly defended the boundary when defending it was costly, which is the behaviour of an institution that treats the boundary as real.

The founding design also fixed the party's ideological presentation. Mukherjee's Jana Sangh was not the Hindu Mahasabha under a new name. The Mahasabha, in its statements of the 1940s, had framed its politics in explicitly religious terms and had drifted into electoral insignificance, winning four Lok Sabha seats in 1952 and fading thereafter. The Jana Sangh's programme spoke of Bharatiya culture and national integration, demanded a uniform civil code and the abrogation of Article 370 as measures of equal citizenship, and pitched its economics at swadeshi production and the protection of small industry. Critics in the Congress and the communist parties charged that the vocabulary was a relabelling, and the charge, at its strongest, held that a party staffed by the RSS and led by a former Mahasabha president was the Mahasabha's politics with better organisation. Defenders, then and in the later scholarship, answered that the programmatic differences were substantive: an open membership, a constitutional route to every stated objective, and an economic programme that owed nothing to the Mahasabha's record. The dispute is marked here as disputed, because both characterisations were held by named contemporaries and both can be documented from the period's texts. What the experimental reading adds is a behavioural test: the party's conduct in coalition governments and in the Janata merger, examined in the later sections, repeatedly subordinated doctrinal display to institutional persistence, a pattern the Mahasabha never exhibited.

The founding, finally, was geographically specific. The units organised in 1951 sat in Bengal, Punjab, Delhi, the United Provinces, Madhya Bharat and Rajasthan, a northern and central arc that reflected the RSS's own pre-1947 strength and the refugee politics of Partition in Punjab and Bengal. No southern unit of consequence existed at the founding, and the party's southern weakness persisted across the whole phase, a limitation that the regional-base pattern, examined later in this article, turned into a deliberate strategy of depth over spread. The founding design, in sum, specified a separate electoral party, a cadre supply line, an integrationist programme in cultural vocabulary, and a northern base. Each specification was a hypothesis about how electoral Hindutva could operate. The twenty-six years that followed were the test.

## The Programme in Detail: Integration, Swadeshi and the Open Party Claim

The eight-point programme adopted at the founding, and the manifestos that elaborated it for the elections of 1952, 1957, 1962, 1967 and 1971, reward close reading because they fix what the party asked voters to endorse before the apparatus and the doctrine existed to interpret the asking. On the constitution, the programme demanded the full integration of Jammu and Kashmir, the abrogation of Article 370, a uniform civil code for all citizens, and a ban on cow slaughter enacted at the centre. On the economy, it demanded swadeshi production, the protection of village and small industry against both foreign imports and domestic monopoly, land to the tiller within ceilings, and the abolition of zamindari without compensation disputes left festering. On foreign policy, the programme demanded a hard line toward Pakistan and China, full diplomatic relations with Israel, and an exit from what the party's resolutions called the Congress's tilt toward the Soviet bloc. On the refugee question that dominated its Bengal and Punjab bases, it demanded the rehabilitation of the displaced from Pakistan as a charge on the state and a reciprocal policy toward minorities that mirrored the two governments' treatment of their own.

Two features of this programme matter for the experimental reading. The first is that every demand was framed as a measure of equal and uniform citizenship, actionable through Parliament: Article 370 was to be abrogated by the constitutional process, the civil code enacted as a statute, the cow ban legislated. The party's critics, in the Congress and in the communist press of the 1950s, answered that the uniform vocabulary concealed a majoritarian substance, since the code demanded of minorities an assimilation not demanded of the majority's personal law, which the same programme left largely unexamined. The party's defenders answered that the Hindu Code Bills of 1955 and 1956 had already reformed Hindu personal law by statute, so that the code's remaining demand fell on the unreformed remainder. Both positions were argued in Parliament at the time, Mukherjee himself intervening in the Hindu Code debates of the first Lok Sabha, and the article marks the question as disputed in the form the period left it. The second feature is the programme's economic distinctiveness. Swadeshi in the 1951 text was not nostalgia: it specified decentralised production, labour-intensive technique and suspicion of heavy-industrial concentration a full decade before those positions acquired a Gandhian-socialist vocabulary elsewhere, and it gave the party a standing argument with the trading and artisan constituencies of the northern towns that the Congress's planning state taxed and licensed. The manifestos of 1962 and 1967 kept the structure, adding the uniform civil code's explicit pledge in 1967 and a commitment to abolish the permit system for entry into Jammu and Kashmir that events had already half delivered.

The programme also carried the party's answer to the question of who could belong. Mukherjee's founding statements opened membership to every Indian accepting the programme, and the party recruited, and in a few constituencies fielded, Muslim and Christian candidates across the phase, a record its defenders cite against the relabelling charge and its critics dismiss as tokenism given the numbers involved. The documentary record supports a narrow factual statement and no more: the constitution was formally open, the leadership remained overwhelmingly Hindu and Sangh-recruited, and the tension between the open claim and the cadre fact was managed rather than resolved for the entire twenty-six years. The BJP inherited that tension along with the patterns, and the inheritance sections of this article count it among the phase's deposits precisely because the experimental reading is a claim about institutions, not an acquittal.

## Phase One: Mukherjee's Tenure and the Kashmir Campaign, 1951 to 1953

The party's first electoral examination came within months of the founding, in [the post-1951 first general election context](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/) of a republic voting for the first time on universal adult franchise. The Election Commission's statistical report for 1951-52 records the Jana Sangh's return precisely: 94 candidates contested, 3 elected, 3.06 percent of the valid national vote. The three members were Mukherjee himself from Calcutta South-East, Durga Charan Banerjee from Midnapore-Jhargram, and Uma Shankar Trivedi from Chittor in Rajasthan. The Hindu Mahasabha won four seats and the Ram Rajya Parishad three, so that the entire Hindu nationalist family held ten seats in a house of 489 against the Congress's 364. In Parliament, the like-minded members organised with others in a National Democratic bloc with Mukherjee as its leading figure, and his parliamentary interventions of 1952, on the Hindu Code Bill, on East Pakistan, on Kashmir, carried a weight that the seat count understates: the bloc's leader was a former cabinet minister whom the house heard as a plausible alternative prime minister in a way no other opposition figure of the first Lok Sabha was heard.

The numbers nonetheless fixed the party's starting position, and the founding generation read them correctly. A 3.06 percent vote distributed across 94 constituencies, with deposits forfeited in most, described a party with pockets of urban and refugee support and no rural machinery. The vote also described the electoral ceiling of Hindu nationalist politics conducted as protest: the Mahasabha's four seats on a comparable vote showed where the older road ended. The Jana Sangh's response to the 1952 arithmetic was the decision that defined Phase One: to convert a policy grievance into a mass campaign that could build the organisation the election had shown to be missing. The grievance chosen was Jammu and Kashmir.

The constitutional position of Jammu and Kashmir under Article 370, the permit system that required Indian citizens from outside the state to obtain permission to enter it, and the separate constitution and flag of the state under Sheikh Abdullah's government formed, in the Jana Sangh's programme, the standing refutation of the claim that India was one nation under one constitution. The Jammu Praja Parishad, founded in November 1947 with Balraj Madhok among its organisers, had agitated on the issue inside the state for years under the slogan of one constitution, one head of state, one flag for the whole country. In the winter of 1952-53 the agitation intensified, the state government arrested large numbers of Parishad workers, and Mukherjee announced that he would enter the state without a permit to test the system in his own person. The decision was taken against the advice of colleagues who priced the risk correctly, and it converted the party's integration demand from a manifesto line into the founder's personal wager.

### Why did Mukherjee stake the party's first campaign on Jammu and Kashmir?

Kashmir joined the founding programme to the founder's biography and to the one region, Jammu with its Partition refugees, where the party already possessed organised strength. A campaign on the permit system dramatised the integration demand in a single act, entry without permission, and forced Delhi and Srinagar to defend an arrangement at odds with their own integrationist claims.

Mukherjee entered Jammu and Kashmir on 11 May 1953, crossing at Lakhanpur, and was arrested by the state police the same day. He was held at a cottage in Srinagar's Nishat Bagh. On 23 June 1953, forty-two days after the arrest, he died in detention, aged fifty-one. The official account given by the Jammu and Kashmir government recorded death by heart attack, in a patient with a history of coronary disease and pleurisy. The circumstances remain disputed. Mukherjee's mother, Jogmaya Devi, wrote to Nehru demanding an inquiry; the family and the party held that a detained man who had reported illness had been denied the medical attention and the access to his own physicians that his condition required, and that the refusal of any inquiry was itself an admission. Nehru declined a public inquiry, holding the state government's account sufficient. Defenders of the official account, then and since, note the documented cardiac history and the absence of evidence of violence. Critics answer that detention without trial, of the leader of a parliamentary bloc, ending in an unexplained custodial death, required the inquiry that was refused. The article records the official account and the family's charge side by side because the record supports both statements and settles neither.

The parliamentary record of the twenty months deserves a paragraph of its own, because it set the template for the party's Lok Sabha conduct across the phase. Mukherjee used the first Lok Sabha's debates as the party's national platform, intervening on the Hindu Code Bill with a lawyer's brief against its selective application, on the Preventive Detention Act's renewals against the government's civil-liberties record, and on East Pakistan's Hindus with a persistence that kept the refugee question on the house's agenda through 1952. The bloc he led was small, and its leader knew the arithmetic; the interventions were addressed past the treasury benches to the press gallery and the country, in the expectation, vindicated by the party's later growth, that a parliamentary reputation compounded faster than a seat count. Vajpayee, entering the Lok Sabha in 1957, inherited the method and made it the party's second voice-channel alongside the apparatus: the set-piece speech, argued on the government's own documents, that the newspapers carried whole. A cadre party that could also be heard in Parliament possessed both halves of the era's opposition technology, and the founding generation assembled both before June 1953.

The political consequences were immediate and double-edged. The permit system was abolished in 1954, the Praja Parishad agitation was settled in negotiation with Delhi, and the integration demand acquired the martyr that movements convert into permanent capital; within the Sangh Parivar's own memory, 23 June is observed as a day of sacrifice. But the party had lost, twenty months after its founding, the only leader it possessed of national stature, a former cabinet minister whose name had carried the founding. The Jana Sangh of June 1953 held three Lok Sabha seats, a thin organisation staffed by borrowed organisers, and a presidential chair that fell vacant without an heir. Phase One therefore ended with the experiment's second problem posed in its sharpest form: whether the founding design's machinery could generate leadership from within, or whether the party had been a vehicle for one man's position all along.

## The Succession Decade: What the Churn of Presidents Revealed, 1953 to 1965

The presidential succession after Mukherjee is usually told as an embarrassment, a carousel of names that a reader of the standard histories struggles to retain. Told institutionally, the succession is the most informative decade of the phase, because it settled the question the founding design had left open: whose party the Jana Sangh was. Mauli Chandra Sharma, a senior figure of the older political generation, took the presidency in 1954. Within the year he resigned, after a reconstitution of the party's working committee in which RSS pracharaks took the organisational offices and the wing of career politicians Sharma represented found itself outvoted inside its own party. Sharma's resignation statement charged interference; the Sangh's defenders answered that a party staffed by deputed organisers could not be governed against the organisers' judgement. Prem Nath Dogra of Jammu followed in 1955. Debaprasad Ghosh, the Bengal scholar-politician, held the presidency for the longest stretch of the decade, from 1956 to 1959, and returned to it in 1962 and again in 1964. Pitamber Das presided in 1960, Avasarala Rama Rao in 1961, Raghu Vira in 1963, and Bachhraj Vyas in 1965. Twelve presidencies in twenty-six years, five of them in the decade after Mukherjee, is a record that the antecedent reading cites as drift.

The experimental reading cites the same record as a settlement arrived at by stages. The Sharma episode of 1954 decided that leadership of the party would be drawn from the cadre stream rather than from autonomous political recruitment, and no subsequent succession reopened the decision. What looks like churn at the presidential level coexisted with total continuity at the organisational level: Upadhyay remained general secretary from 1951 to 1967, the office that controlled appointments, candidate selection machinery and the state units. The presidency rotated among acceptable names; the apparatus did not rotate at all. Andersen and Damle documented the arrangement's Sangh-side logic in 1987: the RSS preferred a political affiliate whose public leadership could change without disturbing the organisational chain, because the chain was the asset the Sangh was banking. Graham, in 1990, read the same facts as proof that the party lacked an independent political personality in these years. The disagreement is genuine and the article marks it as such. Graham's reading explains the 1950s vote stagnation better than any alternative. The experimental reading explains a different fact that Graham's frame leaves idle: when the party's electoral opportunities finally widened in 1967, the apparatus that exploited them was already built, staffed and disciplined, which is not what a decade of drift deposits.

The decade's electoral record, from the Election Commission's statistical reports, traces the apparatus being built under a low ceiling. In 1957 the party won 4 seats on 5.93 percent of the vote, nearly doubling its vote share on a similar seat return, the signature of support thickening inside a limited geography rather than spreading. In 1962 the return moved: 14 seats on 6.44 percent, with the party's strength concentrating in Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Delhi. The geography was the strategy. The Jana Sangh of the succession decade invested in the Hindi belt's towns and district headquarters, in municipal bodies, in the market associations and refugee colonies of the northern cities, and let the south and east go largely uncontested. Bruce Graham's constituency-level analysis in *Hindu Nationalism and Indian Politics* showed the party's vote pooling in urban and trading constituencies of the north, a pattern the party's own organisers described as building the base before the bid. The regional-base pattern that the BJP later inherited was not announced in any single document; it accumulated across the 1950s as the succession decade's organisers chose depth over spread with the resources the cadre supply made available.

The 1962 return carried a second lesson that the party's reviews absorbed along with the seat count. The Jana Sangh fielded 196 candidates in 1962, more than double its 1951 slate, and the deposit losses remained heavy outside the base geography, confirming that the cadre supply, though real, was finite and that indiscriminate expansion diluted it. From 1962 the party's candidate strategy tightened: fewer paper candidacies, concentration on segments the municipal ladder and the fronts had prepared, and a willingness to leave allied territory to partners where a partner existed. The tightening is visible in the 1967 distribution, where the party's 35 seats came from a slate of 251 with its vote share in contested seats far above its national share, the arithmetic of a party that had learned to aim. Graham's constituency analysis treated this concentration as the Jana Sangh's distinguishing electoral skill in the 1960s, and the skill, like the others, was a deposit of the succession decade's unglamorous years rather than a gift of 1967's tide.

The decade also assembled the party's movement repertoire, the set of extra-parliamentary campaigns through which the cadre party converted policy positions into organisation. In August 1955, Jana Sangh workers under Jagannathrao Joshi joined the satyagraha for the liberation of Goa, entering Portuguese territory in batches; Portuguese police fired on unarmed satyagrahis during the August 1955 actions, and the shootings entered the party's campaign literature as evidence of the Nehru government's passivity. The party opposed the Berubari transfer to Pakistan under the Nehru-Noon agreement of 1958 and campaigned through the parliamentary fight that ended in the Ninth Amendment of 1960. On language, the party held for Hindi as the official language of the Union while conceding, in its resolutions of the mid-1960s, the continued use of English as an associate language, a position that put it against the Congress's indefinite bilingual drift and against the southern anti-Hindi agitations at once. On cow protection, the party carried into the 1960s a demand for a central law banning cow slaughter, rooted in the Directive Principles' own text, and built toward the mass demonstration at Parliament in November 1966 that the coalition phase would inherit. Each campaign recruited a constituency the party's parliamentary presence could not have reached: refugees in the 1950s, traders and small manufacturers under the permit-and-licence economy, Hindi-belt students, the cow-protection movement's religious networks. The repertoire matters to the experimental reading because it shows the cadre-electoral separation working as designed: the Sangh's shakha network supplied the campaigners, the party supplied the demand and the parliamentary echo, and neither body had to become the other to make the machine run.

## The Apparatus in Ordinary Time: Fronts, Journals and Municipal Bases

Between the campaigns, the phase's real work was done by an apparatus whose components the standard narratives mention in passing and whose combined effect they understate. The party's press consisted of the English weekly *Organiser*, founded in 1947 under RSS auspices and edited in the party's first years by K. R. Malkani, and the Hindi weekly *Panchjanya*, edited by Vajpayee from its founding in 1948, with the daily *Swadesh* serving Madhya Pradesh. These journals were not ornaments: in a party without money for advertising, the weeklies carried the line to the districts, trained the writers who became the party's spokesmen, and gave the apparatus a memory longer than any presidency. The student front, the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad, founded in 1949 with Madhok among its founders and formally registered in 1949, recruited on the campuses of the northern universities and fed graduates into the party's full-time stream. The labour front followed in 1955, when Dattopant Thengadi, a pracharak deputed to labour work, founded the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh at Bhopal on 23 July 1955, with the Jana Sangh's blessing and the Sangh's organisers. The fronts extended the separation pattern by replication: each front was a formally independent body, staffed from the same cadre pool, addressing a constituency the party could not reach under its own name. The BJP of the 1980s inherited the fronts as going concerns, which is why its founding balance sheet, unlike the Jana Sangh's in 1951, already included student and labour wings of national reach.

The municipal record deserves the same weight, because it was the party's only continuous experience of administration before 1967. In Delhi, the party built through the 1950s a position in the Municipal Corporation that made it the capital's standing opposition and, after the corporation elections of the early 1960s, a governing presence in the city's wards. In Indore, Bhopal, Gwalior and the district towns of Uttar Pradesh, Jana Sangh councillors and mayors learned the unglamorous disciplines of budgets, octroi and water supply that a cadre party of protest does not naturally acquire. Graham's constituency research recorded the correlation the party's organisers claimed: the assembly segments the Jana Sangh won in 1962 and 1967 sat disproportionately where the party held municipal office, because the councillor's ward work converted shakha discipline into voter recognition. The lesson entered the pattern book as the municipal ladder: capture the corporation, learn administration at ward scale, and let the assembly bid follow the councillor's record. The BJP's later urban strategy in the Hindi belt, visible in its corporation-first advances in Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat, is the ladder climbed at scale.

The apparatus also explains the phase's resilience under the two shocks the narrative sections record. When Mukherjee died in 1953, the journals kept publishing, the fronts kept recruiting and the state units kept their organisers, because none of those assets had been the founder's personal property. When the Emergency closed the party's offices in 1975, the apparatus went underground as a system rather than improvising as a crowd: the same weeklies' mailing lists, the fronts' membership rolls and the pracharaks' district knowledge became the resistance's logistics. The experimental reading's central claim rests substantially on this stratum of the record. Parties of notables die with their notables and dissolve under bans. The Jana Sangh's apparatus was built to be indifferent to both accidents, and the indifference was a design outcome, traceable to the October 1951 decision to staff a party from a cadre body that did not itself contest elections and therefore could not be voted or banned out of its function.

## Phase Two: Integral Humanism and the Consolidation of a Doctrine, 1965 to 1968

A party whose leadership rotates and whose apparatus persists has a doctrinal problem: the programme must be carried by texts rather than by persons, or each succession reopens first principles. The Jana Sangh solved the problem in 1965. At its Vijayawada session in January 1965, the party adopted the statement of principles and policies whose philosophical core was Upadhyay's doctrine of Integral Humanism, and in Bombay, across four lectures delivered from 22 to 25 April 1965, Upadhyay set out the doctrine in full. The adoption converted sixteen years of programmatic accretion, the eight points of 1951 and the manifestos that followed, into a named philosophy that a worker could be taught and a candidate could be examined against. For the experimental reading, the Vijayawada adoption is the hinge of the phase: it is the moment the party acquired the ideological-positioning instrument that the fourth inherited pattern describes.

The doctrine's content can be stated with precision. Integral Humanism held that the human person is an integrated whole of body, mind, intellect and soul, and that a political economy must be judged against the four purusharthas, dharma, artha, kama and moksha, rather than against production aggregates alone. Against capitalism, the doctrine charged the reduction of the person to a consumer and the concentration of economic power; against Marxism, the reduction of the person to a class unit and the concentration of political power. The constructive programme followed: swadeshi in production, decentralisation of economic and political authority, the small and the indigenous preferred to the imported and the gigantic, and the last man of the village, the antyodaya figure, as the test of policy. The vocabulary was drawn from Indian philosophical categories and was meant to be: the doctrine's claim was that India required an indigenous modernity rather than a choice between two Western systems. [Golwalkar's framework as ideological backdrop](/2019/09/18/golwalkar-bunch-of-thoughts-explained/) supplied the cultural premises, the nation as an organic cultural whole, within which Upadhyay's political economy operated; the Jana Sangh's adoption gave those premises a policy form the RSS itself, as a non-electoral body, had never required.

### What changed inside the party when Integral Humanism was adopted in 1965?

Adoption gave the party a teachable doctrine that outlasted individual leaders, an economic position distinguishable from both Congress planning and Swatantra free enterprise, and a recruitment instrument: a worker trained on the Bombay lectures carried a portable creed into any constituency. It also disciplined coalition behaviour, marking which planks were philosophy and which were negotiable programme.

The SVD governments of 1967 would test that distinction within two years of the adoption, in cabinets where the party's partners held philosophies of their own.

The scholarly adjudication of Integral Humanism's significance divides along the familiar line. Jaffrelot, in *The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics* (1996), treated the doctrine with respect as an ideological construction and noted its function inside the movement: a philosophy capacious enough to hold the Sangh's cultural nationalism and the party's electoral needs in one frame, and vague enough at the operational level to admit wide interpretation. Critics of the doctrine, in the academic literature of the left and in Congress polemic of the period, held that the vagueness was the point, that Integral Humanism rebranded Hindu nationalist premises in philosophical vocabulary while committing the party to little that could be falsified at the polls. Defenders answered with the doctrine's economic content, which was neither vague nor derivative: the decentralisation and swadeshi planks placed the Jana Sangh to the economic left of the Swatantra Party and in defined opposition to the Congress's heavy-industry strategy, and the party's subsequent conduct in the 1967 coalition governments included land and small-industry measures argued explicitly in the doctrine's terms. The article marks the dispute as disputed and rests its own claim on the narrower institutional ground: whatever the doctrine's philosophical merits, its adoption demonstrably stabilised the party's identity across the succession churn and armed the coalition negotiators of 1967 with a hierarchy of commitments. That is what the fourth pattern names.

The doctrine's institutionalisation ran through training as much as through texts. The party's study camps for workers, the shikshan shivirs held at state and national level through the late 1960s, took the Bombay lectures as their syllabus, examining cadres on the doctrine's categories in the same manner as the RSS's own training camps examined the Sangh's. A rival codification tested the settlement from inside: Madhok's *Indianisation*, published in 1970, proposed a harder definition of national belonging than Upadhyay's lectures contained and was received by the apparatus as a challenge to the adopted doctrine by the man the apparatus was in the process of removing. The episode is instructive for the experimental reading. A party held together by a founder's charisma cannot prefer one text to another by procedure; the Jana Sangh of 1970 could and did, treating the Vijayawada adoption as settled law and its author's rival as a dissenter, which is how doctrines behave inside institutions and not inside followings.

The phase closed with a second custodial death. At the party's Calicut session of 29 to 31 December 1967, Upadhyay was elected president, the first holder of the office to unite it with the organisational authority he had exercised as general secretary since 1951. He held the presidency for forty-three days. On the night of 10 to 11 February 1968, travelling by train toward Patna, he disappeared from his compartment; his body was found beside the railway tracks near Mughalsarai station. The circumstances of the death remain contested. The official investigation proceeded on a robbery-murder theory and prosecuted two men found with his belongings; the party and Upadhyay's family rejected the theory's adequacy, holding that the organiser of a national party had been killed for political reasons that no inquiry pursued. The government of Uttar Pradesh and the Central Bureau of Investigation between them produced no account that the party accepted, and the file has never yielded a settled public version. The article records the contest without resolving it, and notes the institutional fact that matters here: the party absorbed the loss of its organiser-ideologue in 1968 without the paralysis that had followed Mukherjee's death in 1953. Vajpayee succeeded to the presidency in 1968 and held it to 1972, the succession executed by the apparatus in ordinary form. The contrast between 1953 and 1968 is the succession decade's lesson made visible: by 1968 the institution, not the founder, was carrying the party.

## The Security Record: China, Pakistan and Three Wars

The party's foreign-policy and security positions, fixed at the founding and argued in Parliament across the phase, formed a fifth strand of its programme that the institutional sections have so far left implicit, and the strand matters because it supplied the party's sharpest parliamentary identity in the decades when its domestic programme was outvoted. The Jana Sangh held that the Congress's non-alignment conceded initiative to the communist bloc, that Pakistan's hold on a third of Jammu and Kashmir was an open wound the state had a duty to close, that China under Mao was an expansionist power whose assurances on the Himalayan boundary were worthless, and that India should establish full relations with Israel as a fellow state surrounded by hostile neighbours. The positions were argued by parliamentarians of genuine calibre. Vajpayee's speeches in the Lok Sabha of the late 1950s, on the boundary question and on the government's China policy, built his reputation as the opposition's foreign-policy voice; his demand, as the border crisis deepened in 1962, that Parliament be summoned to debate the nation's defence was part of the pressure that produced the emergency session of November 1962. The party's criticism of the government during the October to November 1962 war itself, and its post-war charge that the Nehru government had neglected defence preparation across a decade of warnings, placed the Jana Sangh inside the nationalist mainstream of the post-war reckoning rather than at its fringe, one reason the party's 1962 seat tally rose while the war's aftershocks were still rearranging the opposition.

The 1965 war with Pakistan tested the position differently. The party supported the war effort unconditionally and then attacked the Tashkent Declaration of January 1966 for returning the Haji Pir pass and the Tithwal positions, territorial gains the party held had been purchased with soldiers' lives and surrendered at a conference table. On the Rann of Kutch, the party opposed the reference of the boundary dispute to international arbitration after the 1965 clashes and attacked the tribunal's award of February 1968, which granted Pakistan roughly a tenth of the claimed territory, as the fruit of a policy that negotiated from apology. In 1971 the party's position reached its phase peak of public alignment: the Jana Sangh demanded recognition of Bangladesh during the refugee crisis of that spring, its workers campaigned on the liberation cause through the year, and during the December war the party's parliamentary support for the government's conduct was unstinting, a stance that made the Grand Alliance's defeat of the preceding March the more striking, since the electorate rewarded the war's conductor rather than the war's supporters. The security record is relevant to the experimental reading for a structural reason. A party whose domestic programme could be painted as sectarian held, on the questions of territory and defence, positions that a wide nationalist constituency shared, and the security strand repeatedly carried the party into rooms, parliamentary committees, all-party delegations, solidarity campaigns, where its other strands would not have admitted it. Coalition capacity is built from such rooms. The SVD partners of 1967 and the Janata partners of 1977 had sat with Jana Sangh leaders across a decade of security debates before they sat with them in cabinets, and the familiarity was part of what the party's negotiators spent.

## The Cow Protection March and the Approach to 1967

The coalition phase opened with a movement, not an election. On 7 November 1966, a procession of Hindu religious orders, cow-protection organisations and political supporters, estimated by the press of the day in the hundreds of thousands, marched on Parliament in Delhi to demand a central law banning cow slaughter. The Jana Sangh supported the campaign and its workers marched, though the procession's organisers were the religious bodies grouped in the Sarvadaliya Goraksha Maha-Abhiyan Samiti and the party was careful to stand as supporter rather than owner. When a section of the crowd pressed toward the Parliament House complex, the police fired; the official toll given in Parliament was eight dead, and over a hundred injured, with hundreds arrested. The Home Minister, Gulzari Lal Nanda, resigned within days, accepting responsibility for the handling. The march matters to the phase's record on two counts. It demonstrated the mobilisation ceiling of a religious campaign conducted outside the party's discipline: enormous turnout, no negotiable demand structure, and a violent edge the party could not control and did not want. And it taught the party's negotiators, three months before the 1967 elections, the difference between movement capital and electoral capital. The Jana Sangh contested February 1967 on its programme and its apparatus, not on the cow campaign, and its best-ever result followed. The BJP's later movement politics, above all the temple campaign's managed escalation of the late 1980s, shows the 1966 lesson absorbed: a movement held on the party's own leash, its calendar set by electoral arithmetic rather than by the religious calendar.

The election of 1967 also altered the party's internal distribution of authority in a way the coalition experiments would expose. Madhok, as president, had led the campaign and claimed its result; Upadhyay, as general secretary until his elevation at Calicut in December 1967, controlled the apparatus that had delivered it; and Vajpayee, the party's parliamentary face since his first election from Balrampur in 1957, embodied its public moderation. Three centres of authority, founder-president, organiser and parliamentarian, could coexist in opposition. Government participation in eight states would force choices among them, and the choices of 1967 to 1973, ending in Madhok's expulsion, were the apparatus centre absorbing the other two. The experimental reading counts that absorption as part of the coalition pattern's tuition: a party that intends to bargain with allies must first settle who at home holds the pen.

## Phase Three: The Coalition Experiments, 1967 to 1977

The general election of February 1967 broke the Congress system open, and the Jana Sangh's return measured its share of the break. The Election Commission's statistical report for 1967 records 35 Lok Sabha seats on 9.31 percent of the national vote, the party's phase peak on both measures, making the Jana Sangh the largest single opposition party in the fourth Lok Sabha. In Delhi the party won six of the seven parliamentary seats and took control of the Metropolitan Council and the Municipal Corporation, converting the capital's administration into a showroom of Jana Sangh governance. In the Madhya Pradesh assembly the party won 78 of 296 seats, the largest bloc in the state after the Congress. The state elections of 1967 left the Congress without a majority in eight states, and in most of them the replacement was a Samyukta Vidhayak Dal, a united legislators' front whose components agreed on little beyond the removal of the Congress. Into those fronts the Jana Sangh walked, in several states as the largest or second-largest component, and the walk was the experiment the phase is remembered for.

The Madhya Pradesh case is documented in the state archive with unusual completeness, and the brief for this article names the SVD records of Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar as the under-cited primary source of the phase. In Madhya Pradesh, the Congress ministry of Dwarka Prasad Mishra fell in July 1967 when Govind Narayan Singh led a defection of Congress legislators and organised the Lok Sewak Dal; on 30 July 1967 Singh took office as chief minister of an SVD coalition in which the Jana Sangh, with its 78 members, was the largest single constituent. The coalition held office until March 1969. In Bihar, the SVD government formed in March 1967 under Mahamaya Prasad Sinha, with Karpoori Thakur of the Samyukta Socialist Party as deputy chief minister, joined the two socialist parties and the Communist Party of India on the left with the Jana Sangh on the right, an ideological span that the NCERT's later textbook summary would single out as the emblem of the fronts' incongruence. In Uttar Pradesh, Charan Singh's Bharatiya Kranti Dal formed the government of April 1967 with Jana Sangh participation. In Haryana, Rao Birender Singh's Vishal Haryana Party led an SVD ministry. In Punjab, the Akali Dal and the Jana Sangh governed in coalition under Gurnam Singh. The Jana Sangh, a party whose founding programme anathematised communism, sat in a Bihar cabinet with the CPI; a party of Hindi-belt integrationism governed Punjab in harness with the Akali Dal. The fronts were not betrayals of the programme in the party's own reasoning. They were the arithmetic of 1967: no front could be assembled without components that contradicted one another, and the party's negotiators, armed with the doctrine's hierarchy of commitments, traded programme for office in proportions the party centre approved state by state.

### What did the coalition experiments of 1967 to 1971 teach the party?

The experiments taught that a cadre party could enter ideologically mixed governments without cadre demoralisation if terms were set before the ministry formed, that ministries fell to partner defections rather than doctrinal disputes, and that the party's legislators required protection against the office-seeking the fronts rewarded. The lessons entered the party's negotiating habits as standing method.

The habits were specific: written common programmes agreed before portfolios were distributed, portfolios chosen for organisational value rather than prestige, and a standing readiness to exit a front when its arithmetic died rather than purchase survival with doctrine.

The teaching was expensive. The SVD governments fell, most of them, within two years, to defections, partner withdrawals and the Congress's counter-operations; the Singh ministry in Madhya Pradesh ended in March 1969, the Bihar front collapsed in stages by early 1969, and the midterm elections and President's Rule episodes of 1968 to 1971 closed the experiment in most states. The party also paid internally. Balraj Madhok, president during the 1967 triumph, pressed a wider right-bloc strategy, an alignment with the Swatantra Party and with Congress dissidents after the 1969 Congress split, and charged the party's dominant group with a leftward drift in economic policy; the Vajpayee leadership resisted, holding that the Jana Sangh's identity would not survive absorption into a generic right. Madhok's marginalisation ended in 1973, when the president who succeeded Vajpayee, L. K. Advani, expelled him from the party for three years on grounds of indiscipline, an act Advani himself described in later years as the sacking of a founder by the institution the founder had built. The episode is the separation pattern's hardest internal case: the expulsion was executed through the party's own constitutional machinery, against a founding president, in defence of a line the apparatus had settled. Madhok's own later writings charged that the RSS leadership had engineered the expulsion through Advani; the party's account held it a routine disciplinary act. The article marks the charge as disputed and notes what is not disputed: the institution could remove its own founder-president and continue without a split, which the Congress of the same years conspicuously could not.

The 1971 midterm election closed the phase's coalition chapter at the centre. The Jana Sangh entered the Grand Alliance of opposition parties assembled against Indira Gandhi after the 1969 Congress split, alongside the Congress (Organisation), the Swatantra Party and the Samyukta Socialist Party, and contested on the single slogan of removing her government. The alliance was routed in the election of March 1971; the Jana Sangh's own return, from the Election Commission's statistical report, was 22 seats on 7.35 percent of the vote, a fall from the 1967 peak that nonetheless left the party the alliance's largest surviving component in Parliament. The lesson the party's organisers drew, recorded in the post-election reviews that Graham's research drew on, was the mirror of the SVD lesson: post-poll coalition craft could win office where pre-poll grand alliances, assembled on negation alone, could not win elections. The distinction between the two instruments, and the preference for the post-poll form, passed directly into the inheritance. The BJP's alliance practice of the 1990s, building the National Democratic Alliance from a pre-agreed core and post-poll adhesions rather than from a maximal anti-Congress front, follows the 1967-to-1971 learning curve with a fidelity that the antecedent reading, which treats the phase as a waiting room, cannot explain.

The state-level sequel confirmed the learning at the party's expense. In the state elections of 1972, held in the afterglow of the Bangladesh war, the Congress swept the Hindi belt and the Jana Sangh's assembly strength contracted sharply in Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, the two bases the succession decade had built. The party's reviews of 1972, cited in Graham's research, drew the base lesson with unusual candour: a regional base built on urban and trading constituencies could be outbid in a plebiscitary wave, and base maintenance between elections, the municipal ladder and the fronts' membership work, was not an interlude from politics but the politics itself. When the midterm poll in Uttar Pradesh in 1974 returned the party weakened further, the apparatus did not retrench; it redirected its organisers into the JP movement's structures, the decision that positioned the party for 1977. The pattern visible across 1967 to 1974 is a party converting every defeat into an organisational instruction, and it is the strongest single evidence for the experimental reading: preparatory bodies do not keep laboratory notebooks, and the Jana Sangh's reviews, session resolutions and post-election analyses, preserved in the party's journals and the *Organiser*'s files, are exactly that.

## The Split Congress and the Presidential Test, 1969 to 1971

The Congress split of November 1969 rearranged the board on which the coalition experiments had been played, and the Jana Sangh's conduct across the split's crisis years completes the phase's coalition record. In the presidential election of August 1969, the party backed Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, the Congress organisation's nominee, against V. V. Giri, the independent candidate whom Indira Gandhi, breaking with her own party's decision, called on Congress electors to support on conscience. Reddy's defeat by a narrow margin on the second count announced the split before it was formalised. The Jana Sangh's support for the organisation's candidate was the Madhok line in action, an anti-Indira front with the Congress's own right; its failure, and the party's subsequent experience of the Grand Alliance, discredited the line inside the party and armed the Vajpayee group's insistence that the Jana Sangh's identity was not a negotiable instrument of anti-Congress arithmetic. The argument between the two lines, founder-president against apparatus, ran from the presidential election of 1969 to the expulsion of 1973, and its resolution in the apparatus's favour is the coalition pattern's internal half: the party that would bargain hard with allies first settled that its own doctrine was outside the bargain.

Two policy fights of the same years fixed the party's parliamentary identity for the electorate the 1971 verdict would judge. On the nationalisation of fourteen major banks by ordinance in July 1969, the party's attack fell on the measure's manner and motive, an ordinance issued days after the Congress's Bangalore session and aimed, in the party's charge, at the prime minister's position inside her party rather than at the credit system. The party's own doctrine complicated a frontal defence of private banking: Integral Humanism's suspicion of concentrated economic power kept the Jana Sangh's opposition short of the Swatantra Party's property-rights brief, an ambiguity that Madhok's wing attacked as drift and the leadership defended as doctrine. On the privy purses, the party's position was unambiguous and was held to the end: the covenants of 1947 to 1949, by which the princely states had acceded and merged, were solemn agreements of the Indian state, and their unilateral abolition by constitutional amendment dishonoured the instrument on which the Union itself had been assembled. The party voted against the abolition bill of 1970, which fell short of the required majority in the Rajya Sabha, and against the Twenty-Sixth Amendment of December 1971, which completed the abolition after the Supreme Court had struck down the presidential derecognition of the rulers. The position cost the party in the plebiscitary season of 1971 and was held anyway, which its historians cite as the doctrine-over-arithmetic habit in its purest parliamentary form.

At the state level the same years renewed the Punjab experiment on harder terms. The midterm election of February 1969 returned the Akali Dal in Punjab, and in March 1969 Gurnam Singh again headed a coalition in which the Jana Sangh sat, the two parties governing a state whose politics ran on the Punjabi Suba settlement the Jana Sangh had once opposed. When Gurnam Singh's government gave way in March 1970, Parkash Singh Badal succeeded him at the head of the same Akali-Jana Sangh arrangement. The Punjab coalitions were the SVD pattern's longest-lived products, surviving in successive forms to 1971, and they carried a lesson the northern focus of the standard narrative misses: the Jana Sangh's coalition craft worked best where the partner's identity was regional rather than ideological, a distinction the BJP's later alliance choices, in Punjab above all, would preserve for decades.

## The Advani Presidency, the JP Movement and the Emergency, 1973 to 1977

Advani's presidency, from 1973, repositioned the party for the confrontation that closed the phase. His early presidential statements described the Jana Sangh as neither a party of the left nor of the right but a forward-looking national party, a formulation that widened the party's coalition surface while the apparatus held its doctrine. The repositioning met its opportunity in the movement that Jayaprakash Narayan built from the Bihar student agitation of 1974 into a national campaign for total revolution. The Jana Sangh joined the JP movement as its most organised component after the RSS network itself, supplying the student and labour fronts' cadres in state after state; Nanaji Deshmukh, a pracharak on deputation to the party, became one of the movement's principal organisers and a member of the coordination structures around Narayan. The party's calculation, stated in its resolutions of 1974 and 1975, was that a movement against Congress authoritarianism could carry the Jana Sangh's cadre into districts and constituencies the party had never organised, at the price of subordinating its own banner to the movement's. The calculation was the coalition pattern extended from governments to movements, and it was vindicated by the event.

The Emergency proclaimed on 25 June 1975 fell on the Jana Sangh with the full weight of the state, as [the Emergency of 1975 to 1977](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) fell on every opposition party. Advani and Vajpayee were arrested in Bangalore, where the party's national executive had been meeting; the party's leadership across the states was detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act; the RSS was banned on 4 July 1975. Balraj Madhok, expelled two years earlier, spent eighteen months in prison alongside the men who had expelled him, a detail the article records because it measures the Emergency's indiscriminate pressure on the whole Hindu nationalist family. The underground resistance that formed through 1975 and 1976 ran substantially on the Sangh network's infrastructure: the Lok Sangharsh Samiti, the coordinating body of the underground, drew on RSS and Jana Sangh organisers for its courier lines, safe houses, cyclostyled bulletins and the distribution of banned literature, and Deshmukh was among its central organisers until his arrest. Detention itself produced a deposit the phase carried forward. Advani, held at Bangalore and later at Rohtak, kept the prison diary published after the Emergency as *A Prisoner's Scrap-Book* (1978), a day-by-day record that fixed the contingent's own account of the nineteen months while the events were fresh. Hundreds of the party's district organisers spent the Emergency in prison under MISA or the Defence of India Rules, and the shared detention of 1975 to 1977 functioned inside the cadre as a second founding ordeal, retold in the party's training literature alongside Mukherjee's death in custody in 1953. The pairing was not accidental. A movement that teaches its workers that its founder died in a Congress government's detention and its next generation was jailed by a Congress government's emergency acquires a usable doctrine of the opposing party's authoritarian character, and the Jana Sangh's post-1977 presentation of the Congress drew on exactly that taught memory. The party's own historians and its critics agree on the scale of the Sangh network's role in the underground while disputing its share: sympathetic accounts, Andersen and Damle's among them, make the network the resistance's backbone; critical accounts note that the underground also ran on socialist, Sarvodaya and trade-union circuits that owed the Sangh nothing. The article marks the share as disputed and records the undisputed point: when the Emergency ended, the Jana Sangh possessed a national reputation for resistance, earned in detention and in the underground, that no election of the phase had given it, and that reputation was a deposit in the bank the Janata merger would draw on within weeks.

## Phase Four: The Janata Merger and the Preservation of the Cadre, 1977 to 1980

The merger that ended the phase began as an electoral instrument. In January 1977, with the Emergency still formally in force and elections announced for March, the Congress (Organisation), the Bharatiya Lok Dal, the Socialist Party and the Bharatiya Jana Sangh combined as the Janata Party, with the Congress for Democracy joining within weeks; [the 1977 Janata verdict and merger](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/) returned the combined party with 295 Lok Sabha seats, of which the Jana Sangh's candidates, contesting on the Janata symbol, won 93, the largest single contingent after the Bharatiya Lok Dal's. Morarji Desai took office as prime minister on 24 March 1977. The Jana Sangh's ministers entered the government at its senior levels: Vajpayee at External Affairs, Advani at Information and Broadcasting, Brij Lal Varma at Industry. The party's formal dissolution into the Janata Party was completed at the merger convention of 1 May 1977, at which the constituents surrendered their separate constitutions. Twenty-six years after the Delhi founding, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh ceased to exist as a legal party.

The merger's terms contained the asymmetry on which the phase's last lesson turns. The Jana Sangh surrendered its name, symbol and constitution, everything an electoral party visibly is. What it did not surrender, because the merger instruments could not reach it, was the cadre architecture outside the party: the RSS remained a separate body, unbanned after the Emergency's revocation, its shakhas and pracharak system intact, and the Jana Sangh's organisers inside the Janata Party remained swayamsevaks whose organisational allegiance ran through a channel the Janata constitution did not govern. Jayaprakash Narayan had blessed the merger on the understanding that a united party would transcend its components. The Jana Sangh's leadership accepted the merger on terms that preserved the component that mattered most to it. Both understandings were sincere, and they were incompatible, which is why the merger's crisis, when it came, came on exactly the seam the asymmetry had left.

The contingent's performance in office added a governing record to the inheritance. Vajpayee at External Affairs conducted the Janata government's foreign policy reset, visiting Beijing in February 1979, the first Indian foreign minister to do so after the 1962 war, and addressing the United Nations General Assembly in Hindi in October 1977, an act the party's base read as the integrationist programme arriving at a world podium. Advani at Information and Broadcasting dismantled the Emergency's censorship machinery, restored the autonomy of the official media in form, and piloted the repeal of the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, converting the party's underground credentials into a legislative record on press freedom. The Jana Sangh ministers emerged from the government's general wreckage with personal reputations intact, a fact the contingent's critics attributed to the brevity of the government's life and its defenders to the apparatus's discipline in office. Either way, the BJP of 1980 inherited not merely a cadre but a cadre with ministerial biographies, the first generation of Hindu nationalist politicians whom the republic's voters had seen govern.

### Why did the merger preserve the cadre while dissolving the party?

The merger instruments governed parties, and the cadre architecture was never the party's property to surrender: the pracharaks were RSS members on deputation, the shakha network answered to Nagpur, and the Jana Sangh's constitution controlled only the electoral shell. The founding design of 1951, keeping the two bodies legally distinct, therefore determined the merger's outcome a quarter-century in advance.

The state elections of June 1977, which carried the Janata wave into the assemblies the Emergency had frozen, displayed the contingent's depth inside the merged party. In Madhya Pradesh, Kailash Joshi, a Jana Sangh organiser of the succession decade's school, took office as chief minister on 24 June 1977 at the head of the Janata government. In Himachal Pradesh, Shanta Kumar, likewise of Jana Sangh origin, became chief minister the same month. In Delhi, the Janata Party took the Metropolitan Council, returning the capital's administration to the political family that had governed it a decade earlier under a party name of its own. Jana Sangh men held district and state office across the Hindi belt in numbers the party's own colours had never won, and the experience of governing states between 1977 and 1979, budgets, transfers, the management of a merged party's factions from inside a ministry, entered the cadre's training as the earlier municipal years had. When the regrouping of 1980 came, the BJP's state leaderships were not drawn from a defeated opposition's memory. They were drawn from men who had run governments within the previous three years.

The crisis arrived on the dual-membership question. Through 1978 and into 1979, Madhu Limaye and other Janata leaders of socialist origin pressed the demand that members of the former Jana Sangh choose between the Janata Party and the RSS: a member of a secular governing party, on the demand's strongest statement, could not simultaneously belong to an organisation committed to Hindu nationalist aims, and the dual allegiance made the Janata Party's secular professions hollow. The Jana Sangh contingent's answer, at its strongest, held that the RSS was a cultural and social organisation, that membership in it was no more a dual political membership than a Congressman's membership of a trade union or a caste association, and that the demand singled out one constituent for a test applied to no other. Desai, who had initially resisted the demand, moved toward it under pressure; the Jana Sangh members refused to sever the RSS connection. In July 1979 Desai's government lost its majority amid the defections the crisis accelerated, Desai resigned on 15 July 1979, Charan Singh's brief government followed, and the Janata Party went to the election of January 1980 as a broken coalition. The Jana Sangh contingent's members, their cadre channel untouched by two years inside the merger, regrouped within months: on 6 April 1980 the Bharatiya Janata Party was founded in Delhi with Vajpayee as its first president, its membership drawn overwhelmingly from the former Jana Sangh and its apparatus staffed by the same pracharak line that Upadhyay had built. The phase that the merger appeared to terminate had, in institutional fact, been placed in escrow and recovered.

The first pattern, cadre-electoral separation, is the deepest. Its operation across the phase has been traced in every section: designed in October 1951, tested by the Sharma resignation of 1954, hardened by the Madhok expulsion of 1973, and vindicated by the merger's asymmetry in 1977 to 1980. Within [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) that this pillar applies to the movement's ideology, the separation is the institutional expression of the same distinction: the electoral vehicle argues, bargains and governs in public, while the cadre body reproduces the movement's personnel and doctrine outside the electoral cycle. The BJP did not have to invent the arrangement in 1980 because the Jana Sangh had already paid its tuition across three decades.

The second and third patterns, regional-base building and the general-secretary apparatus, belong together. The succession decade's choice of depth over spread gave the party, by 1967, winnable bases in Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Delhi and Punjab rather than a thin national presence, and the apparatus that made the choice was the organisational chain running from Upadhyay's general secretaryship through the state organising secretaries to the constituency. The BJP's expansion method of the 1980s and 1990s, the patient conversion of the Hindi belt into a base before the national bid, replicates the succession decade's method at larger scale, and [the 1998 NDA's coalition-architecture inheritance](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) shows the fourth pattern, the coalition architecture, in its mature form: a core party with a written common agenda, allies admitted on terms settled in advance, and the core's organisation never merged into the alliance. The Jana Sangh learned each element at a dated cost: written terms from the SVD falls of 1968 and 1969, post-poll construction from the Grand Alliance defeat of 1971, exit readiness from the fronts' collapses, and cadre preservation from the dual-membership crisis of 1979.

The inheritance was real but not automatic, and the BJP's first years supply the control case that proves the point. At its founding in April 1980, the new party deliberately muted the doctrinal pattern, adopting Gandhian socialism as its stated philosophy and presenting the Jana Sangh's programme in softened form, a choice its leadership defended as the price of the breadth the Janata experiment had taught. The apparatus pattern and the separation pattern operated at full strength from the first day, since both were organisational facts rather than presentations. The electoral return on the muted doctrine was the two Lok Sabha seats of December 1984, the BJP's phase floor and the Jana Sangh inheritance's clearest negative test: cadre, base and coalition craft without the doctrinal signal retained the machine and lost the vote. The party's subsequent reactivation of the fourth pattern, the calibrated restoration of Hindutva to the centre of its presentation by the end of the 1980s, was conducted by the same men who had run the 1980 experiment, and their own accounts of the reversal cite the 1984 result as the datum that settled the argument. An inheritance that can be switched off and on, with measurable consequences in each position, is not a metaphor. It is equipment, and the Jana Sangh phase is where the equipment was built, tested and priced.

## The Complication: Modest Arithmetic and the Learning Metric

The strongest counter to the institutional-experimental reading deserves its full force. The Jana Sangh's electoral performance across the phase was modest by any measure the party itself would have chosen. The Election Commission's reports fix the ceiling exactly: 35 seats and 9.31 percent in 1967, the best of five attempts; 22 seats and 7.35 percent in 1971, the last. The party never won a state outright, never held the centre, and ended not by independent decision but by merger, dissolving at the moment of its greatest success as a contingent inside another party's victory. On the arithmetic, the antecedent reading's proponents conclude, the phase contributed personnel and little else: a ladder the BJP climbed and kicked away, its doctrines revised at the BJP's founding into Gandhian socialism, its name abandoned, its programme superseded. Madhok's later writings pressed a version of the charge from inside, holding that the party after 1969 had already surrendered its distinctiveness to the apparatus that managed it. Scholars of the movement's later growth have a cooler version: the BJP's ascent of the 1990s ran on the Ram Janmabhoomi movement and the Congress's decay, fuel the Jana Sangh phase did not produce and could not have predicted.

A second counter, pressed by the movement's sociological critics, attacks from the direction of inclusion rather than arithmetic. The phase's party, on this account, was a narrow institution wearing a national claim: absent from the south, thin in the east after 1953, led at every level by men drawn from a small set of upper-caste, urban, Hindi-speaking networks, and dependent for its mass contact on campaigns, cow protection above all, that mobilised one religious community's sentiments while its constitution professed an open door. The critics' evidence is strong on its own ground. The party's parliamentary contingent across five elections contained a bare handful of Muslim and Christian members, its southern organisation remained a token presence at dissolution, and no woman held a leading office in the party across the twenty-six years. The experimental reading does not answer this counter by denying its facts, which are documented in the party's own returns and rosters. It answers by scope: the claim defended in this article concerns institutional learning, and an institution can learn durably while remaining narrow, as the phase's deposits, the apparatus, the doctrine, the coalition method, in fact served a broadening that the phase itself never achieved. The narrowness is part of the record the BJP inherited, and the inheritance included the unfinished work along with the finished equipment.

The counter is correct about the arithmetic and about the fuel, and the experimental reading concedes both. Its answer is a distinction of metrics that the record sustains. Electoral-success metrics measure what a party wins in a period; institutional-learning metrics measure what a party can do at the end of a period that it could not do at the start, and the Jana Sangh's end-of-phase capacities are not in dispute: a national apparatus with a permanent organisational spine, a doctrine that survived three leadership generations, a tested method for entering and leaving coalitions, a base geography in the Hindi belt, and a demonstrated capacity to survive the death of founders, the expulsion of presidents, nineteen months of illegality and a full merger, and to re-emerge with the machine intact. The BJP of April 1980 began with two assets no other Indian party founding of the century began with: a trained cadre it had not had to recruit and a set of institutional solutions it had not had to invent. The fuel of the 1990s was new. The engine that burned it was the phase's product, and an account of the phase that reports only the fuel gauge has mismeasured its subject.

## What the Phase Settles

The adjudication can be closed on the evidence assembled. The antecedent reading is not false; it is a description of the phase's personnel flow, and as far as it goes it is accurate: the BJP's founding leadership, its first president, its organising secretaries and much of its membership came out of the Jana Sangh, and any history of the BJP must begin in 1951. The reading fails as an account of the phase itself, because it prices the twenty-six years by their successor and therefore cannot see what the years produced in their own right. The experimental reading prices the phase by its problems and its deposits, and on that pricing the phase is the most consequential quarter-century in the movement's institutional history: the period in which electoral Hindutva learned, at the cost of a founder's death in detention, a doctrine contested and codified, coalitions built and lost, and a merger survived, how to persist inside a democratic system that its founding generation entered with three seats and 3.06 percent of the vote. Two subjects that this article has touched only as the phase requires them, the philosophy of Integral Humanism as a system in its own right and the founding choices of April 1980 that converted the inheritance into a new party, receive their own full treatments elsewhere in this pillar, and the reader who follows the phase forward will find the four patterns at work in both.

A last word on the pillar's stake in the verdict. This series reads Hindutva's history by separating its ideological strands and testing each against the documentary record of the institutions that carried it. The Jana Sangh phase is the first sustained occasion on which the electoral strand can be tested at all: twenty-six years of dated elections, sessions, manifestos, coalition instruments and merger documents, generated by men who believed the test mattered and recorded their reasoning as they went. The institutional-experimental reading honours that record by taking its problems seriously, and the four patterns it extracts are offered as the pillar's citable deposit from the phase, available for the treatments of the BJP's decades that follow in this series and open, as every claim in the series is open, to correction from the archives on which it rests.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Bharatiya Jana Sangh?

The Bharatiya Jana Sangh was an Indian political party founded at Delhi on 21 October 1951 by Syama Prasad Mukherjee, which carried Hindu nationalist politics into electoral competition until it merged into the Janata Party in 1977. It was constituted as an open, membership-based party, staffed substantially by organisers deputed from the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, and it contested five general elections, peaking at 35 Lok Sabha seats on 9.31 percent of the vote in 1967. Its programme joined the full integration of Jammu and Kashmir, a uniform civil code and a central ban on cow slaughter to swadeshi economics and decentralisation, a combination codified philosophically in Deendayal Upadhyay's Integral Humanism of 1965. After the Janata merger broke on the dual-membership question, the party's personnel and apparatus regrouped in April 1980 as the Bharatiya Janata Party.

### Q: Who founded the Bharatiya Jana Sangh?

Syama Prasad Mukherjee founded the Bharatiya Jana Sangh and served as its first president. Mukherjee had been president of the Hindu Mahasabha from 1944 to 1946 and Minister for Industry and Supply in Jawaharlal Nehru's cabinet from August 1947 until his resignation in April 1950 over the Nehru-Liaquat Pact. Through 1951 he built the party in stages, organising a Bengal unit in April, while Balraj Madhok organised the Punjab and Delhi units in May, before the national founding convention met in Delhi on 21 October 1951 and elected him president. The founding also depended on the RSS's deputation of full-time organisers, among them Deendayal Upadhyay, who became general secretary, and Atal Bihari Vajpayee. Mukherjee led the party into the first general election and died in detention at Srinagar on 23 June 1953, twenty months after the founding.

### Q: When did the Jana Sangh merge into the Janata Party?

The Jana Sangh combined with the Congress (Organisation), the Bharatiya Lok Dal and the Socialist Party to contest the March 1977 general election as the Janata Party, and the formal merger, at which the constituents surrendered their separate constitutions, was completed at the merger convention of 1 May 1977. The Jana Sangh's candidates, contesting on the Janata symbol, had won 93 Lok Sabha seats in the March verdict, and its leaders entered Morarji Desai's government, Atal Bihari Vajpayee at External Affairs and L. K. Advani at Information and Broadcasting. The merger ended the Jana Sangh's twenty-six years of separate legal existence. It did not dissolve the party's cadre architecture, because the organisers were RSS members on deputation, and when the Janata Party broke over the dual-membership question in 1979, the regrouping that produced the Bharatiya Janata Party in April 1980 drew on that preserved apparatus.

### Q: What happened to Syama Prasad Mukherjee?

Syama Prasad Mukherjee died in detention at Srinagar on 23 June 1953, during his campaign against the permit system that governed entry into Jammu and Kashmir. Mukherjee had announced that he would enter the state without a permit to test the arrangement in his own person, in solidarity with the Jammu Praja Parishad's agitation for the state's full integration under one constitution, one head of state and one flag. He entered the state on 11 May 1953 and was arrested the same day, then held at a cottage in the Nishat Bagh area of Srinagar. The Jammu and Kashmir government recorded his death, forty-two days later, as a heart attack in a man with a history of coronary disease. His family and his party disputed the adequacy of the official account, pointing to his reported illness in custody and demanding a public inquiry, which Prime Minister Nehru declined. The circumstances remain disputed in the record.

### Q: Who led the Jana Sangh after Mukherjee?

No single successor inherited Syama Prasad Mukherjee's position in 1953, and the presidency rotated for a decade while the organisational leadership remained constant. Mauli Chandra Sharma took the presidency in 1954 and resigned within the year after a working-committee reconstitution strengthened the RSS organisers' hold, an episode that settled the principle that leadership would come from the cadre stream. Prem Nath Dogra followed in 1955; Debaprasad Ghosh held the office from 1956 to 1959 and returned to it in 1962 and 1964; Pitamber Das presided in 1960, Avasarala Rama Rao in 1961, Raghu Vira in 1963 and Bachhraj Vyas in 1965. Balraj Madhok led the party through the 1967 election, Deendayal Upadhyay held the presidency briefly from December 1967 until his death in February 1968, Atal Bihari Vajpayee led from 1968 to 1972, and L. K. Advani from 1973 to the 1977 merger. Throughout, Upadhyay's tenure as general secretary, from 1951 to 1967, supplied the continuity.

### Q: How did the Jana Sangh become the BJP?

The Jana Sangh became the Bharatiya Janata Party by regrouping after a merger rather than by a fresh founding. The Jana Sangh dissolved into the Janata Party in May 1977, its candidates having won 93 seats on the Janata symbol, and its members served in the Janata government. When the dual-membership crisis of 1979 demanded that former Jana Sangh members sever their RSS membership, they refused, the Desai government fell, and the Janata Party broke apart. The former Jana Sangh contingent then founded the Bharatiya Janata Party at Delhi on 6 April 1980, with Atal Bihari Vajpayee as its first president. The new party took over the Jana Sangh's membership base, its state organisations, its fronts in student and labour politics, and its organising-secretary apparatus staffed by RSS pracharaks. The continuity was of personnel and institutions; the name, the symbol and, initially, the stated philosophy, Gandhian socialism, were new.

### Q: What was the Jana Sangh's electoral performance?

Across five general elections, recorded in the Election Commission of India's statistical reports, the Jana Sangh grew from marginality to the position of largest opposition party and then contracted. In 1951-52 the party contested 94 seats, won 3, and took 3.06 percent of the national vote. In 1957 it won 4 seats on 5.93 percent. In 1962 it won 14 seats on 6.44 percent, with strength concentrating in Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Delhi. Its peak came in 1967, with 35 seats on 9.31 percent, six of Delhi's seven parliamentary seats, and 78 assembly seats in Madhya Pradesh, followed by participation in Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalition governments across the Hindi belt. In 1971, contesting inside the Grand Alliance against Indira Gandhi, the party won 22 seats on 7.35 percent. Its candidates won 93 seats as part of the Janata Party in 1977.

### Q: Why did Syama Prasad Mukherjee resign from Nehru's cabinet in April 1950?

Mukherjee resigned as Minister for Industry and Supply in protest against the Nehru-Liaquat Pact, the agreement on minority protection that Jawaharlal Nehru signed with Pakistan's prime minister Liaquat Ali Khan at Delhi on 8 April 1950. The pact committed both governments to protecting their minorities after the violence and exodus that had driven large numbers of Hindus from East Pakistan into West Bengal in the early months of 1950. Mukherjee held that the agreement failed the East Pakistani Hindus in substance, since it relied on the assurances of the government whose failure to protect them had caused the exodus, and that India had surrendered the leverage of reciprocal pressure without gaining enforceable guarantees. His resignation, delivered within days of the signing, made him the senior-most figure outside the Congress and cleared the ground for his founding of the Jana Sangh the following year. The resignation also fixed the party's founding constituency: the refugees and the integration question.

### Q: What programme did the Jana Sangh adopt at its founding in 1951?

The Delhi founding convention of 21 October 1951 adopted an eight-point programme that the party's manifestos elaborated across the phase. Its constitutional planks demanded the full integration of Jammu and Kashmir with the abrogation of Article 370, a uniform civil code for all citizens, and a central law banning cow slaughter. Its economic planks demanded swadeshi production, protection for village and small industry, decentralisation of economic power, and land reform that carried land to the tiller within ceilings. Its foreign-policy planks demanded a firm posture toward Pakistan and China, full diplomatic relations with Israel, and distance from the Soviet bloc. Its social planks included the rehabilitation of refugees from Pakistan as a charge on the state. Every demand was framed as actionable through Parliament by statute or constitutional amendment, a feature the party's defenders cited against the charge that its programme was religious rather than political, and its critics answered that the uniform vocabulary concealed a majoritarian substance.

### Q: How did the RSS and the Jana Sangh divide organisational work after 1951?

The division fixed at the founding assigned elections to the Jana Sangh and cadre reproduction to the RSS, with personnel flowing between the two under a deputation system. The Jana Sangh was a formally open, membership-based party that contested elections, ran parliamentary work and published political programmes. The RSS remained a non-electoral body of shakhas, daily local assemblies that trained swayamsevaks in its own discipline. The Sangh deputed full-time organisers, pracharaks, to staff the party's apparatus, Deendayal Upadhyay as general secretary being the founding case, and the sarsanghchalak's approval governed the deputations. The party's allied fronts replicated the pattern in student politics through the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad and in labour through the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh from 1955. Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle documented the arrangement in *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987) as a standing personnel system. The division's consequence appeared in 1977: the Jana Sangh could merge into the Janata Party while the cadre channel, never the party's property, survived outside the merger.

### Q: Why did Mauli Chandra Sharma resign the Jana Sangh presidency in 1954?

Mauli Chandra Sharma, a leader of the older political generation who took the presidency after Mukherjee's death, resigned in 1954 after the party's working committee was reconstituted in a manner that placed its organisational offices in the hands of RSS pracharaks and reduced the weight of the career politicians Sharma represented. Sharma's resignation charged that the party was being governed from outside its own elected structures. The Sangh's defenders answered that a party whose district machinery was staffed by deputed organisers could not be directed against the organisers' judgement, and that the reconstitution merely aligned the committee with the party's real composition. Bruce Graham, in *Hindu Nationalism and Indian Politics* (1990), treated the episode as the moment the post-Mukherjee leadership passed from politicians to the RSS cadre stream. Its institutional consequence was permanent: no later succession reopened the question, and every subsequent president, from Prem Nath Dogra to L. K. Advani, came from within the cadre consensus the 1954 settlement established.

### Q: What were the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalitions of 1967?

The Samyukta Vidhayak Dal, or united legislators' front, was the form taken by non-Congress government formation after the 1967 elections left the Congress without a majority in eight states. The fronts assembled every party willing to combine against the Congress in a state assembly, regardless of ideology, and the Jana Sangh participated in several as a leading component. In Madhya Pradesh, where the party held 78 assembly seats, the defecting Congress leader Govind Narayan Singh took office on 30 July 1967 at the head of an SVD coalition in which the Jana Sangh was the largest constituent, holding office until March 1969. In Bihar, the SVD government of March 1967 under Mahamaya Prasad Sinha joined the socialist parties and the Communist Party of India with the Jana Sangh in one cabinet. Further fronts governed in Uttar Pradesh under Charan Singh, in Haryana and in Punjab, where the Jana Sangh governed with the Akali Dal. Most fell within two years to defections and partner withdrawals.

### Q: Which states formed the Jana Sangh's strongest regional bases?

The party's strength concentrated in the Hindi belt and in Delhi, a geography set by the founding units of 1951 and deepened deliberately across the phase. Madhya Pradesh was the strongest base: the party won 78 of the state's 296 assembly seats in 1967, was the largest constituent of the SVD government that followed, and held municipal office in Indore, Bhopal and Gwalior. Uttar Pradesh supplied the second base, built through district towns and the politics of the state's western and central constituencies. Rajasthan returned one of the party's three members in 1952, Uma Shankar Trivedi from Chittor, and remained a steady contributor. Delhi formed a base of a different kind: the party won six of the capital's seven parliamentary seats in 1967 and controlled the Metropolitan Council and the Municipal Corporation. Punjab gave the party a coalition-tested presence alongside the Akali Dal. Bengal, Mukherjee's own ground, faded after 1953, and the south remained, across the whole phase, the party's acknowledged weakness.

### Q: What did the Jana Sangh demand on cow protection in November 1966?

The campaign of November 1966 demanded a central law banning the slaughter of cows across India, a demand the Jana Sangh carried in its programme with a textual anchor in the Directive Principles, which direct the state to organise animal husbandry and prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle. On 7 November 1966 a procession organised by the religious bodies of the Sarvadaliya Goraksha Maha-Abhiyan Samiti, with the Jana Sangh among its political supporters, marched on Parliament in Delhi in numbers the press estimated in the hundreds of thousands. When a section of the crowd pressed toward the Parliament House complex, the police fired; the official toll stated in Parliament was eight dead and over a hundred injured, and the Home Minister, Gulzari Lal Nanda, resigned within days. The party drew a durable lesson from the episode: a religious mobilisation outside party discipline produced scale without negotiability, and the Jana Sangh contested the February 1967 election on its programme and apparatus instead.

### Q: What part did the Jana Sangh play in the underground resistance to the Emergency?

When the Emergency was proclaimed on 25 June 1975, the Jana Sangh's leadership was detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, L. K. Advani and Atal Bihari Vajpayee being arrested at Bangalore, and the RSS was banned on 4 July 1975. The resistance that organised through 1975 and 1976 ran substantially on the infrastructure the Sangh network had built: the Lok Sangharsh Samiti, the underground's coordinating body, drew on RSS and Jana Sangh organisers for courier lines, safe houses, cyclostyled bulletins and the distribution of banned literature, and Nanaji Deshmukh was among its central organisers until his arrest. Sympathetic accounts, including Andersen and Damle's, make the network the resistance's backbone; critical accounts note the socialist, Sarvodaya and trade-union circuits that operated independently of it. The share is disputed; the scale is not. The phase consequence is also undisputed: the party entered the 1977 merger carrying a national reputation for resistance that its electoral record alone had never earned.

### Q: Why was Balraj Madhok expelled from the Jana Sangh in 1973?

Balraj Madhok, a founder of the party who had organised its Punjab and Delhi units in 1951 and served as president through the 1967 election victory, was expelled in 1973 by the party president L. K. Advani for three years on grounds of indiscipline, after a long estrangement from the party's dominant group. Madhok had pressed a strategy of a wider right bloc, aligned with the Swatantra Party and with Congress dissidents after the 1969 split, and had publicly charged the leadership with a leftward drift in economic policy and with subordinating the party to the RSS's organisational preferences. The leadership answered that a president's public campaign against his own party's settled line, pursued after internal defeats, was a disciplinary matter under the party constitution. Madhok's later writings charged that the RSS leadership engineered the expulsion through Advani, a charge the party's account rejects. Advani, in later years, described the act plainly as the sacking of a founder, and the institution continued without a split.

### Q: What pledge on a uniform civil code did the Jana Sangh make in its 1967 manifesto?

The Jana Sangh's 1967 manifesto pledged that the party would enact a uniform civil code if it came to power, carrying into an election document a demand the party had held since its founding programme of 1951. The pledge placed the code inside the party's integration argument: a single citizenship, the party held, required a single civil law governing marriage, inheritance and adoption for all Indians, replacing the separate personal laws of the religious communities. The demand had a parliamentary history by 1967. Mukherjee had intervened in the first Lok Sabha's Hindu Code debates against the selective reform of Hindu personal law alone, and the party's defenders argued that the Hindu Code Acts of 1955 and 1956, which codified and reformed Hindu law by statute, left the code's remaining demand falling on the unreformed remainder. Critics answered that the asymmetry of the demand's incidence made it majoritarian in effect whatever its vocabulary. The pledge recurred in the party's later manifestos and passed, with the rest of the programme, into the BJP's platform after 1980.

### Q: How did the Jana Sangh govern Delhi through the Metropolitan Council after 1967?

The 1967 elections gave the Jana Sangh six of Delhi's seven Lok Sabha seats and control of both tiers of the capital's local government, the Delhi Metropolitan Council created under the Delhi Administration Act of 1966 and the Municipal Corporation. Vijay Kumar Malhotra, the party's leading Delhi figure, became the Metropolitan Council's chief executive councillor, the effective head of the capital's administration below the Lieutenant Governor. The party used the office as its governing showroom: the council years were presented in the party's literature as proof that the cadre party could administer, run budgets and manage a metropolis's services, against the Congress charge that the Jana Sangh was a party of agitation only. Delhi also functioned as the party's national stage, since the capital's verdicts were read as all-India signals, and as its municipal ladder in miniature, with councillors graduating to assembly and parliamentary candidacies. The party's Delhi organisation, built from the unit Madhok organised in May 1951, remained its most complete urban machine across the phase.

### Q: Which ministries did Jana Sangh leaders hold in the Janata government of 1977?

The former Jana Sangh held two of the Janata government's most visible portfolios. Atal Bihari Vajpayee became Minister of External Affairs, conducting the government's foreign policy reset, addressing the United Nations General Assembly in Hindi in October 1977, and travelling to Beijing in February 1979, the first visit by an Indian foreign minister to China since the 1962 war. L. K. Advani became Minister of Information and Broadcasting, where he dismantled the Emergency's censorship machinery and piloted the repeal of the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, converting the movement's underground press record into legislation. Brij Lal Varma, another leader of Jana Sangh origin, held the Industry portfolio. The contingent's ministers left the government's collapse of 1979 with their personal standing largely intact, a fact their critics attributed to the brevity of the government's life and their defenders to the apparatus's discipline in office. The ministerial biographies passed into the BJP's founding assets in 1980.

### Q: What did Deendayal Upadhyay do as the Jana Sangh's general secretary?

Deendayal Upadhyay served as the Jana Sangh's general secretary from its founding in 1951 until 1967, the longest continuous tenure of any office in the party's phase, and the office under his hand became the party's permanent spine. An RSS pracharak deputed at the founding, Upadhyay controlled the organisational work that rotating presidents did not: the appointment and transfer of organising secretaries, the building and supervision of state units, candidate-selection machinery, worker training, and the party's internal discipline. He drafted much of the party's programmatic literature, edited its Hindi weekly *Panchjanya* in its founding years alongside Vajpayee, and authored the doctrine of Integral Humanism, delivered in the Bombay lectures of April 1965 and adopted as the party's philosophy at the Vijayawada session of January 1965. At the Calicut session of December 1967 he was elected president, uniting the two offices for the first time, and held the presidency for forty-three days before his death near Mughalsarai in February 1968. The general-secretary system he built passed intact into the BJP's organising-secretary structure after 1980.
