Every argument about a Money Bill in India is an argument about a single word. Article 110 of the Constitution provides that a Bill shall be deemed to be a Money Bill if it contains only provisions dealing with a short list of fiscal matters. The list is generous. The word only is not. If a Bill contains the listed matters and nothing else, it travels a fast track on which the Rajya Sabha, the Council of States, may recommend changes but may neither amend nor reject the Bill, and on which the directly elected Lok Sabha has the final word within a fixed and short timetable. If the Bill contains anything beyond the listed matters and matters incidental to them, the fast track is not available, the Bill must run the ordinary course, and the Council of States holds the same powers over it as the Lok Sabha, including the power to amend it, to reject it, and to force the disagreement toward a joint sitting. The classification therefore decides, before a single clause is debated, whether one of the two Houses of Parliament has a real say over a statute or only a voice.

That is why the Money Bill question has become the most consequential unresolved issue in Indian legislative procedure. Several major statutes have been enacted on Bills certified as Money Bills where the certification was contested on the ground that the Bills contained substantial matter outside the Article 110 list. A Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court upheld one such certification by a majority in 2018, in the Aadhaar case. A later Constitution Bench, in 2019, doubted the reasoning of that majority, recorded that the earlier bench had not examined the provisions in the detail the question required, and referred the scope of Article 110 to a larger bench. The reference has not been decided. Until it is, the provision that determines whether the second chamber participates in making a law at all rests on a majority view that a coordinate bench has expressly questioned, and every government drafting a Bill with fiscal elements must guess how the larger bench will read one word.

This article does the work the debate usually skips. It sets out the constitutional test in full, limb by limb, including the exclusions that are quoted far less often than the inclusions. It states the special procedure under Article 109 and the two categories of Financial Bill under Article 117, which are the neighbours most often confused with a Money Bill. It supplies a decision table that applies the test to a Bill step by step and routes the Bill to its correct category, with the consequence for the Council of States stated at each outcome. It then states the contested certifications neutrally, gives the judicial record in order, presents the broad and the narrow readings of Article 110 at full strength in the voices of those who have made them, and closes with the decision the comparison earns: dimension by dimension, which instrument carries which House advantage, and which classification carries which exposure to judicial review.

## The definition: Article 110 and the word only

Article 110(1) provides that, for the purposes of the chapter of the Constitution dealing with the legislative procedure of Parliament, a Bill shall be deemed to be a Money Bill if it contains only provisions dealing with all or any of seven matters. The first matter is the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax. The second is the regulation of the borrowing of money or the giving of any guarantee by the Government of India, or the amendment of the law with respect to any financial obligations undertaken or to be undertaken by the Government of India. The third is the custody of the Consolidated Fund of India or the Contingency Fund of India, the payment of moneys into or the withdrawal of moneys from any such Fund. The fourth is the appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund of India. The fifth is the declaring of any expenditure to be expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, or the increasing of the amount of any such expenditure. The sixth is the receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund of India or the public account of India, or the custody or issue of such money, or the audit of the accounts of the Union or of a State. The seventh, in clause (g), is any matter incidental to any of the matters specified in the six clauses before it.

Three features of that text control everything that follows. The first is the word only, which sits in the opening words of the clause and qualifies the whole list. A Bill does not become a Money Bill because it contains a tax provision, or because its dominant purpose is fiscal, or because most of its clauses fall within the list. It becomes a Money Bill only if every provision it contains falls within the list or within the incidental clause. One substantive provision outside the list, if it is not incidental to a listed matter, is enough to deny the classification. The second feature is the width of clause (g). Matters incidental to taxation, borrowing, custody of the Funds, appropriation, charged expenditure, receipts and audit are inside the definition, and the contest in every litigated case has been over how far that incidental reach extends: whether a provision that creates an institution, confers an identity, restructures tribunals or rewrites a regulatory statute can be described as incidental to the spending or taxation provision it accompanies. The third feature is that the definition is exhaustive in form. Article 110 does not say that a Money Bill includes Bills dealing with the listed matters. It says a Bill shall be deemed to be a Money Bill if it contains only provisions dealing with them, which is the language of a closed test, and the litigation has been about whether the courts will enforce the closure or read the test by reference to the Bill's main object.

### Does a licence fee or a fine turn a Bill into a Money Bill?

No. Article 110(2) expressly provides that fines and other pecuniary penalties, fees for licences, fees for services rendered, and taxes imposed by a local authority for local purposes do not make a Bill a Money Bill. A regulatory Bill gains nothing toward Money Bill status from charging fees or imposing penalties.

The text of the exclusion is worth holding in full: a Bill shall not be deemed to be a Money Bill by reason only that it provides for the imposition of fines or other pecuniary penalties, or for the demand or payment of fees for licences or fees for services rendered, or by reason that it provides for the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax by a local authority or body for local purposes. A municipal tax provision in a Bill is likewise outside the definition. The exclusions close the three easiest routes by which an ordinary regulatory statute might have been dressed in fiscal clothing.

The exclusions matter beyond their immediate content, because they show the Constituent Assembly turning its mind to borderline fiscal matter and resolving the borderline against inclusion. Fines, fees for licences, fees for services and local taxation are all money in a loose sense, and a draftsman working without clause (2) might have argued that each was a matter incidental to taxation or to the receipt of money. The Constitution forecloses that argument in terms. That drafting choice is one of the strongest textual supports for the narrow reading of Article 110, because it demonstrates that where the framers wanted a fiscal matter excluded from the Money Bill category, they said so, and where they wanted a matter included, they listed it. The definition and the exclusions are a single design: a closed list, a controlled extension for incidental matters, and express carve-outs for the three fiscal imposts that belong to ordinary regulatory and local legislation.

It is worth pausing on the audit limb of clause (f), because it is the limb least noticed in public discussion and it illustrates the same design. The audit of the accounts of the Union or of a State is a listed matter, so a Bill dealing only with the audit function in relation to those accounts, or with the custody and issue of public money, sits inside the definition. The Comptroller and Auditor General's institutional statute, the Comptroller and Auditor General's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act of 1971, was not a Money Bill, because it dealt with the tenure, salary structure in its institutional aspect and conditions of service of a constitutional office, matter well beyond the audit limb itself. The distinction between a Bill about the audit of accounts and a Bill about the auditor is exactly the distinction Article 110 requires to be drawn provision by provision, and it is the distinction the contested cases have been about: whether the Bill's provisions are the listed fiscal operations themselves, or an institutional and regulatory structure that merely touches those operations at one point.

## The two Funds and the public account

The definition cannot be applied without a working map of the accounts it names. Article 266 establishes the Consolidated Fund of India, into which all revenues received by the Government of India, all loans raised by the issue of treasury bills or otherwise, and all moneys received in repayment of loans are paid, and from which no money may be withdrawn except under appropriation made by law. Article 266 also establishes the public account of India, into which all other public moneys received by or on behalf of the Government of India are credited: provident fund deposits, judicial deposits, savings deposits, departmental deposits and remittances that the government holds in a banker or trustee capacity rather than as its own revenue. Article 267 provides for the Contingency Fund of India, placed at the disposal of the President to meet unforeseen expenditure pending authorisation by Parliament, with advances later recouped from the Consolidated Fund.

### What is the difference between the Consolidated Fund and the public account?

Money in the Consolidated Fund belongs to the government and may be spent only under appropriation made by law, which is why the appropriation limb of Article 110 exists and why the annual Appropriation Bill is constitutionally indispensable. Money in the public account, by contrast, is held by the government for others.

It is not subject to appropriation or to a vote of Parliament, because paying it out is the discharge of a custody rather than an act of public spending. Article 110(1)(f) covers the receipt, custody and issue of public account money as a Money Bill matter in its own right, so a Bill confined to the mechanics of that custody can qualify, while the substantive rights of the depositors and beneficiaries whose money it is lie outside the fiscal operation and outside the definition.

The distinction explains why the fiscal fast track exists and why it is confined. Taxation, borrowing, appropriation and charged expenditure are the points at which the state takes money from the citizen and commits money to purposes. The Constitution gives the House that represents taxpayers directly the controlling voice at those points, for the same reason that supply in the Westminster tradition belongs to the Commons. But custody mechanics, audit mechanics and receipts are included only as operations on the Funds. A Bill that used the public account as the vehicle for a general scheme of entitlements, identity or regulation would be doing something different in kind from regulating the custody of money, and the question the courts have had to face is whether the incidental clause can stretch from the operation to the scheme. On the text, the operation is listed and the scheme is not, which is why the word only keeps returning to the centre of the argument.

Charged expenditure completes the map. Article 112(3) lists expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund: the emoluments of the President, the salaries of the presiding officers of the two Houses, the salaries and pensions of Supreme Court judges, the salary and pension of the Comptroller and Auditor General, debt charges, sums required to satisfy judgments and awards, and any other expenditure declared by the Constitution or by Parliament by law to be so charged. Charged expenditure is not submitted to the vote of the Lok Sabha, although it may be discussed, because the independence of the offices and obligations it funds is meant to stand outside annual majorities. Article 110(1)(e) makes the declaring of expenditure to be charged, or the increasing of charged expenditure, a Money Bill matter, so the creation of a new charged item travels the fast track. That is a significant power to place beyond the amending reach of the Council of States, and it is one reason the classification question is not a technicality: a certificate decides not only how fast a Bill moves, but whether the second chamber can touch provisions that remove spending from annual voting altogether.

## The special procedure: Article 109

### Can a Money Bill be introduced in the Rajya Sabha?

No. A Money Bill may be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and only on the recommendation of the President under Article 117(1), a recommendation that in practice is the decision of the Council of Ministers, which is why a Money Bill is in substance always a government Bill.

Article 107(1) and Article 109 together confine a Money Bill to the House of the People, and Article 117(1) adds that a Bill making provision for any of the Article 110 matters shall not be introduced except on that recommendation. After the Lok Sabha has passed it, the Bill is transmitted to the Council of States for its recommendations. The Council must return the Bill within fourteen days from the date of its receipt, with or without recommendations. If the Lok Sabha accepts any of the recommendations, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses with the amendments recommended and accepted. If the Lok Sabha accepts none, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form in which the Lok Sabha passed it, without any of the amendments recommended by the Council. If the Council does not return the Bill within the fourteen days, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses, at the expiration of that period, in the form passed by the Lok Sabha.

Every element of that procedure is a deliberate subtraction from the ordinary powers of a second chamber. An ordinary Bill may be introduced in either House, may be amended by either, may be rejected by either, and if the Houses disagree, Article 108 permits the President to summon a joint sitting at which the larger House's numbers ordinarily prevail but at which the Bill can still be defeated or amended. A Money Bill admits none of that. There is no joint sitting for a Money Bill, because Article 108 does not extend to it. There is no power of amendment in the Council, only a power of recommendation that the Lok Sabha may discard without consequence. There is no power of delay beyond fourteen days, after which the Bill passes in the Lok Sabha's form by constitutional deeming. And at the assent stage, Article 111 gives the President a further limited role: the President may return a Bill for reconsideration, but the proviso to Article 111 withholds that power for a Money Bill, which the President must either assent to or withhold assent from, without the option of sending it back.

The fourteen day period deserves attention because it is the only interval in the whole design in which the Council of States holds the Bill at all. Within it, the Council debates, may appoint its select processes in the limited form its rules allow for recommendations, and returns its views. The debate is not pointless: recommendations from the Council have on occasion been accepted by the Lok Sabha, and the discussion creates a public record on fiscal legislation. But the constitutional position is that the Council's participation is advisory in the strict sense. Its consent is not a condition of the Bill becoming law, its amendments have no force unless the Lok Sabha adopts them, and its silence has the same legal effect as its approval. When critics of a contested certification say that the Rajya Sabha was bypassed, this is the precise legal content of the complaint: not that the Council was denied a courtesy, but that a statute which in their submission required its consent under the ordinary procedure became law without that consent being constitutionally necessary.

### What does the Speaker's endorsement on the Bill actually record?

It records the decision that determines the whole procedure. Article 110(3) provides that if any question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill or not, the decision of the Speaker of the House of the People on the question shall be final. The endorsement of that decision on the Bill is the certificate.

Article 110(4) provides that there shall be endorsed on every Money Bill, when it is transmitted to the Council of States under Article 109 and when it is presented to the President for assent under Article 111, the certificate of the Speaker, signed by the Speaker, that it is a Money Bill. The endorsement is therefore made twice in the Bill's journey, once when the Bill leaves the Lok Sabha for the Council and once when it goes for assent, and its content is a single proposition of classification with large procedural consequences attached.

The certificate is not a statement of the Bill's contents and it gives no reasons. It does not identify which clauses fall under which limb of Article 110(1), it does not address clause (g) separately, and it does not record any consideration of the exclusions in clause (2). That economy of form is itself part of the controversy. Defenders of the design point out that the Speaker is a constitutional authority, that classification is a question arising within the proceedings of the House, and that Article 122 protects the validity of parliamentary proceedings from challenge on the ground of irregularity of procedure. Critics answer that a decision recorded without reasons, by an office holder who in the Indian practice retains party affiliation in a way the British Speaker does not, determines the rights of the other House, and that the absence of a reasoned certificate makes judicial review harder to mount and easier to evade. Both descriptions of the same document are accurate as far as they go, and the pending reference will have to decide how much constitutional weight a one line certificate can bear.


## The neighbours: Financial Bills under Article 117

The commonest confusion in this territory, in examination answers and in journalism alike, is the treatment of Money Bill and Financial Bill as two names for one thing, or of the annual Finance Bill as a third constitutional category. The Constitution's own structure is cleaner than the usage. Article 110 defines the Money Bill. Article 117 then provides for two kinds of Bill that are not Money Bills but carry special rules because they touch the same fiscal ground, and the annual Finance Bill is not a constitutional category at all but the name of the Bill through which the government's taxation proposals for the year are enacted, a Bill which is in practice introduced and certified as a Money Bill.

A Financial Bill of the first kind is a Bill that contains any of the matters in the Article 110(1) list together with other matters. Article 117(1) governs it, and its text reaches beyond Money Bill matter to a Bill making provision for any of the matters specified in Article 110(1)(a) to (f). Such a Bill shares two features with a Money Bill: it shall not be introduced or moved except on the recommendation of the President, and it shall not be introduced in the Council of States. It must begin in the Lok Sabha, on the government's initiative in substance. There the resemblance ends. A Financial Bill of the first kind is otherwise passed like an ordinary Bill. The Council of States may amend it, may reject it, and may insist on its amendments, and if the two Houses disagree, Article 108 applies and a joint sitting may be summoned. The President may return it for reconsideration under Article 111, because it is not a Money Bill. No Speaker's certificate attaches to it, because its classification raises no Article 110 question: the presence of matter beyond the list is the very thing that defines it, and that presence restores the full bicameral procedure.

A Financial Bill of the second kind is a Bill that contains no Article 110 matter at all in the strict sense, but which, if enacted and brought into operation, would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India. Article 117(3) governs it: a Bill which, if enacted, would involve such expenditure shall not be passed by either House of Parliament unless the President has recommended to that House the consideration of the Bill. This category may be introduced in either House, with or without the President's recommendation at the introduction stage; the recommendation is a condition of passage, not of introduction. It travels as an ordinary Bill in every other respect: amendable and rejectable in both Houses, open to a joint sitting on disagreement, and returnable by the President. Spending Bills of this kind are common, because any statute that creates a programme, an authority or a benefit payable from the Consolidated Fund falls within it, and the constitutional control is deliberately light: the executive's financial initiative is protected by the recommendation requirement, but the bicameral process is untouched.

The annual Finance Bill and the Appropriation Bill belong to the budget procedure of Articles 112 to 116 rather than to the classification scheme of Articles 110 and 117, and keeping the two schemes apart resolves most of the confusion. The President causes the annual financial statement to be laid before both Houses under Article 112. The estimates are voted as demands for grants by the Lok Sabha alone under Article 113, charged expenditure being discussable but not votable. The Appropriation Bill is then introduced under Article 114 to authorise the withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of the moneys voted and charged; no amendment may be proposed to it that varies the amount or alters the destination of a grant or varies charged expenditure, and it is in practice certified as a Money Bill, since appropriation is squarely within Article 110(1)(d). The Finance Bill gives effect to the taxation proposals of the budget: it is introduced immediately after the budget presentation, the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act allows its tax proposals to take effect from the start of the financial year pending enactment, and it must be passed within the period that statute and the budget timetable allow. Its route is the Money Bill route because its contents are, in the standard case, taxation and matters incidental to taxation. The difficulty of the last decade has arisen when the Finance Bill has carried, alongside its tax clauses, provisions amending regulatory statutes, restructuring tribunals, or altering the law of political funding and foreign contributions, so that the Bill certified as a Money Bill contained long stretches of matter with no evident home in Article 110(1)(a) to (f).

### How long does the full Money Bill journey take from introduction to assent?

The constitutional timetable can be completed inside three weeks. A Money Bill is introduced in the Lok Sabha on the President's recommendation, debated and passed there, transmitted to the Council of States with the Speaker's certificate endorsed on it, and returned or deemed returned inside fourteen days of its receipt by the Council.

It then goes to the President with the certificate endorsed again, and assent completes the enactment. No stage outside the Lok Sabha can extend the journey, because the Lok Sabha stage itself moves at the speed the House's majority and timetable allow, because the Council's fourteen days run whether or not it acts, and because the President has no power to return the Bill for reconsideration.

Set against that timetable, the journey of a Financial Bill of the first kind or an ordinary Bill has no constitutional clock at all. Each House controls its own consideration, a select committee may take months, the second House may amend and insist, and a joint sitting requires the disagreement conditions of Article 108 to mature, including the passage of time where a Bill is not passed by the other House. The speed of the Money Bill route is therefore not an incidental convenience. It is a structural advantage that a government with a Lok Sabha majority and no majority in the Council of States has every incentive to use, which is precisely why the boundary of Article 110 is policed, if it is policed at all, by the courts rather than by Parliament itself. A majority that benefits from a generous classification will not vote to narrow it, and the presiding officer who certifies is elected by that majority, so the institutional checks internal to Parliament point in one direction. That structural fact, more than any single statute, explains why the question reached the Supreme Court and why the Court's answer matters.

## Apply the test: the decision table

The findable artifact for this article is the test itself, set out as the sequence of questions a reader must ask of any Bill whose classification is in issue. Read the Bill clause by clause. Ask of each clause which limb of Article 110(1) it falls under, treating clause (g) as available only where the clause is genuinely incidental to a listed matter. Then apply the exclusions. Then ask the decisive question whether anything remains outside. The route and the consequence for the Council of States follow mechanically, which is the point: the argument in the cases is almost never about the routing, and almost always about the clause by clause classification that feeds it.

| Step | Question to ask of the Bill | If the answer is yes | If the answer is no |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does every clause fall within Article 110(1)(a) to (f), or within clause (g) as a matter incidental to one of those clauses? | Continue to step 2 | The Bill is not a Money Bill; go to step 4 |
| 2 | Is any clause inside the list only because it imposes fines or pecuniary penalties, charges fees for licences or services, or deals with a tax levied by a local authority for local purposes? | That clause does not count toward Money Bill status under Article 110(2); retest the Bill at step 1 without it | Continue to step 3 |
| 3 | Does the Bill contain anything beyond the listed matters and matters incidental to them? | The Bill is not a Money Bill; go to step 4 | Money Bill: Lok Sabha only, President's recommendation, Speaker's certificate, Council of States recommends within fourteen days and cannot amend or reject, no joint sitting, President cannot return it |
| 4 | Does the Bill contain any Article 110(1)(a) to (f) matter together with other matter? | Financial Bill of the first kind under Article 117(1): Lok Sabha only, President's recommendation, otherwise ordinary passage, Council of States may amend or reject, joint sitting available, President may return it | Continue to step 5 |
| 5 | Would the Bill, if enacted, involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India? | Financial Bill of the second kind under Article 117(3): either House may introduce it, President's recommendation required before passage, otherwise ordinary passage with full powers in both Houses | Ordinary Bill: no special fiscal rule applies |
| 6 | At any route, is the classification certified by the Speaker as a Money Bill although step 3 was answered yes? | The certificate is open to judicial review on the ground of illegality or unconstitutionality, and the scope of that review is the question referred to a larger bench in 2019 and awaiting decision | The classification stands on the procedure of its own category |

Two features of the table are worth stating in prose. The first is that the test is applied to the Bill as introduced and passed, provision by provision, and the unit of analysis is the clause, not the Bill's title, its statement of objects, or its dominant purpose. A Bill whose long title announces a fiscal object but whose operative clauses build a regulatory institution fails step 3 on the clauses, however fiscal the preamble sounds. The second is that clause (g) does the contested work. Nobody litigates about a pure tax Bill or a pure appropriation Bill. The litigation is about how much institutional and regulatory matter can be carried as incidental to a tax, a fund or an appropriation, and the table cannot answer that question because the Constitution's text gives the adjective and no measure. That unmeasured adjective, read with the word only, is the whole of the pending reference.

## The contested certifications

The objection in each of the following instances is a matter of record and is stated here as what was done, without any finding about motive. The pattern is constant: a Bill was certified as a Money Bill, or carried in the annual Finance Bill on the Money Bill route, and the challenge has been that identified provisions of the Bill lay outside Article 110(1)(a) to (f) and were not incidental to any of them, so that the certification was not available and the enactment was passed without the consent of the Council of States that the Constitution required.

The Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act of 2016 is the central instance. The Aadhaar Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha as a Money Bill and certified accordingly. The enacted statute established a statutory authority, the Unique Identification Authority of India, created a system of unique identity numbers for residents, provided for enrolment and authentication, and attached legal consequences to the possession and use of the number across public and, in the enacted design, private contexts. Section 7 of the Act made the receipt of subsidies, benefits and services funded from the Consolidated Fund conditional on authentication or proof of possession of an Aadhaar number, and it was on Section 7 and the expenditure it governed that the certification was defended: the argument was that the Act's provisions were directed at the targeted delivery of money drawn from the Consolidated Fund and were incidental to that expenditure. The objection was that the greater part of the Act, the identity infrastructure itself, the authority, the enrolment machinery, the authentication regime and the offences, had no necessary connection with any single item of expenditure and constituted a general identity law that happened to include one spending provision. The Council of States returned the Bill with recommendations, including amendments that would have narrowed the scheme, and the Lok Sabha rejected the recommendations, as Article 109 entitled it to do. The constitutional history of the Aadhaar Act, including the certification litigation that followed, is carried in the series at [the Puttaswamy and Aadhaar judgment article](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/), which treats the privacy and money bill strands of that litigation together.

The Finance Act of 2017 supplied the second instance and the one that produced the reference. Part XIV of that Act amended a series of existing statutes governing tribunals and appellate bodies, merged and restructured a number of them, and by Section 184 empowered the Central Government to make rules governing the qualifications, appointment, tenure, salaries and conditions of service of the chairpersons and members of the tribunals listed in the Eighth Schedule to the Act. The objection was that the administration of tribunals, the composition of adjudicatory bodies and the service conditions of their members are not taxation, borrowing, fund custody, appropriation, charged expenditure, receipts or audit, and are not incidental to any of them, so that a Finance Act containing Part XIV could not be a Money Bill in its entirety and the tribunal provisions had been enacted without the Council of States being able to amend or reject them. The Finance Act 2017 tribunal restructuring and its aftermath in the Tribunals Reforms legislation are treated in the series at [the Tribunals Reforms Act article](/2021/08/15/tribunals-reforms-act-2021-explained/), and the general passage of a Bill through both Houses, against which these special routes are exceptions, is set out at [the article on how a Bill becomes law in India](/2022/02/01/how-a-bill-becomes-law-india/).

The Finance Acts also carried the political funding amendments. The Finance Act of 2017 amended the Representation of the People Act of 1951 to remove the requirement that a political party disclose contributions above the threshold in a way that identified donors under the new scheme, amended the Income Tax Act of 1961 in relation to political contributions and cash donations, amended the Reserve Bank of India Act of 1934 to enable the issue of electoral bonds, and amended the Companies Act of 2013 to remove the cap on corporate donations expressed as a proportion of profits and the obligation to disclose the recipient party in the profit and loss account. The amendments to the foreign contribution law travelled the same route in adjacent years: the Finance Act of 2016 amended the definition of foreign source in the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act of 2010 with retrospective effect reaching back to 2010, and the Finance Act of 2018 carried the retrospective reach further back to 1976, the year of the earlier foreign contribution statute, in each case altering the legal position of donations that a court had examined under the unamended law. The objection to each of these was the same in form: electoral law, company law and the regulation of foreign donations are not within Article 110(1), and amending them through a certified Money Bill removed the Council of States from legislation on the funding of the political system itself. The electoral bonds scheme and the statutory framework it built are treated in the series at [the electoral bonds article](/2018/02/15/electoral-bonds-law-india-explained/).

The amendments to the Prevention of Money Laundering Act of 2002 form the fourth instance. Between 2015 and 2019, a series of Finance Acts amended the money laundering statute repeatedly: the amendments widened the definition of proceeds of crime, altered the predicate offence architecture, changed the procedure of attachment and adjudication, and adjusted the powers of the enforcement authorities, each time through the Money Bill route as clauses of the annual Finance Bill. The objection taken in the challenges to those amendments was that a criminal enforcement statute regulating the proceeds of crime is not within Article 110(1), that its amendments could not ride on the Finance Bill's certificate, and that the validity of the amendments depended on the money bill question being answered in the government's favour. The Supreme Court's consideration of the money laundering statute, and the place of the money bill objection inside that litigation, is treated in the series at [the PMLA judgment article](/2013/03/15/pmla-supreme-court-judgment/). In the 2022 judgment in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v Union of India, the Court upheld the challenged provisions of the money laundering statute on their merits and left the money bill challenge to the amendments unanswered on the ground that the question stood referred to a larger bench, so the amendments stand, their route unexamined, until the reference is decided.

## What the Rajya Sabha can and cannot do, category by category

The comparison the title promises can be reduced to the position of the second chamber under each route, because that is where the categories diverge in consequence. Over a Money Bill, the Council of States can debate, can recommend amendments, and must finish within fourteen days; it cannot amend, cannot reject, cannot delay beyond the period, cannot trigger a joint sitting, and cannot make its consent a condition of enactment. Its recommendations return to a Lok Sabha that is free to accept every one, some, or none, and the Bill that emerges is the Bill the Lok Sabha chooses. Over a Financial Bill of the first kind, the Council's position is transformed: the Bill begins in the Lok Sabha, as a Money Bill does, but once transmitted it is an ordinary Bill in the Council's hands, amendable clause by clause, rejectable in whole, and capable of producing the disagreement that Article 108 resolves by joint sitting. Over a Financial Bill of the second kind, the Council's position is stronger still in one respect, since the Bill may originate in the Council itself. Over an ordinary Bill the two Houses are, subject to the joint sitting arithmetic, equals.

The executive's position moves in the opposite direction across the same categories. A Money Bill requires the President's recommendation and is therefore a government instrument, but once certified it is beyond the reach of the second chamber and of presidential return, and it moves on a clock. A Financial Bill of the first kind also requires the recommendation, protecting the government's financial initiative against private members and against the Council originating fiscal matter, but it exposes the government to amendment, rejection and delay in the chamber it may not control. A Financial Bill of the second kind protects the initiative least: the recommendation is needed only for passage, the Bill may begin in either House, and the government must carry both Houses in the ordinary way. An ordinary Bill carries no fiscal privilege at all. A government choosing the vehicle for a measure is therefore choosing a distribution of power, and where the measure contains both fiscal and non fiscal matter, the Constitution has already made the choice: the presence of the non fiscal matter routes the Bill to Article 117(1), and the Money Bill route is not on the menu. The entire controversy exists because, in the contested instances, the routing decision was taken the other way at the certification stage, and the only institution positioned to correct the routing was the court.


## The courts: finality, review and the reference

The doctrinal core of the controversy is the meaning of the word final in Article 110(3). The earlier line of authority treated the Speaker's decision as conclusive in the strong sense. In Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui v State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 2014 on the corresponding provision for state legislatures in Article 199, a three judge bench of the Supreme Court held that the Speaker's decision that a Bill was a Money Bill could not be challenged in court, resting on the finality clause and on the protection of legislative proceedings. On that view, the certificate closed the question, and a statute enacted on the Money Bill route could not be attacked for having taken the wrong route. The position has since moved. In the Aadhaar judgment, the majority accepted that the Speaker's decision, although declared final by Article 110(3), is open to judicial review where it is challenged on grounds of illegality or unconstitutionality, the finality clause protecting the decision against challenge for mere procedural irregularity rather than against constitutional scrutiny. Finality, on the settled reading after 2018, governs the relations between the Speaker and the Houses; it does not place a classification that determines the law making power of a House beyond the reach of the courts.

The Aadhaar decision itself, K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar-5J) v Union of India, reported at (2019) 1 SCC 1, was delivered in September 2018 by a Constitution Bench of five judges. The majority, in the judgment delivered by Sikri J. for a bench majority of four, upheld the certification of the Aadhaar Bill as a Money Bill. The majority reasoned from Section 7 of the Act: the Act's purpose was the targeted delivery of subsidies, benefits and services financed from the Consolidated Fund of India, expenditure within Article 110(1), and the remaining provisions, the authority, the enrolment and authentication machinery and the identity infrastructure, were incidental to that expenditure purpose within clause (g), being the machinery by which the expenditure was delivered and its leakage prevented. On that reasoning the Bill contained only provisions dealing with listed matters and matters incidental to them, and the certificate stood. Chandrachud J., dissenting, held that the Aadhaar Act contained substantial provisions unrelated to the enumerated categories, that the identity architecture was the substance of the Act and Section 7 a small part of it, that treating the whole as incidental to one spending provision inverted the test in Article 110, and that passing the Act as a Money Bill had bypassed the Rajya Sabha in a manner he described as a fraud on the Constitution. The dissent would have held the enactment unconstitutional for want of valid passage.

Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank Ltd, decided in November 2019 by a Constitution Bench of five judges presided over by Gogoi C.J., arose on the Finance Act of 2017 and its tribunal provisions. The Court struck down the rules framed under Section 184 of that Act on other grounds and addressed the money bill question directly. The bench recorded its difficulty with the Aadhaar majority's treatment: the majority in the Aadhaar case had not examined the provisions of that Act in the detail the classification question required, had not laid down a clear test for the reach of the incidental clause, and had left the interplay between Article 110 and the bicameral scheme insufficiently analysed. The bench therefore referred the issue and question of the Money Bill, as defined under Article 110(1), and the certification accorded by the Speaker in respect of Part XIV of the Finance Act of 2017, to a larger bench. The reference is a reference of the question, not a decision on it: the Finance Act of 2017 was not held invalid, the Aadhaar certification was not overruled, and the tribunal provisions continued in force on their other footing. As the record stands, no larger bench decision on the referred question has been delivered, and the reference remains pending and undecided.

That pending status is the single most important fact in the field and the one most often omitted from accounts of it. Its consequences are concrete. Every statute and amendment carried on a contested certificate, the Aadhaar Act, the Finance Act 2017 tribunal provisions, the political funding amendments, the foreign contribution amendments and the money laundering amendments among them, rests on a classification whose governing authority has been doubted by a coordinate bench and whose correctness awaits a larger bench. Litigants raise the money bill objection and courts note it and pass on, as the Court did in the money laundering litigation in 2022, because the answer is reserved. Governments continue to draft and to certify under the Aadhaar majority's approach, because it remains the operative authority until displaced. And the Council of States continues to receive certified Bills under a procedure whose constitutional boundary the Supreme Court has said, in terms, needs a larger bench to settle. None of that is a prediction about the reference. It is a description of a legal system working, for an interval that has already run for years, with its central procedural question formally open.

## The two readings at full strength

The case for the broad reading begins with the text of clause (g). The framers included matters incidental to the listed categories, and a modern fiscal statute cannot be severed into fiscal and non fiscal atoms: a subsidy statute needs a delivery mechanism, an expenditure programme needs an administering authority, a tax needs a collection and adjudication machinery, and each piece of machinery will look, in isolation, like institutional law rather than finance. If every institutional provision defeats the certificate, the incidental clause does no work and governments must split every finance measure into a Money Bill and a trail of ordinary Bills moving at different speeds through two Houses, with the spending authorised in one session and its machinery enacted, if the Council of States agrees, in another. The broad reading also rests on the institutional argument that the Constitution assigned classification to the Speaker and declared the decision final because classification disputes are internal to the legislative process: the alternative is litigation over the route of every contested Bill, with statutes suspended in uncertainty while courts reclassify them clause by clause, a function for which courts are not designed and which Article 122's protection of proceedings discourages. Its supporters add that the Council of States is an indirectly elected chamber whose members represent states through their legislatures, and that the democratic claim of the directly elected House to control taxation and supply, the claim on which the whole of Article 109 rests, supports resolving borderline classification in favour of the Lok Sabha's route rather than against it. The Aadhaar majority's reasoning is the judicial form of this case: identify the expenditure or fiscal core, and treat the machinery as incidental to it.

The case for the narrow reading begins where the broad reading does not, with the word only. Article 110(1) does not ask whether a Bill's main object is fiscal, or whether its fiscal provisions are its most important, or whether its non fiscal provisions can be described as machinery for a fiscal end. It asks whether the Bill contains only provisions dealing with the listed matters and matters incidental to them, and a provision that creates a general identity system, restructures the tribunal sector, rewrites the law governing donations to political parties or amends a criminal enforcement statute is, on the narrow view, not incidental to a tax or an appropriation in any sense the word will bear: it is a freestanding legislative object travelling under a fiscal passport. The narrow reading draws structural support from the design of bicameralism itself. Article 110 exists as an exception to the ordinary rule that legislation requires the agreement of both Houses, and an exception that can be expanded by the very majority that benefits from it, certified by an officer that majority elects, ceases to be an exception and becomes an option. The finality clause, on this view, settles who decides as between the Speaker and the Houses and protects the decision from attack for irregularity; it was never designed to protect a classification that removes a House from the legislative process from review for illegality, and the Aadhaar majority itself accepted review of that kind even while upholding the certificate before it. Chandrachud J.'s dissent is the judicial form of this case, including its sharpest point: that a device which lets the Lok Sabha legislate alone on any subject by attaching a spending provision does not merely stretch Article 110 but negates the Council of States as a legislative chamber for whole fields of law. Between these two readings the larger bench must choose, and the choice will be made on the text of clause (g) and the word only, on the structure of Articles 107 to 111 taken together, and on how much institutional weight the Court is willing to place on an unreasoned certificate. This article predicts nothing about that choice.

## Where the design came from

The Money Bill is not an Indian invention, and its genealogy explains both its shape and the anxieties it produces. The principle that the upper House does not control supply is a product of the long conflict between the British Commons and Lords over taxation, settled in the Commons' favour by practice and then, after the Lords rejected the budget of 1909, by statute. The Parliament Act of 1911 defined a Money Bill for the United Kingdom, confined the Lords to a delaying period, and provided for certification by the Speaker of the House of Commons. The Government of India Act of 1935 carried a version of the device into the federal legislature it designed for India, with a definition of a Money Bill, a special procedure and a certification function for the presiding officer. The Constituent Assembly, working with that inheritance, adopted the structure into Articles 109 and 110: a closed definition, introduction only in the popular House, recommendations only from the Council of States, a short return period, and a final decision on classification by the Speaker.

One difference between the British model and the Indian text has grown in importance as the litigation has developed. Section 3 of the Parliament Act of 1911 provides that the Speaker's certificate there shall be conclusive for all purposes and shall not be questioned in any court of law. Article 110(3) contains no such ouster. It says the Speaker's decision shall be final, and stops. The Constituent Assembly had the British formula available as a model and did not reproduce its bar on the courts, and the Supreme Court's post 2018 position, that finality governs within the legislative process while unconstitutionality remains reviewable, is a reading of that deliberate textual difference. Supporters of strong finality answer that final in the Indian text was meant to do the same work as the British formula and that the absence of the extra words is a drafting economy rather than a reservation of judicial power. The text alone does not compel either answer, which is why the structural arguments, about bicameralism on one side and about the autonomy of legislative proceedings on the other, carry so much of the weight in the cases.

The state legislatures reproduce the design in miniature. Article 199 defines a Money Bill for a state in terms parallel to Article 110, Article 198 lays down the special procedure in the Legislative Assembly with a fourteen day return period for a Legislative Council where one exists, Article 207 imposes the Governor's recommendation requirement in parallel to Article 117, and the Speaker of the Assembly certifies. The Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui decision of 2014 arose under Article 199, and its strong finality holding therefore governs, in the first instance, the state provision, although its reasoning was framed in general terms. State practice supplies a steady stream of classification decisions that rarely reach the courts, and a larger bench decision on Article 110 will structure the reading of Article 199 as well, because the texts are materially identical and the state provision has been read in step with the Union provision throughout. The reference is therefore not only about a handful of Union statutes. It will fix the working constitution of every state legislature that has a second chamber and of every Assembly Speaker who certifies.

## The budget cycle around the classification

Classification disputes arrive at the margin of a yearly machine whose main parts are not disputed, and the machine is worth describing because it shows how much of the fiscal process the Money Bill route legitimately carries. The annual financial statement under Article 112 is a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure, distinguishing charged expenditure from voted expenditure and revenue from capital. It is laid before both Houses, and the general discussion that follows involves both. The estimates in respect of voted expenditure are then submitted as demands for grants to the Lok Sabha under Article 113, and the Lok Sabha may assent to a demand, refuse it, or reduce it; the Council of States has no part in the voting of demands, which is the sharpest single expression of the Lok Sabha's control of supply. Article 114 then requires the Appropriation Bill, which gathers the grants voted and the charged expenditure into a single authorisation to withdraw from the Consolidated Fund, and Article 115 and Article 116 provide for supplementary and additional grants and for votes on account and votes of credit where the year's business outruns or precedes the full procedure.

The Finance Bill enters this cycle at its head. The taxation proposals announced in the budget speech require legal authority, and the Finance Bill supplies it, introduced in the Lok Sabha immediately after the budget is presented. Because tax changes are announced for a financial year that begins on the first of April, the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act of 1931 allows declared provisions of the Bill to have immediate effect, subject to refund or adjustment if Parliament alters them, so that the revenue proposals operate while the Bill is being passed. The Appropriation Bill enters at the cycle's end, after the demands are voted. Both Bills are, in their standard contents, within Article 110: the Finance Bill because taxation and matters incidental to it exhaust its clauses, the Appropriation Bill because appropriation out of the Consolidated Fund is clause (d) of the definition. The annual rhythm explains the temptation that produced the controversy. Every year, without fail, at least two Bills travel the fast track with the whole government's legislative energy concentrated on them and a hard deadline behind them. A department seeking an amendment to its regulatory statute, facing a crowded legislative calendar and an uncertain Council of States, has an obvious incentive to place its amendment in the Finance Bill, where passage is swift and the second chamber cannot amend. The accretion of non fiscal matter into Finance Bills was not an accident of one year. It is the predictable product of a fast vehicle passing on schedule through a congested system, and any doctrine the larger bench lays down will have to reckon with the incentive, because a permissive test will be used every year and a strict test will force the splitting of finance legislation into its fiscal and non fiscal parts as a matter of routine drafting discipline.

## Applying the test clause by clause: three worked examples

Because the test is applied to clauses and not to titles, worked examples fix its operation better than abstraction. Take first a Bill that raises the rate of income tax, adjusts the surcharge, alters the machinery for assessment and collection of that tax, and creates offences for evasion of it. Every clause deals with the imposition, alteration or regulation of a tax, or with machinery incidental to that taxation: assessment, collection and enforcement of a tax are the paradigm of clause (g) matter, since a tax that cannot be assessed or collected is not regulated at all. The Bill is a Money Bill, subject to the exclusion check: if it also imposes a fee for a licence unconnected with the tax, that fee clause does not help it under Article 110(2), but neither does it hurt it, because a fee for services or licences is simply disregarded in deciding whether the Bill contains only listed matter; the difficult question would be a clause regulating the licensed trade itself, which is outside the list and outside the exclusions, and would defeat the certificate.

Take second a Bill that creates a national regulator for a sector, gives it licensing, rule making and adjudicatory powers, funds it by a levy on the regulated firms, and provides that sums for its first years shall be paid out of the Consolidated Fund. The Consolidated Fund provision and the levy give the Bill fiscal contact, and on the broad reading the whole might be presented as a Money Bill. On the test as written, the Bill fails step 3 of the decision table: the creation of the regulator, its licensing regime and its adjudicatory powers are not taxation, since the levy is a fee for regulation of the kind Article 110(2) addresses rather than a tax in the constitutional sense, are not borrowing, custody, appropriation, charged expenditure, receipt or audit, and are not incidental to the single appropriation clause, because the institution would exist and exercise its powers in identical form if the funding line were removed and replaced by fee income. The honest routes for that Bill are Article 117(1) if a genuine Article 110 matter is present besides the appropriation, or Article 117(3), since its enactment involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, and on either route the Council of States has its full powers. The example shows why the incidental clause cannot be tested by asking whether the fiscal provision is important to the scheme. Importance is not the test. The test is whether the non fiscal provision does anything beyond operating a listed fiscal matter, and a regulator plainly does.

Take third the hardest recurring case, a Bill that spends money from the Consolidated Fund on a benefit and builds the delivery system for that benefit: eligibility rules, an administering authority, enrolment of beneficiaries, a database, authentication at the point of delivery, portability across states, offences for diversion, and a grievance mechanism. This is the Aadhaar shape in generic form. The spending is listed matter under clauses (c), (d) and (f). The delivery system is where the readings divide. The broad reading says the delivery machinery is incidental to the expenditure because expenditure on a benefit cannot occur at all without identifying beneficiaries and paying them, and every clause serves the spending. The narrow reading says some of the machinery exceeds delivery: a general purpose identity infrastructure usable for purposes far beyond the benefit, obligations imposed on private actors, and uses of the system unconnected with any payment from the Fund are legislative objects in themselves, and their presence means the Bill does not contain only listed and incidental matter. The clause by clause discipline at least identifies the exact point of disagreement, which is the status of the clauses that outrun the expenditure, and it exposes the weakness of deciding the question at the level of the Bill's overall purpose, since both sides agree on the purpose and disagree on the clauses.

## What would follow from an invalid certificate

The consequences of the larger bench adopting the narrow reading are part of the reason the question is heavy, and they are genuinely uncertain, which is itself a reason the reference has weight. If a certification is held invalid, the Bill was required to be passed by both Houses under the ordinary procedure or under Article 117, and it was not. The orthodox consequence of a Bill becoming an Act without constitutionally required passage is that the enactment is void for want of valid enactment, and Chandrachud J.'s dissent in the Aadhaar case accepted that consequence for the Act before him. Against that stand the practical magnitudes: statutes certified over years have created rights, liabilities, appointments, collections and payments, and a declaration of invalidity reaching back across all of them would unsettle closed transactions on a large scale. Courts faced with consequences of that order have techniques available, including prospective operation of a ruling and the protection of acts done under a statute while it stood, although the fit of those techniques to a defect of passage, as distinct from a defect of content, is untested in Indian law. Severability, the doctrine by which invalid provisions are cut away and the rest of a statute saved, sits awkwardly here too: the defect in a wrongly certified Act is not located in particular provisions but in the route of the whole, although one possible holding is that the fiscal provisions validly within Article 110 could have been enacted as a Money Bill and the remainder required the other House, producing a statute valid in part and void in part along exactly the line the classification test draws. Rojer Mathew referred the question without deciding consequences, and the larger bench will have to address them, because governments, litigants and the Council of States all need to know not only what the test is but what an incorrect certificate does to a statute that has been administered for years. The uncertainty about consequences is a further prudential argument for the drafting discipline the comparison section recommends: a Bill routed correctly under Article 117 carries no tail of validity risk at all.

## The Speaker, the certificate and the comparative point

The office that certifies deserves a section of its own, because arguments about the certificate often resolve into arguments about the Speaker. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha is elected by the House from among its members, presides over its proceedings, and holds a series of constitutional discretions of which certification under Article 110(3) is one, the acknowledgment of the leader of the opposition for statutory purposes and decisions under the Tenth Schedule on defection being others. Unlike the Speaker of the British House of Commons, the Indian Speaker does not by convention resign from party membership on election, and Speakers in India have come to the chair from the treasury benches of the governing majority in the ordinary course. Defenders of the certification design respond that the Speaker's constitutional functions are exercised under the discipline of the office and are frequently exercised against the government's convenience, and that the alternative decision makers are worse: classification by the House itself would be classification by the majority whose Bill it is, and classification by a court in advance of passage would draw the judiciary into the legislative process before a law exists. Critics respond that the combination of an unreasoned decision, a finality clause and a party affiliated decision maker is exactly the combination that constitutional design ordinarily avoids where the decision determines the rights of another institution, and that the least the design could require, if the certificate is to carry its present consequences, is a reasoned certificate addressing the clauses of the Bill against the limbs of Article 110(1), which would discipline the decision, inform the Council of States, and make judicial review a review of reasons rather than a reconstruction of a decision never explained.

The comparative position sharpens the point. Under the Parliament Act of 1911, the Commons Speaker consults, where practicable, two members appointed from a chairmen's panel before certifying, and the certificate is by statute conclusive and unreviewable. India adopted the certification without the statutory conclusiveness and without the consultation practice, leaving a discretion that is at once less fettered in form and more consequential in effect, since the Indian Council of States, unlike the post 1911 Lords in fiscal matters, retains full powers over every Bill except a Money Bill, so that certification in India moves a Bill between full bicameralism and none, a wider swing than the British certificate produces. That wider swing is the strongest structural reason for taking the classification question as seriously as the courts have taken it: the difference between the two routes in India is not the difference between delay and speed, but the difference between a chamber legislating and a chamber advising.

## Classification is not competence: the pith and substance distinction

A recurring error in argument on this subject is the importation of the pith and substance doctrine from the law of legislative competence. When a court decides whether a legislature had the power to enact a statute under the distribution of subjects in the Seventh Schedule, it asks for the statute's pith and substance, its true character, and permits incidental encroachment on another legislature's field, because the entries in the lists are wide and overlapping and a strict clause by clause exclusivity would make legislation impossible. Parties defending certificates have at times argued by analogy that a Bill whose pith and substance is fiscal should be treated as a Money Bill although some provisions travel beyond the list. The analogy fails on the texts. Legislative competence turns on entries that are fields of legislation, expressed in broad language and construed liberally by settled doctrine. Article 110 turns on a definition that the Constitution itself frames with the word only, in a provision whose function is not to distribute power between legislatures but to allocate power between two Houses of the same legislature for a defined class of Bills. A doctrine designed to save statutes from invalidity for encroachment is a poor tool for a provision designed as a gate, and the Aadhaar majority's reasoning is best understood not as pith and substance but as a claim about clause (g): that the provisions in question were, on examination, incidental to the expenditure at the Act's centre. Whether that examination was conducted clause by clause with sufficient rigour is the criticism Rojer Mathew recorded. The distinction matters for how the larger bench's task is framed. The question is not whether the impugned statutes are in substance fiscal, a question to which identity law, tribunal administration and political funding law would receive doubtful answers in any event. The question is the reach of the incidental clause applied provision by provision, and the burden the word only places on a certificate that would carry non fiscal provisions through it.

There is a second distinction of the same kind, between the classification of a Bill and the validity of its provisions. A statute may be within Parliament's competence, violate no fundamental right, and still be invalidly enacted because it was passed by a procedure the Constitution did not authorise for a Bill of its contents. Conversely, a Bill may be impeccably classified and its provisions still fall to a rights challenge. The Aadhaar litigation contained both kinds of question: the privacy and proportionality challenges to the Aadhaar Act were content challenges, decided on their own terms in the same judgment, while the money bill challenge was a passage challenge that would have invalidated the Act without reaching its contents at all. Keeping the two apart explains features of the case law that otherwise look inconsistent, including the money laundering litigation, where the Court in 2022 decided extensive content challenges to the statute, upheld the provisions, and expressly left the passage challenge untouched because it depended on the pending reference. A reader who merges the two questions will expect the upholding of provisions to settle the route, or the doubting of the route to unsettle the provisions. Neither follows, and the separation is the analytically correct posture for everything the larger bench will decide.

## The fourteen days in practice, and the litigation since the reference

The Council of States has used its fourteen days in the contested instances in the only way Article 109 allows, by debating and recommending. In the passage of the Aadhaar Bill in 2016, the Council returned the Bill with recommended amendments of substance, which would among other things have altered the mandatory character of the identification requirement and the reach of the Act beyond benefits and subsidies. The Lok Sabha considered the recommendations and rejected them, and the Bill was deemed passed in the Lok Sabha's form. The episode is the cleanest modern illustration of the procedure's meaning: the second chamber examined the Bill, identified the provisions it considered objectionable, proposed changes through the only channel open to it, and the enactment proceeded without any of them. Whether one regards that outcome as the design working, supply and its delivery machinery being the Lok Sabha's province, or as the design being exploited, a general identity statute escaping amendment by the chamber of the states, depends entirely on the antecedent classification question, which is why the classification question and not the procedure is where argument concentrates.

Since the reference in 2019, the money bill question has surfaced wherever a certified statute is challenged, and the pattern of judicial handling has been uniform: note the objection, record that it awaits the larger bench, and decide the case on the remaining grounds. The electoral bonds litigation followed that pattern in part. The scheme of anonymous bearer bonds for political funding, built by the Finance Act of 2017 amendments and the notification of the scheme in 2018, was challenged both as a violation of the voter's right to information and on the money bill ground that the amending provisions could not have been enacted through a certified Bill. In February 2024 a Constitution Bench struck down the electoral bond scheme and the connected amendments as violating the right to information under Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution, deciding the case on the content ground and restoring the disclosure position under the Companies Act and the Representation of the People Act as it stood before the amendments. The significance for the classification question is twofold. The political funding amendments, among the most prominent of the contested Money Bill provisions, fell in the event on a rights ground rather than on the passage ground, so their fate does not await the reference. And the episode demonstrates that content review and passage review operate as alternative protections: a provision that escapes the second chamber may still meet the courts on its merits, although the protection is incomplete, because rights review reaches only provisions that violate rights, while passage review would reach every provision that travelled on an invalid certificate, including provisions, like tribunal service conditions and foreign contribution definitions, whose vice is procedural rather than substantive.

The money laundering amendments remain in the opposite posture: challenged on passage, upheld or unexamined on content, and waiting. So does the Finance Act of 2017 tribunal architecture in its original form, much of it since overtaken by the Tribunals Reforms Act of 2021, which was itself enacted as an ordinary Act through both Houses after the earlier ordinance and litigation, an instructive contrast in route selection for materially similar subject matter. The later statute's ordinary passage shows the Financial and ordinary routes functioning for tribunal legislation when the government chooses them, and it removes any suggestion that the subject matter could not have travelled bicamerally in 2017. Examples of that kind will feature in the larger bench's hearing, not as law but as context: the question is one of constitutional meaning, and the demonstrated workability of the bicameral route for the same subjects bears on the claim that fiscal administration requires the fast track for the non fiscal matter that accompanies it.

## The federal dimension of a bypassed chamber

The Council of States is not a generic second chamber. Article 80 constitutes it as a council of representatives of the states and the union territories, elected by the elected members of the state legislative assemblies, and its place in the constitutional scheme is the federal element inside the Union legislature. Legislation on subjects in the State List is closed to Parliament in normal circumstances, and legislation on the Concurrent List is shared, but a large part of the contested Money Bill matter, tribunals administering state connected subjects, the funding of political parties that contest state elections, the regulation of contributions with state level effects, touches the interests of states directly. When such matter is enacted without the possibility of amendment or rejection by the chamber in which the states sit as units, the federal objection adds itself to the democratic one. The narrow reading draws on this: Article 110 withdraws a defined and exceptional class of business from the federal chamber because taxation and supply for the Union are the Union executive's lifeblood and the popular House's charge, and a withdrawable class defined by the beneficiary's certificate would let the Union legislature legislate alone, in field after field, over subjects on which the states' chamber was designed to be heard. The broad reading answers that the Council's federal character does not enlarge Article 110's text, that the subjects of the contested statutes fall within Union or Concurrent competence in any event, and that the federal protection for states lies in the Seventh Schedule and in Article 368's ratification requirements for constitutional change, not in a restrictive reading of a fiscal definition. Both arguments are structural rather than textual, and both will be before the larger bench, because the reference, although framed on the meaning of Article 110(1), cannot be answered without some account of what the second chamber is for.

The federal point also explains the composition of the coalition that has pressed the narrow reading. Challenges to the certifications have drawn support from members of the Council of States across parties, from state governments whose regulatory space the contested statutes occupied, and from parliamentarians whose objection is institutional rather than partisan: a member of the governing party in the Council has the same constitutional interest in the chamber's amending power as a member of the opposition, because the power belongs to the chamber and not to its temporary majority. That institutional character is visible in the parliamentary record of the contested passages, where the objection to certification was frequently raised as a point of order about the Bill's route before it was raised as an argument about the Bill's merits, and where presiding officers were asked to protect the classification boundary from within. The internal remedies having failed to move the boundary, the question migrated to the courts, and the migration itself is a datum about the design: a classification system whose only effective check is external and judicial will generate litigation as a matter of course, and the volume of money bill litigation since 2016 is the system working out its own logic rather than an aberration within it.

## The questions a court must answer on the reference

When the larger bench is constituted, the reference will require it to answer a sequence of questions that are worth separating, because agreement on some does not settle the others. The first is the test itself: whether Article 110(1) is satisfied by a Bill whose provisions are, in the main, directed to a listed matter with the remainder incidental, or whether every provision must independently answer to a listed clause or to a strictly confined incidental clause, and by what standard incidental is to be judged, necessity, subservience, or mere connection. The second is the unit and moment of assessment: whether classification is judged on the Bill as introduced, as passed by the Lok Sabha, or as enacted, and whether a Bill that exceeds the definition in part can be treated as a Money Bill in part. The third is the standard of review of the certificate: the Aadhaar majority accepted review for illegality and unconstitutionality while upholding the certificate, so the larger bench must fix what a challenger must show, whether the court examines the provisions de novo against Article 110(1) or defers to the Speaker's assessment within a margin, and what material, including the absence of reasons in the certificate, the court may consider. The fourth is the consequence of a failed certificate: invalidity of the whole enactment, invalidity of the provisions outside the definition, prospective relief, or protection of acts done, questions the referring bench left open and on which the fates of the listed statutes turn. The fifth, running beneath the others, is the precedential question of the Aadhaar majority itself: whether its treatment of the identity statute survives as a correct application of the test the larger bench lays down, whatever that test is.

For the working reader, the practical yield of separating the questions is a map of what is settled and what is not. Settled, unless the larger bench disturbs it: the definition's text, the exclusions, the fourteen day procedure, the two Financial Bill categories, the absence of a joint sitting and of presidential return for Money Bills, and the reviewability of a certificate for unconstitutionality in principle. Unsettled: the operative test for the incidental clause, the rigour of review, the severability of a mixed enactment, and the consequences for statutes already administered. A treatment of the subject that does not mark that frontier, that recites the definition and the Aadhaar result as though the law were closed, misleads its reader on the point of greatest practical importance, because advice given to a drafter, a legislator or a litigant on the strength of the Aadhaar majority alone is advice given on authority that a Constitution Bench has flagged for reconsideration by a larger one.

## The Finance Bill's passage in practice

The annual Finance Bill concentrates every feature of this article into a few weeks of parliamentary time, and its passage shows the Money Bill route operating at full stretch. The Bill is introduced in the Lok Sabha on the day of the budget or immediately after, and the tax proposals it contains are given provisional effect under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act of 1931, so the revenue machinery works from the first of April on the strength of the Bill as introduced. The Lok Sabha's consideration is organised around the demands for grants of the ministries, debated ministry by ministry for as many as are selected, with the remaining demands put to vote together at the appointed hour, the practice known in parliamentary usage as the guillotine. The Finance Bill itself is then taken up, its clauses debated under severe time pressure, and amendments by members are constrained by the rule that an amendment to a taxation proposal which increases the charge requires the President's recommendation to be moved at all, a further expression of the executive's financial initiative running through Articles 117 and the rules of the House. The Bill passes to the Council of States with the certificate, the Council debates the budget's fiscal policy with full freedom of speech and no power of amendment, returns the Bill inside fourteen days with any recommendations, and the Lok Sabha disposes of the recommendations, ordinarily briefly, before the Bill goes for assent. The whole fiscal enactment of the year, tax rates, exemptions, amendments to the direct and indirect tax codes, and in the contested years a volume of unrelated statute law, becomes law inside this corridor.

Two features of that practice bear on the doctrine. The first is that the Council of States' debate on a Finance Bill is not a nullity in the political process even though it is one in the amending process: the debate is televised, reported, and directed at the government's fiscal choices, and ministers answer it, so the chamber's advisory role has a public accountability content that the bare text of Article 109 does not capture. The second is that the corridor's speed is a function of the budget's calendar rather than of any emergency in the non fiscal matter riding on the Bill. A tribunal restructuring or an electoral amendment placed in the Finance Bill acquires the budget's deadline as its own, although nothing in the subject requires enactment by the first of April, and the deadline then operates as an argument against scrutiny in both Houses, in the Lok Sabha because the Bill must pass, and in the Council because it cannot amend in any event. The larger bench will decide the meaning of Article 110 as a matter of law, but the practice described here is the reason the meaning matters beyond the courtroom: the classification governs the real distribution of legislative labour and scrutiny across the whole annual output of financial legislation, and a permissive meaning makes the Finance Bill a general purpose vehicle for any amendment a government wishes to protect from the second chamber, in every year, as a standing feature of the legislative system.

A short word on the recommendation mechanism completes the procedural picture, because it is the point at which the executive's initiative is formally marked. The President's recommendation under Articles 109 and 117 is communicated to the House before introduction or before consideration, as the category requires, and it is obtained by the minister in charge of the Bill from the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Its presence in the record of a Bill is therefore evidence of the government's sponsorship, and its absence is fatal in the categories where it is required: a Money Bill or a Financial Bill of the first kind introduced without it would be introduced in breach of Article 117(1), and a Bill within Article 117(3) could not be passed without it. Private members cannot in practice deploy any of the fiscal categories, since the recommendation will not be forthcoming for a Bill the government does not sponsor, which confines the whole field of fiscal legislation to government business and makes the classification controversy a controversy about the government's Bills alone.

## The incidental clause: a spectrum of cases

Since the reference will be decided on clause (g), the spectrum of provisions that claim its shelter is worth arranging in order, from the plainly incidental to the plainly freestanding, because the larger bench's test will have to draw its line somewhere along it. At the secure end stand the machinery provisions of a tax: assessment, collection, recovery, refunds, penalties for evasion of that tax, and adjudication of disputes under it. These operate the listed matter and nothing else, and no plausible test excludes them. Next stand the institutional provisions that exist only to perform a listed fiscal function: a fund's custodian arrangements, an audit office's procedure for the audit of Union accounts, the machinery for the payment of charged expenditure. These create offices and procedures, but their whole content is a listed operation. Further along stand delivery provisions for a spending programme: eligibility determination, enrolment of beneficiaries for that programme, payment channels and recovery of wrongful payments under it. These begin to govern citizens' lives outside the act of payment, but each provision is tethered to a specific expenditure and lapses in meaning if the expenditure is removed. Further still stand dual use provisions: an identity infrastructure built for benefit delivery but designed and used for general identification, a database maintained for payments but opened to other state and private purposes, obligations imposed on persons unconnected with any receipt of public money. At the far end stand provisions with no fiscal tether at all: the service conditions of tribunal members, the disclosure law of political donations, the definition of a foreign source for contributions, the elements of a money laundering offence. A Bill containing provisions from the far end of the spectrum is a Money Bill only if the incidental clause can be stretched to cover matter whose removal would leave the Bill's fiscal provisions wholly intact and operable, and the very statement of that proposition shows why the far end cases are hard to defend on the word only: a provision is not incidental to a matter if the matter proceeds unchanged without it. The middle of the spectrum, delivery and dual use provisions, is where the genuine difficulty lies and where the Aadhaar case was fought, because tethering is a question of degree. The larger bench's contribution will be to convert that degree into a rule, whether by a test of dominant fiscal character, a test of necessary connection, or a clause by clause insistence that incidental matter must serve a listed provision and serve nothing else. Until then, the spectrum is the most honest statement of the law available: secure at the ends, contested in the middle, and governed in the middle by a majority decision whose reasoning stands referred.

## The decision the comparison earns

A comparison article owes the reader a decision, and the comparison here yields one on the instruments themselves, distinct from the open judicial question. Dimension by dimension, the position is as follows.

On the trigger, a Money Bill is defined by content alone: only listed matters and matters incidental to them. A Financial Bill of the first kind is defined by mixture: listed matter plus anything else. A Financial Bill of the second kind is defined by effect: expenditure from the Consolidated Fund if enacted. A drafter who knows the content of the measure knows the category, and the category is not a choice among equals. On the House of introduction, the Money Bill and the first kind of Financial Bill begin in the Lok Sabha on the President's recommendation; the second kind may begin in either House. On the second chamber, the gradient is exact: recommendations without power for a Money Bill, full amending and rejecting power for both kinds of Financial Bill. On disagreement, a Money Bill admits no joint sitting and no insistence; both Financial Bill categories run the ordinary Article 108 course. On assent, a Money Bill cannot be returned by the President; both Financial Bill categories can. On speed, the Money Bill is the fastest instrument in the Constitution, bounded by the Council's fourteen days; the Financial Bills move at the pace of bicameral negotiation. On review exposure, the Money Bill is uniquely exposed, because its route depends on a certificate that the courts have held reviewable for illegality and unconstitutionality and whose governing test stands referred; a Financial Bill carries no certificate and therefore no classification risk of that kind, its validity turning on its contents and on ordinary legislative competence.

The decision follows. Where a measure is genuinely confined to taxation, borrowing, the Funds, appropriation, charged expenditure, receipts and audit, with machinery that does no more than operate those matters, the Money Bill is the correct instrument and its advantages, speed, certainty of passage and freedom from a second chamber veto, are advantages the Constitution intends the government of the day to have over supply. Where a measure mixes fiscal matter with institutional, regulatory, electoral or criminal law, the correct instrument is a Financial Bill of the first kind if the fiscal matter falls within Article 110(1)(a) to (f), and an ordinary or second kind Financial Bill if it does not, and the price of the correct instrument, exposure to amendment and rejection in the Council of States, is not a defect of the route but the constitutional consideration for legislating beyond the fiscal list. Routing mixed matter through a certificate purchases speed at the price of validity risk that survives enactment and compounds with every year the reference remains undecided, because a statute struck down for defective passage falls with everything built on it. The comparison therefore decides against the expansive certificate on prudential grounds that hold whichever way the larger bench rules: if the narrow reading prevails, the mixed statutes are exposed; if the broad reading prevails, the Financial Bill route was available all along at the cost only of time. The one outcome the comparison cannot defend is the treatment of classification as a drafting convenience. Article 110 gives the word, the word is only, and the reference is pending.

For the reader who must use this material rather than merely follow the controversy, the working summary is short. Apply the test to the clauses and not to the title. Treat fines, licence fees, service fees and local taxes as disregarded by Article 110(2) rather than as qualifying matter. Treat the presence of any freestanding regulatory, institutional, electoral or criminal provision as decisive against certification, and treat delivery machinery as the contested middle ground on which authority is divided and a larger bench has been asked to rule. Route a mixed Bill under Article 117(1) and accept the second chamber's full powers as the cost of legislating beyond the fiscal list. And when reading any account of the subject, check whether it records the reference of 2019 and the absence of a decision on it, because an account that presents the law as settled after the Aadhaar judgment is working from a record that the Supreme Court itself has marked as incomplete. The definition, the procedure and the categories are stable and can be stated with confidence. The reach of one adjective in clause (g), on which the participation of a House of Parliament turns, awaits the bench that will settle it.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which matters make a Bill a Money Bill?

Article 110(1) lists the matters: the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax; the regulation of the borrowing of money or the giving of a guarantee by the Government of India; the custody of the Consolidated Fund or the Contingency Fund of India and payments into or withdrawals from them; the appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund; the declaring of expenditure to be charged on the Consolidated Fund or the increasing of such expenditure; the receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or the public account, its custody or issue, and the audit of the accounts of the Union or of a State; and any matter incidental to those matters. A Bill qualifies only if it contains nothing beyond that list.

### Q: What is the difference between a Money Bill and a Financial Bill?

A Money Bill contains only the matters listed in Article 110(1) and matters incidental to them, and the Rajya Sabha may only recommend changes to it within fourteen days. A Financial Bill contains one or more of the listed matters together with other matter. Under Article 117(1) it shares the Money Bill's starting conditions, introduction only in the Lok Sabha and only on the President's recommendation, but it is otherwise passed like an ordinary Bill: the Rajya Sabha may amend or reject it, a joint sitting may resolve a disagreement, and the President may return it for reconsideration. No Speaker's certificate attaches to a Financial Bill.

### Q: What powers does the Rajya Sabha have over a Money Bill?

The Rajya Sabha receives a Money Bill after the Lok Sabha has passed it and must return it within fourteen days of receipt, with or without recommendations. It may debate the Bill and recommend amendments, and the Lok Sabha may accept or reject any recommendation. The Rajya Sabha cannot amend the Bill itself, cannot reject it, cannot delay it beyond the fourteen days, and cannot insist on its views, because disagreement does not lead to a joint sitting for a Money Bill. If the Council does not return the Bill within the period, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form the Lok Sabha gave it.

### Q: Who decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill?

The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides. Article 110(3) provides that if any question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill or not, the decision of the Speaker of the House of the People on the question shall be final. Article 110(4) requires the Speaker's certificate, signed by the Speaker, to be endorsed on the Bill when it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha and again when it is presented to the President for assent. The certificate records the classification without reasons, and the classification determines the procedure the Bill follows, including whether the Rajya Sabha can amend it or only recommend changes.

### Q: Is the Speaker's certificate open to judicial review?

Yes, on a limited ground. The earlier view, expressed in the Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui decision of 2014 on the corresponding state provision, treated the Speaker's decision as final and beyond challenge. In the Aadhaar judgment of 2018 the Constitution Bench majority accepted that the Speaker's decision under Article 110(3), although final, may be judicially reviewed where the certification is challenged on the ground of illegality or unconstitutionality, as distinct from a mere irregularity of procedure, which Article 122 protects. The scope and rigour of that review is part of the question referred to a larger bench in the Rojer Mathew case in 2019, which awaits decision.

### Q: Which statutes were passed as Money Bills and why is that contested?

The prominent instances are the Aadhaar Act of 2016; the tribunal restructuring in Part XIV of the Finance Act of 2017; the political funding amendments in the Finance Act of 2017 covering electoral bonds and disclosures; the retrospective amendments to the foreign contribution law in the Finance Acts of 2016 and 2018; and the repeated amendments to the Prevention of Money Laundering Act carried in Finance Acts between 2015 and 2019. In each case the objection is that the Bill contained substantial matter outside the matters listed in Article 110(1) and not incidental to them, so the certification was unavailable and the enactment proceeded without the Rajya Sabha's power to amend or reject being engaged.

### Q: What did the reference to a larger bench on Money Bills leave open?

In Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank in 2019, a Constitution Bench doubted the Aadhaar majority's treatment of Article 110, recorded that the earlier bench had not examined the provisions of the Aadhaar Act in the detail the question required, and referred the issue of the Money Bill and of the Speaker's certification of Part XIV of the Finance Act of 2017 to a larger bench. The reference leaves open the operative test for the incidental clause in Article 110(1)(g), the standard of judicial review of a certificate, whether a mixed enactment can survive in part, and the consequences for statutes already enacted and administered on contested certificates. No larger bench decision has been delivered.

### Q: How do a Finance Bill and an Appropriation Bill differ?

Both belong to the annual budget process and both travel as Money Bills in practice, but they do different work. The Finance Bill gives effect to the government's taxation proposals for the coming financial year: it imposes, alters and continues taxes, and its declared provisions take provisional effect under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act of 1931 while it is being passed. The Appropriation Bill follows the voting of the demands for grants and authorises the withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India for the voted grants and the charged expenditure, under Article 114. Amendments that would vary a grant or its destination cannot be moved to an Appropriation Bill.

### Q: What does the word only in Article 110(1) require of a Bill?

It requires exclusivity. A Bill is a Money Bill only if every provision it contains deals with one of the matters listed in Article 110(1)(a) to (f) or with a matter incidental to one of them under clause (g). It is not enough that the Bill contains a tax provision, that most of its clauses are fiscal, or that its dominant purpose is financial. A single substantive provision that falls outside the list and is not incidental to a listed matter prevents the Bill from being a Money Bill, and routes it instead to the Financial Bill procedure under Article 117(1), where the Rajya Sabha has full powers of amendment and rejection.

### Q: What happens if the Rajya Sabha does not return a Money Bill within fourteen days?

The Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form in which the Lok Sabha passed it. Article 109(5) provides that if a Money Bill passed by the Lok Sabha and transmitted to the Rajya Sabha for its recommendations is not returned to the Lok Sabha within fourteen days from the date of its receipt by the Council, it shall be deemed to have been passed by both Houses at the expiration of that period in the form passed by the Lok Sabha. Silence therefore has the same effect as approval, and the Council has no power to extend the period or to hold the Bill.

### Q: Where may a Financial Bill of the first kind be introduced, and whose recommendation does it need?

A Financial Bill of the first kind, a Bill containing an Article 110(1) matter together with other matter, may be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and Article 117(1) provides that it shall not be introduced or moved except on the recommendation of the President. The recommendation is obtained by the government and marks the Bill as a government measure, since the executive holds the financial initiative. After introduction, however, the Bill follows the ordinary course: it may be amended or rejected by the Rajya Sabha, disagreement may be resolved at a joint sitting under Article 108, and the President may return it for reconsideration under Article 111.

### Q: How does a Financial Bill of the second kind differ from one of the first kind?

A Financial Bill of the second kind contains no Article 110(1) matter as such; it is a Bill which, if enacted and brought into operation, would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India, and Article 117(3) governs it. It may be introduced in either House of Parliament, and the President's recommendation is not required at introduction. The recommendation is a condition of passage: neither House may pass such a Bill unless the President has recommended its consideration to that House. In every other respect it travels as an ordinary Bill, with full powers of amendment and rejection in both Houses and access to a joint sitting.

### Q: Can the President return a Money Bill for reconsideration?

No. Article 111 empowers the President, when a Bill is presented for assent, to assent, to withhold assent, or to return the Bill to the Houses with a message requesting reconsideration of the Bill or of specified provisions. The proviso to Article 111 states that the President shall not return a Money Bill for reconsideration. The exclusion fits the design of Article 109: a Bill whose passage is guaranteed within a fixed timetable, and over which the second chamber has only a recommending voice, is also placed beyond presidential return, leaving the President the choice of assent or withholding of assent without the suspensive option available for other Bills.

### Q: What did Justice Chandrachud hold in his dissent in the Aadhaar case?

Dissenting in K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar-5J) v Union of India, Chandrachud J. held that the Aadhaar Act contained substantial provisions unrelated to the matters enumerated in Article 110(1), that the identity architecture created by the Act was its substance and could not be treated as incidental to the expenditure provision in Section 7, and that treating the whole Act as a Money Bill on the strength of that provision inverted the constitutional test. He held that passing the Act as a Money Bill bypassed the Rajya Sabha and amounted to a fraud on the Constitution, and he would have declared the enactment unconstitutional for want of valid passage through both Houses as the Constitution required.

### Q: How did the Aadhaar majority justify treating the Aadhaar Act's provisions as incidental?

The majority, in the judgment delivered by Sikri J., reasoned from Section 7 of the Aadhaar Act, under which subsidies, benefits and services funded from the Consolidated Fund of India were linked to Aadhaar authentication. The Act's object, on this reading, was the targeted delivery of expenditure drawn from the Consolidated Fund, a matter within Article 110(1), and its remaining provisions, the identification authority, enrolment, authentication and the identity number itself, were the machinery by which that expenditure was delivered and protected against leakage, and therefore matters incidental to the listed expenditure within Article 110(1)(g). On that footing the Bill contained only listed and incidental matter, and the Speaker's certification was upheld.

### Q: Is every Finance Bill a Money Bill?

The annual Finance Bill is in practice introduced and certified as a Money Bill, because its standard contents, the imposition and alteration of taxes and machinery incidental to taxation, fall within Article 110(1). But Finance Bill is the name of a budget instrument, not a constitutional category separate from the test: a Finance Bill is a Money Bill only so far as its contents satisfy Article 110. That is why the controversy attaches to Finance Bills that carry provisions outside the fiscal list, such as the tribunal provisions of the Finance Act of 2017. Those provisions were certified along with the rest of the Bill, and the certification of Part XIV is the subject of the pending larger bench reference.

### Q: Can a private member introduce a Money Bill?

In practice, no. A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and Article 117(1) provides that a Bill making provision for any of the matters in Article 110(1)(a) to (f) shall not be introduced or moved except on the recommendation of the President. The President's recommendation is given on the advice of the Council of Ministers and is not available to a private member for a Bill the government does not sponsor. Money Bills are therefore government Bills, moved by a minister, and the financial initiative of the House rests with the executive throughout the fiscal categories, including both kinds of Financial Bill so far as introduction in the Lok Sabha is concerned.

### Q: Why does the Constitution confine the introduction of Money Bills to the Lok Sabha?

The design follows the principle that taxation and supply belong to the House directly elected by the people who pay the taxes. The Lok Sabha is the directly elected chamber, and Articles 109, 110 and 113 give it the exclusive power to introduce Money Bills, to vote demands for grants, and to have the final word on fiscal legislation, while the Rajya Sabha, the Council of States elected indirectly by the state legislatures, is given a recommending voice on Money Bills and full legislative power over everything else. The arrangement reserves the raising and appropriating of public money to the chamber answerable at general elections, while preserving bicameral legislation as the general rule for all other subjects.

### Q: How does the Money Bill procedure in a state legislature compare with the procedure in Parliament?

The state scheme is a close parallel. Article 199 defines a Money Bill for a state legislature in terms corresponding to Article 110, including the requirement that the Bill contain only the listed matters, and the decision of the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly on classification is final. Article 198 lays down the special procedure: introduction only in the Legislative Assembly, transmission to the Legislative Council where the state has one, recommendations only, and a return period of fourteen days, after which the Bill is deemed passed in the Assembly's form. Article 207 corresponds to Article 117 on the Governor's recommendation. The 2014 decision in Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui arose under Article 199.

### Q: What counts as a matter incidental to the matters listed in Article 110?

Article 110(1)(g) covers any matter incidental to the matters in clauses (a) to (f), and the uncontested core of the clause is machinery: the assessment, collection and enforcement provisions of a tax, the administrative arrangements for the custody of the Funds, and the procedure for audit. Contention begins where a provision does more than operate a listed matter, for example where it builds a delivery system for expenditure that also serves purposes beyond the expenditure. The Aadhaar majority treated the identity machinery as incidental to the spending under Section 7 of that Act. Rojer Mathew doubted the treatment and referred the scope of the clause to a larger bench, where the question remains open.
