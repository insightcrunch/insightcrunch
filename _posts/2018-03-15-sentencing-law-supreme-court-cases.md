## Introduction

A reader who follows the sentencing cases from In re Winship through Erlinger v. United States can trace the doctrinal line that reshaped American sentencing across two decades and identify which constitutional provision did the work. The answer surprises almost everyone. It was the jury trial right, not the prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The Eighth Amendment has done almost nothing for adult sentences, and the Sixth Amendment has done nearly everything, and understanding why explains the shape of modern sentencing law.

![Sentencing law in the Supreme Court: the jury rights revolution - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Four doctrinal lines carry that story. The first runs from Winship's reasonable doubt standard through Mullaney and Patterson on who carries the burden, McMillan on the line between elements and sentencing factors, and Apprendi, Ring, Blakely, and Alleyne on who must find the facts that raise the ceiling or the floor of punishment, with the prior conviction exception as the one fact that never crossed to the jury's side. The second is the remedy: Booker severed the federal guidelines into an advisory system without invalidating the statute, and Rita, Gall, and Kimbrough defined reasonableness review. The third is the near silence of the Eighth Amendment, which struck down only one adult prison sentence on proportionality grounds and then spent two decades explaining why it would not strike down another, until the juvenile cases gave the doctrine its only substantial movement. The fourth runs through statutory construction, where Dorsey, Dillon, Concepcion, and Pulsifer reshaped who received relief under Congress's own reforms while leaving every statute standing. A [parallel constitutional line](/2021/07/01/second-amendment-supreme-court-cases/) shows how a different amendment handled firearms questions through its own doctrinal sequence.

The claim that binds the four lines is direct: modern sentencing doctrine is a jury rights story rather than a punishment severity story, which means the constitutional path to shorter sentences has run through who finds the facts rather than through how long the sentence may be. Litigants who frame challenges as proportionality claims have chosen the losing doctrine, while litigants who asked who decides have redrawn the system from within. Readers who want to log each holding as it arrives may use the [VaultBook legislation study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) as a companion.

This article follows the litigation as the stage where a statutory scheme is reshaped without being invalidated. No court in these lines struck down the federal guidelines, the mandatory minimum statutes, or the juvenile sentencing provisions at issue. The doctrine advanced through reinterpretation and severance: a mandatory guideline system made advisory by removing one subsection, enhancement facts moved from judges to juries, and lenity provisions read as written. That pattern, more than any single holding, is what the cases teach.

## The foundations of the sentencing fact-finding doctrine

Long before the jury trial right took over sentencing law, the Court spent three decades answering a narrower question: which facts must be proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, and which may be found by a judge by a preponderance of the evidence? The answers arrived piecemeal, in cases about juvenile larceny, a Maine homicide statute, a Pennsylvania firearms law, illegal reentry after deportation, and a carjacking. Together they built the vocabulary of the entire field: elements against sentencing factors, and a single exception for prior convictions that survived every assault.

The sequence has a clean arc. The Court first constitutionalized the reasonable doubt standard. It then decided who carries the burden when a fact moves punishment up or down, and gave legislatures most of the power to allocate that burden by definition. It then drew the formal boundary between elements, which belong to the jury, and sentencing factors, which may be left to the judge. Finally, in the two decisions closest in time to the modern era, the Court created the prior-conviction exception and then, in a footnote, stated the rule that would dismantle the whole framework one year later.

### What did In re Winship require the prosecution to prove?

In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), held that the Due Process Clause requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. The ruling applied in juvenile delinquency proceedings as well as adult criminal trials, and it constitutionalized a standard that had previously rested on common law practice alone.

The case began with a 12-year-old boy, Samuel Winship, charged in New York Family Court with stealing $112 from a woman's pocketbook, an act that would have been larceny if committed by an adult. Section 744(b) of the New York Family Court Act required only a preponderance of the evidence at the adjudicatory hearing. The trial judge acknowledged that the proof might not have established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt and convicted anyway. Three successive New York courts sustained the scheme. The Supreme Court, by a 6-3 vote, reversed.

Justice Brennan, writing for the majority, held that the Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." Brennan traced the standard to early American history, found nearly unanimous adherence among common law jurisdictions, and emphasized its role in reducing factual error and sustaining public confidence in criminal judgments. He rejected the argument that juvenile proceedings, designed to save rather than punish the child, could dispense with the protection. Justice Harlan, concurring, supplied the reasoning that became the decision's moral center: the standard encodes society's judgment that convicting an innocent person is far worse than letting a guilty one go free, so the law deliberately stacks the risk of error against the prosecution.

The holding contained the seed of everything that followed, and the limit of everything that followed. Winship required proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime. It did not say who decides which facts constitute the crime. That silence became the battleground for the next thirty years, because legislatures soon learned that by defining a fact as something other than an element, they could decide the standard of proof and the identity of the factfinder along with it.

### Who must carry the burden when a fact mitigates criminal liability?

Mullaney and Patterson together mean that when a fact negates an element the legislature defined, the prosecution must disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt, but when the legislature frames a mitigating fact as an affirmative defense, the defendant may bear the burden of proving it by a preponderance.

Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), tested a Maine statute under which murder was an unlawful killing with malice aforethought, punishable by life imprisonment, while manslaughter was an unlawful killing in the heat of passion on sudden provocation, punishable by a fine of up to $1,000 or up to 20 years in prison. Malice was to be conclusively inferred unless the defendant proved by a preponderance of the evidence that he had acted in the heat of passion. Stillman Wilbur Jr., convicted of murder after a trial at which the judge instructed the jury along these lines, challenged the burden allocation. A unanimous Court, through Justice Powell, struck the scheme down. Once the heat-of-passion issue was properly presented, the Due Process Clause required the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt the absence of provocation. Powell called provocation critical because it determined "the degree of culpability attaching to the criminal homicide." Under Maine's arrangement, a defendant could be sentenced to life for murder when the evidence showed it was as likely as not that he deserved only manslaughter, a result the Court called intolerable.

New York redrafted around the holding and, two years later, won. In Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), Gordon Patterson Jr., convicted of second-degree murder after a fatal shooting, challenged a statute that defined murder as intentionally causing death and gave the defendant an affirmative defense: if he proved by a preponderance that he had acted under the influence of extreme emotional disturbance, the offense was reduced to manslaughter. The Court upheld the law and distinguished Mullaney. The majority, written by Justice White and joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices Stewart, Blackmun, and Stevens, with Justice Rehnquist taking no part and Justice Powell dissenting with Justices Brennan and Marshall, drew a definitional line. In Mullaney, absence of provocation negated malice, an element the legislature had made part of the crime. In Patterson, the defense did not negate any fact the state had to prove to convict; the state had proved intentional killing beyond a reasonable doubt, and the mitigating circumstance was a separate issue. A state that chooses to recognize a mitigating factor, the Court reasoned, may insist the fact be established with reasonable certainty rather than undertake to disprove its nonexistence in every case where it is raised.

Read together, the pair handed legislatures the power to move burdens by labeling. Call the fact an element and the prosecution proves it beyond a reasonable doubt. Repackage the same fact as an affirmative defense and the defendant may carry it by a preponderance. That definitional power, announced here in the burden-of-proof context, became the premise of the elements-versus-sentencing-factors doctrine that McMillan formalized a decade later.

### May a judge find the facts that trigger a mandatory minimum sentence?

In McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), the Court answered yes by a 5-4 vote: a fact that triggers a mandatory minimum, but does not authorize a sentence above the statutory ceiling, may be treated as a sentencing factor and found by the judge at sentencing by a preponderance of the evidence.

Pennsylvania's Mandatory Minimum Sentencing Act, adopted in 1982 and codified at 42 Pa. Cons. Stat. 9712, provided that anyone convicted of certain enumerated felonies faced a mandatory minimum of five years if the sentencing judge found, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the person had "visibly possessed a firearm" during the offense. The statute expressly declared that visible possession was not an element of the crime. It stripped the judge of discretion to impose less than five years but did not authorize any sentence above the maximum otherwise allowed for the underlying felony. Four defendants sentenced under the Act challenged it under the Due Process Clause and the Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial.

Justice Rehnquist, writing for a majority joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices White, Powell, and O'Connor, upheld the scheme. The opinion drew the formal distinction that would organize a generation of sentencing law. Elements are facts necessary for conviction and must be found by the jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Sentencing factors are facts that guide the selection of a sentence within the range the conviction already authorizes, and a judge may find them by a preponderance. The legislature's definition of the elements is usually dispositive, the Court said, and visible possession fell on the sentencing-factor side because it raised only the floor, not the ceiling: it did not increase the maximum penalty to which the defendant was exposed. The Court also rejected the Sixth Amendment claim, holding that there is no right to jury sentencing even where the sentence turns on specific factual findings.

The dissents stated the counter-reading with equal force. Justice Marshall, joined by Justices Brennan and Blackmun, argued that the firearm finding functioned as an element in everything but name, because it guaranteed a five-year term and altered punishment in a way indistinguishable from the elements the jury had found. Justice Stevens dissented separately. Both dissents warned that the majority's formalism would let legislatures evade Winship simply by relabeling.

McMillan reserved one constitutional limit, and it became the hinge of the later revolution. The majority cautioned that the legislature's labeling power was not wholly unconstrained; states could not define elements and sentencing factors however they pleased, and a sentencing factor could not be allowed to dominate the underlying offense. The boundary between a factor and an element, in other words, was real even if Pennsylvania had not crossed it. Fourteen years later, the Court found the crossing point.

### Why did the prior conviction exception survive every later assault?

The question was whether the exception could survive the jury rights revolution. It did, though each later decision narrowed its ground. Apprendi carved it into its own rule, Alleyne called it a narrow exception and declined to revisit it, and Erlinger confirmed it persists for the fact of a prior conviction while moving associated findings to the jury.

Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224 (1998), created the exception. Title 8 U.S.C. 1326(a) made it a crime for a deported alien to return to the United States without permission, carrying a maximum of two years in prison. Congress later added subsection (b)(2), authorizing up to 20 years where the deportation had followed a conviction for an aggravated felony. Hugo Roman Almendarez-Torres pleaded guilty to violating section 1326, admitting that he had been deported, had unlawfully returned, and that the deportation had followed three aggravated-felony convictions. He was sentenced to 85 months under the applicable guideline range. Because his indictment had not charged the prior convictions, he argued the court could impose no more than the two-year maximum of subsection (a). The Court disagreed, 5-4. Justice Breyer, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Kennedy, and Thomas, held that subsection (b)(2) was a penalty provision authorizing an enhanced sentence, not a separate offense, so the government was not required to charge the earlier conviction in the indictment. Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg.

The majority's rationale became the exception's lasting justification. Recidivism, the Court said, is a traditional, if not the most traditional, basis for a sentencing court to increase an offender's sentence, and treating it as an element would mark an abrupt departure from longstanding practice. The fact of a prior conviction differs from other enhancing facts because it has already been established through procedures carrying their own guarantees of fair notice, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and jury trial. A judge who finds a prior conviction is confirming a fact that a jury already found, not deciding guilt anew.

The fuller account of that survival belongs to the mandatory minimum line, where the exception was tested against facts that look indistinguishable from the ones it covers.

### How did the carjacking case set up Apprendi?

In Jones v. United States, 526 U.S. 227 (1999), a 5-4 majority construed the federal carjacking statute to define three separate offenses, holding that serious bodily injury and death were elements requiring indictment, jury submission, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

The facts were stark. In December 1992, Nathaniel Jones and two others held up two men, going through their pockets at gunpoint before Jones forced one victim into the victim's car and drove off. Jones was charged with carjacking under 18 U.S.C. 2119, which at the time provided three penalties: not more than 15 years for the basic offense, not more than 25 years if serious bodily injury resulted, and any number of years up to life if death resulted. The indictment did not charge serious bodily injury, the jury was instructed only on the basic offense, and Jones was told at arraignment that he faced a maximum of 15 years. After conviction, the district court imposed 25 years because one victim had suffered serious bodily injury. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, treating subsection (2) as a sentencing factor.

Justice Souter, joined by Justices Stevens, Scalia, Thomas, and Ginsburg, reversed. The Court held that section 2119 established three separate offenses, with serious bodily injury and death as elements of the greater ones. Justices Stevens and Scalia concurred separately. Justice Kennedy, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor and Breyer, dissented, calling the reading strained and warning that the constitutional rule behind it would disrupt state sentencing reform.

The decision's real work was done through the canon of constitutional avoidance. Souter conceded that at first glance the subsections looked like sentencing factors, but structural considerations pointed the other way, and any interpretive uncertainty had to be resolved against a reading that raised grave constitutional doubt. The majority then stated the doubt out loud in a footnote: under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause and the Sixth Amendment's notice and jury-trial guarantees, any fact, other than prior conviction, that increases the maximum penalty for a crime must be charged in an indictment, submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Because earlier cases suggested rather than established the principle, the majority presented it as doubt rather than holding. One year later, Apprendi converted the doubt into the rule, and Jones's footnote became the doctrine's preview.

The pre-Apprendi line framed everything that followed around three propositions. Winship guaranteed a standard of proof, but only for facts the law treats as elements. Mullaney and Patterson let the legislature decide which facts those are, and to move burdens across the line by redefinition. McMillan added that a fact raising only the floor, not the ceiling, could be left to the judge. Apprendi cracked the third proposition by treating the ceiling as the jury's domain, then redefined the ceiling as the maximum available on the jury's findings alone. Jones supplied the warning label and Almendarez-Torres supplied the one fact that never crossed to the jury's side. The elements-versus-sentencing-factors distinction survived the revolution, but after 2000 it was the Constitution, not the legislature, that drew the line.

## Apprendi v. New Jersey and the Maximum Rule

At 2:04 on the morning of December 22, 1994, Charles C. Apprendi Jr. fired several .22-caliber bullets into the home of an African-American family that had moved into a previously all-white neighborhood in Vineland, New Jersey. Arrested within the hour, Apprendi told police that he did not want the family in the neighborhood because they were Black, a statement he later retracted. Charged with several offenses, he pleaded guilty to three counts, including two counts of second-degree possession of a firearm for an unlawful purpose, each carrying a prison term of five to ten years. The plea agreement left two questions open: the State reserved the right to seek an enhanced sentence under New Jersey's hate crime law, and Apprendi reserved the right to challenge that law's constitutionality.

The hate crime statute, N.J.S.A. Section 2C:44-3(e), authorized an extended term of imprisonment when a trial judge found, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the defendant had acted with a purpose to intimidate a person or group because of race, color, gender, handicap, religion, sexual orientation, or ethnicity. For a second-degree offense, the extended term ran from ten to twenty years, effectively doubling the maximum the jury-supported conviction otherwise allowed. The trial judge found that the shooting was motivated by racial bias and imposed a twelve-year sentence, two years past the ceiling the conviction alone authorized. The New Jersey appellate courts affirmed, treating the biased-purpose finding as a traditional sentencing factor a judge could resolve by a preponderance of the evidence. The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether the Constitution permitted that procedure, and its answer restructured American sentencing law.

### What did the New Jersey statute do, and why did it reach the Supreme Court?

New Jersey made racially motivated crime a sentencing issue, not a separate offense. Apprendi pleaded guilty to a weapons charge carrying five to ten years, then the judge found racial bias by a preponderance and imposed a sentence in the extended ten-to-twenty-year range, which Apprendi argued required a jury finding beyond a reasonable doubt.

The dispute presented the classic conflict between elements and sentencing factors. New Jersey had placed its hate crime provision among the code's sentencing provisions rather than its offense definitions, and the State argued that the placement settled the question: the biased-purpose finding merely guided the judge's selection of a sentence within a legislatively authorized range, so the preponderance standard sufficed. Apprendi's position, foreshadowed a year earlier in Jones v. United States, 526 U.S. 227 (1999), was that a fact which doubles the maximum penalty a defendant faces cannot be a mere sentencing consideration, regardless of where the legislature files it. The New Jersey Supreme Court sided with the State, making the case the vehicle for deciding whether the label or the function of the finding controlled.

### What rule did the majority announce, and who joined it?

In a five-to-four decision delivered June 26, 2000, Justice Stevens held that any fact other than a prior conviction that increases the penalty beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must go to a jury and be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Justices Scalia, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg joined the majority; Justices O'Connor and Breyer wrote dissents.

Writing for the Court, Justice Stevens rooted the rule in the Due Process Clause and the Sixth Amendment's notice and jury trial guarantees. The opinion's logic was functional rather than formal: what matters is not the name the legislature gives a finding but its effect on the defendant's exposure. A jury's verdict had authorized ten years; only the judge's additional finding authorized twenty. That finding, the majority explained, operated as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense, carrying both a greater deprivation of liberty and the heightened stigma of a bias-driven crime. The rule kept a single exception, drawn from Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224 (1998): the fact of a prior conviction, which rests on an earlier proceeding with its own procedural safeguards, need not be submitted to the jury anew.

The functional test drew its vocabulary from Jones v. United States, 526 U.S. 227 (1999), where the Court had construed a federal carjacking statute to avoid the same constitutional question, and the majority now answered the question it had sidestepped. The reasoning turned on exposure: the question was never how much punishment the defendant ultimately received but how much punishment the jury's verdict alone authorized before the judge's finding. A twelve-year sentence sat comfortably within the ten-to-twenty-year extended range, yet it exceeded by two years the maximum the conviction supported, and that excess was what the Constitution reached. The opinion was careful about what it left alone. Judges retained broad discretion to weigh facts and choose sentences within the range the jury's verdict authorized, and the decision did not disturb the legislature's power to set severe maximums in the first place. What the legislature could no longer do was create a two-tier system in which the jury fixed a modest ceiling and the judge, on a lower standard of proof, supplied the facts that lifted it.

### What did the concurrences and dissents add?

Justice Scalia defended the jury as a check on the state, while Justice Thomas questioned whether the prior conviction exception could survive the new rule. Justice O'Connor warned the decision would burden courts and dismantle structured sentencing, and Justice Breyer stressed the practical difficulty of submitting complex sentencing facts to juries.

Justice Scalia's concurrence framed the jury right as a substantive reservation of power to the people rather than a procedural formality, insisting that every fact the law makes essential to punishment belongs to the jury. Justice Thomas wrote separately, in a concurrence joined in part by Justice Scalia, to develop the originalist history of the jury trial guarantee and to question whether the prior conviction exception could survive the majority's logic, suggesting that Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224 (1998) had been wrongly decided. The majority left that precedent in place. Justice O'Connor's dissent, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Kennedy and Breyer, warned that the decision would unsettle structured sentencing systems across the country, flood the courts with challenges, and force legislatures into unworkable choices. Justice Breyer, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist, accepted that enhancement facts should be charged and proved beyond a reasonable doubt but argued they could still be decided by a judge, warning that forcing complex sentencing determinations onto juries would distort the trial itself.

The opinions divided less over the jury's importance than over where the doctrine would stop. Justice O'Connor's dissent warned that the majority's functional logic could not be confined to statutory maximums, since the same reasoning would eventually reach mandatory minimums and structured sentencing schemes that depended on judge-found facts, a prediction Harris would test two years later and the guideline cases would test after that. Justice Breyer offered a middle course: charge the enhancement facts in the indictment and prove them beyond a reasonable doubt, but let the judge decide them, preserving both the defendant's notice and the workability of the sentencing process. The majority rejected the compromise, holding that the jury trial right means what it says: the jury, not the judge, finds the facts that raise the ceiling.

### Why was the rule revolutionary rather than a small correction?

Before Apprendi, legislatures could attach extra punishment to judge-found facts simply by calling them sentencing factors. The majority made the label irrelevant: any fact that exposes a defendant to punishment above what the jury's verdict alone authorizes functions as an element, shifting a large body of sentencing power from judges to juries.

The prevailing understanding before Apprendi, embodied in McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), allowed legislatures to designate punishment-triggering facts as sentencing factors for judges to find by a preponderance, as long as the sentence stayed within the statutory maximum. Apprendi ended the labeling game by making the constitutional inquiry functional: does the finding expose the defendant to punishment beyond what the jury's verdict alone authorizes? The revolution lay in the choice of constitutional engine. The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment had produced almost no constraint on the length of adult sentences, and it did none of the work here. The Sixth Amendment's jury trial right, a guarantee usually understood as procedural, turned out to have substantive force. By relocating fact-finding authority, the decision constrained punishment indirectly: the government could still pursue longer sentences, but it had to prove the aggravating facts to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, with the notice, confrontation, and deliberation rights a jury trial carries.

The exposure idea also explains why the jury right rather than the punishment clause did the work. A proportionality challenge asks the Court to second-guess the legislature's judgment about severity, a task the justices had declined for adult sentences. A jury right challenge asks only who applies the legislature's own judgment, a question courts can answer without grading punishments. Apprendi therefore constrained severity without ever declaring any sentence too long: by moving the decisive facts to the jury and the higher burden of proof, it raised the cost of every enhancement the legislature wrote.

### How did Ring v. Arizona carry the rule into capital sentencing?

Ring applied Apprendi to capital cases. Timothy Ring faced a jury verdict whose maximum authorized punishment was life imprisonment, yet an Arizona judge imposed death after finding two aggravating factors alone. By a seven-to-two vote, the Court held those factors were the functional equivalent of elements and had to be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt.

Timothy Ring stood convicted by an Arizona jury of first-degree murder under a felony murder theory for the November 1994 robbery of an armored car in Glendale, during which the driver, John Magoch, was shot and killed. Under Arizona law, the jury's verdict alone authorized a maximum sentence of life imprisonment; the death penalty became available only after the trial judge found at least one aggravating factor. The judge found two, pecuniary gain and an especially heinous, cruel, or depraved manner of killing, and imposed death. The Court had sustained this arrangement in Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), and the Apprendi majority had left that precedent undisturbed. In a seven-to-two decision announced June 24, 2002, Justice Ginsburg's majority resolved the tension by overruling Walton to the extent it allowed a sentencing judge, sitting without a jury, to find the aggravating circumstance necessary for a death sentence. Arizona's enumerated aggravating factors operated as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense, and the majority concluded that capital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants, were entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in maximum punishment. Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice O'Connor dissented.

The tension with Walton had been visible from the start. Justice O'Connor's Apprendi dissent had quoted Arizona's own supreme court describing the state scheme in Apprendi's terms: a defendant convicted of first-degree murder in Arizona could not receive a death sentence unless a judge made the factual determination that a statutory aggravating factor existed, and without that finding the maximum exposure was life imprisonment. The Arizona court had felt bound by Walton all the same. Ring resolved the conflict in Apprendi's favor, and the majority declined to treat capital sentencing as a special domain: the Sixth Amendment drew no line between death and imprisonment when the question was who must find the fact that raises the ceiling.

### Why did Harris v. United States draw a different line for mandatory minimums?

Harris concerned a judge-found fact that raised a mandatory minimum from five to seven years without changing the maximum. Justice Kennedy's opinion treated brandishing under 18 U.S.C. Section 924(c) as a sentencing factor under McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), so Apprendi's maximum rule did not apply. Alleyne v. United States (2013) overruled Harris.

William Joseph Harris sold narcotics at his pawnshop with an unconcealed semiautomatic pistol at his side and was prosecuted under 18 U.S.C. Section 924(c)(1)(A), which prescribed five years for using or carrying a firearm during a drug crime, seven if the firearm was brandished, and ten if discharged. The indictment said nothing about brandishing. After conviction, the judge found by a preponderance that Harris had brandished the gun and imposed the seven-year minimum. Justice Kennedy's controlling opinion treated brandishing as a sentencing factor and held, following McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), that judicial fact-finding which raises a mandatory minimum without raising the statutory maximum does not offend the Sixth Amendment. The reasoning was that such a finding merely narrows the judge's discretion within the range the jury's verdict already authorized, exposing the defendant to no punishment the verdict alone did not permit. Justice Breyer, concurring in the judgment, admitted he could not easily distinguish the case from Apprendi in terms of logic but declined to extend the rule for practical reasons. Justice Thomas, joined by Justices Stevens, Souter, and Ginsburg, dissented, arguing that Apprendi's logic applied with equal force to facts that increase a mandatory minimum. The distinction endured for eleven years, until Alleyne v. United States (2013) overruled Harris and closed the gap the dissenters had identified.

The controlling opinion's reasoning leaned heavily on McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), which had sustained a Pennsylvania statute requiring a five-year minimum when a judge found visible possession of a firearm. Because the brandishing finding changed the mandatory minimum without changing the statutory maximum the jury's verdict had authorized, the plurality concluded, the verdict had already permitted everything the judge imposed; the finding only channeled discretion inside that authorization. Justice Breyer's separate opinion captured the unease this produced: he wrote that he could not easily distinguish Apprendi from the case in terms of logic, yet he joined the judgment because extending the rule would, in his view, damage the workability of sentencing. Justice Thomas's dissent answered that stare decisis carries little weight for procedural rules implicating fundamental protections, and that McMillan could not survive Apprendi's reasoning. Eleven years later, Alleyne adopted the dissent's position.

The maximum rule arrived as a hinge for the sentencing law that followed. It left legislatures free to set severe penalties but stripped them of the power to assign the decisive facts to judges by another name. The dissents' warnings of disruption proved accurate, although the disruption took a particular shape: the functional test the majority announced proved portable, reaching capital sentencing in Ring and, in the years after this section's close, state and federal sentencing guidelines. The question Justice Thomas pressed in dissent, whether facts that raise a mandatory minimum could remain with judges, waited only for a fifth vote. Apprendi had redrawn the constitutional map of punishment around a single axis, the boundary between what the jury found and what the judge added. That boundary, drawn in a case about a New Jersey hate crime enhancement, would go on to reorganize capital sentencing, state guidelines, the federal guidelines, and finally mandatory minimums, which is why the decision reads less like a ruling about one defendant and more like the opening of a two-decade doctrinal sequence.

## Blakely v. Washington and the Summer the Guidelines Shook

On June 24, 2004, the Supreme Court decided Blakely v. Washington, 542 U.S. 296 (2004), by a vote of five to four, and in doing so turned a state sentencing dispute into the most destabilizing criminal procedure decision in a generation. Four years earlier, in Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), the Court had held that any fact other than a prior conviction that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Lower courts had read that rule narrowly, treating the relevant maximum as the legislated ceiling for the offense of conviction, the number printed in the criminal code. Blakely rewrote that understanding. Justice Antonin Scalia's majority opinion defined the relevant maximum as the highest sentence a judge may impose solely on the basis of the facts reflected in the jury verdict or admitted by the defendant, and because Washington's exceptional sentence rested on facts the judge found alone, it could not stand. The decision's logic pointed directly at every mandatory sentencing guidelines system in the country, including the federal one, and the months that followed produced a circuit split, an emergency petition from the Justice Department, and a grant of expedited review in United States v. Booker and United States v. Fanfan that set argument for the first Monday in October.

### What made Ralph Blakely's case the right vehicle for the rule?

Ralph Howard Blakely Jr. pleaded guilty to second-degree kidnapping of his estranged wife, a class B felony with a standard guideline range of 49 to 53 months. The sentencing judge found deliberate cruelty and imposed 90 months, 37 months above the range, so the increase rested entirely on judge-found facts.

The facts were as stark as any appellate record produces. Blakely had bound his estranged wife with duct tape, placed her inside a wooden box in the bed of his truck, and driven her across state lines. He eventually entered guilty pleas to second-degree kidnapping involving domestic violence and use of a firearm, a class B felony under Washington law punishable by up to ten years in prison. Washington's Sentencing Reform Act, however, channeled the sentence into a standard range of 49 to 53 months based on the offense and his offender score, and the prosecution recommended a sentence at the top of that range in exchange for the plea. Before the sentencing hearing, Blakely filed a motion arguing that any upward departure based on judge-found facts would violate Apprendi, and the trial court denied the motion. After an evidentiary hearing at which the court made dozens of factual findings, the judge concluded that Blakely had acted with deliberate cruelty, a statutorily enumerated ground for an exceptional sentence in domestic violence cases, and imposed 90 months on the kidnapping charge to run concurrently with a 14-month sentence on a related assault charge. The Washington Court of Appeals affirmed, rejecting the Apprendi argument, and the Washington Supreme Court denied review, after which the Supreme Court granted certiorari in 540 U.S. 965 (2003). The result was a record in which every element was clean: a guilty plea that authorized 53 months at most, a judicial finding that added 37 months on top, and a preserved constitutional objection to the procedure. The judge had made 32 factual findings after the evidentiary hearing, including the determination that the repeated and protracted confinement of the victim in a coffin-like box manifested deliberate cruelty within the meaning of the statute, so the constitutional question arrived uncluttered by factual ambiguity.

### What did Scalia's definition of the statutory maximum change?

Apprendi had been read as policing only the legislated maximum for the offense of conviction, which left guidelines ranges untouched. Scalia's redefinition made the relevant maximum the top of the range authorized by the verdict alone, so any judge-found fact that raised the ceiling became an element requiring jury proof.

The majority consisted of the same five Justices who had anchored the Apprendi rule: Scalia, joined by Stevens, Souter, Thomas, and Ginsburg. The opinion explained that the relevant statutory maximum was not the maximum a judge may impose after finding additional facts, but the maximum the judge may impose without any additional findings. Applied to Blakely, that meant 53 months, the top of the standard range supported by his guilty plea, and the 90-month sentence therefore inflicted punishment the plea alone did not allow. Scalia wrote that when a judge imposes punishment the jury's verdict alone does not authorize, the jury has not found all the facts the law makes essential to the punishment, and the judge exceeds proper authority. He described the jury right as a fundamental reservation of power in the constitutional structure rather than a procedural formality. The opinion also met the objection that the rule would destroy determinate sentencing head on, explaining that legislatures could comply by submitting aggravating facts to juries or structuring ranges around admitted facts, and that the constitutional allocation of power justified whatever cost compliance imposed.

This redefinition mattered more than Apprendi itself because it closed the interpretive door through which mandatory guidelines systems had walked. After Apprendi, federal courts of appeals had uniformly held that the Apprendi principle was triggered only by the legislated maximum for the offense, not by the top of a guidelines range that sat beneath that ceiling. Blakely's exceptional sentence fell well within the ten-year legislated maximum for his class B felony, and Washington had argued that Apprendi therefore had nothing to say about it. The majority rejected that reading, treating Washington's standard range as itself a legally prescribed ceiling that no judge could exceed without new findings. The opinion acknowledged that legislatures remained free to pursue determinate sentencing through jury findings or stipulated facts, but it held that the jury trial right set the price of exceeding a verdict-based ceiling. For guidelines systems built on mandatory ranges driven by judge-found facts, which is to say nearly all of them, the holding was a direct threat.

### Why did the dissenters warn of an earthquake?

Three dissenting opinions, filed by O'Connor, Kennedy, and Breyer, warned that the majority's logic would dismantle decades of sentencing reform and return the system to unguided judicial discretion. O'Connor called the decision a Number 10 earthquake because she expected it to reach every mandatory guidelines regime in the country.

Justice O'Connor, joined by Breyer and by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Kennedy except as to one part, called the decision a Number 10 earthquake. Her dissent argued that the majority's rule would sweep far beyond Washington's exceptional sentence provision to reach the sentencing guidelines of the federal government and many states, regimes Congress and state legislatures had built over two decades to cure the disparities of indeterminate sentencing. She warned that the Court had effectively told legislatures to choose between jury trials on every sentencing factor and a return to the very discretion the guidelines were designed to restrain, and that the practical cost would fall on courts already carrying heavy criminal dockets.

Justice Breyer, joined by O'Connor, wrote separately to develop the practical consequences in detail. As the principal architect of the federal Sentencing Guidelines during his service on the Sentencing Commission, he emphasized that mandatory guidelines had been designed to replace a system in which similarly situated defendants received wildly different sentences for reasons of race, geography, or judicial temperament. He predicted that applying the majority's rule to the federal guidelines would destroy the uniformity the system had achieved, forcing either unguided discretion or an unworkable scheme of jury sentencing on hundreds of factual issues. His dissent treated the majority's logic as irreconcilable with the realities of a modern sentencing system and warned that the remedy would prove worse than the constitutional defect.

Justice Kennedy, joined by Breyer, filed a third dissent that approached the problem from the standpoint of institutional structure. He argued that the majority had extended Apprendi past its breaking point by converting every guidelines departure into a jury question, and that the result would intrude on legislative judgments about how to structure punishment. He emphasized federalism concerns as well, noting that the decision would force states to redesign sentencing systems that had been carefully balanced among legislatures, judges, and commissions. Together the dissents presented the case as a choice between constitutional formalism and workable governance, and each predicted that the fallout would arrive quickly.

### How did one state case unsettle the entire federal system?

The federal guidelines ran on the same machinery as Washington's scheme, mandatory ranges driven by judge-found facts, so the decision raised the same question for every pending federal sentence. Within weeks, a three-way circuit split and thousands of challenges forced an emergency grant of expedited review in Booker and Fanfan.

The mechanism of the disruption was straightforward. The federal Sentencing Guidelines, in effect since November 1, 1987, under the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, operated as mandatory ranges driven overwhelmingly by judge-found facts. Offense levels rose on judicial findings of drug quantity, loss amount, role in the offense, and obstruction of justice, found by a preponderance of the evidence at sentencing, and those findings regularly moved defendants far above the range authorized by the verdict alone. Under Blakely's redefinition, every such enhancement raised the Apprendi question. Defense lawyers filed challenges in thousands of pending cases within weeks, district judges divided over whether the federal system could survive, and the Justice Department shifted into an emergency posture. Federal prosecutors began adding sentencing factors to indictments and seeking jury findings as a precaution, judges divided over whether to proceed with sentencings under a system the Supreme Court might soon dismantle, and defense motions challenging pending sentences multiplied across the districts.

The courts of appeals split almost immediately. In United States v. Booker, 375 F.3d 508 (7th Cir. 2004), the Seventh Circuit applied Blakely to the federal guidelines and invalidated a sentence enhanced on judge-found facts, over a dissent by Judge Frank Easterbrook arguing that the federal guidelines differed from Washington's scheme because federal law contained only one legislated maximum, the maximum for the offense of conviction, and because the guidelines had an administrative rather than statutory provenance. In United States v. Pineiro, 377 F.3d 464 (5th Cir. 2004), the Fifth Circuit reached the opposite conclusion, holding that the federal guidelines survived Blakely on essentially the ground Easterbrook had advanced. The Second Circuit, sitting en banc, took the extraordinary step of certifying questions to the Supreme Court in United States v. Penaranda on July 12, 2004, rather than deciding the issue itself, a procedure it had not used for the issue in decades. With the circuits in open disagreement and sentencings proceeding under a cloud, the Solicitor General sought expedited review, including certiorari before judgment in a companion case, and asked that the cases be heard on an accelerated schedule.

The companion case was United States v. Fanfan. Ducan Fanfan had been convicted by a jury of conspiracy to distribute and to possess with intent to distribute at least 500 grams of cocaine, a verdict that carried a guidelines maximum of 78 months on the jury's findings alone. At sentencing, the judge found additional drug quantities and an aggravating role adjustment that would have required a sentence of 188 to 235 months under the mandatory guidelines, but concluded that Blakely barred the enhancements and imposed sentence based solely on the verdict's facts. The government appealed directly to the Supreme Court, seeking review before the court of appeals had ruled. On August 2, 2004, the Court granted certiorari in Booker and granted certiorari before judgment in Fanfan, consolidated the two cases, and set an expedited briefing schedule with oral argument for October 4, the first day of the new term. The Solicitor General had asked for argument during the summer recess; the Court adopted an alternative schedule that preserved most of the summer while still treating the matter as an emergency.

### What constitutional stakes faced the Court as October approached?

The Court faced the possible destruction of the central machinery of federal sentencing, a mandatory regime that had bound every federal judge since 1987. The grant framed two questions: whether Blakely applied to the federal guidelines and, if so, how much of the system survived.

The regime had been sustained against separation of powers attack in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989). Seventeen years of practice, hundreds of thousands of sentences, and an entire institutional architecture of commissions, presentence reports, and appellate review now rested on a constitutional premise five Justices had just rejected.

The questions presented captured the full range of the emergency. The first asked whether the Sixth Amendment was violated by the imposition of an enhanced sentence under the federal Sentencing Guidelines based on a sentencing judge's determination of a fact, other than a prior conviction, that was not found by the jury or admitted by the defendant. The second asked, if the answer were yes, how much of the guidelines or of the Sentencing Reform Act under which they were created remained viable, a severability question that forced the Court to consider whether the guidelines could be saved in part or would have to fall as a whole. Those two questions divided the constitutional issue from the remedial one, and the division would prove decisive: the same five-Justice majority that had carried Apprendi and Blakely would answer the first question, while a different alignment would decide the second.

Congress, for its part, moved quickly to put its concerns on the record. On July 13, 2004, the Senate Judiciary Committee held a hearing titled Blakely v. Washington and the Future of the Sentencing Guidelines, at which witnesses described the decision's questions as flooding an already burdened criminal justice system and debated whether the federal guidelines could survive. The testimony reflected a consensus on one point: whatever the Supreme Court decided in Booker and Fanfan, the seventeen-year project of mandatory federal sentencing could not continue in its existing form if Blakely applied. The only open questions were whether it applied and what would replace the system if it did. The answers, when they came in January 2005, would reshape federal sentencing without striking down the statute that created it, completing the arc that Blakely had begun: the Sixth Amendment, not the Eighth, as the engine of modern sentencing doctrine.

## United States v. Booker and the Advisory Turn

The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 created the United States Sentencing Commission and directed it to issue [the guidelines](/2018/01/01/sentencing-reform-act-1984-guide/) intended to make sentences for comparable offenses and offenders more uniform. Under 18 U.S.C. section 3553(b)(1), a sentencing court had to impose a sentence within the calculated guideline range absent an aggravating or mitigating circumstance of a kind or degree not adequately considered by the Commission. The guidelines therefore operated as binding legal limits, not advice.

That mandatory structure collided with the Supreme Court's developing Sixth Amendment doctrine. In Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), the Court held that any fact, other than a prior conviction, that increased the penalty beyond the prescribed statutory maximum had to be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. In Blakely v. Washington, 542 U.S. 296 (2004), the Court applied that principle to a state guideline system and defined the relevant maximum as the maximum available on the jury's findings alone. Because mandatory federal enhancements required higher sentences whenever a judge found specified facts by a preponderance of the evidence, the federal guidelines raised the same question. United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), answered it and then rebuilt federal sentencing through a remedy that left the statute standing.

### Who were the defendants, and why were their cases consolidated?

Booker and Fanfan were two federal drug defendants whose sentences exposed the constitutional problem from opposite procedural directions. The Court decided both cases together in United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), because each asked whether judge-found facts could raise the mandatory sentence beyond the range supported by the jury's findings.

Freddie Booker was convicted of possessing at least 50 grams of cocaine base with intent to distribute. On the jury's findings alone, the federal guidelines authorized 210 to 262 months of imprisonment. At sentencing, the judge found by a preponderance of the evidence that Booker had distributed 566 grams more than the amount necessarily found by the jury and that he had obstructed justice. Those findings raised the mandatory range to 360 months to life, and the judge imposed 360 months. The Seventh Circuit reversed the sentence on Sixth Amendment grounds.

Ducan Fanfan was convicted of conspiracy to distribute and to possess with intent to distribute at least 500 grams of cocaine. The jury verdict supported a maximum guideline sentence of 78 months. The judge found by a preponderance of the evidence that Fanfan was responsible for 2.5 kilograms of cocaine powder and 261.6 grams of crack and that he had acted as an organizer, leader, manager, or supervisor. Those findings would have required 188 to 235 months. Because Blakely had been decided only days earlier, the judge declined to use the findings and imposed the 78-month sentence supported by the jury verdict. The government sought correction of the sentence, then appealed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari before judgment in Fanfan and consolidated the case with Booker's to decide whether the Apprendi line applied to the federal guidelines and which parts of the scheme survived.

The pairing mattered because it framed the remedy. Booker's case asked how to handle a sentence already enhanced by judge-found facts. Fanfan's case asked whether a judge could refuse to impose the enhanced mandatory range in anticipation of the constitutional objection. Together, they required the Court to address both the merits question and the consequences of its answer.

### What did the two five-justice majorities decide?

Booker produced two distinct five-justice majorities, with Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg as the only justice to join both. Each majority answered a different question. One majority decided whether the mandatory federal guidelines violated the Sixth Amendment. The other decided what to do about that violation.

The merits majority was written by Justice John Paul Stevens and joined by Justices Antonin Scalia, David Souter, Clarence Thomas, and Ruth Bader Ginsburg. It held that Blakely applied to the federal sentencing guidelines. Because the guidelines were mandatory, the top of the guideline range supported by the jury's verdict functioned as the statutory maximum for Apprendi purposes. Judge-found facts that increased the applicable range therefore violated the right to trial by jury. The opinion affirmed the Seventh Circuit's reversal of Booker's sentence and vacated Fanfan's sentence, sending the cases back for resentencing.

This 5-4 holding turned on compulsion. A mandatory scheme that required a higher sentence whenever the judge found an enhancing fact by a preponderance of the evidence gave judges the power that Apprendi and Blakely had assigned to juries. The Court did not forbid all sentencing fact-finding. It held that the violation occurred when a legal rule made the enhanced sentence mandatory because the judge found facts the jury had not found beyond a reasonable doubt.

The remedial majority was written by Justice Stephen Breyer and joined by Chief Justice William Rehnquist and Justices Sandra Day O'Connor, Anthony Kennedy, and Ruth Bader Ginsburg. It did not try to save the mandatory system by requiring juries to find every sentencing fact. Instead, it severed and excised the provisions that made the guidelines mandatory. The Court removed 18 U.S.C. section 3553(b)(1), which directed district courts to impose sentences within the guideline range, and 18 U.S.C. section 3742(e), which set out appellate review standards tied to the mandatory scheme. The result was to make the guidelines effectively advisory. That remedial opinion also was 5-4.

Justice Ginsburg's position explains the alignment. She joined the holding that mandatory judge-found enhancements violated the jury-trial right, and she also joined the remedy that preserved the Sentencing Reform Act by converting its mandatory commands into advisory guidance. The remedial majority reasoned that Congress would have preferred this surgical revision to either total invalidation or jury procedures for every sentencing fact, and that an advisory system better preserved the connection between sentences and real offense conduct.

### How did the remedy leave the statute standing?

The remedial majority did not declare the Sentencing Reform Act unconstitutional as a whole. It used severability, a familiar judicial technique for preserving legislation after a constitutional defect is found. The Court excised the mandatory language while leaving the remainder of the Act, the Sentencing Commission, and the guidelines themselves intact.

The key change was from shall to consider. Section 3553(b)(1) had told sentencing courts to impose a sentence within the guideline range except in specified circumstances. After severance, that binding command was gone. Section 3553(a) still directed courts to consider the guidelines, but the range became one factor among others, including the offense and the defendant's history, available sentences, unwarranted disparity, restitution, and the instruction to impose a sentence sufficient but not greater than necessary.

The Commission continued to issue ranges, presentence reports continued to calculate them, and judges continued to use them as the starting point. What changed was the legal consequence of finding a fact. In the mandatory system, a judge's finding of drug quantity, role, or obstruction automatically moved the required sentence upward. In the advisory system, such findings informed the sentence but did not compel a result. That distinction was why the remedial majority believed the Sixth Amendment problem disappeared.

The remedy also changed appellate review. The Court replaced the excised review provisions with review for reasonableness. That term needed elaboration, and the elaboration arrived in a trio of 2007 decisions.

The immediate consequence for the two defendants was procedural rather than a ruling on their ultimate punishments. The merits holding vacated the unconstitutional mandatory application, while the remedial holding directed the district courts to calculate the guidelines and then impose sentences under section 3553(a). Their convictions remained intact; only the method for choosing the sentence changed. That distinction captured Booker's larger design: the Court repaired the procedure without dismantling the statutory scheme.

### How did Rita, Gall, and Kimbrough define advisory sentencing?

The 2007 reasonableness trilogy gave Booker operational content. Rita v. United States, 551 U.S. 338 (2007), Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38 (2007), and Kimbrough v. United States, 552 U.S. 85 (2007), addressed how district courts should use the advisory guidelines and how appellate courts should review the resulting sentences.

Rita held that a court of appeals may apply a presumption of reasonableness to a sentence within the properly calculated guideline range. The decision was 8-1, with Justice Breyer writing for the majority. The presumption applied only on appellate review. It did not permit a district judge to presume that the guideline range was reasonable in the first instance. The district court was required to make an individualized assessment of the defendant and the offense under section 3553(a). The appellate presumption reflected the idea that both the Commission and the sentencing judge had arrived at the same conclusion about the appropriate sentence, one at wholesale and the other at retail.

Gall concerned a sentence below the guideline range. Brian Gall had joined an ecstasy distribution enterprise while in college, withdrew after seven months, stopped using and selling illegal drugs, completed his education, and worked steadily. More than three years after leaving the conspiracy, he pleaded guilty. The guideline range was 30 to 37 months of imprisonment, but the district court imposed 36 months of probation. The Eighth Circuit reversed, holding that such a large downward variance required extraordinary circumstances. The Supreme Court reversed that judgment in a 7-2 decision written by Justice Stevens.

Gall established a two-part framework for appellate review. First, the appellate court ensures that the district court committed no significant procedural error, including failure to calculate the guideline range, treating the guidelines as mandatory, ignoring the section 3553(a) factors, relying on clearly erroneous facts, or failing to explain the sentence adequately. Second, the court reviews substantive reasonableness under a deferential abuse-of-discretion standard, considering the totality of the circumstances and the extent of any variance. The Court rejected a presumption against non-guideline sentences, a rigid rule requiring extraordinary justification for large variances, and a mathematical formula tying the required justification to the percentage difference. Appellate disagreement alone did not justify reversal.

Kimbrough addressed a different issue: whether a district judge could vary from the guidelines because the judge disagreed with the Commission's policy choice. Derrick Kimbrough pleaded guilty to crack and powder cocaine offenses and a firearms offense. The advisory guideline range was 228 to 270 months. The district court concluded that the 100-to-1 crack-to-powder quantity ratio embedded in the guidelines produced a disproportionate and unjust sentence in the case and imposed the 15-year statutory minimum instead. The Fourth Circuit vacated the sentence, treating policy-based disagreement with the crack guideline as unreasonable as a matter of law.

The Supreme Court reversed in a 7-2 decision written by Justice Ginsburg. It held that the cocaine guidelines, like all other guidelines, were advisory only. A district judge had to include the guideline range among the factors warranting consideration, but could determine that a within-range sentence was greater than necessary to serve the objectives of sentencing. In making that determination, the judge could consider the disparity between the guidelines' treatment of crack and powder offenses. The Court emphasized that the crack guideline had implemented a quantity ratio established by Congress rather than reflecting the Commission's characteristic expert judgment, so disagreement with it did not reject the Commission's institutional role.

The three decisions together clarified the boundaries of advisory sentencing. Rita supplied a limited appellate presumption for within-range sentences. Gall supplied a deferential review standard and rejected special burdens for variances. Kimbrough confirmed that advisory meant advisory even when the disagreement was with the Commission's policy rather than its application to particular facts. The trilogy thus replaced mandatory compliance with structured discretion, not with standardless choice.

### Why did advisory guidelines not mean shorter sentences?

One of the most persistent misunderstandings about Booker is the assumption that making the guidelines advisory reduced federal sentences across the board. The evidence did not support that simple story. Advisory guidelines gave judges more freedom to impose below-range sentences, but they did not remove several constraints that continued to shape punishment.

First, Congress's statutory minimum and maximum penalties remained binding. Booker addressed the guidelines, not the statutes defining the offense and setting outer punishment limits. A judge could not go below a statutory mandatory minimum merely because a lower sentence seemed sufficient under section 3553(a), except where Congress provided a safety valve or another exception. Kimbrough illustrated the point: the district court varied from the guideline range but still imposed the 15-year statutory minimum. Advisory guidelines operated inside congressional limits.

Second, calculating the guidelines remained a mandatory procedural step. Gall required district courts to begin with the range and remain cognizant of it, while Rita emphasized correct calculation before individualized assessment. A judge who skipped the calculation, calculated it incorrectly, or treated the advisory range as binding committed procedural error. The Commission's work therefore still anchored sentencing, even though the anchor no longer fixed the sentence.

Third, the measured effect on sentence length was uneven. The Sentencing Commission's early analysis found that most federal cases continued to conform to the guidelines and that average sentence length had not declined substantially in the first year after Booker. Other research found greater behavioral effects, including increased judge-to-judge variation. One Massachusetts study found that the effect of judge assignment on sentence length more than doubled after Booker, Gall, and Kimbrough, producing an average difference of more than two years between more lenient and more severe judges in cases without mandatory minimums. The average decline was measurable but modest, roughly several months.

That pattern resists simple characterization. Defendants gained opportunities to seek below-range sentences through individualized section 3553(a) arguments and, after Kimbrough, through policy disagreement with particular guidelines. Yet appellate review remained deferential, guideline calculations continued to anchor decisions, and statutory minimums limited downward movement. Advisory sentencing expanded discretion without removing the forces pushing toward uniformity.

The constitutional structure of Booker also explains why the decision did not invalidate federal sentencing. The merits majority found a Sixth Amendment violation in mandatory judge-found enhancements. The remedial majority answered by removing the mandatory command rather than the guideline system. That choice preserved the Sentencing Reform Act, the Commission, and the guidelines as guidance. It also preserved the distinction between a legal requirement that a sentence increase because a judge found a fact and a judge's discretion to consider facts when choosing a sentence within statutory limits. The first remained unconstitutional. The second remained permissible.

The litigation after Booker therefore concerned procedure and review rather than the statute's survival. Judges calculate the range, consider the statutory factors, individualize the sentence, and explain their reasoning. Appellate courts check for procedural error and then assess substantive reasonableness under a deferential standard. That framework came not from later congressional action but from the Court's excision of two provisions.

The recurring error that Booker struck down the guidelines misses the decision's central point. The guidelines survived as law, while their mandatory application did not. The resulting system was neither the indeterminate regime that preceded the Sentencing Reform Act nor the rigid scheme Congress enacted in 1984. It was a hybrid in which the guidelines guided without commanding, appellate courts reviewed for reasonableness, and the constitutional constraint came from the jury-trial right rather than a judgment that the punishment was too severe.

## Alleyne v. United States and the Mandatory Minimum Frontier

After Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), drew its bright line around facts that increase the statutory maximum, one question remained pointedly unresolved. The rule spoke of facts that raised the ceiling. What about facts that raised the floor. Congress and state legislatures had long written statutes in which a jury verdict authorized a broad sentencing range while a judicial finding at sentencing triggered a mandatory minimum within that range. Under the logic of Apprendi, those floor raising findings looked like elements of an aggravated offense. Under a different line of precedent, they looked like traditional sentencing factors that judges had always found on their own. The resolution of that tension came in two stages: first the Court refused to extend the jury right to the floor, then eleven years later it reversed itself and held that the floor is governed by the same constitutional command as the ceiling.

### Why did Harris v. United States treat the maximum and the minimum differently?

A five justice majority read Apprendi as reaching only the maximum. Harris upheld a judge-found brandishing finding that raised the mandatory minimum from five years to seven, treating it as a sentencing factor within a range the verdict authorized. Breyer conceded the logic was hard to distinguish from Apprendi; Thomas dissented on that ground.

### What did Alleyne hold about facts that increase a mandatory minimum?

Alleyne held that any fact that increases a mandatory minimum is an element the jury must find beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge-found brandishing that had raised Alleyne's minimum from five to seven years violated the Sixth Amendment: a raised floor narrows the legally prescribed punishment just as a raised ceiling does. Harris was overruled.

The majority's reasoning treated the range of authorized punishment as a single whole. Thomas wrote that a mandatory minimum affects the penalty in the same essential way that a maximum does: it narrows the band of sentences the court may impose and deprives the judge of the option of choosing a sentence below the raised floor. When a fact increases the floor, the defendant faces a harsher legally prescribed punishment than the jury's findings alone authorized, which is precisely the harm the jury trial right exists to prevent. The essential inquiry, in the majority's formulation, is whether a fact aggravates the legally prescribed punishment; if it does, the fact is an element regardless of whether it moves the top or the bottom of the range.

The majority acknowledged the force of stare decisis but concluded that Harris was an outlier that had proven unworkable and irreconcilable with the Court's broader Sixth Amendment jurisprudence. Harris had created a framework in which the same factual finding, brandishing a firearm, would be an element if the statute said the maximum was seven years but a mere sentencing factor if the statute said the minimum was seven years. That asymmetry, the majority said, had no grounding in the constitutional distinction between elements and sentencing factors, which turns on whether the fact defines the legally prescribed penalty. Four justices dissented, led by Chief Justice Roberts, who argued that Harris rested on a defensible historical and functional understanding and that overruling it after only eleven years damaged the stability of sentencing law.

The practical consequences were immediate. After Alleyne, the government could no longer leave minimum triggering facts to the sentencing judge in federal court. Indictments began to allege brandishing and discharging separately, juries began to answer special verdict questions about those facts, and sentences previously imposed on judicial findings became vulnerable on direct appeal. The decision also cast a long shadow over state systems with similar floor raising structures. Drug statutes with quantity based [mandatory minimums](/2018/02/01/mandatory-minimum-sentencing-law/), firearms statutes with enhancement tiers, and recidivist provisions with predicate finding requirements all had to be reexamined to determine whether a judge or a jury had to find the triggering fact.

The dissent's concerns also proved worth remembering. The dissenters warned that requiring jury findings for minimum triggering facts would complicate charging decisions, multiply trial complexity, and strain the resources of prosecutors and courts. In practice, prosecutors adapted by alleging the enhancing facts in the indictment and submitting them to juries, often through special verdict forms that added a line or two to deliberations. The administrative burden turned out to be real but manageable. The more durable consequence was strategic: plea negotiations now had to account for the fact that a defendant who went to trial was entitled to jury resolution of every floor raising fact, which shifted bargaining leverage in cases built around mandatory minimums. Yet the Court was careful to preserve one boundary: judges retain broad discretion to find facts when selecting a sentence within the range the jury's verdict authorizes. Alleyne limits the legally prescribed range, not the exercise of discretion inside it.

### Does the Apprendi rule apply to criminal fines as well as imprisonment?

Yes. Southern Union Co. v. United States, 567 U.S. 343 (2012), held that the Apprendi rule applies to criminal fines as it applies to imprisonment. The six justice majority saw no principled distinction between a punishment denominated in years and one denominated in dollars: the Sixth Amendment protects the jury's role in setting the maximum penalty whatever its form.

The Court, in a six to three opinion by Justice Sotomayor, held that the Apprendi rule applies to criminal fines with the same force it applies to terms of imprisonment. There is no principled distinction, the majority explained, between a punishment denominated in years and a punishment denominated in dollars. The Sixth Amendment protects the jury's historic role in determining the facts that set the maximum penalty, and fines are punishment. The dissent, authored by Justice Breyer, argued that fines differ from imprisonment because the historical evidence about jury practice regarding fines was thinner and because fine setting has always involved wide judicial discretion. The majority answered that Apprendi's logic turns on the jury's function as an intermediary between the state and the defendant, a function that does not change character when the punishment is monetary.

Southern Union mattered for sentencing law in two ways. First, it extended the jury fact finding requirement into the corporate criminal context, where fines rather than imprisonment are the primary sanction. Environmental and regulatory prosecutions, in which per day or per violation penalties multiply into large maximums, now required jury findings on the multiplying facts. Second, it demonstrated that the Court's Sixth Amendment revolution was not confined to prison sentences or even to individuals. The protection attached to the authorized punishment whatever its form, which reinforced the reasoning that Alleyne would adopt a year later for mandatory minimums.

### What did Erlinger v. United States decide about prior convictions and jury findings?

Erlinger held that the jury must determine beyond a reasonable doubt whether the defendant's prior offenses were committed on occasions different from one another under the Armed Career Criminal Act. That occasions inquiry raises both the minimum and the maximum, so it belongs to the jury. The prior conviction exception survived: only the occasions finding exceeds what it can bear.

The decision drew on Wooden v. United States, 595 U.S. 360 (2022), which had explained that deciding whether offenses occurred on different occasions requires examining timing, location, character, and relationship, a multi factored factual inquiry. Because that inquiry goes well beyond the bare existence of a prior conviction, the majority concluded it could not be swept into the narrow exception that permits judges to find the fact of a prior conviction. The government itself agreed with Erlinger's position, conceding that the occasions question must be resolved by a jury. The Court appointed an amicus to defend the judgment below, and rejected the amicus's argument that the exception covered the occasions finding.

Erlinger's significance lies partly in what it did not do. The majority expressly preserved the core of Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224 (1998), describing that decision as an outlier and an exceptional departure from historic practice but leaving intact its narrow permission for judges to find the fact of a prior conviction itself. The Court held only that the occasions inquiry, which demands findings about how and when past crimes occurred, exceeds what the exception can bear. Justice Thomas concurred separately to say the Court should revisit Almendarez-Torres altogether. Justices Kavanaugh and Alito dissented, joined in part by Justice Jackson, arguing that Almendarez-Torres resolves the question and that recidivism has traditionally been treated as a sentencing factor rather than an element.

### Does the prior conviction exception still stand after Erlinger?

It does, though on weakened foundations. Almendarez-Torres held that the fact of a prior conviction, when it raises the maximum, may be found by a judge. Apprendi carved out that exception, Alleyne repeated the carve-out in a footnote, and Erlinger reaffirmed it while calling the decision an outlier. Through the Court's 2024 term, no majority had overruled it.

Yet the exception's intellectual support has eroded steadily. The Apprendi majority acknowledged that Almendarez-Torres was arguably decided incorrectly but declined to revisit it because the issue was not squarely presented. Justice Thomas, who wrote the original Almendarez-Torres majority opinion, later concluded it was wrongly decided and has repeatedly called for its overruling. The Erlinger majority's language, calling the decision at best an exceptional departure, invites future litigants to press the question again. The practical consequence is a holding pattern: the exception governs, but counsel in federal sentencing cases routinely preserve objections to it, and the Court's own descriptions suggest its days may be numbered.

What remains open after this line of decisions is substantial. Courts continue to work out how Alleyne applies to facts that trigger mandatory consecutive sentences, to restitution amounts, and to supervised release conditions that carry penalties for violation. The retroactivity question has produced mixed answers, with several circuits holding that Alleyne does not apply retroactively on collateral review because it announced a procedural rather than substantive rule. And the broader structural question persists: whether the jury right will eventually swallow the entire edifice of judicial sentencing fact finding, or whether the Court will settle on the current equilibrium, in which judges freely choose sentences within ranges that only juries may set. The trajectory from Harris to Alleyne to Erlinger suggests a Court steadily expanding the jury's domain, but the preserved exception for prior convictions and the preserved discretion within authorized ranges show that the revolution has defined edges. Sentencing law now turns, more than at any point in the modern era, on a single institutional question: who found the fact.

## The Eighth Amendment's Near Silence on Adult Sentences

The surprise at the heart of this article is that the constitutional provision most associated with punishment has done almost none of the work of reshaping American sentencing. The Eighth Amendment bars cruel and unusual punishment, and generations of law students learn that the clause must surely constrain extreme sentences. In practice, for adult offenders serving ordinary prison terms, the provision has sustained challenge after challenge and struck down almost nothing. The doctrine that did reshape sentencing belonged to the Sixth Amendment, which concerns the right to a jury trial. To see why, follow the proportionality line from its one high point through its long failure, and watch the reasons the Court gave for declining to intervene accumulate into a doctrine of deference.

### Which sentence did the Court strike down as disproportionate?

In Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277 (1983), a 5 to 4 majority invalidated life imprisonment without parole for a man whose triggering offense was uttering a one hundred dollar no account check. Justice Powell announced a three part test weighing the offense against the penalty and comparing the sentence with other punishments in the state and elsewhere.

The facts in Solem were designed to provoke disbelief. Jerry Helm had been convicted of six prior nonviolent felonies between 1964 and 1975, including three burglaries, obtaining money under false pretenses, grand larceny, and third offense driving while intoxicated. In 1979 he passed a one hundred dollar check drawn on an account that did not exist, a felony under South Dakota law that ordinarily carried a maximum of five years. Because of his record, the state's recidivist statute enhanced the penalty to life without parole, the harshest punishment South Dakota could impose for any crime, including murder.

Powell's majority opinion held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits not only barbaric methods of punishment but also sentences that are disproportionate to the crime. The record showed that Helm had received the penultimate sentence for relatively minor criminal conduct, that he had been treated more severely than offenders who committed more serious crimes in South Dakota, and that he appeared to have been treated more severely than he would have been in any other state. Chief Justice Burger, joined by three others, dissented on the ground that the decision invaded state sentencing discretion with no limiting principle. Solem stands as the only Supreme Court decision to invalidate a noncapital adult sentence on proportionality grounds, and that singularity is itself the point. Every later case would distinguish it, narrow it, or decline to follow its lead.

### How did the Court sustain a life sentence for three minor property crimes?

In Rummel v. Estelle, 445 U.S. 263 (1980), a 5 to 4 majority upheld mandatory life imprisonment, with parole possible, for a man whose three felonies involved about two hundred thirty dollars in frauds. Justice Rehnquist held that the length of a prison term was primarily a matter of legislative judgment.

William James Rummel's offenses were, in dollar terms, almost comically small: credit card fraud for eighty dollars in 1964, a forged check for twenty eight dollars and thirty six cents in 1969, and one hundred twenty dollars and seventy five cents obtained by false pretenses in 1973. Texas law mandated life imprisonment for a third felony conviction once the offender had twice been convicted and actually imprisoned for earlier felonies. Rehnquist's opinion emphasized two features of the scheme. First, the legislature's interest was not merely punishing the final theft but dealing more harshly with a person who had twice demonstrated that conviction and imprisonment would not deter him. Second, the life sentence carried the possibility of parole, estimated at roughly twelve years, so the Court declined to treat the penalty as a literal life term. Justice Powell, joined by three others, dissented with the observation that such a sentence would be viewed as grossly unjust by virtually every layman and lawyer. The majority's answer was that the judgment about what justice requires belongs to legislatures, not to courts. Rummel set the tone for everything that followed: recidivist statutes, even when triggered by trivial underlying offenses, would be assessed through legislative deference rather than judicial moral accounting.

### Why did the Court refuse proportionality review of a forty year drug sentence?

In Hutto v. Davis, 454 U.S. 370 (1982), the Court reversed a federal appellate decision that had vacated two consecutive twenty year terms for possession with intent to distribute and distribution of nine ounces of marijuana. The unsigned opinion warned that successful proportionality challenges should be exceedingly rare.

Roger Davis was convicted in Virginia state court after officers raided his home and seized about nine ounces of marijuana along with drug paraphernalia, and after officers tape recorded him selling marijuana and other controlled substances to a police informant. The jury imposed twenty years and a ten thousand dollar fine on each of two counts, to run consecutively, within a statutory range reaching forty years on each count. The federal district court, applying a four factor test borrowed from the Fourth Circuit's decision in Hart v. Coiner, concluded that the forty year total was so grossly disproportionate as to violate the Eighth Amendment, and the court of appeals agreed even after the Supreme Court had remanded the case for reconsideration in light of Rummel. The Supreme Court's per curiam opinion was impatient, holding that the lower courts had ignored Rummel's proposition that federal courts should be reluctant to review legislatively mandated prison terms and that comparative analyses were precisely the sort of line drawing that belongs to legislatures. Justice Powell concurred in the judgment while describing the sentence as unjust and disproportionate, deferring nonetheless to the legislature. Hutto's message was that proportionality review was becoming a doctrine in name only, acknowledged in dicta and withheld in practice.

### What did the Court decide about mandatory life without parole for drug possession?

In Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957 (1991), a 5 to 4 Court upheld mandatory life without parole for possessing six hundred seventy two grams of cocaine. Seven justices agreed that the Eighth Amendment imposes some proportionality requirement, and Justice Kennedy's concurrence made gross disproportionality the threshold test.

Ronald Harmelin had been convicted under a Michigan statute that made possession of more than six hundred fifty grams of a controlled substance punishable by mandatory life without parole. He challenged the sentence as cruel and unusual on two grounds: that it was disproportionate to his offense, and that the Eighth Amendment required the sentencer to consider mitigating circumstances such as his lack of any prior felony record. The Court split into three camps. Justice Scalia, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist, announced the judgment but carried only two votes for his central proposition that the Eighth Amendment contains no proportionality guarantee at all outside capital cases and that Solem had been wrongly decided. Justice Kennedy, joined by Justices O'Connor and Souter, concurred in the judgment while preserving a narrow proportionality principle. On the Kennedy account, the Eighth Amendment forbids only extreme sentences that are grossly disproportionate to the crime, and courts should make a threshold comparison of the crime and the sentence before reaching the intra jurisdictional and inter jurisdictional comparisons that Solem had described. Applied to Harmelin, the concurrence concluded that no inference of gross disproportionality arose: the six hundred seventy two grams in his possession had a potential yield of between thirty two thousand five hundred and sixty five thousand doses, and the Michigan legislature could reasonably conclude that possession of so large an amount warranted the deterrence and retribution of a life term. Justice White, joined by Justices Blackmun and Stevens, dissented on the ground that the sentence was grossly disproportionate, and Justices Marshall and Stevens filed separate dissents. On the second claim, five justices agreed that the individualized sentencing requirement developed in capital cases does not extend to noncapital offenses, so the mandatory character of the penalty raised no constitutional problem. Harmelin thus kept a proportionality principle nominally alive while hollowing it out: the three part Solem inquiry was formally available but practically unreachable, because a court that found no gross disproportionality at the threshold would never reach the comparative analyses.

### How did the three strikes cases treat sentences of twenty five years and fifty years to life?

In Ewing v. California, 538 U.S. 11 (2003), the Court sustained twenty five years to life for stealing three golf clubs worth about twelve hundred dollars. The same day, Lockyer v. Andrade, 538 U.S. 63 (2003), sustained two consecutive twenty five year to life terms for shoplifting about one hundred fifty dollars in videotapes.

Under California's three strikes law, a defendant convicted of a felony who has two or more prior serious or violent felony convictions receives an indeterminate life term with a minimum of twenty five years. The trial court declined to exercise its discretion to reduce the grand theft to a misdemeanor or to dismiss prior strike allegations, and Justice O'Connor, writing for a plurality joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Kennedy, held that the resulting sentence was not grossly disproportionate. The opinion emphasized the state's public safety interest in incapacitating and deterring recidivist felons and warned that courts should not act as a super legislature by second guessing the policy choices embedded in sentencing law. Justices Scalia and Thomas concurred in the judgment on the broader ground that proportionality review of prison terms has no constitutional basis, while four justices dissented.

Leandro Andrade stole five videotapes worth eighty four dollars and seventy cents from one store and, two weeks later, four videotapes worth sixty eight dollars and eighty four cents from another. Each petty theft with a prior conviction was charged as a felony, and each triggered a separate application of the three strikes law, producing two consecutive twenty five year to life terms. The California Court of Appeal had affirmed after questioning whether Solem's analysis survived Harmelin, and the Ninth Circuit granted habeas relief. Justice O'Connor, writing for the 5 to 4 majority, reversed. Reviewing the case under the deferential standard of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996, the Court held that the only clearly established federal law was the gross disproportionality principle, that the Court's own precedents gave no clear answer about what marks a sentence as grossly disproportionate, and that the state court's reliance on Rummel rather than Solem was not an unreasonable application of that law. Justice Souter, joined by three others, dissented, arguing that fifty years before parole eligibility for shoplifting videotapes was the kind of sentence the proportionality principle should catch. The two decisions, read together, completed the arc: a doctrine that had begun with Solem's careful three part comparison ended with the Court declaring that the controlling standard was so indeterminate that a state court could hardly misapply it.

### Why has proportionality doctrine accomplished so little for adult sentences?

The narrow proportionality principle forbids only extreme sentences that are grossly disproportionate to the crime, and the Court has combined that threshold with deep deference to legislatures on questions of penology. Because courts resolve most challenges at the threshold comparison, the comparative inquiries into other sentences and other states almost never occur, and sentences measured in decades routinely survive.

Several doctrinal commitments explain the pattern. The Court treats the length of prison terms as quintessentially legislative, a view Rehnquist stated plainly in Rummel and that later majorities repeated. Legislatures may pursue any combination of recognized penological goals, including retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation, and the Court has refused to rank those goals or to require that a particular sentence serve the goal a reviewing judge would prefer. Recidivism occupies a privileged place in this framework: the state is punishing not only the triggering offense but the offender's demonstrated refusal to conform to the law after prior convictions and imprisonment, which is why sentences that look shocking when measured against a single shoplifting offense look defensible when measured against a criminal career. Federalism supplies a further reason for restraint, because the absence of constitutionally imposed uniformity means that some state will always treat particular offenders more severely than others, and that fact alone cannot make the harshest sentence cruel and unusual. Harmelin's threshold test then operationalizes all of this deference by directing courts to ask first whether the sentence is grossly disproportionate, an inquiry that lets judges consult their own sense of the crime's gravity while discouraging the objective comparisons that gave Solem its bite. The result is a doctrine that is formally alive and functionally inert: acknowledged in every opinion, applied to strike down a sentence only once, and repeatedly cited to deny relief.

The contrast with the Sixth Amendment line sharpens the namable claim of this article. While proportionality challenges asked the Court to declare that a sentence was simply too long, and failed nearly every time, the jury trial cases asked a different and more procedural question: who gets to find the facts that determine the sentence. That question proved far more tractable. The Court held that facts increasing the penalty beyond the statutory maximum must go to a jury, extended the rule to state guidelines and to the federal guidelines, remedied the federal violation by making the guidelines advisory rather than invalidating the statute, and later extended the principle to facts that trigger mandatory minimums. Each of those decisions constrained what judges could do at sentencing without requiring the Court to say that any particular sentence was excessive. The constitutional path to reforming sentences ran through procedure rather than through substance, through the identity of the factfinder rather than through the length of the term.

One caution belongs with this account, because it corrects the most common misreading of the jury trial line. The decision that made the federal guidelines advisory did not reduce sentences across the board, and the word advisory misleads more than it clarifies. Statutory minimums were untouched by the ruling, so mandatory sentences continued to operate exactly as before. District judges were still required to calculate the guideline range correctly as a procedural matter, and appellate courts continued to review sentences for reasonableness against that range. Empirical study of the aftermath found the effect on sentence length to be uneven, with some categories of sentences moving modestly and others barely at all. Advisory describes who has the final word on the sentence, not how constrained the sentencing judge remains. The Sixth Amendment did the work of restructuring American sentencing, but restructuring is not the same as leniency, and no decision in either line held that long sentences for adults are themselves unconstitutional. That is the state of the doctrine: the jury right reshaped the process, the Eighth Amendment left the outcomes largely alone, and litigants who frame their challenges as proportionality claims continue to choose the doctrine with the longest record of failure.

## The Juvenile Line and the Statutory Construction Line

The two most consequential lines of litigation after the jury right decisions moved in opposite constitutional directions, and each illustrates how the Court reshaped sentencing without striking down the statutes that generated the sentences. In one line, the Eighth Amendment finally produced a substantial doctrine, though only for juvenile offenders. In the other, the Court answered narrow questions about the meaning of Congress's reform statutes, leaving the statutory scheme intact while changing who received relief and on what terms. Together they show that litigation redraws the boundaries of a sentencing system from the inside, one holding at a time.

### Where the proportionality line began to move

The juvenile cases sit on the foundation laid by Roper v. Simmons, 543 U.S. 551 (2005), which barred the death penalty for offenders under eighteen at the time of the offense. Roper held that juveniles differ from adults in ways that diminish culpability: immaturity, susceptibility to pressure, and unformed character. That reasoning supplied the bridge to non-capital punishment.

### What did Graham v. Florida hold for juveniles in non-homicide cases?

In Graham v. Florida, 560 U.S. 48 (2010), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment bars life without parole for juvenile offenders in non-homicide cases, and that states must give such offenders a meaningful opportunity to obtain release based on demonstrated maturity and rehabilitation. The majority concluded that the punishment was disproportionate in light of diminished juvenile culpability.

Justice Kennedy's majority opinion extended Roper's framework from capital punishment to a non-capital sentence for the first time. The Court reasoned that the age of the offender and the nature of the crime each bore on the analysis, and that a juvenile who did not kill or intend to kill possessed twice diminished moral culpability when compared with an adult murderer. Chief Justice Roberts concurred in the judgment only, preferring a case by case approach to proportionality rather than a categorical rule. Justice Thomas, joined in part by Justices Scalia and Alito, dissented, arguing that the Eighth Amendment's original meaning and the Court's precedents supported the sentence and that the majority's categorical approach displaced legislative judgment. The disagreement was not only about the result but about method: whether proportionality review should proceed through categorical rules drawn from objective indicators and independent judicial judgment, as in the capital cases, or through the deferential excessiveness inquiry the Court had traditionally applied to terms of years. Graham chose the categorical method, and that choice mattered because it made the holding capable of extension to new categories of cases rather than resolving a single sentence on its own facts.

### Did Miller extend Graham's rule to homicide offenses?

In Miller v. Alabama, 567 U.S. 460 (2012), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids a sentencing scheme that mandates life without parole for juvenile offenders. The decision did not bar discretionary life without parole for juveniles in homicide cases, but it required the sentencer to consider the offender's youth and attendant characteristics before imposing life without parole.

Justice Kagan's majority opinion drew on both Roper and Graham. Where Graham had established that juveniles are less culpable, Miller drew the procedural consequence: mandatory sentencing schemes that preclude the sentencer from considering youth are incompatible with the principle that children are constitutionally different from adults for sentencing purposes. The opinion specified the factors the sentencer should weigh, including the juvenile's chronological age, immaturity, family and home environment, the circumstances of the homicide offense, and the possibility of rehabilitation. The Court wrote that appropriate occasions for sentencing juveniles to life without parole would be uncommon. Chief Justice Roberts dissented, joined by Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Alito, arguing that the majority had moved from proportionality review of a particular punishment to micromanagement of state sentencing procedures. The dissent warned that the opinion's logic would prove difficult to contain, since the same reasoning could reach any mandatory sentencing scheme involving any offender whose characteristics diminished culpability. The majority answered, in effect, that juveniles were a constitutionally distinctive class, and that distinction did the containment work.

### Was Miller retroactive to cases already decided?

In Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190 (2016), the Court held that Miller applied retroactively to cases that had become final before Miller was decided. The majority concluded that Miller had announced a substantive rule of constitutional law, because it barred mandatory life without parole for a class of defendants: juveniles whose crimes did not reflect permanent incorrigibility.

Justice Kennedy wrote for the majority that the essential holding of Miller was the substantive one, the prohibition on imposing the harshest penalty on juveniles who could be rehabilitated, and that any procedural requirement of individualized consideration was only the means of implementing that prohibition. The result was that states had to provide a meaningful opportunity to obtain release to juveniles serving mandatory life without parole sentences, either through resentencing or through parole eligibility. Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito, arguing that Miller was a procedural rule dressed in substantive clothing, and that the majority was rewriting Miller to achieve retroactivity. Montgomery also contained language that later became the center of the next dispute, describing the juveniles eligible for life without parole as those whose crimes reflected permanent incorrigibility. Whether that phrase created a factual predicate that a sentencer must find on the record, or merely described the narrow class of offenders for whom the punishment remained available, was the question the Court took up next.

### Did Jones v. Mississippi require a separate finding of incorrigibility?

In Jones v. Mississippi, 593 U.S. 60 (2021), the Court held that the Constitution does not require a sentencer to make a separate factual finding of permanent incorrigibility before imposing life without parole on a juvenile. Discretionary consideration of youth, the majority explained, was the constitutional safeguard, not an on-the-record finding.

The case arose from the resentencing of Brett Jones, who had committed murder at age fifteen and received a new sentencing hearing after Miller. The Mississippi court again imposed life without parole after considering his youth, and the Supreme Court affirmed. Justice Kavanaugh's majority opinion held that Montgomery had made clear the retroactivity of Miller, but that it had not transformed Miller's procedural requirement into a substantive factual-finding requirement. The opinion stated that sentencing discretion, informed by youth and its attendant characteristics, was what the Constitution demanded, and that it was enough for the sentencer to possess that discretion and to consider the relevant facts. Justice Thomas concurred in the judgment on narrower grounds. Justice Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justices Breyer and Kagan, arguing that the majority had gutted Montgomery's substantive holding by permitting life without parole for juveniles without any determination that the juvenile was among the rare permanently incorrigible offenders the earlier opinions had described. After Jones, the juvenile line stood as follows: categorical bans on life without parole for non-homicide juveniles and on mandatory life without parole for all juveniles, with discretionary life without parole for homicides left available to sentencers who considered youth. That was the only area in which the Court's proportionality doctrine had moved substantially for any class of offenders in two decades, and it had moved because the offenders were children, not because the Court had grown skeptical of long sentences in general.

### How did Dorsey v. United States address the Fair Sentencing Act?

In Dorsey v. United States, 567 U.S. 260 (2012), the Court held that the lower mandatory minimums of [the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010](/2018/02/15/fair-sentencing-act-2010-guide/) applied to offenders who committed their crimes before the Act took effect but were sentenced after its effective date of August 3, 2010.

The Act reduced the crack to powder penalty disparity from one hundred to one to eighteen to one, raising the crack quantities that trigger the five year and ten year mandatory minimums.

The five to four majority, in an opinion by Justice Breyer, reasoned that Congress had intended the more lenient penalties to apply as expeditiously as possible and to all sentencings on or after the effective date. The opinion relied on six considerations, including the Act's remedial purpose, the Sentencing Commission's emergency guideline amendments implementing the Act, and the anomaly that would result if two offenders who committed identical crimes weeks apart received vastly different mandatory minimums based only on their sentencing dates. Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas and Alito, arguing that the federal general saving statute, 1 U.S.C. section 109, preserved the old penalties for pre-Act conduct and that Congress had given no clear indication of an exception. The dispute was a pure question of statutory interpretation, not of constitutional rights: Congress had changed the penalties, and the Court had to decide the temporal reach of the change. Dorsey answered that question for one category of defendants, those not yet sentenced when the law changed, while leaving untouched the sentences of those sentenced before the Act. Congress later addressed that remaining category through the First Step Act of 2018, which made the Fair Sentencing Act's reforms retroactively available, and that provision in turn generated its own litigation.

### What are the limits of sentence reduction proceedings under Dillon?

In Dillon v. United States, 560 U.S. 817 (2010), the Court held that the Booker remedy does not extend to sentence reduction proceedings under 18 U.S.C. section 3582(c)(2), where the Sentencing Commission's policy statement remains binding. A defendant receives only a limited modification of a final sentence, not a full resentencing.

The opinion, written by Justice Sotomayor, described a two step inquiry for section 3582(c)(2) motions. First, the district court determines whether a reduction is authorized under the Commission's policy statement and calculates the extent of the reduction the statement permits, including its instruction that the reduced term may not fall below the minimum of the amended guideline range. Second, the court considers the sentencing factors in 18 U.S.C. section 3553(a) to decide whether the authorized reduction is warranted in whole or in part. The Court rejected the argument that treating the policy statement as mandatory violated the Sixth Amendment, reasoning that section 3582(c)(2) proceedings cannot increase punishment and instead constitute a congressional act of lenity. Justice Stevens dissented, arguing that the majority had allowed the Commission to exercise, in this one corner of sentencing law, the binding authority that Booker had struck down, and that the distinction between a full resentencing and a modification proceeding was too thin to carry constitutional weight. The practical consequence of Dillon was that the relief available through guideline retroactivity would remain cabined by the Commission's rules: defendants could benefit from lowered ranges, but courts could not use the proceeding to conduct a fresh sentencing under advisory guidelines.

### How broadly may courts resentence under the First Step Act?

In Concepcion v. United States, 597 U.S. 481 (2022), the Court held that district courts adjudicating motions under [section 404 of the First Step Act of 2018](/2018/01/15/first-step-act-2018-guide/) may consider intervening changes of law or fact in exercising their discretion to reduce a sentence.

Section 404 made the Fair Sentencing Act's lower crack cocaine penalties retroactively available, permitting courts to impose a reduced sentence as if the 2010 reforms had been in effect at the time of the offense. The five to four majority, in an opinion by Justice Sotomayor joined by Justices Thomas, Breyer, Kagan, and Gorsuch, reasoned that sentencing courts have traditionally considered the defendant as the defendant stands on the day of sentencing, not as the defendant stood at the time of the original offense, and that nothing in the First Step Act limited the information a court could consider. The Court drew on Pepper v. United States, 562 U.S. 476 (2011), which had held that courts at resentencing may consider post-sentencing rehabilitation. Because the Act neither required a reduction nor restricted the scope of the court's inquiry, the Court concluded that district courts must consider nonfrivolous arguments about intervening changes when the parties raise them, while retaining full discretion to grant or deny relief. Justice Kavanaugh dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Alito and Barrett, arguing that section 404 authorized only a limited recalculation keyed to the Fair Sentencing Act and that the majority's approach turned a narrow retroactivity provision into a full resentencing. The holding clarified the relationship between the two strands of statutory construction: where Dillon confined section 3582(c)(2) proceedings to a narrow modification governed by the Commission's policy statement, Concepcion treated First Step Act motions as an exercise of sentencing discretion in which the court could look beyond the retroactive penalty change to the defendant's present circumstances.

### How did Pulsifer interpret the safety valve?

In Pulsifer v. United States, 601 U.S. 124 (2024), the Court held that a defendant is eligible for the federal drug sentencing safety valve under 18 U.S.C. section 3553(f)(1) only if the defendant satisfies each of the provision's three criminal history conditions, not merely one or two of them.

The First Step Act of 2018 had revised the safety valve criteria, which permit a court to impose a sentence below an otherwise applicable mandatory minimum when the defendant meets certain requirements, including a limited criminal history. The amended text provided that the defendant is ineligible if the defendant has more than four criminal history points, a prior three point offense, or a prior two point violent offense.

The dispute concerned the word joining those conditions. The majority, in an opinion by Justice Kagan joined by five other Justices, held that the provision's structure made the criteria conjunctive: the defendant must satisfy each condition, meaning the defendant must not have more than four criminal history points, must not have a prior three point offense, and must not have a prior two point violent offense. A defendant failing any one of the three conditions was ineligible. Justice Gorsuch dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson, arguing that the ordinary meaning of the disjunctive phrase supported the reading that a defendant was ineligible only if the defendant had all three, and that the rule of lenity favored the defendant's construction. The result narrowed the class of defendants eligible to escape mandatory minimums, demonstrating that statutory construction in this field can contract as well as expand relief.

Across both lines, the pattern of the series holds: litigation as the stage where a statutory scheme is reshaped without being invalidated. No court in this line struck down the federal guidelines, the mandatory minimum statutes, or the juvenile sentencing provisions at issue. Graham and Miller left state sentencing systems standing while removing two categories of punishment from the menu for one class of offenders; Montgomery extended that removal backward in time; Jones clarified the boundary of what the removal required; and the four statutory cases construed Congress's own reforms without questioning Congress's authority to set the penalties in the first place. The doctrine advanced, in other words, while the statutes endured.

This pattern explains the two surprises that run through the section. The first is the identity of the constitutional provision that did the work. The Eighth Amendment, the clause that speaks directly to punishment, supplied almost nothing for adult sentences and substantial law for juveniles only, while the Sixth Amendment's jury right redrew federal sentencing practice for everyone. The second is the character of the litigation itself. The most consequential outcomes were not invalidations but reinterpretations and severances: a mandatory guideline system made advisory by removing one subsection, a lenity provision read to permit broad discretion, a safety valve read conjunctively rather than disjunctively. Each decision altered who decides, who is eligible, and on what terms, while the underlying statutory architecture remained in place. That is why the line between litigation and legislation in sentencing law is so thin, and why understanding the cases means following not the statutes' text alone but the sequence of judicial readings that gave the text its operative meaning.

The section's two parts converge on a single caution for litigants and reformers. The juvenile line shows that proportionality doctrine moves when the Court recognizes a constitutionally distinctive class of persons, and it has not moved for adults. The statutory construction line shows that relief arrives when Congress writes it into law, and that the Court will read the grant of relief exactly as written, sometimes broadly, sometimes narrowly. Neither line rewards the strategy of treating every harsh sentence as a constitutional grievance. The constitutional path that has actually shortened sentences runs through who finds the facts and through the precise terms Congress chose for its own reforms, not through a general doctrine of proportionate punishment. The cases, taken together, describe a sentencing system that litigation can reshape from within, but only along the lines the litigation itself has drawn.

## What Sentencing Law Teaches About Who Decides

The most common misreading of this doctrine holds that making the guidelines advisory reduced sentences broadly, and the record does not support it. Booker left Congress's statutory minimums and maximums fully binding, so mandatory sentences continued to operate exactly as before. Calculating the guideline range remained a mandatory procedural step, with appellate courts checking for procedural error before reaching substantive reasonableness. The measured effect on sentence length was uneven: most cases continued to conform to the guidelines, the average decline was modest, and one study found that the effect of judge assignment on sentence length more than doubled. Advisory describes who has the final word, not how constrained the sentencing judge remains. Statutory minimums were untouched, guideline calculations remained mandatory as a procedural matter, and the measured effect on sentence length was uneven, so advisory does not mean unconstrained. That caution matters because it corrects the instinct to read constitutional victories as leniency, when what the jury right delivered was a redistribution of power over who finds the facts.

The namable claim of this article follows from the whole sequence. Modern sentencing doctrine is a jury rights story rather than a punishment severity story. The constitutional path to shorter sentences has run through who finds the facts rather than through how long the sentence may be, and litigants who frame challenges as proportionality claims have chosen the losing doctrine. The Eighth Amendment sustained challenge after challenge to adult sentences and struck down almost nothing. The Sixth Amendment, a guarantee usually understood as procedural, turned out to have substantive force, because moving decisive facts to the jury and the higher burden of proof raised the cost of every enhancement the legislature wrote. That is why the doctrine looks the way it does: the guidelines survived as law while their mandatory application did not, capital aggravating factors became elements, minimum triggering facts followed the same path as maximums, and corporate fines drew the same protection as years of imprisonment.

The series thesis thread runs underneath all of it. Litigation served as the stage where a statutory scheme was reshaped without being invalidated. No court in this line struck down the federal guidelines, the mandatory minimum statutes, or the juvenile sentencing provisions at issue. The Sentencing Reform Act endured after Booker excised two of its provisions. The Fair Sentencing Act's reforms reached backward through Dorsey and the First Step Act without Congress's penalty structure falling. The juvenile statutes remained on the books while Graham and Miller removed two categories of punishment from the menu. Each decision altered who decides, who is eligible, and on what terms, while the underlying statutory architecture remained in place. The constitutional engine was procedure, the vehicle was the jury right, and the statutes, remarkably, survived the journey.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did Apprendi decide about sentencing law?

Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), held that any fact other than a prior conviction that increases the penalty beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Charles Apprendi pleaded guilty to a weapons offense carrying five to ten years, then a judge found racial bias by a preponderance and imposed twelve years under a hate crime enhancement. Justice Stevens, for a 5-4 majority, treated the bias finding as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense, because the jury's verdict alone had authorized only ten years. The decision ended the labeling game: legislatures could no longer assign punishment-raising facts to judges by calling them sentencing factors, though judges retained discretion within the authorized range.

### Q: What did Booker do to sentencing law guidelines?

United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), held that mandatory federal guidelines violated the Sixth Amendment when judge-found facts raised the required sentence, then severed the offending provisions to make the guidelines advisory. Two five-justice majorities, with Justice Ginsburg joining both, decided the merits and the remedy separately. The merits majority applied Blakely to the federal system, while the remedial majority excised 18 U.S.C. section 3553(b)(1) and section 3742(e) rather than invalidating the Sentencing Reform Act. Courts must still calculate the guideline range correctly and consider it under section 3553(a), but the range guides rather than commands. Rita, Gall, and Kimbrough later defined appellate review for reasonableness within that advisory framework.

### Q: Must a jury find facts that raise a sentencing law minimum?

Yes. Alleyne v. United States, 570 U.S. 99 (2013), overruled Harris v. United States, 536 U.S. 545 (2002), and held that any fact increasing a mandatory minimum is an element for the jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Allen Alleyne's jury convicted him of using a firearm during a crime of violence, carrying a five-year minimum, but the judge found brandishing and imposed seven years. Justice Thomas's majority reasoned that a raised floor aggravates the legally prescribed punishment the same way a raised ceiling does, narrowing the band of sentences the court may impose. Prosecutors responded by alleging minimum-triggering facts in indictments and submitting them through special verdict forms, while judges retained broad fact-finding discretion within the range the verdict authorizes.

### Q: Is the Eighth Amendment useful in sentencing law?

For adult sentences, almost never. Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277 (1983), is the only Supreme Court decision to invalidate a noncapital adult sentence on proportionality grounds. Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957 (1991), then made gross disproportionality the threshold test, and Rummel, Hutto, Ewing, and Lockyer sustained life and decades-long terms for minor offenses under deep legislative deference. The doctrine is formally alive but functionally inert, because courts resolve challenges at the threshold and almost never reach comparative analysis. The one substantial movement came in the juvenile line, from Graham through Miller, Montgomery, and Jones v. Mississippi, where diminished juvenile culpability supported categorical limits. For adults challenging sentence length, proportionality remains the losing doctrine.

### Q: Why is the prior conviction exception in sentencing law controversial?

Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224 (1998), held that a prior conviction increasing the maximum may be found by a judge as a penalty provision, and Apprendi carved that exception into its own rule. The rationale was that recidivism is the most traditional sentencing factor and that a prior conviction was already proved with full procedural safeguards. The controversy is that the exception contradicts Apprendi's functional logic: a fact that doubles the maximum exposure should belong to the jury regardless of its history. Justice Thomas, who wrote Almendarez-Torres, later called it wrongly decided. Erlinger v. United States, 602 U.S. 821 (2024), narrowed the exception to the fact of conviction itself while leaving it standing, and no majority has overruled it.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court uphold three strikes in sentencing law?

Yes. Ewing v. California, 538 U.S. 11 (2003), sustained twenty-five years to life for stealing three golf clubs worth about twelve hundred dollars, and Lockyer v. Andrade, 538 U.S. 63 (2003), sustained two consecutive twenty-five-to-life terms for shoplifting about one hundred fifty dollars in videotapes. The plurality in Ewing emphasized the state's interest in incapacitating recidivists and warned courts against acting as a super legislature. In Lockyer, the Court held under deferential habeas review that the state court's reliance on Rummel rather than Solem was not an unreasonable application of the gross disproportionality principle. Four justices dissented in each case, and together the decisions marked the practical end of proportionality challenges to recidivist sentences.

### Q: What is the juvenile life without parole line in sentencing law?

Graham v. Florida, 560 U.S. 48 (2010), barred life without parole for juveniles in non-homicide cases and required a meaningful opportunity for release. Miller v. Alabama, 567 U.S. 460 (2012), barred mandatory life without parole for juveniles, requiring the sentencer to consider youth and its attendant characteristics while leaving discretionary life without parole available for homicides. Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190 (2016), made Miller retroactive as a substantive rule. Jones v. Mississippi, 593 U.S. 60 (2021), held that no separate factual finding of permanent incorrigibility is required, since discretionary consideration of youth suffices. The line rests on Roper's premise that juveniles are categorically less culpable, and it is the only area where proportionality doctrine moved substantially.

### Q: Which amendment does most of the work in sentencing law?

The Sixth Amendment. Its jury trial right reshaped American sentencing through Apprendi's maximum rule, Ring's extension to capital aggravating factors, Blakely's verdict-based definition of the maximum, Booker's advisory remedy, Alleyne's extension to mandatory minimums, and Southern Union's application to criminal fines. The Eighth Amendment's cruel and unusual punishments clause, the provision most associated with punishment, sustained challenge after challenge and struck down only one adult prison sentence, in Solem. The jury right proved tractable because it asks who applies the legislature's judgment rather than whether a sentence is too severe. The result is that modern sentencing doctrine is a jury rights story rather than a punishment severity story.

### Q: What did Winship require the prosecution to prove?

In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), held that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged, in juvenile delinquency proceedings as well as adult trials. Samuel Winship, twelve years old, had been adjudicated delinquent under a New York statute requiring only a preponderance of the evidence. Justice Brennan constitutionalized the reasonable doubt standard, emphasizing its role in reducing factual error and sustaining public confidence, while Justice Harlan's concurrence supplied the moral rationale that convicting an innocent person is far worse than acquitting a guilty one. The holding left one question open: who decides which facts constitute the crime, a silence legislatures exploited for three decades by labeling punishment facts as sentencing factors.

### Q: How did Mullaney and Patterson divide the burden of proof?

Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), struck down a Maine scheme that presumed malice and forced the defendant to prove heat of passion by a preponderance, because absence of provocation determined the degree of culpability and the prosecution had to disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt. Two years later, Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), upheld a New York statute that let the defendant prove extreme emotional disturbance by a preponderance as an affirmative defense, because the state had already proved intentional killing and the mitigating fact was a separate issue. Read together, the pair handed legislatures the power to move burdens by labeling: define a fact as an element and the prosecution carries it, repackage it as an affirmative defense and the defendant may.

### Q: What separates an element from a sentencing factor?

Elements are facts necessary for conviction that a jury must find beyond a reasonable doubt, while sentencing factors are facts that guide the selection of a sentence within the range the conviction authorizes, which a judge may find by a preponderance. McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986), formalized the distinction by upholding a Pennsylvania law that imposed a five-year mandatory minimum when a judge found visible possession of a firearm, since the finding raised only the floor and not the ceiling. After Apprendi, the Constitution rather than the legislature draws the line: any fact that increases the maximum penalty, or the mandatory minimum, functions as an element regardless of its label. Judges still find facts freely when exercising discretion inside the range the jury's verdict authorizes.

### Q: What did Ring change about capital sentencing?

Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 853 (2002), overruled Walton v. Arizona and held that aggravating factors necessary for a death sentence are the functional equivalent of elements that a jury must find beyond a reasonable doubt. Timothy Ring's jury verdict authorized a maximum of life imprisonment, yet an Arizona judge imposed death after finding two aggravating factors alone. Justice Ginsburg's 7-2 majority concluded that capital defendants are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in maximum punishment, declining to treat death as a special domain. The decision extended Apprendi into the capital context and ended judge-imposed death sentences in states where the jury's verdict alone authorized only life.

### Q: What did Blakely change about the statutory maximum?

Blakely v. Washington, 542 U.S. 296 (2004), redefined the relevant maximum as the highest sentence a judge may impose solely on the facts reflected in the jury verdict or admitted by the defendant. Ralph Blakely pleaded guilty to second-degree kidnapping with a standard range of 49 to 53 months, but the judge found deliberate cruelty and imposed 90 months. Justice Scalia's 5-4 majority held that the 53-month ceiling, not the ten-year legislated maximum, was the Apprendi maximum, because only the plea authorized that range. The redefinition mattered because it closed the door through which mandatory guidelines systems had walked: every judge-found enhancement that raised the verdict-based ceiling became a jury question, which sent the federal system into the emergency that Booker resolved.

### Q: How do Rita, Gall, and Kimbrough review federal sentences?

The 2007 trilogy gave Booker's advisory system operational content. Rita v. United States, 551 U.S. 338 (2007), allowed appellate courts to presume a within-range sentence reasonable, without permitting district judges to presume the range reasonable in the first instance. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38 (2007), established two-part review: procedural error first, then substantive reasonableness under a deferential abuse-of-discretion standard, rejecting special burdens for large variances. Kimbrough v. United States, 552 U.S. 85 (2007), held that judges may vary from the guidelines based on policy disagreement, as when a court rejected the 100-to-1 crack-to-powder ratio and imposed the statutory minimum instead. Together the decisions replaced mandatory compliance with structured discretion anchored by correct guideline calculation.

### Q: Does the Apprendi rule reach criminal fines?

Yes. Southern Union Co. v. United States, 567 U.S. 343 (2012), held that the Apprendi jury-finding requirement applies to criminal fines with the same force it applies to imprisonment. The company was convicted of storing liquid mercury without a permit, and the judge set the maximum fine based on a finding of 762 days of violation, yielding potential liability above 38 million dollars, though the jury never determined the duration. Justice Sotomayor's 6-3 majority explained that there is no principled distinction between punishment denominated in years and punishment denominated in dollars. The decision extended the Sixth Amendment revolution into corporate criminal practice, where fines are the primary sanction, and reinforced the reasoning Alleyne adopted a year later for mandatory minimums.

### Q: Why do adult proportionality challenges almost always fail?

The Court treats prison length as quintessentially legislative and applies Harmelin's gross disproportionality threshold with deep deference. Most challenges end at that threshold comparison, so the comparative inquiries into other sentences and other states from Solem almost never occur. Legislatures may pursue any mix of retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation without ranking them, and recidivism occupies a privileged place because the state punishes the offender's demonstrated refusal to conform, not just the triggering offense. Federalism supplies further restraint, since the absence of required uniformity means some state will always punish more severely. The result is a doctrine formally alive and functionally inert, acknowledged in every opinion and applied to strike down an adult sentence only once.

### Q: What did Dorsey decide about the Fair Sentencing Act?

Dorsey v. United States, 567 U.S. 260 (2012), held that the lower mandatory minimums of the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 applied to offenders who committed their crimes before the Act but were sentenced after its August 3, 2010 effective date. The Act had reduced the crack-to-powder disparity from 100-to-1 to 18-to-1. Justice Breyer's 5-4 majority reasoned that Congress intended the lenient penalties to apply as expeditiously as possible, citing the Act's remedial purpose, the Sentencing Commission's emergency amendments, and the anomaly of identical offenders receiving vastly different sentences based only on sentencing dates. Justice Scalia dissented, invoking the federal saving statute. Dorsey left untouched those sentenced before the Act; the First Step Act of 2018 later made the reforms retroactively available.

### Q: What limits apply when a lowered guideline range opens a sentence reduction proceeding?

Dillon v. United States, 560 U.S. 817 (2010), held that the Booker remedy does not extend to sentence reduction proceedings under 18 U.S.C. section 3582(c)(2), where the Sentencing Commission's policy statement remains binding. Justice Sotomayor's opinion described a two-step inquiry: the court first determines whether a reduction is authorized under the policy statement, including its instruction that the reduced term may not fall below the amended range's minimum, then considers the section 3553(a) factors to decide whether the authorized reduction is warranted. The Court rejected the Sixth Amendment challenge because the proceedings cannot increase punishment and constitute a congressional act of lenity. Defendants benefit from lowered ranges, but courts cannot use the proceeding to conduct a fresh advisory sentencing.

### Q: How broad is resentencing discretion under the First Step Act?

Concepcion v. United States, 597 U.S. 481 (2022), held that district courts adjudicating motions under section 404 of the First Step Act of 2018 may consider intervening changes of law or fact. Section 404 made the Fair Sentencing Act's lower crack penalties retroactively available as if they had been in effect at the time of the offense. Justice Sotomayor's 5-4 majority reasoned that sentencing courts traditionally consider the defendant as the defendant stands on the day of sentencing, drawing on Pepper v. United States, and that nothing in the Act restricted the information a court could consider. Courts must consider nonfrivolous arguments about intervening changes when raised, while retaining full discretion to grant or deny relief. The dissent argued the provision authorized only a narrow recalculation.

### Q: What did Pulsifer decide about the safety valve?

Pulsifer v. United States, 601 U.S. 124 (2024), held that a defendant qualifies for the federal drug sentencing safety valve under 18 U.S.C. section 3553(f)(1) only by satisfying each of the provision's three criminal history conditions. The First Step Act of 2018 had revised the criteria permitting sentences below otherwise applicable mandatory minimums, and the dispute concerned the word joining the conditions: more than four criminal history points, a prior three-point offense, or a prior two-point violent offense. Justice Kagan's majority read the criteria as conjunctive, so failing any one condition made the defendant ineligible. Justice Gorsuch dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson, arguing the ordinary disjunctive meaning and the rule of lenity favored the defendant.
