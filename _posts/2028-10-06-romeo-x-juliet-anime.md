A television anime that moves Verona into the sky, exterminates one of the two households before the story opens, and hands Juliet a sword is not an adaptation that failed to read the play. *Romeo x Juliet*, the 24-episode series produced by the studio Gonzo with SKY Perfect Well Think and broadcast in Japan in 2007, read the play closely enough to know exactly which load-bearing walls it was removing. The verdict argued through this article is that Gonzo's series is the rare loose adaptation that is faithful at the level of architecture while faithless at the level of plot. Shakespeare's tragedy ends with two private deaths converted into a public settlement: a prince's judgment, two reconciled fathers, golden statues promised over the bodies. Gonzo discards nearly every mechanism that produces that ending in the play, the missed letter, the sleeping potion, the double suicide in the tomb, and then rebuilds the ending's function from fantasy materials. The lovers still die, and their deaths still remake the city. What the anime changes is the currency of the sacrifice, and that change, not the floating island or the dragon steeds, is its real argument about the play.

The series also makes a second argument, quieter than the first, about ownership. Shakespeare's play gives the feud no cause: the Prologue calls it an "ancient grudge" (Prologue.3) and the text never supplies a reason. Gonzo supplies the most legible reason a feud can have, a coup d'état, and in doing so turns a tragedy of unexplained hatred into a drama of tyranny and restoration. Whether that trade strengthens or betrays the source is the disagreement this article adjudicates, using the play in Jill Levenson's Oxford edition as the standard, the scholarship of Veronica Popescu as the case for the defence, and the reviewing record, led by Theron Martin of Anime News Network, as the case for the prosecution. The conclusion reached is that the anime passes the only test that matters for a loose adaptation. It keeps the doomed-love core intact, it earns its ending rather than borrowing the play's, and its inventions cluster where the play itself is silent.

## What Is Romeo x Juliet? The Anime Series at a Glance

The documented production record is compact. *Romeo x Juliet* was produced by Gonzo and SKY Perfect Well Think, directed by Fumitoshi Oizaki, and written under chief screenwriter Reiko Yoshida. It aired on Chubu-Nippon Broadcasting from April 4 to September 26, 2007, running 24 episodes, with later broadcasts on TBS, KBS Kyoto and SUN-TV in Japan and on Animax channels abroad. Hitoshi Sakimoto composed the score, performed by the Eminence Symphony Orchestra. The opening theme was Lena Park's cover of "You Raise Me Up"; the closing themes were "Cyclone" by 12012 for the first fourteen episodes and "Good Bye, Yesterday" by Mizrock for episodes 15 to 23, with an English version of the opening song reserved for the finale. Fumie Mizusawa voiced Juliet and Takahiro Mizushima voiced Romeo in Japanese. Funimation licensed the series in North America and released it in two DVD sets in 2009, the Romeo Collection on June 23 and the Juliet Collection on August 11, with Chris Burnett as Romeo and Brina Palencia as Juliet. A manga version, written by Yoshida and drawn by the artist credited as COM, ran in Kadokawa Shoten's *Monthly Asuka* from March 24, 2007, and the reference record states plainly that the manga differs extensively from the anime it accompanies.

The shape of the story can be stated in one paragraph, because its boldness is structural rather than intricate. Fourteen years before the main action, Leontes Montague leads a coup in the floating city of Neo Verona and murders the ruling Capulets to the last child, or so the regime believes. One daughter escapes. Juliet grows up hidden among Capulet retainers in and around a theatre, raised as a boy named Odin, and at sixteen learns her name on her birthday. By night she is already famous under a third name, the Red Whirlwind, a masked vigilante who robs the regime's agents and shields its victims. At the Montagues' Rose Ball she meets Romeo, the tyrant's son, and the play's engine starts: "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5). Around that engine Gonzo builds a serialized plot of rebellion, exile, a pastoral village, a dying world-tree, and a sacrifice foretold from the heroine's birth.

### How many episodes does Romeo x Juliet run, and who made the series?

The series runs 24 episodes, broadcast in Japan between April and September 2007. It was made by the studio Gonzo with SKY Perfect Well Think, directed by Fumitoshi Oizaki and written under Reiko Yoshida, with music by Hitoshi Sakimoto. Funimation released the English version in two DVD collections during 2009.

The quickest way to see what Gonzo did is to set the two works side by side, element by element. That comparison is the article's findable artifact, named here as the InsightCrunch anime map, and the sections that follow argue from its rows one at a time.

Two features of the map deserve emphasis before the detailed argument begins. The first is that almost every row inverts rather than decorates. The play's Juliet is the feud's most sheltered prisoner; the anime's Juliet is its most wanted criminal. The play's Romeo is banished for a killing; the anime's Romeo is disgraced for mercy and spends his exile building rather than brooding. The second is that the map's boldest row concerns a name. Shakespeare's Prince Escalus is the feud's external judge, the voice that opens the play's civic frame with a threat: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). Gonzo deletes the prince and gives his name to the world-tree on which the city's survival depends. Judgment from outside the feud is replaced by a debt written into the world's foundations, and the whole difference between the play's theology and the anime's follows from that single substitution.

The Rose Ball itself shows the method in miniature. Shakespeare's feast is held at the enemy's house, and Romeo enters it masked as a pilgrim guest, so the meeting happens inside the feud's stronghold under the protection of festivity. Gonzo keeps the structure and reverses the ownership: the ball is the Montagues' Rose Ball, and the intruder is the Capulet, smuggled in as a guest while her other self is the regime's most wanted criminal. Tybalt's recognition scene, the play's first detonation, is defused and deferred, since the anime's Tybalt hunts a masked vigilante rather than a gatecrashing Montague, and the lovers' first exchange is allowed to complete itself. The change looks like softening and is actually engineering: a serial cannot detonate its feud in episode one and still have twenty-three episodes of escalation, so the ball's danger is banked rather than spent, and the interest compounds across the season. The first meeting also establishes the series' visual signature for the lovers, recognition across a crowded floor at a distance, two people singled out of a choreographed crowd. Anime, a medium of held frames and emphatic close-ups, is unusually well equipped for love at first sight, and the production leans on that equipment without embarrassment.

The season's architecture follows the play's five movements at television scale. The first half builds the world and the love: coup's aftermath, disguise, ball, revelation, betrothal refused and courtship under false descriptions. The second half, corresponding to Funimation's Juliet Collection, darkens in sequence: identities public, the marriage and the village idyll, the disgrace and the mines, the rising and the sacrifice. Martin's complaint about a tonal change at the ending is partly a complaint about this hinge, since the second movement trades the first's swashbuckling for prophecy. The hinge is nonetheless the play's own, the turn at Mercutio's death where comedy curdles; Gonzo moves the turn to the season's midpoint and assigns the curdling not to a death but to a disclosure, the opening of the Escalus lore. Serialized tragedy needs its pivot where a serial can use it, and the adaptation's instinct for placing it is sound.

## Gonzo's House Method: Rewriting the Classics for Television

Romeo x Juliet was not a studio's first raid on a classic. Gonzo had built a mid-2000s identity on exactly this operation: Samurai 7 relocated Kurosawa's Seven Samurai into a science-fiction future, and Gankutsuou retold Dumas's The Count of Monte Cristo through a visual style of patterned textures that critics treated as a landmark of television animation design. The trade press recognized the pattern at once. ICv2, announcing Funimation's North American acquisition in 2008, introduced the series to retailers as the work of the studio that had updated Kurosawa and described screenwriter Reiko Yoshida and director Fumitoshi Oizaki setting Shakespeare's tragedy in an aerial city. The house method has three constants across those productions, and all three are visible in this one: a canonical source treated as a chassis rather than a script, a setting rebuilt in a genre idiom native to anime, and a thematic core extracted from the source and defended even where the plot is discarded. For Dumas the core was revenge's corrosion; for Shakespeare it is the conversion of private love into public consequence.

Oizaki's direction was his first major assignment in the chair, a fact the reviewing record kept in view when praising the series' energy. Chris Beveridge attributed the narrative's focus to the energy and enthusiasm of a young production team, and the judgement fits the series' construction. The early episodes move with a swashbuckler's confidence, alternating court intrigue, street action and romance in even rotation, and the design commits to a coherent visual thesis: a Renaissance city rendered with anime's cleaner geometry, aerial cavalry in place of horses, and a palace grown around a tree. The coherence frays in the second half, where Martin documented a drop-off in animation quality, and the fraying is part of the honest record of weekly television production. A 24-episode serial is a manufacturing process as much as an artwork, and the series' achievement is that its design intelligence survives its schedule.

Hitoshi Sakimoto's score deserves separate notice, because it performs the adaptation's argument in music. Sakimoto came to the series from celebrated video game scores, and he wrote for the Eminence Symphony Orchestra in a full late-Romantic idiom: broad string themes for the lovers, brass and percussion for the regime, and a sacred hush around the tree. Martin, the series' most exacting friendly critic, praised the score even while counting the plot's rescues, and the praise is diagnostic. The music treats Neo Verona's fantasy with complete seriousness, never winking at the dragon steeds or the floating rock, and that seriousness licenses the audience to do the same. The opening theme extends the argument. Lena Park's cover of "You Raise Me Up", a devotional ballad about being lifted by another's strength, plays over a city that is itself lifted, held up by a tree and, in the end, by a girl. When the English version of the song returns as the finale's closing theme, the lyric's promise has been reassigned: Juliet raises the city by the act of falling with it. Theme songs in anime are usually marketing; this one is a synopsis.

The design of flight deserves its own sentence in the method's ledger. Neo Verona's nobles travel on winged dragon steeds, and the series shoots its courtship in the air: pursuits, escapes and rendezvous staged above the rooftops, with the city's height converted from backdrop into choreography. The balcony scene's vertical grammar, a girl above and a boy below negotiating the distance between her window and his danger, is generalized into a whole physics of the world, in which every meeting must cross open sky and every parting is a fall arrested by wings. Visual motifs in an adaptation are arguments, and this one argues that the lovers' element was never the street or the tomb but the air between two heights, the same element the city's survival depends on. When the continent finally descends, the lovers' element surrenders its privilege, and the whole world comes down to the level where everyone else already lived.

The voice casting completes the design. Fumie Mizusawa's Juliet carries the series' largest assignment, a heroine who must be plausible as a boy stagehand, a masked swordsman, a bashful guest at a ball and a head of state in waiting, and the performance's steadiness across those registers is a condition of the whole experiment: if Juliet's four selves do not cohere in one voice, the character splits into the gimmicks her detractors describe. Takahiro Mizushima's Romeo is cast against the heroic grain, lighter and more tentative than a prince of a military regime is expected to sound, and the tentativeness is the character, a ruler's son whose authority arrives only when he is disobeying. Kazuhiko Inoue voices William with a performer's geniality, making the playwright the production's warmest presence, a choice that flatters the series' thesis that storytelling is the city's surviving civic art. Casting, in this production, is interpretation conducted by other means.

The craft question that shadows the whole production is whether fantasy spectacle and tragic structure can occupy the same series without one dissolving the other. The play itself offers a precedent its adapters rarely cite: Shakespeare's Verona scenes are full of spectacle for their stage, swordplay choreographed for the yard, a masked ball, a double tomb tableau. Gonzo's contribution is to scale the spectacle to its medium while keeping the tragedy's proportions, five movements across twenty-four episodes, meeting, marriage, exile, return and catastrophe in the play's order. The sections below take the major systems one at a time, but the craft verdict can be entered early: the series looks like a fantasy adventure and behaves like a tragedy, and the mismatch between its look and its behaviour is the source of both its detractors' suspicion and its admirers' loyalty.

One more distinction belongs in the method's record, because it separates Gonzo's Shakespeare from the looser borrowings that surround it in popular culture. The studio's reinventions keep their sources' endings. Gankutsuou's Count still ruins himself with his revenge; Samurai 7's farmers still pay the full price of their victory; and Romeo x Juliet, for all its sky, still requires both lovers dead and a city reconstituted over the cost. The surface can be transformed without limit, spaceships, skycontinents, gender-flipped heroes, precisely because the contract with the audience fixes the destination. That contract is the opposite of franchise thinking, in which the surface is protected and the ending is endlessly deferred, and it explains the series' otherwise puzzling confidence: a production that knows its lovers must die can afford to let them live fully first, in disguise, in flight, in marriage, in a village, because none of those mercies will be asked to commute the sentence. Serialized invention is usually the enemy of tragic necessity. Here it is necessity's instrument, giving the sentence twenty-four episodes of evidence to gather before it is carried out.

## Neo Verona: A City in the Sky

The floating continent is the invention most often treated as the series' signature gimmick, and it deserves a more serious reading. Neo Verona hangs in the sky, farmed, taxed and policed like any city in a dynastic drama, its height a fact of geography rather than a marvel its citizens discuss. Transport runs on winged dragon steeds, and Romeo rides one named Cielo. The setting's work in the story is not wonder but pressure. A city that floats can fall. Every fantasy element in the series is wired to that single vulnerability, and the wiring is what separates Gonzo's reinvention from decoration.

### What holds Neo Verona in the sky?

The great tree Escalus holds it there. The tree is the city's root and foundation, tended inside the Montague palace by its priestess Ophelia, and it has been withering under Montague rule. As the tree weakens, earthquakes shake the continent and pieces of its edge begin to break away, so the setting itself carries the plot's clock.

Shakespeare's Verona is also a city under a suspended sentence, though the sentence is legal rather than physical. The Prince's edict hangs over the houses from the first scene, and the play's public spaces, street, square, hall, tomb, are all places where private passion keeps colliding with civic order. Levenson's edition and the criticism gathered around it have long treated Verona as the play's third antagonist, a community whose failures of justice make the lovers' secrecy necessary. Gonzo literalizes that reading. The anime's city is visibly dying under its government, cracking at the edges as Escalus fails, and the lovers' story unfolds inside a polity whose collapse is measurable in falling farmland. The floating island is therefore not an escape from the play's civic dimension but an amplification of it. Where the play asks its audience to infer that Verona is broken, the anime shows the breakage from the air.

The setting also disciplines the romance. In the play, the lovers' meetings are stolen minutes between public dangers, a balcony at night, a cell at dawn. In Neo Verona the meetings carry the same structure at a larger scale: a ball inside the tyrant's palace, encounters at the Capulet graves, flight to a village beyond the regime's reach. The geography keeps restating the lovers' problem, which is that no ground in their world is neutral. Even the pastoral village Romeo builds in exile exists on borrowed soil that the dying tree can no longer feed, a detail the episode record keeps returning to as the village's crops fail with Escalus. Fantasy, in this series, is the feud's bookkeeping rendered visible.

The city's verticality also writes its politics in space. The palace and the tree occupy the heights and the centre; the common districts spread below and outward, taxed by agents who descend from the regime's core; beyond the settled edge lie the mines to which the regime sends its inconvenient, and beyond them the failing farmland where Romeo plants his village. Shakespeare's Verona has a similar geography of power, palace, street and tomb arranged by who may enter them, but the play draws it in dialogue and stage traffic. The anime draws it in altitude. To ride a dragon steed in Neo Verona is to belong to the class that can cross the city at will, and it is not accidental that the resistance's heroine fights on foot and on rooftops while the regime's heir arrives from the air. When the continent finally descends and lands on the sea, the geography is abolished along with the tyranny: the city that lived by height ends level with the world. The setting thus completes the feud's arc in a way dialogue alone could not, making the last image of the series a map of its politics resolved.

Beneath the palace continent the series builds a vertical underworld that does the play's Mantua one better. Romeo's place of punishment is the regime's mine works, where the heir is sent in disgrace after his defiance peaks, and the mines invert the city's geometry: the favourite son of the sky is buried in the ground his father's capital floats above. His work there, founding a settlement with the miners and coaxing a hardy crop from seed an old man gives him, is the series' portrait of the good ruler in exile, and its fate is the prophecy's proof of reach: when the tree sickens further, the earthquakes reach the village too, and the settlement Romeo built with his hands suffers and is abandoned as the sky begins its long failure. No level of the world escapes the tree's ledger. The palace, the markets, the rim farms and the mines are one body with one circulation, which is the anime's final word on the play's civic metaphor: Shakespeare's Verona is a city whose rulers treat the houses' quarrel as a private fever; Neo Verona is a body that cannot privatize anything, because its ground is a single living root.

## The Feud Inverted: A Coup Instead of a Brawl

The play's feud is famously causeless. The Prologue offers "two households, both alike in dignity" and an "ancient grudge" breaking "to new mutiny" (Prologue.1-3), and four centuries of editors have noted that the text never explains what the grudge is about. That silence is a deliberate part of the play's design, as a reading of [the unexplained hatred of the Capulets and Montagues](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) makes clear: the audience watches two houses destroy their children over a quarrel neither can articulate, and the waste is the point. Gonzo's boldest structural decision is to refuse that silence. The anime's feud has a date, a perpetrator and a body count. Fourteen years before the action, Leontes Montague murdered the ruling Capulets and took their city, and the feud continues as counter-insurgency, with the regime hunting Capulet survivors and the survivors arming for restoration.

### What exactly did Gonzo change first: the feud or the lovers?

The feud. The lovers enter a story whose conflict has already been rewritten as political history: a coup, a massacre, a tyranny and a resistance. Every change to Romeo and Juliet themselves, her sword, his conscience, their exile and their deaths, follows from that prior decision to give the ancient grudge a named cause and a living villain.

The gain from the change is legibility across twenty-four episodes. A causeless brawl can sustain five acts; it cannot easily sustain eleven hours of serialized television, which needs factions, plans and reversals. By converting the feud into dynastic conflict, Yoshida's scripts give the resistance a chain of command, the regime a police apparatus in the Carabinieri, and the lovers a political problem that worsens on schedule. The loss is equally real, and the prosecution in the critical record presses it hard. A feud with a villain is a feud with a remedy: kill or convert Leontes Montague and the hatred ends. Shakespeare's feud has no remedy, which is why it can only end in exhaustion and shame. The anime knows this, and its handling of Montague is its most interesting piece of writing. Leontes is given a grievance of his own in the documented record of the series: an illegitimate Capulet offspring, born to a woman the great house used and discarded, raised in poverty, his hatred of the Capulet name learned at his mother's ruin. The tyrant is the feud's product before he is its author. Gonzo thus smuggles the play's causelessness back in through the villain's biography: the coup was itself an act of the older, unexplained hatred, one generation's grudge armed with a sword.

The ending of the feud confirms the design. Montague is not overthrown by the lovers or defeated in a climactic duel with Juliet, though the resistance plot builds toward exactly that confrontation. In the documented episode record he is killed late in the series by Mercutio, his own scheming courtier, at the moment he is near surrender. The restoration the resistance fought for arrives as a palace murder committed for ambition, not justice, and it solves nothing: the tree keeps dying, the earthquakes worsen, and the city's fate passes out of politics altogether into the sacrifice plot. A lesser series would have let the tyrant's fall stand as the ending. This one treats regime change as a subplot and reserves its climax for the older debt underneath the regime, which is a genuinely Shakespearean instinct. In the play, too, the feud's principals are almost beside the point by Act 5; the catastrophe completes itself through accident and timing while the fathers are still posturing.

The resistance's internal argument sharpens the same point from the other side. The loyalists who raised Juliet did not preserve her as a symbol; they preserved her as an instrument, and the instrument's assigned task is the tyrant's death. The episode record shows the series staging that assignment as a genuine temptation rather than a slogan: Juliet, rehearsing William's play about her own story and playing the scene in which the heroine kills the tyrant, weighs the act in earnest, reasoning that although she does not hate Montague, the future of Neo Verona and the honour of her house may require his death. The sequence matters because it locates the feud inside the heroine. Shakespeare's Juliet never has to decide whether to continue the feud; the feud simply surrounds her. Gonzo's Juliet is offered the feud as a vocation, by the people she loves most, and her story becomes the long refusal and final transmutation of that offer. She does in the end go to the palace with a sword, but the death she finally deals is not the one the loyalists commissioned, and the death she accepts is her own. The feud plot is thereby neither endorsed nor merely escaped; it is answered, by a heroine who converts its currency of vengeance into a currency of sacrifice.

The manhunt gives the middle episodes their procedural spine. The regime posts the Whirlwind as its public enemy, and the hunt is conducted in warrants, patrols and informers rather than in duels, so the play's street violence is bureaucratized: the feud's energy, which Shakespeare discharges in brawls, is stored in Neo Verona as procedure. Juliet fights the procedure as much as the men, freeing prisoners from transports and striking at tax convoys, and each strike is answered by a tightening of the apparatus, curfews, searches, exemplary punishments in the markets. The spiral is the series' most political invention, because it shows a tyranny manufacturing the rebel it hunts and a rebel manufacturing the tyranny's excuses, a feedback loop that neither the play's brawling households nor its passive Prince ever models. When the resistance finally moves from strikes to rising, the move is legible as the loop's exhaustion rather than a plot convenience, and the palace confrontation gathers Romeo, Mercutio, Tybalt and the tyrant into a single room where the loop's debts come due at once.

What the inversion finally does is redistribute the play's irony. In the play, the lovers are the only people in Verona not fighting, and the fighting world kills them. In the anime, Juliet is the resistance's figurehead and Romeo is the regime's heir, so the lovers stand at the feud's command heights rather than beneath them, and their love is not an escape from the conflict but a scandal inside both camps. The Capulet loyalists want their heir to kill Montague's line, not marry into it; the court wants Romeo's marriage to Hermione to seal the regime's respectability. Love, in Neo Verona, is treason twice over. That is a coherent and even elegant translation of the play's structure, in which the love is likewise the one act neither house can process.

## Juliet as the Red Whirlwind: The Anime's Boldest Rewrite

Juliet carries the adaptation. The play's heroine is defined by confinement: kept at home, promised to Paris, her movements watched by parents and Nurse, her rebellion conducted entirely in language and in one desperate recourse to the Friar's potion. She is thirteen, "not fourteen" by a fortnight (1.3), and her heroism is interior. Gonzo's Juliet is sixteen, and her heroism is exteriorized into the grammar of action television. She fences, rides, robs tax collectors and duels the Carabinieri in the streets, masked and cloaked in red, while the city cheers a hero it cannot name. The Red Whirlwind, rendered "Scarlet Whirlwind" in some early English-language coverage and "Red Whirlwind" in Funimation's release, is a Robin Hood figure built from the play's raw material: the Capulet daughter's defiance, given a body that can act on it.

### Why does Juliet fight under two false names?

Because her true name is a death sentence. Raised as the boy Odin by the retainers who smuggled her out of the massacre, she is hidden by a fiction of gender; as the Red Whirlwind she is hidden again by a mask, and the two disguises let her survive and fight in a city where Juliet Capulet must not exist.

The disguises are the character's real subject. The series is patient about what hiding costs her. As Odin she works as a stagehand in William's theatre, dressed as a boy, ignorant of her own birth until the retainers judge her old enough at sixteen to be told. The revelation scene is the anime's equivalent of the play's pressures gathering: where Shakespeare's Juliet is crushed between her father's command and her secret marriage, Gonzo's Juliet is crushed between two duties that are both hers by birth, the duty to live hidden and the duty to stand revealed as the Capulet heir. Romeo enters this structure as its solvent. He meets her first as a girl at the Rose Ball, in the borrowed finery of a guest, and loves the person both disguises were built to conceal. The anime thereby keeps one of the play's subtlest effects. Shakespeare's Romeo loves Juliet in a world where her name is the obstacle; the anime's Romeo loves her in a world where her name is a capital charge, and where his own name is the regime's.

Veronica Popescu's scholarly reading of the series, published in 2017 under the title "Japanese Romeo x Juliet as Site of Cultural Cross-Pollination", treats this Juliet as the point where Japanese and Western storytelling conventions genuinely fuse: the magical and martial heroine of anime tradition carrying a heroine whose Western source defines her by speech and suffering. The reading is celebratory, and on the evidence of the character's construction it is largely earned. The swordplay never replaces the play's defining trait, which is decision under pressure. This Juliet's arc turns on choices, to reveal herself, to lead, to refuse assassination, finally to accept the sacrifice, each choice dramatized as deliberation rather than reflex. Where the adaptation weakens her is in the middle episodes' reliance on rescue plotting, a weakness Theron Martin flagged in his Anime News Network reviews when he counted the series' many saved-at-the-last-moment scenes. The criticism lands on the plotting, not the conception, and the distinction matters for the verdict below.

The birthday revelation deserves its own analysis, because it is the anime's equivalent of the play's great pressure scenes and it is built from opposite materials. Shakespeare's Juliet learns what she is by inches: a mother's errand, a Nurse's memory, a suitor's name, until the knowledge of her position has assembled itself around her by Act 3. Gonzo compresses the assembly into a single ceremony. On her sixteenth birthday the retainers tell Odin that he is a fiction, that her name is Juliet Fiammata Asto Capulet, and that the city's suffering has a claimant. The scene hands the heroine an identity as a political fact before she has had one day to live inside it, and the series is honest about the violence of the gift: the heir's first acts are not triumph but grief, visiting her parents' graves in the ruined Capulet ground where Romeo, not knowing her, finds her again. The graves become the lovers' trysting place, and the choice is characteristic. Where the play's lovers meet above a ballroom and outside a bedroom window, the anime's meet among tombs, so that the love story is conducted from the start on the feud's burial ground, with the ending's address already written into the scenery.

## Romeo, the Wistful Heir: Inverting the Lovers

Anime News Network's review of the first DVD set identified the series' structural signature exactly: the adaptation turns the story on its head by juxtaposing the roles of the lovers, making Juliet the primary action figure while Romeo becomes the wistful one. Romeo is the regime's prince and its internal dissident, a young man who rides the streets incognito, frees prisoners his father's men have taken, and dreams of a city where the names Montague and Capulet carry no charge. His father answers that dream with the two instruments dynasties use, a marriage and a punishment. The marriage is to Hermione, a nobleman's daughter, arranged to steady the succession. The parallel with the play is precise and inverted: where Capulet presses Paris on Juliet, Leontes presses Hermione on Romeo, and the unwanted match migrates from the heroine to the hero along with the action role. The punishment, when Romeo's defiance becomes public, is exile in all but name: he is sent in disgrace to the imperial mines, the regime's penal edge.

The exile arc is where the anime's Romeo becomes more than a gender-swapped placeholder. Shakespeare's Romeo spends his banishment in Mantua as pure passivity, waiting for news, and the play is honest that his exile has no content beyond grief. Gonzo gives the exile a project. At the mining settlement Romeo founds a village, works its barren soil, receives from an old wanderer a gift of seeds bred to survive hostile ground, and watches the settlement flourish before the dying of Escalus starves its fields. The arc converts the play's most criticized stretch, the hero's offstage sulk, into the series' thesis about him: this Romeo is a builder, the anti-Montague, a ruler in embryo whose qualification is that he plants things. It also sets the tragedy's trap with unusual fairness. The village proves Romeo could have governed Neo Verona justly; the tree's failure proves that no government, just or unjust, can save a city whose foundations demand a death. His story is the refutation of the idea that better rulers would have been enough, which is as bleak a political moral as the play's own.

The love story between the heirs is handled with a restraint the reviewing record repeatedly singled out. The first meeting at the Rose Ball keeps the play's choreography of instant recognition, and the series lets the relationship develop through meetings at the Capulet graves, through flight, through a secret marriage and a brief pastoral season in Romeo's village, an idyll the plot grants them at unusual length for a doomed romance. That length is structural, not indulgent. The play compresses the love into days so that its destruction feels like theft; the anime stretches it across a season so that its destruction feels like taxation, the slow reassertion of every claim the lovers had escaped. Both are legitimate tragic tempos, and the anime's choice suits its medium, since a weekly serial cannot ask an audience to mourn a four-day affair with the weight the finale requires.

The steed itself is worth a moment's regard. Cielo, Romeo's dragon, is a creature of the air in a city of the air, and the lovers' meetings in flight give the romance an element the play reserves for its language alone, the sense of a love conducted above the city's rules and weather. When Romeo finally rides Cielo to the tree through a collapsing sky, the element turns hostile, and the creature that carried the courtship carries the rescue attempt into the same failure that claims everything else the sky ever held.

The courtship that precedes the exile is worth pausing on, because the series spends its scarcest resource, episodes, on it. Romeo seeks Juliet out after the ball without knowing her name is a charge; he rides the city on Cielo, his dragon steed, in the incognito of a minor nobleman, and the meetings multiply under false descriptions on both sides, she not yet told who she is, he not yet told who she is either. The doubling of ignorance is the anime's replacement for the play's balcony scene, where the lovers know everything except the practicalities. Here they know nothing except each other, and the long middle of the series, in which identities surface one by one and the love survives each surfacing, argues the adaptation's central claim about the source: that the love in the play is not a function of the feud's opposition but a substance that keeps proving itself against successive definitions of impossibility. The secret marriage and the flight to the village then play as the love's brief freehold, a few episodes in which the series lets its lovers be ordinary, keeping house at the edge of a dying world, before the claims arrive to collect them.

What Romeo finally does is the play's Romeo turned inside out one last time. Shakespeare's hero dies by his own hand in the tomb, acting on false information with catastrophic speed. Gonzo's hero dies defending, not despairing: he rides to the tree to stop the sacrifice, fights Ophelia, the tree's priestess, and is mortally wounded by her as he kills her, living long enough to reach Juliet and die in her arms before she completes the rite. The manner matters. The play's Romeo is killed by the feud's machinery of error; the anime's Romeo is killed by the world's machinery of debt, in open combat, with full knowledge. The anime strips out the play's irony of misinformation, no letter miscarries in Neo Verona, and compensates with an irony of futility: everything Romeo does to prevent the ending is competent, brave and useless, because the ending was written into the city's foundations before he was born.

## The Great Tree Escalus: A Prince Turned into a God

Every adaptation of the play must decide what fate is. In the text, fate is a vocabulary: "star-cross'd" lovers (Prologue.6), "the stars" Romeo defies, fortune's fool, the Friar's "greater power than we can contradict". Shakespeare never embodies it. Gonzo embodies it as a tree. Escalus, the great tree rooted in the palace rock, holds the floating continent in the sky, and its health is the city's health. Two such trees once sustained the world, in the lore the series parcels out; one died long ago, and the survivor has been failing under a loveless rule. The tree's keeper is Ophelia, a priestess who tends it and who reads in Juliet the fulfilment of an old economy: the Capulet house, by the tradition Popescu's analysis describes, sustains Escalus through sacrifice, and Juliet is marked from birth as the offering through whom the tree, and the city, can live.

### What holds Neo Verona in the sky, and what does it demand?

Escalus, the great tree, holds the continent aloft, and its price is the life of a Capulet daughter given to it in sacrifice. The tree withers when the ruling heart is without love, and its failure brings earthquakes and collapse, so the city's survival is mortgaged to the heroine's death from the start.

Ophelia herself repays attention, because the series writes her as tragedy's civil servant. She is not a villain in Montague's mould; she does not hate Juliet, and her tendance of the tree has the patience of a gardener and the certainty of a priest. The lore she administers is the series' only theology: two trees once held the world, one died, and the survivor's life is renewed through the Capulet daughters given to it across the generations, a tradition Popescu's analysis of the material describes as the house's founding bargain. That genealogy is the anime's darkest invention, darker than the coup. It means the Capulet restoration the loyalists dream of is mortgaged before it begins, since the house's survival and the house's extinction are the same contract read from opposite ends. It also means Montague's tyranny, however cruel, is not the deepest thing wrong with Neo Verona. The tyrant merely accelerated a dying that the world's design had already scheduled, and the resistance's political victory, when it comes, changes nothing about the schedule. The series' politics and its metaphysics are thus set on a collision course from the middle episodes onward, and every earthquake that cracks the continent in the final movement is the sound of the metaphysics winning.

The name carries a second charge from elsewhere in the canon that the series may or may not have intended, and an adapter's debts are counted the same either way. Shakespeare used the name Escalus once more, for the aged counsellor in Measure for Measure, a humane judge who watches a ruler's deputized severity from the margins and supplies the play's closest approach to mercy. The two Escaluses share a position and little else: both stand beside power without wielding its violence, both embody an older dispensation against a regime of force. Gonzo's tree completes the pattern by removing the man and keeping the position, a silent elder at the regime's heart whose condition is the regime's true report card. Whether the echo was designed or inherited, it deepens the transfer. The Prince in Romeo and Juliet could only judge the city; his namesake in the anime is the city's load-bearing member, judge and structure in one.

The critical argument about the series is really an argument about this tree. Popescu's cross-pollination reading treats the Escalus machinery as the point where the heroine's political and spiritual destinies converge, making her death a necessary and meaningful sacrifice rather than an accident of plotting. On that account the tree is the adaptation's masterstroke: it gives fate a mechanism, gives Juliet's death public meaning, and fuses the Shinto-inflected world-tree motif with the play's civic ending. The counter-case, heard in the reviewing record, is that the tree is a machine for forgiving the writers. Martin, who admired the series enough to name it among the best anime of 2009, was disappointed by the change of tone at the ending; the sacrifice plot arrives from mythology rather than from the characters' errors, and it lets the feud plot off the hook it had spent twenty episodes baiting. Daryl Loomis, reviewing for DVD Verdict, took the opposite side of the same object, judging that the "wild, logic-defying climax" added to the series and made it feel less contrived, a verdict that sounds perverse until its logic is seen: a climax governed by prophecy at least concludes the story the series had been telling about destiny, rather than the political story it had merely been using.

Adjudicated against Levenson's text, the tree survives, with one demerit entered. What the play's ending actually requires, structurally, is that the lovers' deaths be converted into public value: the Prince's closing audit, the fathers' statues, the story told to the city at dawn. Suicide in a tomb accomplishes that conversion in the play by shame. The anime needs an equivalent converter, and Escalus is one: Juliet's death is exchanged, on the story's own ledger, for the city's life, and Neo Verona descends to the sea and survives because she pays. The conversion function is preserved with a fidelity most straight adaptations never attempt. The demerit concerns Romeo. The tree has no claim on him, and the series must wound him by a priestess's hand to keep the double death the source demands; his death is the one place where the machinery shows its seams, felt even by sympathetic reviewers as contrivance. A fair verdict gives the tree the city and charges it with the hero.

## The People Between the Houses: Neo Verona's Populace as the Third Party

Shakespeare's play keeps a third party on stage that adaptations habitually cut: the citizens of Verona. The first scene's brawl draws in bystanders who take sides by reflex, and the Prince's edict is addressed to a city, not to two families. The tragedy is civic in its bookkeeping. Every death is entered in a public ledger, and the closing scene is a public audit, held in a churchyard, with the city's ruler presiding and the city as witness. Gonzo's series restores that third party to a prominence no screen version attempts, because a serial has the hours to build a populace: market traders squeezed by the regime's taxes, farmers on the continent's rim watching their soil die with the tree, prisoners in the mines, playgoers in William's theatre, and the crowds who cheer the Red Whirlwind without knowing her name. The common people of Neo Verona are not background. They are the constituency in whose name the resistance fights, the audience for whose benefit the lovers' story is finally told, and the survivors whose continued existence is the ending's whole justification.

The regime's instrument among them is the Carabinieri, the military police whose patrols, arrests and tax seizures give the tyranny a daily texture. Their presence converts the play's occasional watchmen into a standing apparatus, and the conversion matters for the feud's meaning. In the play, violence between the houses is a breach of the peace that the state struggles to police; in the anime, violence is the state's own instrument, and the houses' quarrel has been nationalized. The Red Whirlwind's robberies and rescues are therefore political acts in a way no escapade of Shakespeare's Juliet could be, and the populace's love for the masked figure is the series' running referendum on the regime. When the resistance finally rises, the rising has been prepared by dozens of small scenes of that referendum being taken in markets and streets, and the coup's reversal feels earned at the level of the crowd rather than merely plotted at the level of the court.

This populace also solves the adaptation's hardest tonal problem, which is how a story about two aristocratic households can justify eleven hours of attention in a medium whose heroes are usually commoners by sympathy. The series answers by making the aristocrats answerable. Juliet's legitimacy is constantly tested against the people's welfare, and her authority in the resistance rests less on her blood than on her record of robberies committed on the poor's behalf. Romeo's counter-claim is built in the village, where his fitness to rule is demonstrated in crops and wells rather than proclaimed. The play's lovers die as private persons whose loss instructs their city; the anime's lovers rule, in embryo, before they die, and their deaths deprive their city of a demonstrated future. The populace is the scale on which that deprivation is weighed, and without it the sacrifice at the tree would be a transaction between a girl and a god. With it, the transaction has witnesses, beneficiaries and mourners, which is to say it has the shape of Shakespeare's ending.

## The Court in Exile and the Empty Chairs: Who the Anime Keeps, Kills and Deletes

An adaptation is read as clearly in its deletions as in its inventions. The play's Juliet is motherless in effect, her real confidante the Nurse, and her father the tragedy's domestic tyrant. The anime deletes the Capulet parents by massacre and replaces the household with a resistance cell: Conrad, the grave retainer who raised her; Cordelia, her friend and attendant; Curio and Francisco, swordsmen of the old house; Antonio, a boy page. The family romance of the play becomes a court in exile, and Juliet's scenes of tenderness run through loyalty rather than kinship. It is a defensible solution to a real adaptation problem, since a hidden heir cannot be given the play's bustling Capulet household, and it quietly preserves the play's emotional geometry, in which Juliet is always surrounded by people who love her and cannot save her.

The Nurse and the Friar, the play's two great enablers, are the most instructive absences. No single figure in Neo Verona does the Nurse's work of intimate mediation, carrying messages, arranging meetings, betraying at the crisis; her functions are distributed among Cordelia, who carries the confidante's tenderness, and Conrad, who carries the counsellor's gravity, and the distribution costs the story the Nurse's particular poison, the beloved voice that advises surrender. The Friar's absence cuts deeper, because the Friar is the play's plot mechanic, the author of the potion and the letter. The anime deletes the mechanic and replaces his machinery with prophecy: where the play's catastrophe is engineered by a well-meaning schemer whose plan fails by hours, the anime's is authored by a cosmic contract whose terms are known in advance. Something is lost in the exchange, the play's sense that disaster is made by people trying to help, and something is gained, the sense that the lovers' enemy was never any person's plan at all. The secret marriage itself survives the Friar's deletion, solemnized during the lovers' flight, but it carries no tactical function in Neo Verona; nobody's scheme depends on it, and its secrecy is a shelter rather than a weapon.

Paris and Rosaline, the play's two instruments of pressure at the margins, vanish as persons and persist as functions. Paris's role, the approved match whose existence turns love into disobedience, migrates across the gender line to Hermione, as the anime map records. Rosaline has no counterpart at all: this Romeo has loved no one before Juliet, and the deletion quietly raises the love's evidentiary weight. Shakespeare's Romeo enters the play fluent in a borrowed Petrarchan idiom, sighing for a woman the audience never sees, so that his love for Juliet can be measured against a rehearsal. Gonzo's Romeo enters innocent of the idiom, and his love is a first language. Critics of the play have argued for centuries over whether Romeo's speed discredits him; the anime settles the question by removing the exhibit, which is either a simplification or a verdict, and this article counts it a verdict, the adaptation ruling for the lovers on a point where the text stays ambiguous.

Mercutio and Tybalt, the play's paired instruments of the catastrophe, receive the boldest redistribution of all. Shakespeare's Mercutio is the play's wit and its first corpse, the friend whose death converts Romeo from lover to killer. The anime's Mercutio is a courtier of the Montague household whose brilliance curdles into ambition: he schemes toward the succession, and his arc ends in the throne room with his knife in the tyrant he served. The wit is reassigned, much of it to William and the theatre company, and what remains in Mercutio is the danger without the charm, a reading that finds in the name only the mercury, quicksilver ambition that cannot be held. Tybalt travels the opposite road. The play's Tybalt is pure feud, "the Prince of Cats", dead by Act 3 for the plot's convenience. The anime's Tybalt begins as the regime's hound hunting the Red Whirlwind and is revealed, in the back-story the series assembles, as Montague's illegitimate son by a Capulet woman named Volumnia, a child of both houses in his blood. He ends the series fighting for the Capulet restoration and, in the finale's documented record, urging Juliet to save herself while she refuses. Between them, the two rewrites state the series' whole theory of the feud: enmity is not a property of houses but a career open to individuals, and kinship, even the feud's own hidden kinship, is the solvent it cannot survive.

## Shakespeare Inside the Anime: William and the Borrowed Names

The anime's most playful layer is its treatment of Shakespeare himself. Living in Neo Verona, keeping the theatre where the Capulet retainers hide, is a playwright named William, called Willy: a cheerful, impecunious dramatist who houses Juliet, stages plays with political teeth, and in the later episodes composes a play reenacting Juliet's own story, a drama within the drama that rehearses the revolution's argument in public. The device is more than a wink. The play's Chorus stands outside the action and tells the audience what the story means; Gonzo moves the storyteller inside the story, gives him rent to pay and actors to feed, and lets his theatre function as the resistance's safe house and printing press. The theatre in the anime is what the theatre in Shakespeare's London partly was: a licensed space where a city rehearses saying dangerous things.

### Why does the series give Shakespeare himself a job in Neo Verona?

Because the adaptation needs its Chorus somewhere. William the playwright houses the hidden heir, shelters the resistance and finally stages Juliet's story back to the city, so the play's framing voice becomes a working character whose theatre is both refuge and weapon for the Capulet cause.

The play within the play is where the device pays its way. In the later episodes William composes and rehearses a drama reenacting Juliet's story, with Juliet herself cast in the scene of the tyrant's killing, so that the resistance's argument is tried in fiction before it is attempted in fact. The episode record shows Juliet using the rehearsal to think with: playing at regicide instructs her in what the real act would cost and whom it would serve, and the thought is completed on stage rather than in soliloquy. Shakespeare's own theatre loved this instrument, the Mousetrap in Hamlet, the mechanicals' tragedy in A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Gonzo's use of it is the adaptation's most literate gesture, a borrowed device doing the same work in the borrowing as in the source. It also completes William's function. He is not a mascot but the story's memory and conscience, the one citizen of Neo Verona whose profession is to insist that events mean something, and the series' final disposition of its story into his keeping is prepared by these rehearsals.

A last comparison fixes William's scale by contrast. The play's own Chorus speaks twice and vanishes, a voice with no body, no interests and no arc, permitted to summarize because it cannot intervene. Gonzo's storyteller can intervene and does, with lodging, money, employment and a stage, yet the series is careful to deny him the one power the Chorus exercises freely: he cannot summarize his way out of the action. William must live through the events he would prefer to dramatize, and his play within the play is an attempt to recover the Chorus's safe distance that the plot refuses him. The device thus measures the distance between the two media. Drama can afford a disembodied commentator; serialized television, committed to a populated world, must give its commentary a body and then make the body pay rent. That the rent in this case is paid in shelter given to the heroine, at risk to the payer, is the series' quiet tribute to its source's most disposable figure.

Around William the series scatters a population borrowed from across the canon. The tree's priestess is Ophelia, Hamlet's destroyed innocent rebuilt as tragedy's officiant, the one who administers the death the plot requires. Juliet's closest friend is Cordelia, Lear's truthful daughter, cast here as the loyal attendant. Romeo's unwanted bride is Hermione, the wronged and patient queen of The Winter's Tale. The Capulet swordsmen include Curio, a name from Twelfth Night's Orsino's court, and Francisco, Hamlet's sentinel. The retainer who raised Juliet is Conrad; the boy who trails the Red Whirlwind is Antonio, the canon's most reusable name; Tybalt's mother, in the series' back-story, is a Capulet woman named Volumnia, borrowed from Coriolanus, where the name belongs to the most formidable mother in Shakespeare. Chris Beveridge, reviewing for Mania, noted that the addition of characters from Shakespeare's other plays "may either please or annoy the hardcore fans", and the annoyance risk is real: name-spotting can become a substitute for writing. The defence is that the borrowings are functional rather than decorative in the cases that matter most. Ophelia's transfer is the boldest and the best judged. Shakespeare's Ophelia is a young woman destroyed by the demands male authority places on her, drowned and mad, her death reported rather than staged. The anime's Ophelia is the demand itself, the serene agent of a system that consumes young women on schedule, and her serene cruelty gives the sacrifice plot its necessary antagonist after Montague's death leaves the throne empty. That is cross-pollination of the kind Popescu describes, a motif taken up and made to do new work, rather than a costume rented for recognition.

The naming system also quietly solves the adaptation's world-building problem. A fantasy Verona needs a court, a resistance and a populace, and populating them from the canon lets the series signal each newcomer's register at sight: a Hermione will be patient, a Volumnia formidable, a Cordelia honest. The device is economical in exactly the way television writing needs to be, and it binds the anime's Verona to the whole Shakespearean map rather than to one play, which is part of the series' thesis about its source. The play, in Gonzo's handling, is not a shrine but a quarry, and the series treats the rest of the canon as the same quarry's other seams.

## The English Dub: Shakespeare Put Back In

The series' strangest chapter was written after the Japanese broadcast ended. Funimation's English version, released in 2009, was directed by J. Michael Tatum and R. Bruce Elliott, with script adaptation by a team led by Taliesin Jaffe, and Tatum himself voicing William. The adaptation team undertook something no dub is obliged to attempt: they wrote Shakespeare back into a script that had largely left him behind. Anime News Network's review of the second DVD set records the method precisely. In the first half, the English script experimented with rendering occasional whole episodes in iambic pentameter; the experiment was relaxed in the second half in favour of a consistent Shakespearean flavour in the dialogue, though the next-episode previews kept the pentameter throughout. Lines borrowed from Shakespeare's works were worked into the dub beyond those the Japanese script already carried, especially in William's revolutionary play.

### Why did the English dub write whole episodes in iambic pentameter?

To restore, in the language of delivery, what the adaptation had removed in the language of plot. The Jaffe team's verse experiments and borrowed lines let English-speaking audiences hear Shakespeare inside the anime, a compensation the reviewers documented and praised, even where the fit to the animation was awkward.

The performances carrying that script were cast with an adaptor's care. Brina Palencia's Juliet and Chris Burnett's Romeo lead the English cast, and Martin's reviews of both DVD sets praised the principal work, singling out R. Bruce Elliott's Conrad and Tatum's own William among the supporting performances, and noting the meticulous effort in the casting of the smaller roles. The detail that matters for the record is the alignment of jobs: the man voicing Shakespeare inside the fiction, Tatum, was also one of the two directors shaping the fiction's English voice, and the script team under Taliesin Jaffe was writing verse for a playwright character played by their own director. The dub is therefore not a translation laid over the series but a second adaptation folded inside it, executed by people who treated the assignment as the series itself had treated Shakespeare, as material to be honoured by transformation. When Jaffe's team relaxed the full-pentameter experiments in the second half, Martin recorded the change without lamenting it, judging the general Shakespearean flavour more consistent and less awkward against the animation than the strict verse had been. The experiment's partial retreat is itself data: even sympathetic adaptors found that the line, restored wholesale, fights the form, which is the whole history of Shakespeare adaptation in miniature.

Holly Ellingwood, reviewing for ActiveAnime, praised the English adaptation for incorporating an older style of language and many more Shakespearean quotations than the Japanese track contains, and the observation points to a genuine oddity in the work's transmission. The Japanese series is a Shakespeare adaptation that rarely quotes Shakespeare; its dialogue is modern, its Shakespearean presence structural and onomastic. The English dub, a translation of a loose adaptation, is measurably more Shakespearean at the level of the line than its original. Purists who object that the anime is Shakespeare in name only are, in the English-speaking market, objecting to a version measurably denser in the playwright's words than the one Japan watched. The episode is a small, perfect parable of global Shakespeare: the text circulates, thins out, and is re-thickened at an unexpected border by a team of adaptors who treated a dub script as an act of scholarship. It also vindicates the series' gamble in a way the producers cannot have planned. A work secure in its source can survive being re-Shakespeared by strangers; a work merely renting the names would have collapsed under the added weight.

## The Scenes Rebuilt: A Balcony of Graves, a Kiss for a Potion, a Tree for a Tomb

The surest way to test a loose adaptation is to walk the source's famous stations and ask what stands at each address. At the address of the balcony scene stands a graveyard. The anime's lovers conduct their courtship at the ruined Capulet graves, where Romeo finds Juliet mourning her murdered house, and the substitution argues itself: Shakespeare's balcony is a threshold between the Capulet interior and the street, a place where Juliet is half in her father's house and half out of it, and the graves are the same threshold drawn by the coup, the ground where Juliet is half in the world of the living city and half in the world of her dead. The orchard scene's danger, discovery by the watch, becomes the graveyard's danger, discovery by the patrol, and the vows exchanged there carry the play's charge with the volume raised.

At the address of the potion stands a kiss. The play's Juliet drinks a deathlike sleep on the Friar's pharmacology, and the device generates the tragedy's final irony, a rescue mistaken for a corpse. The anime's Juliet receives from Ophelia the kiss of death, the sign of the contract with Escalus, after which, in the priestess's words, she will never wake up again as herself. The correspondence is exact in function and opposite in direction. The potion is a false death deployed to cheat a forced marriage and reach a true one; the kiss is a true death accepted to fulfil a cosmic marriage between the Capulet line and the tree. Both heroines consent to a suspended self in a ritual administered by a religious professional, and both consents are the bravest acts their stories allow them. The difference is that the play's device fails and the anime's succeeds, which is why the play ends in irony and the anime in liturgy. An adaptation is entitled to that exchange; what it owes the audience is the recognition, which the series pays by giving its Juliet the potion scene's solitude and terror inside the rite's serenity.

At the address of the duel stands a refusal. The play's catastrophe is ignited by two killings in a street: Mercutio by Tybalt, Tybalt by Romeo, the second converting the lover into the feud's instrument and earning the banishment. The anime strikes both killings from the record. Its Romeo kills no one until the finale, and its Tybalt is never Romeo's victim but the feud's own hidden child, turned ally. The street ignition is replaced by a slow political combustion, arrests, seizures, a rising, a palace murder committed by a third party for gain. The cost is the play's terrible economy, in which twenty minutes of temper undo a world; the gain is a catastrophe that cannot be blamed on temper at all, and must be blamed on design. Critics who miss the duel's machinery in the anime are missing something real, and the series' defenders should concede the point rather than paper it: Gonzo traded the tragedy's most efficient device for its most expansive one, and viewers who prize efficiency will always find the exchange wanting. This article's verdict prices the trade as worth making once, for the sake of what the expansive device reveals, while doubting that the play could survive the same trade twice.

## The Ending: Sacrifice Instead of Suicide

The finale, episode 24, is where every one of the series' decisions comes due, and it must be described exactly, because its defenders and detractors are describing the same events and judging different things. Montague is dead, killed by Mercutio. The resistance has taken the city. Escalus is failing, the continent is shedding land, and Ophelia has marked Juliet with the rite's preliminary sign, the kiss of death, after which Juliet will not wake as herself again. Juliet, who has known her destiny since the lore was opened to her, chooses to complete it: she will give herself to the tree, and the giving, by the tradition that has consumed Capulet daughters before her, will save Neo Verona. Romeo rides to prevent it. He fights Ophelia at the tree, kills her, and takes a mortal wound from her in the exchange. He reaches Juliet, speaks with her, and dies in her arms. Juliet then completes the sacrifice freely, with her vow to Romeo kept in the only form left to it; wings of light unfold, the continent descends slowly and lands upon the ocean, and the city survives as an island. A final shot shows the lovers standing hand in hand before the tree in a place outside the story's geography.

Set against the play, the symmetries and the swerves are equally clear. The lovers die in the same order of devotion and in each other's presence, which no straight reading of the play can give them, since Shakespeare's pair die separately, each in error about the other. The anime grants them the play's most withheld luxury, a death with full knowledge, and then charges the audience for it by making the death useful. This is the point at which to place the series in [the Liebestod tradition of love and death entwined](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/), with a correction. The Liebestod pattern makes the lovers' death a private consummation, the world well lost for love. Juliet's sacrifice is the reverse: a public transaction in which love is the motive but the city is the beneficiary. The closing image, the pair united before the tree, supplies the consummation privately, as a coda the plot has not earned and the audience has. Whether that coda is mercy or cheating divides viewers precisely as the critics divided.

The tomb is the other absence the finale must manage. Shakespeare's catastrophe is staged in a burial vault, among the stacked dead of both houses, and its horror depends on enclosure: two living people and a crowd of corpses in a locked stone room, the truth arriving minutes too late. Gonzo's catastrophe is staged in the open, at the root of a tree, before a city in flight from a falling sky, and its pathos depends on exposure. Nothing is enclosed, nothing is mistaken, and nobody arrives too late; Romeo arrives in time and it does not help. The swap is the adaptation's bleakest single decision, because it removes the play's last mercy, the suggestion that better luck or faster horses could have saved everyone. In Neo Verona the information is perfect and the outcome is identical, which is why the ending divides its audience along a clean line: viewers formed by the play's irony experience the tree as a cheat that abolishes the tragedy's mechanism, while viewers formed by the series' own prophetic grammar experience the tomb's accidents as the cheat, a catastrophe outsourced to a sleeping potion's timing. Both experiences are honestly derived from the two texts, and the dispute between them is the most instructive argument the adaptation generates.

The final shot has drawn the argument out of proportion to its ten seconds, and the disproportion is instructive. A closing image of the lovers alive and hand in hand before the tree can be read as vision, afterlife, or the story's own wish, and the series declines to grade the readings. Defenders treat the shot as Escalus granting Juliet's last wish, a reunion inside the tree's renewed life; detractors treat it as a hedge, a flinch from the double death the series had the courage to stage. The play offers its own precedent for an image that outruns the facts: the golden statues the fathers promise are never shown, existing only as a vow spoken over the bodies, yet no reader calls the ending hedged. Shakespeare closes with an image of the lovers preserved by the city's intention; Gonzo closes with an image of the lovers preserved by the world's. The difference is one of metaphysics, not of nerve, and on the documented record of audience division the shot has done what closing images are for, which is to keep the ending arguable after the argument should be over.

The ending's deepest fidelity is rhythmic rather than doctrinal, and it closes the adjudication. The play's last act is a race that the lovers lose by minutes, its speed the tragedy's final cruelty: the Friar's letter delayed, the watch arriving, the Prince's audit conducted over fresh bodies. Gonzo's last movement inverts the tempo without relaxing the pressure. Everything in the finale is slow: a procession to a tree, a girl mounting to her rite, a continent taking a full episode to fall. Where Shakespeare accelerates past rescue, Gonzo decelerates into ceremony, and the deceleration is the adaptation's final argument about its source, that the play's haste was never the point. The point was irreversibility, and ceremony is irreversibility performed at walking pace, with witnesses. Viewers who feel the finale as a different genre arriving uninvited are registering the tempo change accurately; the adjudication offered here counts the change as interpretation rather than betrayal, because the sensation it produces, a dread that has stopped pretending surprise, is the sensation the play's Prologue promises from its first fourteen lines.

The adjudication offered here rules for the ending, on the play's own evidence. Shakespeare's finale is not the suicides but what the suicides purchase: the Prince's sentence on the houses, the fathers' competition in grief, the promise that the story will be told. The play ends in administration, in the civic digestion of private death. The anime, having deleted the Prince and enthroned his name in a tree, ends in the same administration conducted by other means: the death is audited by a whole city that survives because of it, and the lovers' story passes at once into the keeping of William, the resident teller. Martin's disappointment at the tonal change is documented and understandable, since the sacrifice plot's mythology is colder than the resistance plot's humanism; Loomis's praise of the wild climax is, on the structural evidence, the better aimed of the two verdicts. The ending is the series keeping its oldest promise. The story opened with a city held up by a tree and a girl marked by birth; it ends when the mark is honoured. A finale that had saved Juliet by swordplay would have been the true betrayal, of the source and of the series alike.

## Critics and Scholars on the Anime: The Disagreement Adjudicated

The case for the prosecution, stated at its strongest, runs as follows. Beveridge found the series enjoyable but "not particularly revolutionary or challenging", and the description fits its surfaces: the animation by Gonzo's standards is handsome rather than daring, the political plot resolves into a palace coup, and Martin documented a visible drop in animation quality across the second half alongside those repeated last-moment rescues. On this view the anime is a well-made romance wearing Shakespeare's names, its depth a function of its source's reputation. The case for the defence begins with Popescu's thesis of cultural cross-pollination, in which the series is a genuine two-way exchange: Japanese forms, the martial heroine, the world-tree, the serialized dynastic plot, metabolize the Western text, and the Western text gives the Japanese forms a tragic spine they would not otherwise have. Bamboo Dong, reviewing for Anime News Network's Shelf Life column, praised the reinvention itself as the achievement, calling the series wonderful aesthetically and narratively. Ellingwood's evidence about the dub shows the work generating further Shakespearean text downstream, which costume parties do not do.

The wider DVD-era reception ran warmer than the dispute suggests. Todd Douglass Jr., reviewing the complete series for DVD Talk, called it an experience that shows its material in a whole different light, great fun to watch, while entering the standard caution that the degree of divergence from the source might not sit well with every viewer. DVD Talk's verdict line rated the content highly on the strength of its character work and its blend of action, drama and romance. The pattern across the notices is consistent and worth stating plainly, because it predicts the series' classroom afterlife: professionals who approached the property as anime judged it a success with flaws, professionals who approached it as Shakespeare split on the ending, and the scholarly reading dissolved the split by treating the crossing itself as the achievement. Three instruments, three measurements, one object.

The prosecution's most credible witness is Martin himself, precisely because he is also the defence's. His two Anime News Network DVD reviews constitute the most careful contemporary account of the series in English: the first praised the score, the preserved tone of the source and the great action scenes, while counting the saved-at-the-last-moment rescues; the second recorded the animation's second-half decline and his disappointment in the ending's changed tone; and his year-end judgement then placed the series among the best anime of 2009. The sequence is not inconsistency but a map of the series' actual profile, strong in conception and performance, uneven in manufacture, divisive at the climax. A critic can hold all three positions because the series gives evidence for all three, and any verdict that irons the sequence flat, whether into praise or dismissal, is less accurate than the sequence. This article's adjudication follows Martin's evidence and reverses his conclusion at exactly one point, the ending, for the structural reasons given above: measured against the play's civic finale rather than against the series' own first-half humanism, the tonal change at the climax is a completion, not a swerve.

A researcher assembling this record, episode guides, reviews, scholarship and the manga's divergent text, in a private archive such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) can watch the disagreement organize itself around a single question: is transformation a form of fidelity or a substitute for it? The adjudication here finds for the defence, with the prosecution's counts entered as costs. The series is not revolutionary as television; its revolution is confined to its source relation, and within that relation it is substantial. It identifies the play's least exportable elements, the causeless feud and the ironic suicides, and replaces them with functional equivalents, the dynastic feud and the civic sacrifice, rather than with decorations. Decorations it also has, in the dragon steeds and the borrowed names, but they are load-bearing decorations: the steeds give the city its vertical politics, and the names staff its world. Measured by Levenson's text, the anime preserves the play's deep grammar, two houses, a forbidden love, a city's guilt discharged through the young, while discarding its surface grammar of letters, potions and tombs. Adaptations are usually graded on the reverse ratio.

## A Tragedy in a Girls' Magazine Frame: The Shoujo Inheritance

One documented fact about the property explains more of its tone than any production interview: the manga version ran in Monthly Asuka, Kadokawa's magazine for girl readers, and the anime was built to be received in that weather. The shoujo tradition in Japanese comics and animation, with its heroines of interior resolve, its cross-dressing warriors, its patience with courtship and its devotional approach to doomed love, is the second classic standing behind Gonzo's series, and the adaptation is best understood as a negotiation between two inheritances, the English tragedy and the Japanese girls' romance. Juliet's design carries the shoujo signature in every particular. She is competent before she is beautiful, loyal before she is desirable, and her heroism is expressed as endurance and self-gift rather than conquest. The Red Whirlwind's sword is folded inside that signature rather than opposed to it, the action heroine of the girls' tradition having always been permitted a blade provided the heart wielding it stays legible.

The frame also governs the romance's pacing, which reviewers raised outside that tradition sometimes misread as slackness. A serial courtship conducted through disguises, graveyard meetings, a marriage and a village idyll is the shoujo movement of approach and consecration, and its slowness is ceremonial rather than indecisive. The English dub's audiences encountered the same pacing through a different frame and split over it, some finding the middle episodes swooning where others found them cumulative. The ending's devotional structure, a heroine absorbed into a world-tree amid wings of light, belongs to the same inheritance: sacrifice in the girls' tradition is frequently figured as transfiguration rather than extinction, and the final hand-in-hand image that divides the series' critics is the shoujo ending drawn faithfully, a reunion granted in the register of vision. Naming the frame does not settle the argument about the ending, but it locates the argument correctly. The dispute is between two traditions of tragic closure, not between courage and cowardice, and an adjudication that ignores the manga's magazine is judging the series by only one of its two classics.

## Global Reach: Verona as World Property

The series belongs to a Gonzo pattern that the trade press noted at its announcement: the studio had already reimagined Dumas in Gankutsuou, its science-fiction Count of Monte Cristo, and Kurosawa's Seven Samurai in a futuristic key, and Romeo x Juliet applied the same house method to Shakespeare. ICv2, reporting Funimation's acquisition in 2008, paired the series with that lineage and noted the element "that will no doubt infuriate Shakespeare lovers", Juliet's reinvention as a Robin Hood figure with a mission against the house that murdered her family. The fury, where it existed, was a minority report. The series travelled: Animax broadcasts across Asia and Europe, an Italian run on Rai 4 in 2009, Hungarian, Romanian, Czech and Slovak broadcasts, the Funimation release in North America and Madman and MVM releases in Australia and Britain. A play written for a London stage had become, by way of a Tokyo studio, a floating city watched on four continents, which is the series' thesis about the play's global reach demonstrated by its own distribution.

The franchise's Japanese afterlife adds a footnote with its own lesson. The manga version, running in Monthly Asuka from March 2007 to 2009 and collected in two volumes, shares the anime's premise and little else; the reference record emphasizes that it differs extensively from the broadcast series, developing its own emphases from the same chassis. Two tellings of the same reinvention, diverging in detail within two years, complete the naturalization: the property had entered the condition of folklore, in which variants multiply without scandal and no version, not even the studio's own, can claim to be the single authorized text. Shakespeare arrived at that condition across centuries of adaptation; his Neo Verona retelling reached it before the broadcast had finished. An internet radio programme, hosted by Fumie Mizusawa, Juliet's Japanese voice, streamed alongside the broadcast, and the original soundtrack album followed in February 2008. The pattern is the standard ecology of a successful anime property, and its ordinariness is the point worth recording: Shakespeare in Neo Verona behaved, commercially and culturally, like any other durable Japanese franchise property, neither protected by its source's prestige nor hindered by it. The play had become, in the fullest sense, local material.

The anime arrived, by the account of its own reviewers, surprisingly late in one respect. Anime News Network's coverage remarked that the striking fact was the wait: that it took until 2007 for a direct anime treatment of the play to appear at all, given Shakespeare's saturation of Japanese popular culture. The surprise has a structural explanation that the series itself embodies. The play resists the medium's usual machinery, since its engine is talk rather than incident, and only a studio willing to replace the engine wholesale, as Gonzo was, could bring the property to television. Once that threshold was crossed, distribution was frictionless: Animax carried the series across Asia and into Europe and Latin America, Italian television broadcast it on Rai 4 in 2009, and Funimation's edition anchored the English-speaking market. The floating Verona needed no visa, because the reinvention had already translated the play out of its local dialect into the international language of fantasy serials.

For the wider map of the play's Japanese afterlife, on stage and screen alike, the companion survey of [Japanese film and stage adaptations of the play](/2029/04/06/japanese-film-stage-adaptations/) supplies the context this article compresses: the anime is one entry in a long national conversation with the text, distinguished by being the entry that rebuilt the text most thoroughly. Set inside the still wider argument over screen versions, the series earns a footnote with teeth in [the debate over which film Romeo and Juliet is best](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/), because it is the limiting case: the version with the least Shakespeare in its dialogue and, on this article's argument, an unusually large share of Shakespeare in its structure. Whether structure without lines still counts as the play is the question the whole adaptation cluster keeps asking, and the anime is its purest test.

## The Verdict on Romeo x Juliet

The verdict is that Romeo x Juliet is a major loose adaptation and a minor one only in budget. It understands the play as a machine for converting young love into public consequence, rebuilds that machine in fantasy materials, and runs it honestly to the end it predicts. Its Juliet is the strongest single reinterpretation of the heroine in the play's screen history, because the series sees that her tragedy was never passivity but obedience, and gives her obedience a sword to lay down. Its failures are television failures, an uneven second half, rescue plotting, a hero whose death the machinery has to arrange rather than grow. Judged as the brief for this article demands, as a loose adaptation, it stands near the head of its class: the cliche Verona, earthbound and Italianate, is replaced by a Verona that is global and mutable, and the myth survives the move because the move was made by people who knew which parts of the myth were the myth.

The series' classroom use follows from its method with unusual directness. A teacher can put the anime map on a screen beside the play's dramatis personae and ask the only question adaptation study ever needs: what did the adaptor think the story was about, such that these are the changes? The map answers visibly. A series that thought the play was about fate builds a world-tree; a series that thought it was about girls' obedience builds a heroine who kneels and then stands; a series that thought it was about cities builds a populace and saves it. Each answer is arguable against the text, which is exactly the condition that makes an adaptation worth teaching. The play, after four centuries of reverent staging, profits from an heir willing to argue back in a floating city, and the argument this article has adjudicated, between the sacrifice's defenders and its detractors, is the sound of the profit being counted.

Two tests, applied in closing, state the standard by which the next adaptation in this cluster should be judged. The first is the conversion test: does the ending turn the lovers' deaths into public value by some mechanism the adaptation has honestly built, or does it merely repeat the suicides as a trademark? The anime passes with its tree, its descent and its surviving city. The second is the silence test: are the inventions concentrated where the source is silent, the feud's cause, the city's foundations, the heroine's inner life, or do they overwrite what the source actually says? The anime passes again, its boldest strokes all falling in the play's documented silences, the grudge's origin, the Prince's inner nature, the world above Verona's sky. An adaptation that passes both tests has earned its liberties; one that fails either has only borrowed the names. On the evidence assembled here, Gonzo's series earned them, and the floating city stands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch anime map, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch anime map is this article's comparison table, setting fifteen elements of Shakespeare's play against their counterparts in Gonzo's series: the city, the feud, both lovers, the arranged match, Escalus, Ophelia, Mercutio, Tybalt, the meeting, the exile, the friar's device, the ending and the Chorus. Its pattern is inversion rather than decoration, since nearly every row reverses the play's arrangement instead of merely redressing it, and its boldest row gives the Prince's name to a tree. The map is the quickest evidence for the article's verdict that the adaptation is faithful in architecture while faithless in plot.

### Q: How many episodes does Romeo x Juliet have, and when did it first air in Japan?

The series has 24 episodes. It was first broadcast in Japan on Chubu-Nippon Broadcasting from April 4 to September 26, 2007, with later broadcasts on TBS, KBS Kyoto and SUN-TV, and Animax broadcasts carrying it across Asia and Europe. The finale, episode 24, aired on the closing date of that run. Funimation released the English version in two DVD sets in 2009, the Romeo Collection in June and the Juliet Collection in August of that year, and the series has remained in international circulation through later licensing.

### Q: Who made Romeo x Juliet, and who directed and wrote the series?

The series was produced by the studio Gonzo together with SKY Perfect Well Think. Fumitoshi Oizaki directed it, in his first major assignment in the director's chair, and Reiko Yoshida served as chief screenwriter. Hitoshi Sakimoto composed the score, which was performed by the Eminence Symphony Orchestra. Gonzo had applied the same radical-reinvention method to The Count of Monte Cristo in Gankutsuou and to Kurosawa's Seven Samurai before turning the method on Shakespeare, which is why the series treats its source as a chassis rather than a script.

### Q: What is Neo Verona, and why does the city float in the anime?

Neo Verona is a continent-sized city hanging in the sky, ruled from a palace built into its central rock and crossed by riders on winged dragon steeds. It floats because the great tree Escalus, rooted in that rock, holds it aloft. The tree has been withering under Montague rule, so the city's height is also its vulnerability: as Escalus fails, earthquakes shake the continent, farmland dies and pieces of the rim begin to fall away, giving the whole setting a clock that the political plot alone could not supply.

### Q: Who is the Red Whirlwind in Romeo x Juliet?

The Red Whirlwind is Juliet's masked vigilante identity. Cloaked in red, she robs the regime's tax collectors, rescues its prisoners and duels its military police, the Carabinieri, in the streets of Neo Verona, becoming a folk hero to the common people while the regime hunts her. The name was rendered "Scarlet Whirlwind" in some early English-language coverage; Funimation's release uses "Red Whirlwind". The identity is the adaptation's boldest stroke, converting the play's confined heroine into an action lead without surrendering her defining trait, which is decision under pressure.

### Q: Why was Juliet raised as a boy named Odin?

Because her true name was a death sentence. Juliet was a small child when Leontes Montague massacred the Capulets, and the retainers who smuggled her out of the palace hid her by raising her as a boy, Odin, a stagehand in William's theatre. Only on her sixteenth birthday do they judge the time right to tell her that she is Juliet Fiammata Asto Capulet, the heir of the murdered house, and the revelation hands her an identity as a political fact before she has lived inside it for a single day.

### Q: How does the anime's feud differ from the feud in Shakespeare's play?

The play never explains its feud: the Prologue calls it an ancient grudge breaking to new mutiny, and no cause is given in four hundred years of the text's life. The anime gives the feud a date and a criminal. Fourteen years before the action, Leontes Montague led a coup, murdered the ruling Capulets and seized Neo Verona, and the feud continues as tyranny against armed resistance. The change buys a serialized plot its factions and reversals, at the cost of the play's bleakest effect, a hatred with no remedy because it has no reason.

### Q: Who is Leontes Montague in the anime, and what made him hate the Capulets?

Leontes Montague is the tyrant of Neo Verona, Romeo's father and the author of the coup that exterminated the Capulets. The series gives his hatred a biography: he was born the illegitimate offspring of the Capulet house, the child of a woman the great family used and discarded, and he grew up in poverty nursing a grudge against the Capulet name. The feud's villain is thus its product before he is its author, and the play's unexplained ancient grudge survives inside the anime as the tyrant's own inheritance.

### Q: What is the Great Tree Escalus in Romeo x Juliet?

Escalus is the great tree rooted in the palace rock of Neo Verona, whose life holds the floating continent in the sky and whose health is the city's health. It is tended by the priestess Ophelia. Two such trees once sustained the world; one died long ago, and the survivor has been withering under a loveless rule. By the tradition Ophelia administers, the tree's renewal demands the sacrifice of a Capulet daughter, so the city's survival is mortgaged to the heroine's death from the moment her birth is known.

### Q: Who is Ophelia in Romeo x Juliet, and how does she differ from Hamlet's Ophelia?

The anime's Ophelia is the priestess and keeper of Escalus, a serene supernatural agent who tends the tree, marks Juliet for the rite and administers the sacrifice it demands. Shakespeare's Ophelia, in Hamlet, is a young woman destroyed by the demands that male authority places on her, her death reported rather than staged. Gonzo's transfer reverses the figure: the victim of such a system becomes the system's officiant. In the finale she wounds Romeo mortally as he kills her, the one death in the series dealt by tragedy's own machinery rather than by the feud.

### Q: Which characters in Romeo x Juliet are named from Shakespeare's other plays?

The supporting cast is staffed from across the canon. William the playwright is Shakespeare himself. Ophelia comes from Hamlet; Cordelia, Juliet's friend and attendant, from King Lear; Hermione, Romeo's betrothed, from The Winter's Tale; Curio from Twelfth Night; Francisco from Hamlet; Antonio, the boy page, bears the canon's most reusable name; and Tybalt's mother is named Volumnia, from Coriolanus. Reviewer Chris Beveridge noted that the borrowings may please or annoy devoted fans, though the major transfers, Ophelia above all, do functional work rather than decorative.

### Q: Who is William in Romeo x Juliet, and what does he do in the story?

William, called Willy, is a playwright character who keeps the theatre in Neo Verona where the Capulet retainers hide. He houses Juliet, employs her as a stagehand while she lives as Odin, shelters the resistance, and late in the series composes and rehearses a play reenacting Juliet's own story, in which Juliet tries the role of tyrant-killer in fiction before the act is demanded in fact. He is the play's Chorus moved inside the action: the resident storyteller whose theatre is refuge, weapon and memory at once.

### Q: How does Romeo die in the anime Romeo x Juliet?

Romeo dies defending Juliet rather than by suicide. He rides to the great tree to stop her foretold sacrifice, fights its priestess Ophelia, and kills her, taking a mortal wound from her in the exchange. He reaches Juliet, speaks with her, and dies in her arms before she completes the rite. The play's hero dies by his own hand on false information, alone among the tomb's dead; the anime's dies in open combat, with full knowledge and a witness, trying to prevent an ending that was written into the city's foundations.

### Q: How does Juliet die in Romeo x Juliet, and what does her death accomplish?

Juliet completes the sacrifice the tree demands, giving herself to Escalus after Romeo's death has removed the last reason to refuse it. Her death transforms the tree's power: wings of light unfold, the floating continent descends slowly, and Neo Verona lands upon the ocean and survives as an ordinary island, its people preserved. A closing shot shows the lovers hand in hand before the tree in a place outside the story's geography. Her death is thus a public transaction rather than a private despair, exchanged directly for the city's life.

### Q: Why is Romeo betrothed to Hermione in the anime?

Leontes Montague arranges the match to steady his regime's succession and respectability, binding his house to a noble family through his heir. The device is the play's arranged marriage inverted: where Capulet presses the suitor Paris on Juliet, the anime presses the bride Hermione on Romeo, and the unwanted match migrates to the hero along with the action role. Romeo's resistance to the betrothal is among the defiances that mark him as his father's internal dissident and prepare his later disgrace and exile to the mines.

### Q: What happens to Mercutio and Tybalt in the anime?

Both are inverted. The anime's Mercutio is no loyal friend but a scheming courtier of the Montague household who claws toward the succession and, late in the series, kills Lord Montague himself at the moment of near surrender, so the tyrant falls to ambition rather than to the resistance. Tybalt begins as the regime's hound hunting the Red Whirlwind, is revealed as Montague's illegitimate son by a Capulet woman, and ends fighting for the Capulet cause, surviving into the finale. Shakespeare's pair kill and are killed by Act 3; Gonzo's outlast the feud's author.

### Q: How does the English dub of Romeo x Juliet use Shakespeare's language?

Funimation's dub, directed by J. Michael Tatum and R. Bruce Elliott with script adaptation by a team led by Taliesin Jaffe, wrote Shakespeare back into a series whose Japanese dialogue rarely quotes him. Early episodes experimented with whole stretches of dialogue in iambic pentameter, the episode previews kept the verse throughout, and lines borrowed from Shakespeare's works were added beyond those the original script carried. Reviewer Holly Ellingwood praised the dub for carrying an older style of language and more Shakespearean quotation than the Japanese track, an unusual case of a translation out-quoting its original.

### Q: Who composed the music for Romeo x Juliet, and what songs frame the series?

Hitoshi Sakimoto composed the score, performed by the Eminence Symphony Orchestra in a full late-Romantic idiom that treats the fantasy setting with complete seriousness. The opening theme is Lena Park's cover of "You Raise Me Up", a ballad about being lifted by another's strength, playing over a city held up by a tree. The closing themes are "Cyclone" by 12012 for the first fourteen episodes and "Good Bye, Yesterday" by Mizrock for episodes 15 to 23, with an English version of the opening song reserved for the finale. Critic Theron Martin singled the score out for praise.

### Q: How does the Romeo x Juliet manga differ from the anime?

The manga, written by Reiko Yoshida and drawn by the artist credited as COM, ran in Kadokawa Shoten's Monthly Asuka magazine from March 24, 2007 to 2009, was collected in two volumes, and was licensed in English by Yen Press. The reference record states that it differs extensively from the anime, although it shares the premise of the floating city, the coup and the hidden heir. It is a companion retelling with its own emphases and its own treatment of the Escalus sacrifice, not a transcript of the broadcast series, and the anime article treats it as a separate witness.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch anime map to argue that the anime keeps the play's civic ending?

Begin from the map's final rows. The play converts two private suicides in a tomb into a public settlement: a prince's sentence on the houses, rival statues promised, the story ordered to be told. The anime converts a foretold sacrifice into a city's survival, a continent set gently on the sea and a story passing into its resident playwright's keeping. Set the deleted Prince beside the tree that bears his name, and argue that Gonzo preserved the ending's function, the civic digestion of the lovers' deaths, while replacing every mechanism that produces it.
