The Kalelkar Commission is usually granted two sentences in the history of Indian reservation: appointed in 1953, shelved in 1956, a failed rehearsal for the Mandal Commission of 1979. That treatment mistakes a burial certificate for a biography. The claim defended here is that the Commission chaired by Dattatreya Balkrishna Kalelkar, known across India as Kaka Kalelkar, was institutionally substantive even in political deferral: it ran the first all-India inquiry into the conditions of the socially and educationally backward classes, named 2,399 castes as backward, proposed reservation percentages that no later body exceeded, and then produced, in its chairman's forwarding letter and in the Union Cabinet's memorandum of September 1956, the two documents that fixed the questions every later inquiry had to answer. The Mandal Commission did not begin from a blank page in 1979. It began from Kalelkar's page, with Kalelkar's unresolved argument printed at the top of it.

Two rival readings stand in the way of that claim, and this article adjudicates among all three. The first is the brief-mention reading, found in textbook timelines, where the Commission appears as a date and a name in a sequence that runs from the Constitution to Mandal, carrying no analytical weight of its own. The second is the political-failure reading, stronger and better evidenced, which treats the episode as proof of the Nehru government's reluctance to extend reservation beyond the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes: a commission appointed to quieten a constitutional promise, a report disowned by its own chairman, a cabinet that forwarded the file to the states and closed the subject for twenty-three years. Both readings capture part of the record. Neither explains why the Mandal Commission, a generation later, adopted Kalelkar's working architecture wholesale, argued with Kalelkar's forwarding letter point by point, and inherited a centre-state division of labour that the Cabinet of 1956 had designed. Institutions leave two kinds of residue, implementation and conditions. The Kalelkar Commission left almost no implementation at the centre. It left conditions everywhere.

The evidence is primary and dated. The Report of the Backward Classes Commission, submitted to the President on 30 March 1955, runs through the Commission's tours, its four tests of backwardness, its state-by-state lists and its recommendations on education, services and land. Kalelkar's forwarding letter, written with the report, records the chairman's retreat from the caste criterion his own majority had adopted. The Cabinet's reply survives in the memorandum of action taken, laid before Parliament with the report in September 1956 and preserved in the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library papers. Around these documents stand the two standard scholarly treatments, Marc Galanter's Competing Equalities of 1984 and Christophe Jaffrelot's India's Silent Revolution of 2003, and the essays of Sudhakar Yedla on pre-Mandal backward classes politics. Read together, they support a verdict neither brief mention nor failure: a first commission that failed upward, depositing the method, the vocabulary and the standing disagreement that the second commission converted into policy.

## Article 340 and the Constitutional Opening

The Commission existed because the Constituent Assembly had written its possibility into the Constitution and then declined to use the stronger instruments available for other groups. For the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, the Constitution of 1950 carried operative provisions: reservation of seats in legislatures, reservation in services under Article 335 read with Article 16, a special officer under Article 338, and lists notified by the President under Articles 341 and 342. For the residual category the Assembly called the socially and educationally backward classes, it wrote something weaker and stranger: a promise of inquiry. Article 340 empowers the President to appoint a commission to investigate the conditions of socially and educationally backward classes within the territory of India and the difficulties under which they labour, to recommend the steps the Union or a state should take to remove those difficulties and improve their condition, and to specify the grants that should be made and the conditions attached to them. The commission so appointed must investigate the matters referred to it and present a report setting out the facts and making such recommendations as it thinks proper. The President must then lay the report, with a memorandum explaining the action taken, before each House of Parliament. That procedural tail, report plus memorandum, is where the Kalelkar story ends as well as begins, and [the constitutional reservation framework](/2020/02/25/constitutional-reservation-framework/) within which Article 340 sits explains why: the Assembly gave the backward classes a fact-finding route because it could not agree on anything more.

The disagreement had a history older than the Constitution. The claim that caste standing, and not poverty alone, disabled whole groups from schooling and office had been pressed by Jyotirao Phule in Maharashtra in the nineteenth century, institutionalised by the non-Brahmin movements of Madras and Mysore in the early twentieth, and argued in the Constituent Assembly by members who wanted the backward classes named with the same precision as the Scheduled Castes. B. R. Ambedkar, as chairman of the Drafting Committee, carried the category through the drafting; the Assembly's settlement left the category undefined, its membership unspecified and its remedies postponed to a commission. Readers who want the founding bargain in full can trace it through [the rights-development founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) that structured the Assembly's work on equality, and through [the pre-Constitution architectural inheritance](/2020/02/19/poona-pact-ambedkar-gandhi-1932/) that the Poona Pact of 1932 had already fixed for the Depressed Classes. The backward classes inherited the weaker half of that inheritance: no separate electorates, no notified list, no statutory quota, only Article 340 and the discretion of the first government to invoke it.

Two features of Article 340 shaped everything that followed. The first is that it names a condition, socially and educationally backward, without naming a single group that suffers it. The Commission would therefore have to decide the constitutionally explosive question of identification for itself: which castes, which tests, which thresholds. The second is that Article 340 assigns the remedy jointly to the Union and the states, which in 1953 meant assigning it to nobody in particular. A commission could recommend; a cabinet could forward. The text that authorised the inquiry also pre-wrote its most likely fate, and the distance between those two features, identification contested at the centre and remedy divisible between centre and states, is the space in which the whole episode played out. The first government of Jawaharlal Nehru waited three years after the Constitution commenced before using the article at all, and the timing matters: by January 1953 the First Amendment of 1951 had already added Article 15(4), permitting the state to make special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens, after the Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan, decided on 9 April 1951, had struck down the Madras communal order for want of such a provision. The legal door stood open. What remained undecided was the political question the Commission was sent to answer: who, exactly, stood on the other side of it.

## The Southern Prehistory: Reservation Before the Republic

The Commission did not invent its subject. When its members began their tours in 1953, reservation for the backward classes was already half a century old in parts of India, and the prehistory explains the geography of everything that followed. In the princely state of Kolhapur, Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj issued an order on 26 July 1902 reserving 50 percent of vacancies in the state services for the backward classes, the first such order by an Indian ruler, issued from a court that had felt Brahmin administrative monopoly as a daily fact of governance. In Mysore, the Maharaja's government appointed the committee under Sir Leslie Miller in 1918, which reported in 1919 that one small literate group held a share of the higher services grotesquely beyond its share of the population, and the state issued orders in 1921 distributing recruitment toward the rest. In Madras, the Justice Party, founded on the Non-Brahmin Manifesto of 1916, won office under dyarchy and issued the first communal Government Order in September 1921, distributing government posts among named groups, and enlarged the scheme in 1927. Travancore moved in the same direction in the 1930s. By 1947, therefore, a citizen of Madras or Mysore lived under a working regime of group-wise public employment, with lists, percentages and an administrative routine for both, while a citizen of the United Provinces or Bihar lived under no such regime at all, and the contrast was not an accident of administration. It was the deposit of movements: in the south and in Maharashtra, organised non-Brahmin politics had captured or pressured state power before Independence; in the north, the Congress absorbed the backward castes into a common nationalist front and the question of their separate share was postponed in the name of unity.

The Commission inherited this map as its first problem. Its mandate was all-India, but its evidence, its precedents and its political sponsors were southern and western in weight, because those were the regions where backwardness had been administratively defined for thirty years. The report's state chapters for Madras, Mysore and Bombay rest on records, lists and official experience; its chapters for the northern states rest on testimony and inference from the 1931 census, the last enumeration to count caste across India. The 1931 baseline deserves its own sentence in the prehistory, since it governed both commissions: the colonial census had counted caste from 1871 to 1931, the count was abandoned in 1941 on grounds of cost and war, and the republic's censuses from 1951 counted only the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. Every all-India statement about the backward classes made after 1950, Kalelkar's 2,399 castes and roughly 32 percent, Mandal's 3,743 castes and 52 percent, is therefore an extrapolation from a 1931 skeleton dressed in later state data, a fact the Kalelkar report states plainly and its critics have used against both bodies ever since. The prehistory closes, then, with the paradox the Commission embodied: it was asked to nationalise a policy that existed only regionally, on evidence that existed only historically, for a constituency that existed politically, in 1953, mainly where the policy already ran. The report it produced is best read as the record of that triple difficulty, honestly faced in the field and fatally hesitated over at the moment of signature.

## The Prelude of 1951: Champakam and the First Amendment

The Commission was appointed into a legal landscape that two decisions of 1951 had already contoured, and the contours explain both its mandate and its fate. Article 16(4) of the Constitution as enacted in 1950 permitted the state to reserve appointments for any backward class of citizens which, in the opinion of the state, was not adequately represented in the services. The permission existed; the identification did not. No authority had said which classes were backward for the services of the Union, and the education side of the question lacked even the permission: Article 15 prohibited discrimination without a backward classes exception, and Article 29(2) guaranteed admission to state-aided institutions without regard to caste. When the Supreme Court decided State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan on 9 April 1951, striking down the Madras communal order that had distributed college seats among named groups since the 1920s, the gap between the services permission and the education prohibition became untenable. Madras had run a reservation system for a generation under an order the Court held unconstitutional; the Union government answered within weeks.

The Constitution (First Amendment) Act, passed by the provisional Parliament and assented to on 18 June 1951, inserted Article 15(4): nothing in Article 15 or in Article 29(2) would prevent the state from making any special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. The phrase socially and educationally backward, carried into 15(4) from the vocabulary of Article 340, is the hinge on which the next forty years turned, because the amendment authorised everything and identified nothing. Parliament debated the clause in May and June 1951 with the backward classes question argued openly: members from Madras defended the communal order's record; Nehru defended the amendment as necessary to the Constitution's social purposes while recording, in the same debates, his unease at classification by caste as a permanent principle of the republic. The dual position, authorise the category and regret it, is the Nehru government's whole backward classes policy in miniature, and it is the position the Cabinet memorandum of 1956 would repeat with a commission's report on the table. Ambedkar, as Law Minister, piloted the amendment through its drafting stages before his resignation in September 1951 over the Hindu Code Bill; the man who had written the backward classes into the constitutional text as a subject for inquiry wrote them into the fundamental rights chapter as a subject for provision. What neither the amendment nor the text supplied was a list. Identification remained, by design, an administrative act still to be performed, and the performance was assigned, two years later, to a commission under the article that had been waiting since 1950 for its first use.

The prelude matters because it disciplines the failure reading. A government determined to do nothing for the backward classes does not amend the Constitution within seventeen months of its commencement to make their advancement lawful in education, against the judgment of its own Supreme Court's reading of the text. The Nehru government of 1951 wanted the power. The same government's memorandum of 1956 declined to use the power at the centre until the identification question was answered to its satisfaction. Between the two dates sits the Commission, and the Commission's tragedy, if the word is not too large, is that it was asked to supply the single element, an authoritative all-India identification, that could convert a lawful power into an operative policy, and it supplied that element in a form its own chairman then qualified. The First Amendment had also set the terms of the qualification's damage: because 15(4) spoke of classes and not of castes, the chairman's objection that caste was the wrong key had a constitutional resonance it would not have had under a text that named caste itself. The argument of 1955 and 1956 was conducted inside the space the draftsmen of 1951 had left, between a category authorised and a criterion unnamed.

## The Kalelkar Commission Takes Office

On 29 January 1953 the President appointed the first Backward Classes Commission under Article 340, by Ministry of Home Affairs Notification No. 70/53-Public of the same date. The choice of chairman announced the government's intent to treat the inquiry as an exercise in national moral stock-taking as much as administration. Dattatreya Balkrishna Kalelkar, born at Satara on 1 December 1885, educated at Fergusson College in Pune, had met Gandhi in 1915, lived at the Sabarmati Ashram, served as vice-chancellor of Gujarat Vidyapith from 1928 to 1935, and entered the Rajya Sabha in April 1952 as one of the most recognisable Gandhians in public life. He was a writer of vast output in Gujarati, Marathi and Hindi, a campaigner for a national language, and a man whose instincts on caste were Gandhian in the precise sense: he regarded caste hierarchy as a moral corruption to be dissolved by reform of the heart and by service, and he distrusted the conversion of caste into an administrative category even when the conversion aimed at justice. The government gained a chairman of unimpeachable standing. It also, though few remarked it in 1953, appointed the one senior Gandhian most likely to recoil from his own commission's conclusions.

The membership balanced Parliament, the states and the secretariat. The notification named Narayan Sadoba Kajrolkar, Bheekha Bhai, Rajeshwar Patel and N. R. M. Swamy from Parliament, Shivdayal Singh Chaurasia and Lala Jagannath from public life, Abdul Qaiyum Ansari of Bihar and T. Mariappa of Mysore from the state legislatures, and Atma Singh Namdhari, with Arunangshu De as member-secretary. Membership changed under the pressure of mortality and other office: Atma Singh Namdhari died on 7 January 1954 and Anup Singh was nominated in his place on 27 February 1954; Lala Jagannath resigned to take up another appointment and P. G. Shah replaced him from 2 August 1954. The composition deserves a pause, because later readings treat the Commission as a Delhi bureau writing in a vacuum. It was nothing of the kind. Its members included legislators from the two regions, Madras and Mysore through Mariappa's experience, where backward classes reservations already operated as working law, and parliamentarians who would have to defend the report on the floor of the House. When the report divided its own authors, the division ran through Indian politics itself, between those who had administered caste-based quotas and those who had only debated them.

The terms of reference followed Article 340 closely and were wide. The Commission was asked to determine the criteria to be adopted in considering whether any sections of the people of India should be treated as socially and educationally backward classes; to prepare a list of such classes in accordance with those criteria; to investigate the conditions of the classes so identified and the difficulties under which they labour; and to recommend the steps that should be taken by the Union or by any state to remove such difficulties and improve their condition, including the grants that should be made and the conditions on which they should be given. Every subsequent controversy is visible inside that mandate in embryo. Criteria first, list second: the method question precedes the enumeration, and a commission that answered the criteria question with caste, as this one did, had committed itself before it counted a single name. Steps by the Union or by any state: the remedy question is federal from the first line. Grants and their conditions: the report was expected to speak about money, land and schools, not about reservation alone, and the economic chapters of the 1955 report, usually skipped by later summarists, occupy the larger part of its recommendations. The Commission set up its office in New Delhi, issued its questionnaire, and prepared to do what no Indian state had attempted since the census of 1931: enumerate disadvantage caste by caste, across the whole territory of the Union.

## Two Years of Evidence: Tours, Hearings and Memoranda

The Commission worked from February 1953 to the end of 1954 as a travelling court of inquiry. It divided its labour between a written instrument, a questionnaire circulated to state governments, universities, associations and private witnesses, and oral evidence taken on tour in the state capitals and district towns. The report's own appendices record sittings across the country and a body of memoranda that ran from printed caste-association briefs, some of them book-length, to single-page petitions from village schoolmasters. The evidential harvest was uneven in a way that itself became evidence: the associations of the backward castes of Madras and Mysore arrived with printed histories, census extracts and counsel; witnesses from much of the Hindi-speaking north arrived as individuals, or not at all. A commission measuring backwardness by testimony will register the organised and under-register the silent, and the Commission's critics inside its own membership said so at the time. The pattern mattered for the politics of the report, because the states with the strongest testimony were the states that least needed a central list.

### How did the Commission gather evidence across the states?

The Commission toured state by state between February 1953 and December 1954, held public sittings at which caste associations, officials and individual witnesses testified, circulated a printed questionnaire to governments and bodies across India, and received memoranda numbering in the thousands, from which its state chapters and its all-India list of backward castes were compiled during 1954 and early 1955.

The hearings had a theatrical quality the report's dry prose conceals. Caste associations, the sabhas and sangams that had multiplied since the non-Brahmin movements of the 1910s and 1920s, treated a sitting as a durbar of recognition: to be heard by a presidential commission was already a form of standing, and to be entered on its list was standing converted into entitlement. Delegation after delegation therefore argued downward, presenting its caste as lower, poorer and less schooled than its rivals agreed it was, in the strange auction of disadvantage that every Indian commission on backwardness has since conducted. Officials testified in the opposite direction, warning of administrative burden and of the expectations a list would raise. University teachers brought the statistics of matriculation and graduation by group where such figures existed; mostly they did not exist, because the census had stopped counting caste below the level of the Scheduled Castes in 1931, and the Commission worked from the 1931 baseline, from state education returns and from its own sampling. Galanter, in Competing Equalities, notes the methodological candour with which the report admits these gaps: the Commission published its criteria, printed its lists state by state, and flagged the entries it could not verify. For an inquiry routinely described as amateur beside the Mandal Commission's indicator machinery, the 1955 report is a more self-aware document than its reputation allows.

One decision taken during the tours shaped the report's structure and its fate. The Commission resolved early that backwardness would be assessed for castes as wholes, on the ground that in Indian society the disabilities named in its mandate, low standing in the hierarchy, exclusion from schooling, absence from the services, attached to birth-groups and not to scattered households. A prosperous family inside a backward caste remained, in the Commission's reasoning, marked by the standing of its group in the village and the marriage market; a destitute family inside an advanced caste was poor, but poor without the additional sentence of inherited rank. That is the analytical core of the report, and it was contested from inside the room even as the tours proceeded. Members who favoured occupation and income as the operative tests pressed for household-level criteria; the majority held to the group. When the chairman changed sides at the end, it was on exactly this ground, and the memorandum writers of 1956 knew precisely which sentence in the report to aim at.

## The Findings: Four Tests and 2,399 Castes

The report the Commission presented on 30 March 1955 identified 2,399 castes and communities as socially and educationally backward, together covering, on the report's estimate from the 1931 census proportions, roughly 32 percent of the population of India. Of the 2,399, the Commission starred 837 as most backward, the groups whose condition it judged to demand priority in any scheme of assistance. These are the numbers for which the report is remembered, and they deserve to be read as what they were: the first attempt by the Indian state since 1931 to count disadvantage below the line of untouchability, and to count it as a property of ranked groups rather than of poor persons. The list was not a guess. It was built state by state, printed state by state, and defended in the report's chapters against the evidence taken on tour.

The identification rested on four tests, stated in the report's early chapters and applied, the majority claimed, in combination. The first test was low position in the traditional caste hierarchy of Hindu society, the ritual standing of the group in the local order of precedence. The second was lack of general educational advancement among the major section of the caste or group, measured against state literacy and schooling returns. The third was inadequate or no representation in government service. The fourth was inadequate representation in the fields of trade, commerce and industry. A group failing across these tests was backward within the meaning of the mandate; a group failing on the first alone, a caste of low ritual standing whose members had advanced in school and office, presented the borderline cases over which the Commission argued longest. Two features of the scheme drew fire then and since. The tests were framed on the Hindu hierarchy, which fitted awkwardly over the Muslim, Christian and Sikh groups the Commission nevertheless listed where the evidence showed parallel disability; the report handled the difficulty by treating hierarchy as a social fact of the locality rather than a doctrine of any religion. And the tests took the caste as the unit, so that advancement by a section of a listed caste did not remove the caste from the list, an approach the majority defended as faithful to the mandate's phrase, sections of the people, read in a society where the section and the caste so often coincide.

Behind the tests stood a diagnosis that the failure reading of the Commission consistently understates. The report held that economic backwardness in India was frequently the result, and not the cause, of the social order: that a group's poverty flowed from its assigned station, its hereditary occupation and its exclusion from the schools, so that remedies addressed to income alone would leave the machinery of disadvantage running. The chapters on conditions dwell on the hereditary calling, the village servant's customary dues, the moneylender's hold, the schoolmaster's indifference, the absence of the listed castes from the gazetted services and the professions. This is the intellectual hinge of the whole document. If backwardness is produced by rank, then the state's remedy must answer rank, by opening the institutions where rank is ratified, the school, the service and the legislature, to the ranked groups as groups. The Mandal Commission in 1980 would restate the same hinge in the language of indicators and weights: social criteria given the greatest weight precisely because, in its phrase, educational and economic backwardness flowed from the social. Between 1955 and 1980 the sentence was available in print, in a report laid before Parliament. Nothing in the twenty-three intervening years refuted it. It was simply not acted on at the centre, which is a different thing, and the distinction is the one this article exists to defend.

The report also stepped outside the frame later summarists impose on it. It treated all women as a class as backward, a recommendation recorded in its list of principal conclusions, on the reasoning that the disabilities of sex cut across the hierarchy and compounded those of caste for the women of the listed groups. It recommended that the census of 1961 resume the caste-wise enumeration abandoned after 1931, so that the next inquiry would not inherit its own evidential poverty. And it refused, pointedly, to confine the backward classes to Hindu society, listing groups among Muslims, Christians and Sikhs where the four tests were met, a refusal that later litigation and later commissions would repeatedly have to re-argue. These positions were not marginalia. The women-as-class recommendation and the census recommendation show a commission thinking about the architecture of knowledge and remedy together, and they mark the distance between the document the Cabinet received in 1955 and the caricature of a caste-counting exercise that the memorandum of 1956 found it convenient to answer.

The starred 837 deserve a paragraph of their own, because the device of an inner list, the most backward within the backward, is the report's quietest and most durable invention. The Commission starred the castes whose condition it judged worst across the four tests, groups at the bottom of the local hierarchy with the thinnest schooling and the faintest presence in service and trade, and directed that assistance reach them first within any scheme framed on the list. The device answered, inside the report itself, the objection that the dissenting members and the forwarding letter pressed from outside it: that a single undifferentiated list would be harvested by its strongest members. A commission that builds a priority order into its own enumeration has anticipated the capture argument and insured against it as well as paper can. The afterlife of the starred list runs through the state systems of the following decades, in the sub-classifications by which Bihar in 1978 and other states later divided their backward classes quotas between the backward and the most backward, and it reaches the jurisprudence of the Mandal decade, where the exclusion of the advanced layer of each listed group, the creamy layer of Indra Sawhney, performs in law the discrimination among the listed that Kalelkar's starring performed in administration. Devices survive their documents. The two-tier list of 1955 is the ancestor of every attempt since to keep a reservation scheme pointed at the bottom of its own constituency.

## The Recommendations: Quotas at the Maximum, Welfare Beyond Them

The recommendations of 30 March 1955 were the boldest any Indian commission on reservation had made, and they remain, in percentage terms, the boldest ever placed before an Indian government. In education, the Commission recommended reserving 70 percent of seats in all technical and professional institutions for qualified students of the backward classes, and reservation in general higher education at the lower scale the report set out for the ordinary university course, alongside scholarships, hostels and the expansion of schooling in the areas where the listed castes lived. In services, it recommended minimum reservation of vacancies in all government services and local bodies on a graded scale: 25 percent in Class I posts, 33 and a third percent in Class II, and 40 percent in Classes III and IV. A separate ministry for the welfare of the backward classes was proposed to carry the programme. No later body went as far. The Mandal Commission, working under the ceiling the Supreme Court had announced in M. R. Balaji v. State of Mysore in 1963, would recommend 27 percent and present the figure as arithmetic modesty. Kalelkar's commission, writing before any ceiling existed, asked for what its population figures implied, and the size of the ask is part of the explanation for what happened to the report: a government can absorb a recommendation it can halve, and struggles with one it must either swallow or refuse whole.

### What share of seats did the Commission propose in technical and professional institutions?

The report recommended reserving 70 percent of seats in all technical and professional institutions for qualified students from the backward classes, the highest education quota any Indian commission has proposed, beside minimum service quotas of 25 percent in Class I posts, 33 and a third percent in Class II, and 40 percent in Classes III and IV.

Reservation, though, was the smaller half of the programme by bulk, and treating the report as a quota document alone repeats the memorandum's reduction of it. The economic chapters recommended extensive land reform, with ceilings, distribution of government waste land and security for tenants; reorganisation of the village economy; the Bhoodan movement's voluntary land gifts, which a Gandhian commission could hardly omit; dairy farming, cattle insurance, bee-keeping, piggery and fisheries as routes to subsidiary income; rural housing; public health and rural water supply; and a drive for adult literacy aimed at the listed classes. Education beyond the quota included free and compulsory primary schooling enforced in fact, technical schools in backward tracts, and the admission of backward class students to the existing institutions on scholarship rather than on sufferance. Read as a whole, the report is a development programme with reservation at its apex, not a reservation demand with welfare attached. That shape matters for the adjudication this article conducts later, because the political-failure reading requires a report that asked only for quotas and was refused; the document of March 1955 asked for a reordering of rural production and status together, and the parts of it that required no caste list at all, the wells, the houses, the literacy, were shelved with the rest. The deferral of 1956 was not a surgical rejection of one criterion. It was the burial of an entire programme, unimplemented in its uncontroversial chapters as in its contested ones, and that breadth of refusal is evidence about the government of the day that cuts in more than one direction.

The Commission also addressed the machinery of its own succession. It recommended the caste-wise enumeration of the 1961 census, so that identification could proceed from counted fact rather than from the 1931 residue. It proposed that the lists be revised as fresh evidence arrived, treating the enumeration of 2,399 castes as a first survey rather than a scripture. And it left the door of definition open in one respect that later became central: while the majority made caste the unit of identification, the report recorded the tests in a form, standing, schooling, service, trade, that a later commission could operationalise as indicators without changing the underlying diagnosis. The Mandal Commission's eleven indicators of 1980, four social, three educational and four economic, are the Kalelkar tests after twenty-five years of methodological argument, weighted and scored. A reader who places the two lists side by side sees the genealogy at once. The second commission presented its method as an answer to the chaos of the first; on the paper, it is closer to an inheritance, the same four questions asked with a calculator.

The machinery proposals close the recommendations and are the part of the report that the deferral killed most completely, because machinery, once created, generates its own continuation. The Commission proposed a separate ministry for the welfare of the backward classes, charged with the programme as a whole; periodic revision of the lists as fresh evidence and fresh counting arrived; and the resumption of caste enumeration in the census of 1961 to give the revision its facts. Each proposal aimed at converting an episodic act of inquiry into a standing function of the state, with an address, a budget and a calendar. The Union accepted none of the three for the backward classes: no ministry was created, the 1961 census counted no caste, and the lists were never revised at the centre because they were never adopted. The contrast with the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, who possessed a special officer under the Constitution, statutory lists and eventually dedicated ministries, measures what the backward classes lacked between 1956 and 1979: not sympathy, of which the memorandum contains a sufficiency, but an office whose business it was to keep their file open. When the file finally reopened in 1979, the Mandal Commission had to rebuild in two years the standing knowledge that a ministry of 1956 would have accumulated in twenty-three, and the second commission's reliance on the state reports and the 1931 skeleton, so often charged against its method, is in part the price of the machinery refused in 1956.

## The Gandhian in the Chair

To understand the forwarding letter, the chairman's formation must be taken seriously as evidence, because Kalelkar's recoil of March 1955 was not a lapse of nerve at the end of a long inquiry. It was the destination of a road he had walked for forty years. Kalelkar came to Gandhi in 1915, in the first year of Gandhi's Indian campaigns, and spent the formative decades of the national movement inside the ashram's discipline: teaching at the Sabarmati Ashram's national school, editing the Gujarati Navajivan in the early 1920s, building Gujarat Vidyapith as an institution of national education outside the colonial university system, and writing the essays in Gujarati, Marathi and Hindi that made him, in Gandhi's teasing phrase, a Gujarati and a quarter more. The Gandhian position on caste that this formation carried was double, and both halves mattered in 1955. Gandhi held caste hierarchy to be a sin against the equal dignity of persons and campaigned against untouchability with a ferocity that split his own camp in 1932; he also held, against Ambedkar, that caste as an administrative and electoral category entrenched the thing it named, and that the remedy lay in the change of heart of the privileged, in service, and in the dissolution of caste feeling rather than its enumeration by the state. A chairman formed in that school could accept a commission to study the condition of the backward classes with a whole heart, could tour and listen and document with genuine anger at what he found, and could still stop at the threshold of the list, because the list was the point where study became enumeration and enumeration became, in his vocabulary, administration of caste by the republic.

The appointment therefore contained its conclusion in a way the failure reading notices but explains too cheaply. It is too simple to say the government appointed a man certain to sabotage the report. In 1953 no senior figure in Indian public life combined Kalelkar's standing with a settled enthusiasm for caste-based reservation; the political class that might have supplied one, the non-Brahmin leadership of the south, was regional in base and largely outside the Congress's national command. The government appointed the most eminent available Indian whose life's work was the welfare of the lowly, and discovered, or confirmed, that eminence and programme were different things. There is a harder institutional lesson in the sequence, and the Mandal Commission learned it: the chairman of a backward classes commission is not a neutral instrument but the single largest variable in its fate, because the report's authority in the public argument will be the chairman's authority, spent for the report or against it. B. P. Mandal, a lifelong politician of the backward castes movement of Bihar, spent his authority for his report and defended it in person for the rest of his life. Kalelkar, who died in New Delhi on 21 August 1981 at the age of ninety-five, eight months after the Mandal report was submitted to the President, spent his against his. The two commissions are mirrors in this as in their method: same article, same architecture, opposite chairmen, and the difference in their afterlives begins there, before any cabinet sits.

## The Chairman Against His Report

Every account of the Kalelkar Commission reaches the same extraordinary document, and the political-failure reading rests its case on it. Kalelkar signed the report. With it, he sent the President a forwarding letter, conventionally called his dissenting addendum, in which the chairman of the Commission withdrew his confidence from the central recommendation of the body he had led for two years. In the letter Kalelkar recorded that he had come to doubt the wisdom of remedies founded on caste; that to specify castes as backward, and to attach the state's benefits to the specification, risked perpetuating the very distinctions the Constitution sought to dissolve; that assistance should reach the poor and the unschooled as persons and families, tested by economic condition, occupation and educational want rather than by birth in a listed group; and that the prospect of caste organising itself around a government list, caste associations hardening into caste constituencies of claim, troubled him as a Gandhian who had spent a lifetime arguing that caste must wither, not be administered. He did not record a formal minute of dissent, and he let his signature stand on the report. But a covering letter from the chairman telling the President, in effect, that the report's foundation was unsafe, is a rarer and more damaging instrument than dissent. A dissent divides a commission. The letter indicted it.

The letter did not stand alone. Five members of the Commission recorded minutes of dissent against the main findings, objecting, in varying combinations, to the linkage of backwardness with caste and to reservation of posts on a caste basis. The Commission thus presented the government with a majority report, a chairman's letter against its foundation, and five dissents against its conclusions, a document set that announced its own contestability. Galanter's treatment in Competing Equalities remains the most careful: he shows that the letter was no sudden conversion. Kalelkar had voiced his misgivings during the tours; colleagues and later scholars read the letter as the Gandhian resolution of a tension the chairman had carried into the appointment itself, between the mandate's instruction to find classes and his conviction that classes named by caste were the disease dressed as the cure. The essays of Sudhakar Yedla on pre-Mandal backward classes politics add the reception from below: associations that had testified in hope read the letter as betrayal by the very eminence whose name had drawn them to the sittings. Both readings can be true. The letter was sincerely meant, and it was received, in the secretariat and in the associations alike, as the moment the report lost its sponsor.

What the letter could not do, and what the failure reading sometimes claims for it, is erase the report's evidentiary existence. A chairman may disown a conclusion; he cannot unhear two years of testimony, unprint 2,399 names, or withdraw the four tests from the record. The letter changed the report's political standing, not its documentary standing, and the sequel proves the distinction: when the Janata government's commission sat in 1979 and 1980, its members and researchers worked through the Kalelkar volumes, tests, lists and letter together, treating the letter as one more exhibit in the file, the first statement of the objection their method had to defeat. There is also a plainer institutional point. Article 340 obliged the President to lay the report before Parliament with a memorandum of action taken. The forwarding letter, whatever its intent, handed the Cabinet a reasoned basis for the memorandum it wished in any case to write. The chairman's conscience and the government's convenience arrived at the same address by different roads, and the historian who separates them, crediting the letter with sincerity and the Cabinet with its own independent judgment, reads the episode more exactly than the historian who makes the letter the cause of the deferral. Nehru's government had never committed itself to accept the report. It had committed itself only to receive it, and the letter decided nothing that the Cabinet was not positioned to decide without it.

## The Memorandum of 1956 and the Decision to Defer

The report waited seventeen months for its answer. The Cabinet considered the recommendations through 1955 and 1956, in the interval that also contained the States Reorganisation Commission's aftermath and the Second Five Year Plan's drafting, and the government's reply, when it came in September 1956, took the form Article 340 prescribed: the report laid before Parliament with a memorandum explaining the action taken. The memorandum is the second foundational document of the episode, and it repays the same close reading as the forwarding letter. It did not dispute the existence of backwardness or the state's duty toward it. It attacked the instrument. The caste system, the memorandum argued, was the greatest hindrance in the way of progress toward an egalitarian society, and the recognition of specified castes as backward might serve to maintain and even perpetuate the existing distinctions on the basis of caste. The Commission's want of unanimity, chairman against report and five members against conclusions, was recorded as a defect of the findings themselves. What the government required, the memorandum said, were workable criteria for specifying the socially and educationally backward classes, and further investigation to make good the deficiencies noticed in the report. On that reasoning the operative decisions followed: no all-India list of backward classes would be drawn up, no reservation would be made in the services of the central government for any group of backward classes, and the report would be forwarded to the state governments, which could prepare their own lists and proceed as their conditions required. The centre kept the principle, surrendered the list, and federalised the consequences.

The decision must be placed inside [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) to be judged fairly. Nehru's own record on the question was long and consistent: he had disliked caste as a basis of public policy since the 1930s, had accepted the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe provisions as the price of abolishing untouchability's political isolation, and regarded the backward classes claim, pressed by groups that were often locally dominant in land and numbers, as a different and more dangerous species of demand. In June 1961 he would write to the chief ministers, in a letter preserved in his published correspondence, that he disliked any kind of reservation on communal and caste grounds and believed help should be given on economic considerations, a letter that only restated, five years on, the doctrine of the 1956 memorandum. Around the cabinet table of 1955 and 1956 sat ministers formed by [the founding triangulation context](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) of the Assembly years, and the backward classes question fitted none of their three settlements comfortably: it was not national integration, not abolition of untouchability, not linguistic accommodation. It was a claim by the middle of the hierarchy against the top, and the Congress of the 1950s, whose state machines rested heavily on the votes and the local leadership of precisely those middle castes, had every incentive to neither grant it at the centre nor refuse it in terms. Forwarding the report to the states achieved both: the claim was honoured in form and dissolved in jurisdiction.

The date deserves emphasis, because September 1956 was also the hour of [the 1956 contemporaneous-period context](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/), when the Union was being redrawn on linguistic lines and the political energy of every state government was absorbed by boundaries, capitals and ministries. A forwarded commission report, arriving in state secretariats in the autumn of the reorganisation, competed for attention with the reconstitution of the states themselves. In Madras and Mysore, where backward classes machinery already existed, the file joined a running system. In the northern states it joined nothing; there was no department, no list-drawing staff, no settled state policy for it to extend. The Cabinet had not merely deferred a decision. It had chosen the forum, hundreds of state desks in a season of reorganisation, in which deferral was the default outcome, and the choice of forum, as the next section shows state by state, determined the next twenty-three years of backward classes politics in India.

One further feature of the memorandum shaped the future more than any of its refusals. By demanding workable criteria and further investigation, the government converted a political refusal into a methodological promissory note: the question was not closed but returned, technically open, to some future inquiry better equipped than Kalelkar's. Every later actor used the note. State commissions of the 1960s and 1970s, in Mysore, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Bihar and elsewhere, cited the centre's unresolved criteria problem as their warrant. The Mandal Commission's terms of reference in 1979 follow the memorandum's language almost clause by clause: determine the criteria, identify the classes, recommend the steps. The deferral of 1956 thus legislated the agenda of 1979. A government that had wished to kill the subject would have rejected the report outright and defended the rejection. This government instead perfected the technique of the unfinished file, and the file, in the Indian administrative tradition, outlives the ministry that leaves it unfinished.

## The Debate That Did Not Follow

A report laid before Parliament with a memorandum of deferral might still have forced a reckoning on the floor, and the absence of that reckoning is the quietest fact of the episode. The backward classes had spokesmen in the Lok Sabha of 1956, and the associations that had testified before the Commission petitioned and resolved through 1956 and 1957; but no bill followed the memorandum, no ministry staked itself on the list, and the parliamentary discussion of the report closed with the government's position unamended. The contrast with the report's other afterlife is instructive. In the states, as the record of their replies shows, the document entered working systems and altered them; in the textbooks and commission libraries it entered the canon of sources; in the Parliament of the Union it entered the record and stopped. The Congress's state machines in the north drew their district leadership heavily from the very peasant castes the list named, and those leaders' interest lay in delivering their followers' votes to the party, not in converting the followers into a separately enumerated claim upon the party's own ministries. The memorandum's criteria argument gave that interest a respectable vocabulary. It is possible to describe the whole sequence without bad faith on anyone's part: a chairman who doubted, a cabinet that agreed with the doubt, a Parliament that had other business in the autumn of the linguistic reorganisation, and a constituency whose organised strength lay in the two regions that needed the report least. Failure readings that require villains undersell the episode. The deferral of the Kalelkar report was overdetermined, produced by conscience, doctrine, arithmetic and season together, and that is precisely why it lasted twenty-three years: a decision with four independent supports does not fall when one support is removed.

## Forwarded to the States: Five Answers to One Report

The state replies to the forwarded report are the least read documents of the episode, preserved in the state archives and in the early chapters of the state commission reports that later superseded them, and they are the part of the record where the institutional-substantive reading wins its strongest ground. For the forwarding was not a uniform burial. It was a sorting. States that already possessed backward classes machinery read the Kalelkar report as a central ratification of their own practice and continued; states that possessed none read it as a Delhi opinion they were free to note and shelve. The variable geometry that resulted, reservation entrenched in the south and west, reservation absent at the centre and across most of the north, became the standing condition of Indian politics for the rest of the century, and it was produced not by the report's rejection but by its distribution. Researchers tracing these replies work through bound volumes in state archives whose cataloguing varies from the meticulous to the improvised, a documentary fragility that mirrors the one [described for journalists who keep source material in protected, retrievable form](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), and the replies that survive repay the search, because they show state governments arguing about criteria, percentages and lists in 1956 and 1957 with a concreteness the centre had declined.

### What did Mysore and Madras do with the forwarded report?

Mysore and Madras each continued a system of backward classes reservation that predated the Kalelkar Commission by three decades: Mysore administered quotas descending from the Miller Committee of 1918, Madras quotas descending from the communal orders of the 1920s, and both states treated the forwarded report as confirmation of their own lists rather than as an instruction to begin again.

Mysore's trajectory is the longest in India. The Miller Committee, appointed by the Maharaja's government in 1918 under Sir Leslie Miller, had found the public service of the princely state monopolised by one small group and recommended preferential recruitment for the rest, and Mysore had operated orders favouring the backward classes since 1921. The Kalelkar list for Mysore therefore entered a state that possessed its own list, its own definition and its own politics of the question; the state government's response was to continue its arrangements, and when the system's legality was tested after the Constitution, Mysore appointed its own committee under T. Nagan Gowda in 1960 to revise the list, producing the order of 1962 that the Supreme Court struck down in Balaji the following year for reserving 68 percent and for treating caste as the sole mark of backwardness. The line from Kalelkar through Nagan Gowda to Balaji is direct: the forwarded report kept the state machinery in motion, the machinery overshot, and the Court's answer in 1963 fixed the ceiling and the criterion rules within which the Mandal Commission would later have to work.

Madras presents the companion case. The Justice Party government had issued the first communal order in 1921 and enlarged it in 1927, distributing government posts among named groups in fixed proportions; the Madras of the 1950s was thus the one major state where backward classes reservation was older than the Indian republic. The Supreme Court's Champakam judgment of April 1951 had struck the communal order down as offending Article 29(2), and the First Amendment's Article 15(4) had restored the state's power in terms; the Kamaraj government of the mid-1950s administered its own list and its own percentages, and the Kalelkar report, on arrival, was absorbed into that running practice rather than implemented as a new scheme. Tamil politics after Kamaraj, through the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam governments from 1967, expanded the state system on its own foundations, appointment of the Sattanathan Commission in 1969 and progressive increases in the backward classes share. The Kalelkar Commission's Madras chapters, built on the testimony of the most organised backward classes movement in India, were the strongest in the report; the state's reply was, in substance, that it had been doing this since before the Commission's chairman entered the Rajya Sabha, and intended to go on.

Bombay, Bihar and the north complete the sorting. Bombay State, heir to the non-Brahmin politics of the Kolhapur and Satara tradition and to its own pre-Independence lists, maintained backward classes provisions of its own and, after the bifurcation of 1960, bequeathed them to Maharashtra and Gujarat in different measures. Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, the two states whose backward castes were demographically weightiest, took no implementing action on the forwarded report at all in the 1950s; Bihar moved only in the 1970s, when Karpoori Thakur's government appointed the Mungeri Lal Commission in 1971 and implemented its recommendations, with a sub-quota for the most backward, in 1978, while Uttar Pradesh waited longer still. Madhya Pradesh likewise let the file rest. The map that results is the one Jaffrelot's India's Silent Revolution places at the centre of its argument: a south and west where backward classes politics had institutional depth before 1953, and a north where the Kalelkar report was the first knock on a door that stayed shut until the Mandal decade. The forwarding decision of 1956 did not create that map, since the southern systems predated it; but it licensed the map's persistence for a generation, by renouncing the one instrument, a central list with central services reservation, that might have levelled it.

## The Long Abeyance: 1956 to 1979

Between the forwarding of the report in 1956 and the appointment of the second commission on 1 January 1979, the Union of India made no central list, reserved no central post and appointed no successor body under Article 340. The twenty-three years are often written as a blank, and for the centre's backward classes policy the description is fair. But the blank was filled at the edges by two forces that determined what the second commission would find when it came: the state systems, expanding and litigating in the south and west, and the Supreme Court, which between 1963 and the Mandal appointment converted the open questions of 1955 into a body of doctrine. The abeyance was therefore not empty time. It was the period in which the arena for 1980 was built, and its two builders, the state commissions and the Court, both worked with materials the Kalelkar episode had supplied: the state systems descended from the forwarding decision, the doctrine from the litigation those systems generated.

### What did the Supreme Court decide in Balaji v. State of Mysore?

In Balaji v. State of Mysore, decided in 1962 and reported in 1963, the Supreme Court struck down Mysore's order reserving 68 percent of seats, held that caste is a relevant factor but cannot be the sole criterion of backwardness, and indicated that reservations should ordinarily remain below 50 percent, fixing the doctrinal ceiling later commissions inherited.

The Balaji doctrine reached directly back into the Kalelkar file. Mysore's order had classified backwardness largely by caste, on a list whose ancestry ran through the state's response to the forwarded report and the Nagan Gowda Committee; the Court's insistence that caste, though relevant, could not be the only test, and that occupation, income and habitation must count, restated in constitutional language the objection Kalelkar's forwarding letter and the five dissenting members had made in political language seven years earlier. The state commissions of the following years absorbed the lesson and multiplied their criteria: Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Bihar each appointed backward classes commissions or committees between the 1960s and the late 1970s, each publishing lists, tests and percentages that anticipated the Mandal method. The Kaka Kalelkar Commission had been the first body to attempt an all-India list; by the 1970s a shelf of state lists existed, built by governments that had received the forwarded report or grown from the systems it ratified, and the second central commission inherited them as its working library. The centre's silence and the states' industry are two halves of one story, and the halves join at the forwarding decision of September 1956.

The state commissions of the abeyance years deserve their dates in one place, because their sequence is the proof that the subject lived where the forwarding had sent it. Tamil Nadu appointed the Sattanathan Commission in 1969, whose report of 1970 re-founded the state's lists and percentages for the Dravidian era. Kerala appointed the commission under Nettur P. Damodaran in 1970. Andhra Pradesh appointed the Anantaraman Commission in 1970, whose work governed the state's lists through the decade. Karnataka appointed the Havanur Commission in 1972, which reported in 1975 after the most elaborate state-level inquiry since Kalelkar, and whose classifications the state adopted in 1977. Bihar appointed the Mungeri Lal Commission in 1971, reported in 1976, implemented in 1978. Five major state inquiries in nine years, each with tours, tests and lists in the Kalelkar manner, each citing the unfinished central business as part of its warrant: this was the real legislative history of backward classes policy in the 1970s, enacted in the states while the centre's file slept. When the second central commission was appointed in 1979 it did not restart a subject. It federalised, upward, a going concern, and its members knew the state reports as practitioners know precedents.

The centre returned to the subject once more before falling silent, and the return confirmed the doctrine. In 1961 the Union Home Ministry advised the state governments, in the correspondence the Mandal Commission later printed in its own historical chapters, that it would be better for the states to specify the backward classes by an economic criterion rather than by caste, and that the centre would prepare no list of its own. Nehru's letter to the chief ministers of June 1961, with its declaration of dislike for reservation on communal and caste grounds and its preference for help on economic considerations, gave the advice its political signature. The advice had no legal force over the states, and the states with working systems ignored it in substance, continuing to specify by caste under their own lists; but it fixed the centre's posture for the remainder of the abeyance, and it handed the future a clean statement of the anti-caste-criterion position at the highest level, to be answered, eventually, by a commission whose chairman held the opposite conviction as a matter of biography. The argument of 1955 thus remained fully joined and fully documented on both sides throughout the silence, which is one more reason the silence cannot be read as the question's death.

Politics, meanwhile, was quietly re-pricing the question. The general elections of 1967 broke the Congress monopoly in half the states; the socialist tradition of Rammanohar Lohia, who had demanded preferential opportunities for the backward castes since the 1950s and whose followers staffed the state movements of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, moved from pamphlet to ministry in the coalition decade. Charan Singh in Uttar Pradesh and Karpoori Thakur in Bihar built careers on the political weight of the cultivating backward castes, the same demographic the Kalelkar list had counted at roughly a third of the nation. When the Janata Party came to power at the centre in March 1977 on a coalition in which the backward caste parties of the north were load-bearing members, its election promises included the appointment of a commission for the backward classes. The promise was redeemed on 1 January 1979, when the President appointed the second Backward Classes Commission under the same Article 340, with B. P. Mandal, former chief minister of Bihar, in the chair Kalelkar had occupied. The twenty-three years end, then, not with a change of constitutional instrument but with a change of coalition: the file the Cabinet of 1956 left unfinished was picked up by a government whose majority required the votes the file concerned.

## The Conditions Kalelkar Built for Mandal

The strongest test of the institutional-substantive reading is genealogical: lay the Mandal Commission's work beside the Kalelkar Commission's and ask what the second took from the first. The answer, on the documents, is nearly everything except the conclusions. The Mandal Commission's terms of reference repeated the structure of 1953: determine the criteria, identify the classes, examine their representation in the services, recommend the steps. Its field method repeated 1953 at larger scale: tours of every state, public sittings, testimony from associations and officials, questionnaires, and the compilation of state-by-state lists. Its central methodological problem was Kalelkar's in person: whether caste could serve as the unit and starting point of identification, the very proposition the chairman's forwarding letter had renounced and the Cabinet's memorandum had rejected as perpetuating caste. And its political problem was the memorandum's other legacy: a centre that had spent twenty-three years without a list, confronting state systems that had run ahead in divergent directions. The conditions map set out below, labelled Table 1 for citation, organises the inheritance under the three heads this article has used throughout: method, architecture and politics. It is the findable core of the argument, and readers who cite one element of this article will cite it.

The prose around Table 1 states the claim the table records. On method, the Kalelkar Commission settled that identification proceeds by group and by combined tests, and bequeathed the unsettled question whether caste may anchor the tests; Mandal resolved the question by keeping the caste as the starting point and surrounding it with eleven indicators, four social, three educational and four economic, weighted so that the social indicators counted most, an arrangement whose logic, social standing first because the rest flows from it, is the Kalelkar diagnosis with arithmetic attached. On architecture, Kalelkar proved that a presidential commission could enumerate the backward classes of the whole Union and publish a defensible list, 2,399 castes on 1931 data; Mandal, with the 1931 census again the last caste count available to it, produced 3,743 castes and a population estimate of 52 percent, and the similarity of the two counts, separated by twenty-five years of supposed methodological revolution, measures how much of the second exercise was the first repeated with better staff work. On politics, Kalelkar's fate fixed the two poles between which Mandal's fate would be decided: a chairman's document that could disown a report from inside, which Mandal avoided by holding his commission to a signed, unanimous-enough text submitted on 31 December 1980, and a cabinet memorandum that could defer by demanding better criteria, which the governments of 1980 to 1989 attempted for a decade before V. P. Singh implemented the report in August 1990. The separate study of the Mandal Commission report itself follows in this series and is not anticipated here; what belongs to the Kalelkar article is the demonstration that Mandal's questions were set in 1955 and 1956, by a chairman who doubted and a cabinet that deferred.

Table 1: The Pre-Mandal Conditions Map, recording what the Kalelkar Commission contributed and what the Mandal Commission inherited from each contribution.

| Kalelkar contribution | What the Commission settled, 1953 to 1956 | Condition inherited by the Mandal Commission, 1979 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Group identification by combined tests | Backwardness assessed for castes as wholes under four tests: standing, schooling, services, trade | The standing question whether caste may anchor identification, answered by Mandal with caste as the starting point among eleven weighted indicators |
| All-India enumeration | A published list of 2,399 backward castes, 837 starred as most backward, built on the 1931 census baseline | Proof that an all-India list could be compiled and defended, and the same 1931 baseline problem for the count of 3,743 castes |

Read down the third column, the conditions are not influences in the vague sense of ideas in the air. They are the specific, dated constraints inside which the Mandal Commission's choices were made, and several of them, the 1931 data baseline, the state systems' head start, the open criteria question, were constraints the Kalelkar episode had created rather than merely revealed. That is what institutional substance means when it is claimed for a failed body: not that the body prevailed, but that the terrain on which its successors fought was terrain it had made. The brief-mention reading cannot see the terrain because it reads only outcomes. The failure reading sees the terrain and attributes it to the victors, to southern movements and northern coalitions. The documents assign the making of it, in the first instance, to the commission of 1953 to 1955 and to the two texts, letter and memorandum, with which its story closes.

## The Report as Authority: How a Shelved Text Stayed in Use

A shelved report ordinarily dies the death of shelved reports: it is cited for a season by the disappointed, and then it is furniture. The Kalelkar report escaped that death, and the manner of its survival is the empirical heart of the institutional-substantive reading, because a text that later authorities must use is a text that did institutional work regardless of the fate of its recommendations. The report survived in three distinct repositories. The first was the state commission shelf. Between 1956 and 1979 the governments of Mysore and Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra and Bihar each appointed commissions or committees on the backward classes, and their reports, Nagan Gowda in Mysore, Sattanathan in Tamil Nadu, Mungeri Lal in Bihar and their fellows, open with the same ritual: a historical chapter that begins with Article 340, summarises the Kalelkar criteria and lists, records the forwarding of 1956, and then positions the state inquiry as the continuation of the unfinished central one. The ritual was not decorative. State commissions borrowed the four tests, adapted them to state data, and defended their percentages against the ancestry the Kalelkar figures supplied; when Bihar's Mungeri Lal Commission reported in 1976 and Karpoori Thakur's government implemented its scheme in 1978, with a protected share for the most backward among the backward, the starred 837 of the Kalelkar list were the conceptual grandparent of the sub-classification.

The second repository was the courtroom. Litigation over the state systems kept the report in forensic circulation: the Mysore order that fell in Balaji had been defended with commission materials in the Kalelkar line, and the Supreme Court's backward classes jurisprudence from Balaji through the cases of the 1960s and 1970s repeatedly rehearses the criteria question in the form 1955 had given it, caste as factor versus caste as sole test, group versus household, percentage against population. When the nine judges of Indra Sawhney surveyed the field in 1992, their historical narrative begins where this article begins, with Article 340 and the first commission, and treats the Kalelkar report and its fate as the opening chapter of the law's own story. A document that the Constitution Bench uses to organise forty years of doctrine is not a dead document. The third repository was scholarship. Galanter's Competing Equalities of 1984, the first full scholarly reconstruction, made the Kalelkar episode the type case of compensatory discrimination's Indian career, and Jaffrelot's India's Silent Revolution of 2003 made it the type case of Congress reluctance; the two readings disagree about everything except the premise that 1953 to 1956 is where an explanation of Indian reservation must start. Sudhakar Yedla's essays on the pre-Mandal decades work the same seam from the movement side. When the Mandal Commission's researchers assembled their historical chapters in 1979 and 1980, they were annotating a text that courts, commissions and scholars had kept in continuous use for twenty-five years. The shelf, it turned out, had been a slow form of circulation.

## The Long Arc: From the Shelved Report to Indra Sawhney

The trajectory from the Kalelkar report to the implementation of Mandal spans thirty-five years and four dated stations, and it is worth tracing in one line because each station answered the station before. The report of 30 March 1955 proposed; the memorandum of September 1956 deferred; the second commission, appointed 1 January 1979, reproposed on Kalelkar's architecture with Kalelkar's question refined; the report of 31 December 1980 waited a further decade in the same posture of official non-decision that had consumed its predecessor, until the prime minister who finally acted, V. P. Singh, announced on 7 August 1990 that the Mandal recommendations would be implemented for the services of the Union, beginning with 27 percent reservation for the other backward classes. The announcement detonated the largest protest movement of the decade, the anti-Mandal agitation of the autumn of 1990, and carried the question into the Supreme Court, where a nine-judge bench in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India, decided on 16 November 1992, upheld the 27 percent, constitutionalised the creamy layer exclusion, confirmed the ceiling of 50 percent that Balaji had indicated, and accepted caste as a permissible starting point for the identification of a backward class. Every element of the 1992 settlement has a Kalelkar coordinate. The permissible starting point answers the forwarding letter and the memorandum, against them. The ceiling answers the percentages of 1955, which had been framed in a world without one. The creamy layer answers, in part, the objection the dissenting members pressed in 1955, that a caste list without internal differentiation would deliver its benefits to the advanced sections of the backward groups.

The arc also exposes the asymmetry that the failure reading gets right and then misreads. Twice, a commission's report went to a government that had commissioned it; twice, the government's first instinct was delay; the difference between 1956 and 1990 lay not in the documents but in the coalitions holding them. The Congress of 1956 could defer at no electoral cost, because the backward castes of the north had no party of their own and voted inside the Congress system the party commanded. The governments of 1980 to 1989, Congress and then Janata Dal's predecessors alike, deferred at rising cost, and V. P. Singh acted, on his own account and on the account of his critics, when the arithmetic of his coalition made action cheaper than delay. The lesson the arc teaches about commissions is uncomfortable for admirers of the form: a commission does not move a government. A commission equips a government that politics has already moved, and dis-equips the excuses of one that has not. Kalelkar's commission equipped nobody in 1956 because nobody in office wished to be equipped; its equipment, the tests, the list, the method, lay in store until 1979, when a government arrived that wanted it. To call the commission a failure because the storehouse stood shut for twenty-three years is to blame the equipment for the absence of the customer, and the adjudication that follows returns to that error with the readings laid side by side.

## Three Readings, One Verdict

The brief-mention reading can be dismissed on the evidence of bulk alone. A commission that sat for two years, heard a nation's associations, listed 2,399 castes, proposed the highest reservation percentages in Indian history and provoked a chairman's recantation and a cabinet memorandum is not a footnote to the Mandal Commission; it is the first volume of the story of which Mandal is the second. The textbook timeline that gives Kalelkar a sentence commits the same error as the politician who gives it a shrug: it confuses the absence of implementation with the absence of event. Set against [the three-reading adjudication framework](/2020/02/14/mandal-decisive-moment-indian-politics/) that this pillar applies to the Mandal moment itself, the brief-mention reading fails the first test of adjudication, which is that a reading must explain the documents that survive. The documents survive in embarrassing quantity.

The political-failure reading is the serious rival, and it should be stated at its strongest before it is answered. On this reading, the episode shows a dominant-party government making a constitutional gesture it never intended to honour: appointing a Gandhian elder certain to soften the blow, receiving a report whose own chairman disowned its foundation, and shelving the whole under cover of a criteria argument it had no intention of resolving, while the southern states, driven by their own movements, did the real work of backward classes advancement. Jaffrelot's India's Silent Revolution gives this reading its scholarly spine: the Congress, dependent on upper-caste leadership in the Hindi belt, systematically avoided empowering the backward castes of the north, and the shelving of Kalelkar is his exhibit for the 1950s, the moment the silent revolution was postponed at the centre for a generation. Much of this is true, and this article has recorded the same facts: the choice of forum in 1956 was a choice of burial ground for the northern claim, and Nehru's doctrine was settled long before the report arrived. Where the failure reading overreaches is in its conclusion that the Commission therefore accomplished nothing institutionally. That conclusion confuses the government's verdict on the report with the report's effect on the system. The state sorting followed the forwarding, not a rejection; the Balaji doctrine answered state systems the forwarding had kept in motion; the Mandal template copied the Kalelkar architecture line by line; and the criteria argument, invented as a deferral device in 1956, became the genuine research programme that produced the eleven indicators of 1980. A device invented to bury a question can still organise the work of the generation that unearths it, and a reading that calls the whole episode failure has no vocabulary for that sequence.

The institutional-substantive reading, defended here, absorbs the failure reading's facts and reverses its bookkeeping. Institutions are substantive when they set the terms on which later contests occur, whether or not they win the contest of their own day. By that measure the Kalelkar Commission is one of the consequential bodies of the Nehru years: it fixed the unit of analysis as the caste-group tested by combined criteria; it fixed the commission under Article 340 as the instrument, with tours, sittings and lists; it fixed, through the forwarding letter and the memorandum, the standing objection, that caste-based specification perpetuates caste, which every later defence of reservation has had to answer and which Indra Sawhney finally answered in law; and it fixed, through the forwarding to the states, the federal distribution of the subject that still governs Indian reservation, state lists beside a central list, state percentages beside a central one. The complication pressed hardest against this reading deserves its answer in the open: if the Commission was so substantive, why did central backward classes policy stand empty for twenty-three years? The answer distinguishes, as this article has throughout, the implementation trajectory from the contribution trajectory. The centre implemented nothing because the centre's ruling coalition lost nothing by implementing nothing; the contribution proceeded regardless, in the state systems the forwarding licensed, in the doctrine the litigation of those systems provoked, and in the file that awaited the coalition of 1979. Contribution without implementation is an unstable inheritance, and it can decay; the Kalelkar inheritance very nearly did. That it survived to be claimed by Mandal is the contingent fact at the heart of the story, and contingency is not the same as nullity. The claim stands as the brief for this article stated it: the Kalelkar Commission was institutionally substantive even in political deferral, and the conditions it established shaped the Mandal Commission that followed it, in method, in architecture and in the politics of the centre it confronted. The first Backward Classes Commission did not get its report implemented. It got something slower and, in the end, nearly as large: it wrote the questions in which India still conducts the argument.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Kalelkar Commission?

The Kalelkar Commission was the first Backward Classes Commission appointed under Article 340 of the Indian Constitution. The President appointed it on 29 January 1953, by Ministry of Home Affairs Notification No. 70/53-Public, under the chairmanship of Kaka Kalelkar, the Gandhian writer and Rajya Sabha member. It was directed to determine the criteria by which sections of the people should be treated as socially and educationally backward classes, to prepare a list of those classes, to investigate their conditions, and to recommend steps by the Union or the states to improve them. After two years of tours and hearings it presented its report on 30 March 1955, identifying 2,399 castes as backward and recommending extensive reservation in education and services. The Union Cabinet declined central implementation in September 1956 and forwarded the report to the state governments.

### Q: Who was Kaka Kalelkar?

Kaka Kalelkar was the public name of Dattatreya Balkrishna Kalelkar, born at Satara on 1 December 1885 and educated at Fergusson College in Pune. He met Mahatma Gandhi in 1915, lived and taught at the Sabarmati Ashram, helped build Gujarat Vidyapith, whose vice-chancellor he was from 1928 to 1935, and wrote prolifically in Gujarati, Marathi and Hindi on education, language and the national movement, earning Gandhi's description of him as a Gujarati and a quarter more. He sat in the Rajya Sabha from April 1952 to April 1964. In January 1953 the President appointed him chairman of the first Backward Classes Commission, the office that defines his place in the history of reservation. His forwarding letter of March 1955, doubting the caste criterion of his own commission's report, remains one of the most argued documents in that history. He died in New Delhi on 21 August 1981, aged ninety-five.

### Q: When was the first Backward Classes Commission?

The first Backward Classes Commission was appointed on 29 January 1953, when the President, acting under Article 340 of the Constitution, issued the order constituting the body chaired by Kaka Kalelkar, notified the same day as Ministry of Home Affairs Notification No. 70/53-Public. The Commission held its sittings and tours from February 1953 to December 1954 and presented its report to the President on 30 March 1955. The interval matters for reading the episode: three years of the Constitution's operation passed before Article 340 was first used, and the appointment followed the First Amendment of 1951, which had inserted Article 15(4) and made special provision for backward classes lawful in education. No second commission under Article 340 was appointed until 1 January 1979, when the Mandal Commission succeeded to the same office twenty-six years later.

### Q: Why was the Kalelkar report deferred?

The report was deferred because the Union Cabinet, considering it through 1955 and 1956, declined to draw up an all-India list or reserve central posts, and instead forwarded the report to the state governments for such action as each chose to take. The memorandum of action taken, laid before Parliament with the report in September 1956, gave the reasons: the caste system was itself the hindrance to an egalitarian society, recognition of specified castes as backward might perpetuate caste distinctions, the Commission had not been unanimous, and workable criteria for specifying the backward classes had yet to be devised. Two documents made deferral easy. Kalelkar's forwarding letter had doubted the report's caste foundation from the chairman's own desk, and five members had recorded dissents. Behind the reasons stood Jawaharlal Nehru's settled doctrine that help should be organised on economic considerations rather than caste, restated in his letter to chief ministers in June 1961.

### Q: What did Kalelkar's dissenting note say?

Kalelkar's note was the forwarding letter he sent to the President with the signed report of 30 March 1955, often called his dissenting addendum, though he recorded no formal minute of dissent. In it he stated that, on reflection, he had come to doubt the wisdom of founding remedies on caste: to specify castes as backward and attach state benefits to the specification risked perpetuating and hardening the caste distinctions the Constitution sought to dissolve; caste associations would organise around the list; and assistance should instead reach the poor and the unschooled on tests of economic condition, occupation and educational want. He questioned the readiness with which the majority had treated caste as the unit of backwardness and expressed unease at the scale of the reservations proposed. The letter's effect was institutional as much as intellectual: a report publicly doubted by its own chairman arrived before the Cabinet with its authority already spent, and the government's memorandum of 1956 used the disarray it described.

### Q: How is Kalelkar related to Mandal?

The Kalelkar Commission is the Mandal Commission's predecessor in office, method and argument. Both were appointed by the President under Article 340, Kalelkar's on 29 January 1953 and B. P. Mandal's on 1 January 1979, with materially parallel terms of reference: determine the criteria of backwardness, identify the classes, and recommend the steps for their advancement. The Mandal Commission worked through the Kalelkar record, its four tests, its state-by-state lists and its population reasoning, and recast the tests as eleven weighted indicators, retaining caste as the starting point that Kalelkar's forwarding letter had renounced and the Cabinet memorandum of 1956 had rejected. Where Kalelkar proposed percentages drawn from population share, including 70 percent in technical education, Mandal, bound by the ceiling the Supreme Court had indicated in Balaji, recommended 27 percent in services. The relation is therefore one of inheritance under correction: Mandal answered, point by point, the objections that had buried Kalelkar.

### Q: What is Article 340?

Article 340 is the provision of the Indian Constitution that empowers the President to appoint a commission to investigate the conditions of socially and educationally backward classes within the territory of India and the difficulties under which they labour. The commission must report the facts it finds and recommend the steps the Union or any state should take to remove those difficulties and improve the classes' condition, including the grants to be made and the conditions on which they should be given. The President must cause the report, together with a memorandum explaining the action taken on it, to be laid before each House of Parliament. Article 340 names a condition but no group suffering it, which is why every commission appointed under it has had to decide the contested question of identification for itself. It has been used twice: for the Kalelkar Commission in 1953 and for the Mandal Commission in 1979.

### Q: What were the terms of reference of the Kalelkar Commission?

The terms of reference, set out in the appointment notification of 29 January 1953, charged the Commission with four tasks in a deliberate order. It was first to determine the criteria to be adopted in considering whether any sections of the people of India should be treated as socially and educationally backward classes. Second, in accordance with those criteria, it was to prepare a list of the classes so identified. Third, it was to investigate the conditions of those classes and the difficulties under which they labour. Fourth, it was to recommend the steps that should be taken by the Union or by any state to remove the difficulties and improve their condition, including the grants that should be made and the conditions subject to which they should be given. The order of the tasks shaped the report and its controversy: because criteria came before the list, the Commission's decision that caste would be the unit of identification committed it before a single caste had been counted.

### Q: Who were the members of the Kalelkar Commission?

The Commission appointed on 29 January 1953 had eleven members under Kaka Kalelkar as chairman. The notification named the parliamentarians Narayan Sadoba Kajrolkar, Bheekha Bhai, Rajeshwar Patel, Atma Singh Namdhari and N. R. M. Swamy; Shivdayal Singh Chaurasia and Lala Jagannath from public life; Abdul Qaiyum Ansari of the Bihar legislature and T. Mariappa of the Mysore legislature; and Arunangshu De, who served as member-secretary. The membership changed during the inquiry. Atma Singh Namdhari died on 7 January 1954 and Anup Singh was nominated in his place on 27 February 1954. Lala Jagannath resigned to take up another appointment and P. G. Shah was appointed in his place with effect from 2 August 1954. The composition mixed Parliament, two state legislatures and the secretariat, and included members from regions where backward classes reservation already operated as working law. The body's internal divisions, five members recording dissents and the chairman qualifying the report in his forwarding letter, ran through a membership drawn from the politics it was asked to judge.

### Q: What tests did the Kalelkar Commission use to judge backwardness?

The Commission applied four tests in combination, stated in the report of 30 March 1955. The first was low position in the traditional caste hierarchy of Hindu society, the ritual standing of the group in the local order of precedence. The second was lack of general educational advancement among the major section of the caste or group, judged against the schooling and literacy returns available to the inquiry. The third was inadequate or no representation in government service. The fourth was inadequate representation in the fields of trade, commerce and industry. A group failing across the tests was listed as backward; borderline cases, where a caste of low standing had advanced in school and office, were argued at length inside the Commission. Two features drew lasting criticism. The tests were framed on the Hindu hierarchy although the Commission listed Muslim, Christian and Sikh groups that met them in substance, and the unit of judgment was the caste as a whole, so advancement by one section did not remove the group from the list.

### Q: How many castes did the Kalelkar Commission find backward?

The report of 30 March 1955 listed 2,399 castes and communities as socially and educationally backward, and starred 837 of them as most backward, the groups whose condition the Commission judged to require priority in any scheme of assistance. On the report's estimate, worked from the proportions of the census of 1931, the listed classes together covered roughly 32 percent of the population of India. The figures were compiled state by state from the evidence taken on tour, from state education and service returns, and from the 1931 enumeration, the last census to count caste across the whole country. The evidential basis was therefore old, and the Commission said so in print: it treated its list as a first survey to be revised as fresh counting became possible, and it recommended that the census of 1961 resume caste-wise enumeration. The Mandal Commission, working a generation later from the same 1931 skeleton, listed 3,743 castes and estimated the backward classes at 52 percent of the population.

### Q: What reservation did the Kalelkar Commission propose for technical and professional education?

The report of 30 March 1955 recommended reserving 70 percent of seats in all technical and professional institutions for qualified students belonging to the backward classes. The figure is the highest education quota proposed by any Indian commission on the subject, and it followed the report's own logic: the listed classes formed roughly a third of the population on the conservative 1931 proportions, their presence in the technical and professional institutions was close to negligible, and the Commission treated those institutions as the gate through which the services and the professions were entered. Beside the quota stood the supporting apparatus of scholarships, hostels and expanded schooling in the tracts where the listed castes lived. The recommendation was never implemented by the Union, and no later body repeated it: the Mandal Commission, working under the ceiling the Supreme Court had indicated in Balaji v. State of Mysore, confined its education and service proposals to 27 percent. The distance between 70 percent in 1955 and 27 percent in 1980 measures the doctrinal narrowing that the intervening litigation imposed.

### Q: Did the Kalelkar Commission recommend reservation in government services?

Yes. The report of 30 March 1955 recommended minimum reservation of vacancies in all government services and local bodies for the backward classes on a graded scale: 25 percent in Class I posts, 33 and a third percent in Class II posts, and 40 percent in Class III and Class IV posts. The grading followed the Commission's reasoning about representation: the higher services were the most completely closed to the listed classes, and the scale was presented as a minimum, open to states whose conditions justified more. The recommendation sat within the permission Article 16(4) had contained since 1950, which allows reservation for any backward class of citizens not adequately represented in the services; what the Commission supplied was the identification that the article presupposed. The Union government declined to adopt the scale for its own services in the memorandum of September 1956, deciding that no reservation would be made in central services for any group of backward classes. Several states, administering their own systems, applied scales of their own.

### Q: Why did five members of the Kalelkar Commission record dissent?

Five members recorded minutes of dissent against the main findings of the report because they rejected, in varying combinations, the linkage the majority had made between backwardness and caste, and the reservation of posts on a caste basis that followed from it. Their objections, as the report's appendices and the government's memorandum of 1956 record them, ran along three lines. Caste as the unit of identification would perpetuate the distinctions the Constitution sought to dissolve, converting an instrument of remedy into a permanent register of rank. The benefits of a caste list would be captured by the advanced sections of the listed groups, leaving the most backward where they were. And tests of economic condition, occupation and educational want, applied to families rather than to birth-groups, would identify disadvantage more honestly. The dissents mattered beyond their arguments. Together with Kalelkar's forwarding letter, they allowed the Cabinet to present its deferral of 1956 as fidelity to the Commission's own doubts, rather than as a refusal of the Commission's findings, and they fixed the terms in which the criteria argument would be conducted for the next generation.

### Q: What did the government memorandum of 1956 say about the Kalelkar report?

The memorandum explaining the action taken, laid before Parliament with the report in September 1956 as Article 340 requires, accepted the existence of backwardness and the state's duty toward it, and rejected the report's instrument. It argued that the caste system was the greatest hindrance in the way of progress toward an egalitarian society, and that the recognition of specified castes as backward might serve to maintain and even perpetuate the existing distinctions on the basis of caste. It recorded the Commission's lack of unanimity, the chairman's forwarding letter and the members' dissents among the findings' deficiencies. It held that workable criteria for specifying the socially and educationally backward classes still had to be devised and further investigation undertaken. The operative decisions followed from the critique: no all-India list of backward classes would be drawn up, no reservation would be made in the services of the central government for any such group, and the report would be forwarded to the state governments for their consideration and action.

### Q: How did Mysore respond to the Kalelkar Commission report?

Mysore responded to the forwarded report of 1956 by continuing, and then revising, a system of backward classes reservation that was older than the republic. The princely state had acted on the Miller Committee's report of 1919 with orders from 1921 distributing recruitment beyond the small literate group that had monopolised its higher services, and the state possessed its own list, its own definitions and a serving member of the Kalelkar Commission itself in T. Mariappa of the Mysore legislature. The Kalelkar list for Mysore therefore entered a running system rather than creating one. When the state's arrangements required revision under the Constitution, Mysore appointed the committee under T. Nagan Gowda in 1960, and the order that followed in 1962 reserved 68 percent of seats and posts. The Supreme Court struck that order down in Balaji v. State of Mysore, holding caste relevant but not a sole criterion and indicating a ceiling below 50 percent. Mysore's reply to Kalelkar was thus the most consequential of any state's: continuation, expansion, and a judgment that fixed national doctrine.

### Q: How did Madras State treat backward classes reservation in the 1950s?

Madras treated backward classes reservation in the 1950s as settled state practice to be defended, not as a proposal to be considered. The state's communal order, first issued in 1921 under the Justice Party and enlarged in 1927, had distributed government posts and seats among named groups for a generation before the Kalelkar Commission sat. The Supreme Court's decision in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan on 9 April 1951 struck the order down under the Constitution as enacted, and the First Amendment's Article 15(4), passed in June 1951, restored the state's power to make special provision for backward classes. The government of Madras in the mid-1950s, under K. Kamaraj from 1954, administered its own list and percentages through the decade. When the Kalelkar report was forwarded to the states in 1956, Madras absorbed it into that running practice. The state's backward classes movement was the most organised in India, its testimony had filled the Commission's strongest chapters, and its reply in substance was that the work the report recommended had been under way in Madras since before the Commission's chairman entered Parliament.

### Q: What happened to backward classes commissions between Kalelkar and Mandal?

Between the forwarding of the Kalelkar report in 1956 and the appointment of the Mandal Commission on 1 January 1979, the Union appointed no commission under Article 340, prepared no central list and reserved no central posts, while the states conducted the inquiry tradition on their own account. Tamil Nadu appointed the Sattanathan Commission in 1969. Kerala appointed the commission under Nettur P. Damodaran in 1970, and Andhra Pradesh the Anantaraman Commission in the same year. Karnataka appointed the Havanur Commission in 1972, which reported in 1975 in the most elaborate state inquiry since Kalelkar. Bihar appointed the Mungeri Lal Commission in 1971, whose recommendations Karpoori Thakur's government implemented in 1978 with a protected share for the most backward. Each body used the Kalelkar manner of working, tours, tests and lists, and several cited the unfinished central business of 1956 among their warrants. The second central commission therefore inherited a shelf of state reports and a generation of state practice, and its task was less to begin the subject than to gather it.

### Q: What did the Kalelkar Commission recommend for the 1961 census?

Among its principal recommendations, the report of 30 March 1955 proposed that the census of 1961 undertake caste-wise enumeration of the population. The recommendation followed from the Commission's own evidential difficulties. The census had counted caste from the nineteenth century until 1931; the count was dropped in 1941 and the republic's census of 1951 enumerated only the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The Commission had therefore been obliged to build its list of 2,399 castes on the proportions of 1931, dressed in state returns and testimony, and it said plainly that identification should proceed from counted fact rather than from a twenty-year-old residue. A caste count in 1961 would have given the review and revision of the lists, which the report also proposed, a factual basis, and would have settled by enumeration the population share of the backward classes that the report could only estimate at roughly 32 percent. The recommendation was not accepted. The census of 1961, like every Indian census after it in the period this article covers, counted no caste beyond the scheduled categories, and the Mandal Commission in 1980 inherited the same 1931 skeleton.

### Q: Why did the Kalelkar Commission treat all women as a backward class?

The Commission included among its principal recommendations the treatment of all women as a class as backward. The reasoning recorded in the report was that the disabilities attaching to sex in Indian society cut across the caste hierarchy and compounded those of caste for the women of the listed groups: exclusion from schooling, from property in practice, from the services and from public life bore on women of every rank, and bore hardest where low caste standing and female standing combined. Treating women as a backward class for the purposes of its scheme allowed the Commission to direct educational and welfare provision toward them without forcing the claim through the caste tests, which measured the standing of groups in a hierarchy that ranked men. The recommendation also reveals the breadth of the report's conception of backwardness, which ran beyond the quota chapters into land, literacy, housing and health. It was not implemented by the Union, and later commissions did not adopt the device in the same form, but it stands in the report as evidence that the first commission understood disadvantage as layered, and said so in 1955.
