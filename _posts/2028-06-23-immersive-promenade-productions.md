Immersive and promenade productions of Romeo and Juliet ask the oldest question in the play's staging history in a new form: where should the audience stand? The conventional answer, hardened over three centuries of proscenium practice, seats playgoers apart from Verona, facing it across a lit divide. The promenade answer puts them inside Verona instead, walking its streets behind the actors, crowding its ballroom, and gathering at last around its tomb. The verdict of the documented record is narrower than the mode's publicity suggests. Promenade staging, in which a guided audience follows the whole play from place to place, recovers something the text was built for: a crowd that is itself part of the fiction, citizens of the city whose feud the play anatomizes. Free-roam immersion, in which spectators wander a world and assemble the story for themselves, recovers far less, because Romeo and Juliet is not a world to explore but a sequence of speeches to overhear. The productions that honour the play narrow the audience's freedom exactly where the play narrows to two voices; the productions that surrender the text to the environment become festivals around the play rather than performances of it.

That verdict rests on a distinction the word "immersive" habitually blurs, and the blurring matters more for this play than for any other in the repertory. No Shakespeare play divides so cleanly into public architecture and private architecture. Its street brawls, its masked ball, its market-place confrontations are crowd scenes in a strong sense: their meaning is partly made by whoever stands watching, which is why the Prince's rebuke lands on the crowd as much as on the combatants. Its orchard exchanges, its dawn parting, its potion soliloquy are chamber scenes in an equally strong sense: two voices, or one, engineered for undivided attention in a small acoustic. A staging mode that moves the audience strengthens the first architecture and taxes the second, every time, in every venue. Immersive Romeo and Juliet is therefore not a single experiment but a standing trade, and the trade can be priced scene by scene against the text.

## What Immersive and Promenade Staging Actually Means

Four different arrangements shelter under the one marketing word, and a serious survey has to separate them before judging any production. Promenade staging moves the whole audience together, on foot, following the actors from one playing place to the next, so that the company and its crowd share a single sequence of scenes in a single order. Free-roam immersive staging releases spectators individually into a built environment where scenes proceed simultaneously, and each spectator assembles a partial, personal version of the event. Site-specific staging fixes the audience in one found place whose real identity does interpretive work, a churchyard for a burial, a warehouse for a feud. In-the-round staging surrounds a fixed playing area with seated spectators. Only the first two arrangements move the audience through the fiction; the fourth does not move it at all, however often designers borrow the immersive label for it, and the third moves nobody but changes everything by address.

Josephine Machon, whose Immersive Theatres (2013) remains the form's standard map, defines the immersive event by the audience's placement within it: playgoers are removed from the usual set of rules and conventions of theatrical performance and set inside the world of the piece, where intimacy and immediacy are the prized effects. Gareth White's Audience Participation in Theatre (2013) adds the governing mechanism, the invitation: participation is never spontaneous, because the production designs the terms on which a spectator may act, speak, move or merely watch, and the aesthetic object includes those terms. Adam Alston's Beyond Immersive Theatre (2016) presses the uncomfortable corollary. The spectator whom immersive work celebrates as a productive participant, choosing, exploring and co-authoring, is also a consumer trained by the experience economy to value exactly that feeling of productive choice; the freedom is scripted, the risks are managed, and the terms of empowerment rarely stand up to scrutiny. The disagreement between Machon and Alston is the disagreement this article has to settle for one particular play. Machon measures what the form gives the senses; Alston measures what the form quietly withholds. Both measurements are accurate, and Romeo and Juliet, with its unmatched alternation of crowd and chamber, is the play best built to show where each applies.

### Is free-roam exploration the same thing as promenade?

No. Promenade keeps the audience together and the story in order: the crowd walks behind the actors and receives every scene whole. Free-roam exploration divides the audience and the story, releasing masked spectators to choose paths through simultaneous scenes, so that no spectator receives the whole work and scene order varies by person.

The distinction decides productions. A promenade Romeo and Juliet is a staging of the play, testable against the text line by line. A free-roam Romeo and Juliet is an environment derived from the play, testable against nothing but its own atmosphere, and the difference shows in the record: the form's most celebrated Shakespeare event, Punchdrunk's Sleep No More, dispensed with Macbeth's language almost entirely, because verbal drama cannot survive dispersal. Where the text itself is the event, the audience must be gathered. Where the world is the event, the text becomes one furnishing among many. Everything in the survey that follows sorts itself along that line.

The words themselves carry the history of the split. Promenade is the older term, borrowed from the promenade concerts at which audiences walked about while the music played, and it entered theatre vocabulary with its social meaning intact: a promenade audience is a gathering in motion, visible to itself, out for the evening as much as at the play. Immersive is the newer coinage, codified as a term of art by the scholarship of the 2010s, Machon's book chief among it, and its metaphor is not social but submarine: the spectator is to be submerged, surrounded, held under the element of the work. The two metaphors predict the two aesthetics. Promenade promises company; immersion promises surrender. This play, which is about company, about houses, feasts, streets and crowds, and about the failure of every attempt to submerge private feeling in public life, answers the first promise in its own language and refuses the second at its core. The lovers are the least immersed people in Verona: they are the two figures who insist on a private element, a room, a night, a name renounced, inside a city that offers them only membership. A staging that submerges its audience in Verona repeats the city's offer. A staging that walks its audience through Verona and then gathers it, apart, at the orchard and the tomb, repeats the lovers' refusal as well, and the refusal is where the tragedy lives.

## The Audience Stood Inside the Play Before the Seats Were Fixed

Promenade's deepest claim is historical, and the theatre historian Andrew Gurr, in Playgoing in Shakespeare's London, documented the condition it restores. At the Globe, a penny bought standing room in the open yard, where the groundlings pressed around three sides of a platform stage raised to about chest height, close enough for an actor's aside to be a confidence and a soliloquy a public address to identifiable neighbours. The audience was not inside the fiction as modern immersion understands it, since nobody mistook the yard for Verona, but neither was it sealed away in darkness: playgoers were visible to the actors, audible, mobile within their standing place, and part of the spectacle each other spectator watched. Robert Weimann's account of locus and platea names the dramaturgy that condition produced. The locus, the localized place upstage, held the represented scene; the platea, the open downstage area continuous with the yard, held the speaker who stepped out of the scene to address the crowd directly. Shakespeare's dramaturgy shuttles between the two, and Romeo and Juliet shuttles constantly.

The play's own frame announces that arrangement. The Chorus walks onto the platea and contracts with the standing crowd: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue, 1-3, in Jill Levenson's Oxford edition). The Chorus is a guide before guiding was a staging problem, a figure who belongs half to the fiction and half to the audience's side of it, and who promises to show the crowd a city. Every promenade production since has had to cast that function whether it kept the speeches or not: someone, actor, steward or usher, must lead the audience through Verona and tell it where to stand. The promenade Chorus is the one piece of immersive machinery Shakespeare actually built into the script.

Nor does the machinery retire after the Prologue. The Chorus returns at the head of the second act to re-brief a standing crowd, recapitulating the old foe, the new love and the difficulty of the lovers' meeting, and to promise the pattern of the action to come. The second Chorus is the original production's interval service: a guide re-forming an audience that has drifted, refreshed itself and lost the thread, and re-aiming its attention at a story grown more secret and more interior. Every promenade company reinvents the office, in steward announcements, in programme notes read on the walk, in a friar who lingers to gather stragglers in character. The reinvention is never acknowledged as quotation, but it is one, and it marks the limit of the form's novelty. Shakespeare's theatre already knew that a standing audience is a perishable assembly, that attention outdoors leaks, and that a play which begins in a street and ends in a tomb needs a voice that belongs to the crowd's side of the fiction and can call it back to order. The modern promenade's stewards, torches and whispered instructions are that voice in modern dress, and the plays' survival in the form depends, more than directors admit, on how well the office is filled.

The soliloquies belong to the same settlement. When Romeo steps forward with "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2), the line is aimed outward at hearers who share his concealment and his sightline; the orchard scene is written as overhearing, the audience stationed with Romeo below the window. When Mercutio conjures Queen Mab (1.4.53-95), the speech is a turn performed for a circle of companions and, through them, for the yard. The first audiences of this play experienced its most private moments as public confidences delivered across a few feet of shared daylight. The darkened auditorium, which later centuries invented, is the historical anomaly; the promenade audience, standing in light among the actors, is in that limited sense a restoration. The qualification matters, though. Gurr's groundlings stood still in one yard while the play's geography compressed itself onto a single platform; their mobility was the mobility of attention, not of feet. Promenade disperses across real ground what the Globe compressed into one stage, and pays for the dispersal in walking time, in regrouping, in audibility lost to distance and weather. Restoration of the audience's standing condition is not restoration of the play's spatial economy, and the productions that flourish are the ones that understand the difference.

The seated, darkened, silent house that promenade defines itself against is itself a late invention. The nineteenth century progressively stilled and darkened the auditorium, a discipline crowned at Bayreuth in 1876, where the festival theatre hid its orchestra, dimmed its house and fixed its audience in raked rows facing one way, the better to abolish the social theatre of boxes and promenades that earlier centuries had taken for granted. The twentieth century's reaction, from the environmental theatre Richard Schechner theorized in the 1960s to the site-specific work that followed, set out to recover the older contract: spectators visible to each other, space shared with performers, the event acknowledged as an assembly rather than a window. Promenade Shakespeare belongs to that recovery, and its practitioners, whether they cite the lineage or not, are arguing with Bayreuth as much as with the picture-frame stage. The argument matters for this play because Romeo and Juliet predates the divide: its dramaturgy assumes an audience that can be addressed, marshalled, hushed and implicated, an assembly with a civic existence inside the event. The Chorus's speeches are crowd management in verse. When a promenade steward settles two hundred walkers into a ring and an actor steps forward to speak the Prologue to their faces, the oldest machinery in the play is simply working again.

## The Public Play: Brawls, the Ball, and the Street

The case for moving the audience begins with the play's public architecture, because Shakespeare built Verona as a city of witnessed violence. The opening brawl is not a private quarrel but a civic emergency assembled in layers: servants trade insults, "I will bite my thumb at them, which is disgrace to them if they bear it" (1.1), citizens arm themselves with clubs, the heads of both houses arrive demanding weapons, and the Prince closes the scene by addressing the crowd as a party to the crime: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1.93-94). A seated audience watches that escalation as a picture. A standing audience experiences it as the scene's own bystanders did: bodies press closer, sightlines block and clear, the fight is partly heard before it is seen, and the Prince's sentence falls on the crowd the spectator is standing in. The text itself casts the audience as Verona's citizenry, called to order, named enemies to peace in the Prince's hearing. Promenade merely accepts the casting.

The ball accepts it even more warmly, because Capulet's feast is the one scene in the play where the audience's role is already written. The lovers meet inside a crowd of masked dancers, and the text insists on the crowd's functions: it provides cover, it provides witnesses, it provides the danger of discovery. Romeo enters masked, carrying a torch he does not want, "Give me a torch, I am not for this ambling" (1.4), and finds Juliet across a room in motion: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" (1.5). Their first exchange is a shared sonnet spoken hand to hand inside a dance (1.5.92-105), a private form smuggled through a public event, legible as courtship to anyone watching and deniable to anyone asking. Productions that stand their audience inside the ball, masked or unmasked, fed, danced around, recruited as guests, are not decorating this scene; they are staffing it. The interpretive literature on [staging the ball, its music, masks and dance](/2027/12/24/staging-the-ball-music-dance/) shows how much of the scene's meaning is choreography and social pressure, and promenade staging converts that pressure from something watched into something undergone. The guest who has been jostled, flirted past and watched by Tybalt understands the discovery scene differently from the spectator who has only seen it framed.

The street fight of 3.1 completes the public triptych, and it is the scene promenade staging was invented for, though centuries early. Mercutio's death happens in public, under a code of witnessed honour, and his curse is addressed to the whole city in the plural: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). A crowd that has walked behind these young men through two acts, that has eaten in one house and danced in the other, receives that curse as an implicated party. The public scenes, in short, are not merely compatible with a moving audience; they are incomplete without one, because their subject is what crowds do to private feeling. The feud is a crowd phenomenon. Only a crowd can play it from the inside.

Two further public mechanisms deserve record. The feast itself is assembled by a list. Capulet hands his servant a paper of invited names and the servant, who cannot read, must find a scholar to read it for him, which is how Romeo learns of the ball (1.2). Verona's most consequential party is thus convened by accident, through a document in the hands of a stranger, and the comedy of the scene is a crowd being summoned into existence. An immersive production's advance paperwork, its summonses and assigned identities, replays that mechanism on its audience with a fidelity no one advertises: the spectator, like Rosaline's fellow guests, is at the feast because a list said so. And at the play's far end, the Prince's closing audit in the tomb (5.3) is a public act performed over private corpses, a magistrate apportioning pardon and punishment before the assembled heads of both houses: "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" (5.3). The speech requires an assembly to receive it, a citizen body standing as jury. A promenade audience that has walked the feud's whole geography arrives at that audit as the only body in the theatre qualified to hear it, since it alone has stood in every place the evidence was made. The seated audience judges the play. The promenade audience has, in the fiction's terms, witnessed it, and the difference is the strongest single argument the form possesses.

### Why does the ball scene open so easily to a standing crowd?

Because the text gives the crowd a job. Capulet's feast needs guests to fill the floor, conceal the gatecrashers, watch and threaten discovery, so a standing audience is not imported but recruited. The shared sonnet at its centre (1.5.92-105) is a private form performed in public, and audience-guests supply the public.

This is the strong half of the immersive case, and it survives Alston's critique intact.

Central Park supplied the argument's unplanned proof in 2018. A promenade in a working public park recruits beyond its ticketed or gathered crowd: the company's own account of its audiences stresses spontaneous attendance and passers-by who had never seen a play, and a Panoramic performance assembles its Verona from whoever the park provides, dog-walkers and picnickers folded into the citizenry behind the actors. The fiction's claim is thereby tested against the real thing. When the Prince imposed his peace on a crowd of New Yorkers who had wandered into a brawl, the scene's premise, that a city's public can be assembled, implicated and sentenced in its own streets, was not illustrated but enacted, with the city's actual public as the evidence. No indoor production, however promenade its aisles, can borrow a real crowd from a real city in that way, and the park productions' hold on the survey rests partly on that borrowing: the feud's crowds are the one element of the play that a public place supplies more truthfully than any stage can build. The participation demanded by the brawl and the ball is not the token interactivity of the experience economy, a drawer opened or a note read in an empty room; it is the substantive social participation the drama is about, presence in a crowd whose pressure the plot then weaponizes. Machon's intimacy and immediacy, often vague virtues in the abstract, have precise meanings here: the immediacy of a fight forming around one's own body, the intimacy of standing close enough to the dancers to see a handclasp the Nurse must interrupt. On the public architecture, the form does not illustrate the play. It completes the play's casting.

## The Machinery of Membership: Masks, Summonses, and House Colours

Before any immersive production reaches its first scene, it processes its audience, and the processing is dramaturgy. A ticket admits a spectator to watch; the immersive event replaces the ticket with tokens of membership. Riot Act's Houses issued its Salt Lake City audience a string of plastic beads at a wooden gate in place of a ticket. Iris Theatre dresses its front-of-house staff in clothing akin to the world of the play, so that the first figures a spectator meets at St Paul's belong to the fiction's side of the door. Secret Cinema went furthest: its spectators were assigned to a house in advance, issued an alias and a police summons, and costumed by instruction in the colours of Montague or Capulet, so that five thousand strangers arrived at Gunnersbury Park already sorted into the feud's two camps. Sleep No More handed its masked spectators a white beaked mask at the elevator and forbade speech, converting each playgoer into an anonymous ghost in someone else's story. The devices differ in scale and in spirit, but they perform one operation: they give the spectator a position inside the play's social system before the play begins.

The text authorizes the operation, because membership is the play's own first subject. Verona runs on names and badges. The feud is a membership system with no recorded cause, maintained by servants who fight over a bitten thumb precisely because allegiance, not injury, is the currency: "Do you quarrel, sir? ... No, sir, I do not bite my thumb at you, sir, but I bite my thumb, sir" (1.1). Juliet's great speech of renunciation is an attack on membership tokens themselves, "That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44), delivered by a girl whose tragedy is that the tokens cannot in practice be declined. The lovers meet at a masked ball, where identity is licensed costume: "Give me a case to put my visage in, / A visor for a visor!" (1.4). An immersive production that masks its audience, colours it, summonses it and sorts it is not importing a gimmick into the play. It is operating machinery the play describes, the machinery by which a crowd becomes two houses.

### What does a summons or a house colour do that a ticket cannot?

It assigns a side. A ticket leaves the spectator neutral, a witness with a seat; a house colour, an alias or a summons makes the spectator a Montague or a Capulet before the first line, so the feud's public scenes address a partisan rather than an observer. The device converts attendance into allegiance, which is the play's own first principle.

The adjudication between Machon and Alston sharpens on this machinery. For Machon, the induction devices are thresholds of intimacy: the mask that anonymizes also liberates, the colour that sorts also admits, and the spectator's body becomes a medium of the work. For Alston, the same devices are the experience economy's uniform: the consumer's individuality is processed into a pre-assigned identity whose choices have all been made upstream, and the feeling of belonging is the product being sold. The survey's evidence supports both readings at different scales. At churchyard scale, membership is theatrical: a few hundred walkers need no badge, because proximity does the sorting, and the audience's citizenship of Verona is earned by walking its ground. At festival scale, membership is administrative: sixty thousand spectators cannot be sorted by proximity, so they are sorted by paperwork, and the paperwork is where Alston's audit bites. The deeper the fiction's claim on the spectator's identity, the more industrial the machinery required to sustain it, and the more the machinery itself becomes the show. That inverse proportion, intimacy against scale, is the form's governing law for this play, and every section of the survey obeys it.

One more device deserves record because it is the oldest: the guide. Every induction ends by handing the processed crowd to someone who will lead it, and the promenade companies inherit the office from the Chorus, the play's own member of both worlds. Where the guide is an actor in role, the audience follows a Veronese citizen; where the guide is a steward with a torch or a radio, the fiction thins at exactly the moment of transition, and productions differ in how carefully they disguise the seam. The seam matters because transitions are where promenade audiences are most themselves, chatting, checking, drifting, and where the fiction must work hardest to re-form them into the crowd the next scene requires. The productions in the survey that treat the walk itself as part of the performance, led, paced and sometimes played through, hold their audiences; the ones that treat it as plumbing lose them a little at every station, and the losses compound toward the tomb.

## The Private Play: Where Immersion Taxes the Verse

Turn from the street to the chamber, and the trade reverses. The orchard scene (2.2) is a duet built on stillness and mishearing: two speakers who cannot see each other clearly, exchanging speeches whose tension lives in pauses, asides and the geometry of a window above and a garden below. Its famous cadence of parting, "Parting is such sweet sorrow / That I shall say good night till it be morrow" (2.2.184-185), assumes an audience settled enough to hear reluctance itself. The aubade of 3.5 is an argument conducted in whispers against approaching day. The potion speech of 4.3 is a solo ordeal of the imagination, a young woman talking herself through a catalogue of terrors in an empty room, and its power depends on the audience's enforced passivity: nobody can help her, nobody may move, the speech must be endured in real time. Each of these scenes is engineered for a gathered, seated, hushed body of listeners. Set that body in motion, spread it along a garden path, let half of it arrive late from the last scene and the rest shuffle for a view, and the tax falls exactly where the writing is finest.

The tax is not hypothetical; it is structural, and it has three components. The first is acoustic. Verse spoken outdoors or in found interiors, to spectators distributed at unequal distances, loses its lower register of effects: the aside, the half-line, the whispered aside within a speech. The second is temporal. A promenade audience spends part of every scene transition walking, and productions either cut text or compress the chamber scenes to buy back the walking time, so the private architecture is squeezed from both ends. The third is attentional, and it is the component Machon's vocabulary of immediacy does not capture: a spectator who is navigating, choosing a position and monitoring a crowd is spending cognition that the duet scenes need. The Friar's warning that the whole play might be said to exist to deliver, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9), lands in a hush or it does not land at all.

The wedding concentrates it first. The marriage scene (2.6) is the play's narrowest point: three people in a cell, a handful of speeches, the Friar's warning, and the briefest of ceremonies, "For, by your leaves, you shall not stay alone / Till holy church incorporate two in one" (2.6). It is also the hinge on which the whole action turns, the moment the public play of houses and streets contracts into the private fact that the rest of the tragedy will defend and destroy. A route composed for crowds has no natural station for it: there is no square, no feast, no procession, only a door. The survey's productions handle the hinge by the only means available, shrinking the audience's world to match, a chapel corner of the church for Iris's congregation of walkers, the ring drawn tight in the park, the ceremony half-lost inside the festival's continuing noise at Verona Beach. The scene is the funnel in miniature, and it predicts the tomb: the two most important events in the play happen in its two smallest rooms, before the fewest possible witnesses, and a staging of multitudes must learn, twice, to become a staging of almost nobody.

The tomb concentrates the problem into its purest form. The last scene is a sequence of solos and duets over bodies: Paris's vigil, Romeo's address to the dead, the Friar's arrival, Juliet's waking, the Prince's closing audit, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). Its theatrical requirement is concentration of the most old-fashioned kind, a room full of people looking at the same small space for a long unbroken stretch. Promenade can deliver that concentration, because a crowd that has walked together for two hours arrives at the tomb bonded, hushed by effort, ready to stand in a ring and be still; several of the survey's productions exploit exactly that arrival. What promenade cannot do is make the tomb one station among many, equivalent to the ball or the street. The play's geography is a funnel: many public places, narrowing through chambers to a single vault. Staging that honours the funnel, gathering and slowing its audience as the play gathers and slows, converts the form's liability into its meaning. Staging that treats every station as equal spectacle spends the ending's capital on the journey.

### What does the audience's movement do to the tomb scene?

It can crown the evening or dissolve it, and the difference is pacing. A crowd that has been walked, gathered and quieted through the later scenes reaches the vault as mourners, and its ring of bodies does the monument's office. A crowd still processing arrivals and neighbours during the solos hears the play's most concentrated writing at its distracted moment.

The chamber scenes also share a construction that promenade staging must respect: they are interviews behind doors. Juliet's epithalamium, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1), is a solo address to the night itself, spoken by a girl alone in a room her family believes empty of event. The Friar's cell scenes are consultations, two people and a secret, in which the play's entire machinery of rescue is negotiated at conversational volume. The potion speech is the extreme case, a consultation with no second party, in which Juliet cross-examines her own imagination. What these scenes need from an audience is not presence but privileged absence: the sensation of overhearing what the fiction insists is unheard. A seated house achieves that sensation by convention, four walls of darkness and a pool of light. A promenade crowd achieves it only by discipline, by being gathered close, kept still and made conscious of its own privilege, which is why the survey's successful chamber scenes are all played inside a ring at arm's length, the crowd converted from fellow citizens into fellow conspirators. The conversion is the form's most delicate operation, and its failure mode is bathos: a tomb scene watched by spectators still holding drinks and programmes from the ball is a scene whose world has leaked.

The private-play evidence settles the Machon-Alston disagreement for this text. Alston is right that the form's celebrated freedom is usually a managed product, and the management shows most at the chamber scenes, where the production must quietly withdraw the freedom it advertised: stand here, be quiet, do not drift. Machon is right that co-presence changes the event in ways no seat can replicate, and the change shows most in the public scenes, where the audience's body is itself evidence. An honest immersive Romeo and Juliet is therefore a hybrid by necessity: loose in the streets, strict in the rooms. The survey's successes all obey that law, and its failures are all, at root, refusals of it.

## Sound in Found Space: Hearing Verse Without a Theatre

Every immersive production of this play is also an acoustic experiment, because the found spaces of the form, gardens, parks, warehouses, parking garages, were none of them built to carry unamplified verse. A theatre is a machine for delivering speeches to a thousand ears at matched distances; a promenade distributes its hearers along paths and across lawns at distances the production cannot equalize, and the verse pays the difference. The text itself is written in registers of projection. The Prince's sentences are public oratory, built to cross a square. The balcony exchanges are pitched to cross a garden and no more. The cell scenes are consultation, and the potion speech is soliloquy, the voice of a mind overheard. A proscenium production solves the registers with architecture and amplification. A promenade production must solve them with geography, casting its loud scenes in its open places and its quiet scenes in its enclosures, and the survey's sites sort themselves by how well their geography matches the play's dynamic map. The church garden excels because its walls hold the quiet scenes near. The open park strains, its ambient city leaking into every hush. The parking garage, resonant concrete emptied of traffic, surprised its makers by behaving like a stone chamber.

The technologies of rescue divide the record into eras of candour. The honest solutions are proximity and numbers: play the duets inside a ring of fifty, not across a lawn to five hundred, and let the audience's nearness do what a loudspeaker would falsify. The evasive solutions replace the voice altogether. SONDERimmersive's car radio is the limiting case, a broadcast channel that delivers narration and score to every spectator at identical volume and thereby solves audibility by abolishing the performed voice. Punchdrunk's one-to-one encounters are the opposite pole: a single spectator, mask removed by an actor's hand in a locked room, receiving a fragment of text at whispering distance, the most extreme proximity the form has devised and a privilege rationed to a handful of the two thousand present. Between those poles, the promenade mainstream works unamplified and accepts the weather of real air: wind in the churchyard, traffic beyond the park railings, the audience's own footsteps on gravel as the scene change's percussion. Actors in such conditions develop the open-air technique the original companies owned by default, placement of the voice toward the listener's body rather than the sky, consonants weighted, pace governed by the farthest ear that matters. Reviewers of the park and garden productions return repeatedly to the same observation, that the verse lands differently when the hearer has walked to it: effort expended on arrival converts into attention, and a speech received standing in the place it describes carries a charge no seat supplies. The observation is Machon's immediacy translated into acoustics, and it is the form's best answer to the audibility charge. The walk is not dead time between speeches. It is the price of a hearing, and audiences pay it gladly when the production prices it honestly.

Music is the form's other acoustic instrument, and the play hands it a double brief. Within the fiction, music is the ball's engine and the wedding's omen; around the fiction, music is the promenade's connective tissue, the sound that holds a crowd in the world while it walks between speeches. The survey's productions deploy it at every scale: a score composed for the garage Verona and carried by radio, a gospel choir and live bands inside Verona Beach, party music bleeding from a warehouse doorway as the audience arrives at Houses. The device reaches back to the play's own practice, in which the musicians of 4.5, dismissed from the wedding feast turned funeral, close the treatise on the household's grief with a joke about silver sounds. A promenade that scores its walks is not decorating the transitions; it is refusing to let the audience leave Verona acoustically while it leaves a scene physically, and the refusal is one of the form's genuine inventions, unavailable to any seated house, whose scene changes happen in the dark to people who are allowed, for those moments, to stop believing.

Silence is the third acoustic instrument, and the form wields it in two opposite ways. Punchdrunk imposed silence on its spectators as a rule of the house, masks and muteness manufacturing the ghostliness that let two thousand people haunt a building without becoming a crowd. Promenade cannot impose that silence, because its crowd must be talked to, guided, warned and gathered, and its natural state is the murmur of people outdoors together. The promenade production's hush is therefore an achievement rather than a rule, produced afresh at every chamber scene by the actors' descent in volume and the stewards' example, and lost afresh at every transition. That rhythm of murmur and hush, crowd noise gathered down to a ring's held breath, is the acoustic signature of the form at its best, and it reproduces, in the audience's own body, the play's rhythm of street and chamber more faithfully than any design could. The spectator who has been hushed five times by a friar's cell and a girl's window understands the tragedy's acoustics as knowledge: Verona is loud, love is quiet, and the loud world always re-enters.

## The Comparative Test: Why This Play Invites Promenade and Resists Immersion

The survey's shape becomes clearer when the play is set beside its repertory neighbours, because the immersive stage has adopted Shakespeare unevenly, and the pattern of adoption is a reading of the plays themselves. A Midsummer Night's Dream is the promenade natural: a play of woods, wandering and mistaken identity, whose action already moves through a landscape in the dark and whose lovers are a walking party. Iris Theatre's summer seasons, which pair a Shakespeare play with a family show in the St Paul's gardens, reached the Dream in due course, in 2022, with audiences moving around the grounds and into the church, and the fit between text and form there requires no argument: the wood outside Athens is a garden already, and the audience's own wandering re-enacts the lovers' confusion. Macbeth is the free-roam natural, for the opposite reason: a plot of corridors, thanes' chambers, apparitions and secrets, whose meanings survive translation into architecture and movement, which is why Punchdrunk could build its masterpiece on it while discarding its language. Hamlet resists both forms, being a play whose centre is a consciousness: productions may site it in castles and asylums, but a crowd walking behind the Prince is walking behind a man whose drama is that he does not move.

Romeo and Juliet sits between the Dream and Macbeth, and its position on the scale explains its divided record. Half the play behaves like the Dream: young people moving through a city at night, feasts, streets, gardens, a geography of pursuit and concealment that a walking audience can inhabit. The other half behaves like Hamlet: two intelligences overheard in rooms, a girl alone reasoning with a vial, a friar reasoning with himself among his plants, "O mickle is the powerful grace that lies / In plants, herbs, stones, and their true qualities" (2.3). The immersive record follows the halves exactly. Every production in the survey keeps the walking half with confidence and negotiates the reasoning half by compression, narration, choreography or film. No production has yet kept both halves at full value in free-roam form, and the comparative test suggests none will, because the obstacle is not ingenuity but genre: the play is a tragedy of overheard thought wearing the clothes of a street festival. Promenade, which keeps the halves in sequence and in proportion, is the form that respects the compound. The compound is the play.

## A Churchyard in Covent Garden: Iris Theatre, 2009

Iris Theatre, founded by Daniel Winder in 2007 as the resident company at St Paul's Church, Covent Garden, the Actors' Church, opened its first outdoor promenade summer season in 2009 with this play, Laura Wickham as Juliet and Sam Donnelly as Romeo, played through the church's gardens with the audience following the action from station to station. The choice of play for a young company's first promenade was shrewder than it may have looked. The churchyard gave the production what no built set could: a real consecrated ground for the play's religious furniture, the Friar's cell, the church wedding, the burial, and a garden whose paths and walls made Verona's division into household territories a matter of yards rather than metaphors. The audience's route enacted the plot's geography. To follow the lovers was to cross and recross the feud's boundary lines on foot.

The Iris model also demonstrates promenade's built-in economy of attention. A church garden disciplines a crowd gently: paths channel movement, walls hold sound better than open parkland, and the stations of the action can be arranged so that the audience arrives at each scene already facing the right way. The company's summer seasons went on to make the form a durable London institution, playing to some ten thousand spectators a summer across its productions, with the church itself praised by its actors as a character in the plays. For this play, the garden's gift was the tomb's opposite: not a finale of architectural gloom but a burial in real earth under a real sky, the audience standing among gravestones that were nobody's property design. Site, in the taxonomy of this article, is doing the interpretive work, and promenade is simply the mechanism that lets the audience collect the site's meanings in sequence.

Set against the boundary cases later in this survey, Iris also marks the form's modest pole: a small company, a found sacred site, the whole text, a walking crowd. Nothing was sold except the play and the place. That modesty is not a limitation to apologize for; it is the condition of the form's fidelity. Every enlargement of immersive scale in the record is paid for out of the text.

The company's own account of its site explains the fidelity from the inside. Members of Iris describe the church as a character in the productions, a presence the actors play against rather than a backdrop they play before, and the description is more than publicity: a building with its own congregation, its own dead and its own daily office lends the Friar's scenes and the final burial a documentary weight that no constructed set can invoice. Audience testimony about the gardens seasons returns to the same transaction from the other side, the pleasure of moving about the grounds as the scenes change place, and the open debate, recorded in visitor reviews across the company's seasons, about whether the moving broke the dramatic tension or built it. That debate is the Machon-Alston question asked at garden scale by the people paying for tickets, and its persistence across a decade of seasons suggests the honest answer both camps resist: the tension broke and built by turns, scene by scene, exactly as the text's alternation of crowd and chamber predicts.

## A Guided Crowd in Central Park: New York Classical Theatre, 2018

New York Classical Theatre, founded in 2000 by Stephen Burdman, built its practice on a staging method the company calls Panoramic Theatre: the audience follows the actors from place to place, relocating about every twenty minutes, so that a park becomes a sequence of stages and the crowd's own movement supplies the scene changes. In the summer of 2018 the company gave its first Romeo and Juliet in that form, opening in Central Park, where it played to more than four thousand people, and then travelling the production to Rockefeller Park, the Battery, Carl Schurz Park and Brooklyn Bridge Park through the end of July. Six actors carried the whole play, four of them doubling across the two houses, so that the feud was embodied by performers the audience had watched change sides. Every performance was free.

The company's wider record frames the experiment. By 2018 New York Classical had presented more than forty productions and over seven hundred free performances since its founding, in settings running from Central Park to the Battery and, for Henry V, by ferry to Governors Island; free admission and spontaneous attendance were the company's founding terms, not a summer promotion. Panoramic Theatre was the house style, and Romeo and Juliet was a first staging of the play within an established civic practice rather than a novelty act. That context matters to the survey's judgment: the production's promenade was not a young company's wager but a mature company's method, tested across the canon, meeting the one play whose crowd scenes the method seems designed for.

The production is the survey's strongest evidence for promenade as a textual form rather than a decorative one. Panoramic staging is promenade disciplined to a timetable: the twenty-minute rhythm means the audience never settles long enough to become a seated house, yet never wanders; the company leads, the crowd follows, and the full sequence of scenes arrives in order at full verbal value. The playing time, about a hundred minutes, shows the necessary economy, achieved by pace and doubling rather than by abandoning whole movements of the play. Burdman's parks also solved the public-scene problem by casting the audience as the scene's own population. A brawl in a public park, watched by a standing public that has walked there behind the combatants, is witnessed in the text's own terms; the Prince's peace is imposed on a crowd that includes the spectator's body. Where the parks taxed the production was where the taxonomy predicts: the chamber scenes, played in open air to a ring of standers, traded the hush of the orchard and the cell for the ambient music of a city evening. The company's answer was proximity, playing the duets inside the ring at close quarters, a platea solution to a locus problem.

### Who leads a promenade audience from place to place?

The production does, through a combination of actors and stewards playing the Chorus's old office. In Panoramic staging the company itself sets the rhythm, ending a scene, moving off and drawing the crowd behind it about every twenty minutes, while stewards hold the route's edges. The guidance is choreography, not courtesy: a promenade audience that is not led disperses.

New York Classical also staged the survey's most instructive exception. For one week of the Rockefeller Park engagement, billed as Take a Seat week, the audience stayed in one place for the entire hundred minutes, a deliberate accessibility variant for spectators for whom the walking form is a barrier. The variant matters beyond its week. It concedes, in production practice, the point this article presses in theory: mobility is a means, not the meaning. A promenade Romeo and Juliet that cannot imagine its seated form has confused its mechanism with its argument, and the argument, the audience placed inside Verona's public life, survives translation into stillness better than the mechanism survives its own absolutism. The wider landscape of [open-air and festival stagings](/2028/06/30/open-air-festival-stagings/) is full of seated crowds in found places achieving half of the promenade effect, the half that site alone supplies, and the Take a Seat week honestly measured how much of Panoramic Theatre's effect was the walking and how much was the park.

## Salt Lake City Twice: A Warehouse Feud and a Parking-Garage Verona

The form's small-scale American record is concentrated, improbably, in one city. In the summer of 2017, Riot Act, a company founded in London in 2015 and working in Salt Lake City, staged Houses, Whit Hertford's immersive retelling of the play, at Kilby Court, a small concert venue reached down an alley. The audience entered a warehouse dressed for a party, beads for tickets, actors already among the crowd; Romeo quarrelled with Rosalind at the party's centre, Mercutio worked a microphone, and the story then led its spectators out of the warehouse, into the yard, and at last into the street, where the ending played under string lights and car headlights as night fell. Susannah Whitman's review for Front Row Reviewers records the essential mechanics: a running time of a little over two hours without an interval, and an audience rarely in the same place for longer than fifteen minutes. The text was an excavation rather than a performance, modern language carrying the story with Shakespeare quoted inside it, the houses reimagined as rival households of a queer love story.

Houses is promenade at the scale of a house party, and its evidence cuts two ways. Its intimacy was real and reviewer-certified: actors within arm's reach, grief in a street the audience had to cross, the conclusion lent, in Whitman's phrase, a beauty by the night air that a closed theatre could not recreate. Its textual surrender was equally real: what the audience received was the story of the play, powerfully embodied, at the price of the play's language, which is to say at the price of most of the play. Houses therefore belongs in the survey as a limiting case, the point at which promenade intimacy is purchased by conversion into new writing. The company was candid about the method, billing its practice as the resuscitation of classic texts through incision and excavation, and judged as new writing the production succeeded. Judged as a staging of Romeo and Juliet, it demonstrates the trade rather than refuting it.

The performances themselves complicate any dismissal. Whitman's review singles out McKenzie Steele-Foster's Romeo for a naturalism that dissolved the sense of watching an actress at all, and Haeleigh Royall's Juliet for a sweetness that deepened into courage; proximity, in the warehouse and the yard, rewarded exactly the unforced playing that a large house would have swallowed. The same review's one reservation, fight choreography that looked too choreographed in places, is also a datum about scale: at arm's length there is nowhere for stage combat to hide, and the intimacy that flatters acting exposes craft. Small-scale promenade is thus a high-wire for performers as much as a liberation for audiences, a condition the form's publicity rarely mentions and its practitioners cannot escape.

Three years later the same city produced the form's strangest mutation. SONDERimmersive's Through Yonder Window, directed and choreographed by Graham Brown, first played in 2020 in a parking garage at the Gateway complex, where roughly a thousand spectators encountered a Verona of stations arranged through the structure, and returned in April 2021, in conjunction with Utah Presents, in an expanded version staged on the upper deck of the Alsco parking garage, where the audience watched from their cars and heard the production's narration and Wachira Waigwa-Stone's score through the car radio. The piece ran about an hour, nine scenes, eight characters arranged in four pairs, Romeo and Juliet, the Friar and the Nurse, Mercutio and Tybalt, Lord Montague and Lady Capulet, with the playing area divided into three territories: the church at one edge, Verona in the middle, the two residences at the other. It was dance theatre with narration, not a verse performance; the Utah Review praised its clarity and the intelligence of its compressions.

The drive-in version deserves its place in the survey as a thought experiment that history accidentally performed. Immersion's whole wager is that removing barriers between audience and action increases involvement; the car restored every barrier, glass, steel, a radio dial, while keeping the audience distributed through the world of the play. What survived the experiment was telling: geography, atmosphere, the felt arrangement of a city in zones of church, street and household, which are the public architecture's gifts. What could not survive was the chamber: eight characters distilled to emblems, the verse replaced by narration, the duets by choreography. Through Yonder Window is the survey's purest demonstration that the play's public half is portable into almost any medium, and its private half is not portable at all.

The production's method of compression deserves a closer look, because it shows what a digest keeps when it can keep almost nothing. Brown's eight characters were arranged as four pairs, the lovers, the Friar and the Nurse, Mercutio and Tybalt, Lord Montague and Lady Capulet, and the pairing is itself an interpretation: Verona reduced to its bonds, each figure defined by the one relationship that drives them, the city's feud expressed as a symmetry of households. The narration, much of it carried by Tyler Fox, who also played the Friar, restored the office of the Chorus in its most literal modern form, a voice on the radio guiding a dispersed audience through a story it could not be gathered to hear. Ed Corvera's Romeo and Nadia Sine's Juliet moved among all three territories, church, street and residences, the only figures licensed to cross the whole map, which is precisely the lovers' function in the play. At forty-five dollars a vehicle, the production also repriced the form's oldest question: the unit of admission was no longer a citizen of Verona but a carload of witnesses, sealed in glass, present at everything and able to join nothing. That the result was praised for clarity rather than mourned for distance is the final twist in the survey's evidence. Given a Verona arranged as a diagram and a voice to explain it, audiences accepted the diagram as the play's essence, which suggests how much of the tragedy's popular identity is carried by its map, its pairs and its symmetry, and how completely its verbal soul can be absent while its shape is still recognized.

## Secret Cinema and the Selling of Immersion

In August 2018, Secret Cinema, the London company founded by Fabien Riggall in 2007 to build worlds around film screenings, mounted its largest event to that date in Gunnersbury Park: William Shakespeare's Romeo + Juliet, a Verona Beach built outdoors for Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film. The numbers belong to a different economy from everything else in this survey: some five thousand spectators a night, more than sixty thousand across fifteen showings, tickets from forty-nine pounds. Attendees were assigned to a house before arrival, issued identities and summonses, costumed by instruction in Montague or Capulet colours, and released into a festival of bars, fairground rides, a gospel choir, a Montague garage and a Capulet mansion ball, through which actors played scenes from the story; the evening ended with the crowd seated on the grass before the film itself, with performers miming beneath the screen. Movie Marker's reviewer, in a three-star verdict, named the result a fan convention with an outdoor screening component, and judged the hours of world thinner than the space they filled.

Even the arrival was composed as promenade. Attendee accounts describe a staggered entry at a meeting point outside Acton Town station and a guided fifteen-minute walk to the park gates, phones sealed away, the crowd already costumed in its two colours, so that the audience reached Verona as a procession of partisans rather than a queue of customers. Inside, the ball at the Capulet mansion, the dancing on muscle cars at the Montague garage, the gospel choir and the fairground rides filled the hours before the screening with the city's pleasures, and the actors' scenes threaded through them. The event's dramaturgy was, in effect, the play's first movement expanded to an evening: the summons, the journey, the feast, the street. What it could not expand was everything after, and the film's two hours on the grass, watched cross-legged by a crowd whose world had gone quiet around a screen, measured the distance between inhabiting a story's setting and attending its tragedy.

The production is this article's boundary case, and it is included precisely because it fails the taxonomy's tests in instructive ways. The audience roamed freely, so the form was free-roam; the play's words reached that audience overwhelmingly through a film, so the text was not staged but screened; the immersion on offer was membership of a world rather than attendance at a drama. Yet notice what the event got right, because it is the public play again: the house assignment worked. Five thousand strangers sorted into two colours, drinking in rival territories, dancing in a mansion ballroom, felt the feud as a social fact in their own bodies, which is exactly the knowledge the play's crowd scenes exist to transmit. Secret Cinema staged, at festival scale, the one act of the play its form could carry, the ball and the street, and screened the rest. The marketing called the whole evening immersive theatre. The accurate description is narrower and more interesting: an immersive first act wrapped around a film.

The event also generated the form's characteristic paper trail, and the trail is part of the record. A five-thousand-a-night occupation of a public park required licensing and planning consents, council applications in which the company described its audience and its intentions in the language of event management, and the correspondence of a park's friends' group weighing revenue against disturbance; the proposed return seasons were debated, scaled and in the event abandoned in the language of permissions rather than of art. None of this is a scandal, and all of it is information. Mass immersion is a branch of the events industry, answerable to licensing committees, and its artistic questions are settled downstream of its logistical ones. The promenade mainstream never faces that inversion, because its crowds are small enough to remain guests of their places rather than occupiers of them. Scale, once again, is not a neutral variable. It is the variable that decides which art form, theatre or event management, is actually being practised under the immersive name.

Alston's critique finds its textbook case here. The productive participant of Gunnersbury Park was productive indeed: costumed, badged, spending, photographing nothing, since phones were sealed away, and generating the atmosphere that was the product. The empowerment was real as sensation and empty as agency, since no choice any spectator made could touch the story, which waited, fixed and finished, on the screen. Machon's defence also finds its limit: intimacy and immediacy were present in abundance, but they attached to the world rather than to the work, and the work, the most language-saturated love tragedy in English, cannot be experienced at one remove from its language. The contrast control is Punchdrunk's Sleep No More, which ran in New York from 2011 to 2025: a free-roam Macbeth of a hundred rooms, masked silent spectators, one-to-one encounters in locked closets, and virtually no text at all, because Barrett and Doyle understood that free-roam form and verse drama exclude each other and chose the form. Punchdrunk's honesty is the standard. Immersion of that kind is a great art with a different object. Set against this play, it is a neighbouring art, not a staging method.

### Does a promenade audience see the whole play?

In guided promenade, yes: the crowd follows one sequence of scenes and receives the play entire, which is the form's advantage over free-roam immersion. In free-roam events, no spectator sees everything, a fact the productions concede. The question to ask of any ticket is not whether the evening is immersive but which kind of incompleteness, if any, has been accepted.

A last inflation needs deflating before the verdict. The word immersive is also borrowed, with growing looseness, for fixed-seat staging that merely surrounds its audience: theatre in the round, however athletic, however close the actors pass, does not move the spectator through the fiction, and its undoubted intensities are the older intensities of the arena stage. The borrowing matters because it empties the survey's category; if every close staging is immersive, the term names nothing, and the real experiments in audience movement lose their distinct record. The taxonomy held throughout this article, promenade, free-roam, site-specific, in-the-round, is the price of admission to the argument.

## The Route as Dramaturgy: Order, Intervals, and the Walk Between Scenes

A promenade production has one structural element no seated production possesses: the route. The sequence of places, the distances between them, the ground underfoot and the time the walking takes are all compositional choices, and the survey's productions compose with them consciously or pay for composing carelessly. The route replaces the scene change. Where a seated production blacks out, revolves or interludes, a promenade production moves its audience through real space, and the movement can be made to mean. The play's own geography invites the composition: a city of two households and a church, a friar's cell at its margin, a road of exile running out of it to Mantua, and a tomb at its heart. The second Chorus promises the pattern in advance, "But passion lends them power, time means, to meet" (Act 2 Chorus), and the play's places succeed each other as stations of approach and separation: street, hall, orchard, cell, chamber, street again, cell again, vault. A route that lengthens as the lovers' fortunes shorten, that makes its longest walk the walk of exile and its final approach a narrowing descent into a church, a crypt, a garage's lowest level, is reading the play with its feet. A route that merely strings its available spaces in the order the landlord offered is a venue hire with pretensions.

The interval is the route's hardest problem. A seated audience rests at a fixed point while the fiction suspends; a promenade audience must be suspended mid-journey, and every company in the survey chooses one of three settlements. The whole evening can be played without a break, Riot Act's two hours and New York Classical's hundred minutes both electing momentum over mercy, at some cost to the audience's legs and the actors' voices. The break can be placed at a station built for lingering, a garden with a bar, a ball that continues informally while the formal action pauses, converting the interval into the fiction's own hospitality. Or the break can be abolished as a category and replaced by drift, the free-roam settlement in which each spectator rests at will and the fiction never formally suspends at all. The settlements are not equivalent. The play is built in two great arcs, the ascent to the secret marriage and the descent from the street fight to the tomb, and its middle hinge, the marriage itself, is a scene of two people and a friar in a cell. A promenade that breaks at the hinge honours the architecture; a promenade that cannot stop, because its route has no station small enough for the cell, discovers too late that its geography was planned for the crowds and not for the wedding.

Order is the route's final discipline, and it is where promenade parts company with free-roam for good. A guided crowd receives the play's causes before its effects: the thumb bitten before the ball, the ball before the fight, the fight before the potion. That sequence is not a courtesy but the tragedy's mechanism, a chain of witnessed causes that makes the ending feel inevitable rather than arbitrary, and it is precisely what free-roam surrenders when it lets a spectator meet the tomb before the ballroom. The survey's most disciplined productions guard the order as jealously as any seated house guards its text. The least disciplined treat sequence as one more convention the form has outgrown, and discover that what they have outgrown is causality. An audience that assembles its own order assembles its own play, and the play it assembles, however beautiful its rooms, is not the one whose ending has been breaking hearts in sequence for four centuries.

## The Actor's Problem: Playing to a Moving Crowd

The form's literature is written from the audience's side; its costs are paid on the actor's. A promenade performer plays to a crowd that is also a procession, which means playing several scenes a night to spectators in the act of arriving, settling and craning, and holding a through-line of character across walks during which the fiction is officially suspended. The doubling economies of small companies compound the demand: New York Classical's six actors crossed and recrossed the feud in full view, changing allegiance with a coat while the audience that had just watched them fight processed the transformation. The doubling is legible, even eloquent, but it asks the performer to be both citizen and guide, character and steward, and to switch offices without dropping either. The text offers the actor one great internal model for the condition. Benvolio, the play's resident witness, is forever being drafted from spectator into participant and then into testimony, until in 3.1 he must narrate the fight he watched to the Prince who will judge it, a bystander's account delivered under the pressure of partial sight. Every promenade actor is Benvolio: inside the event, implicated by proximity, and responsible for a story the audience around him has each seen from a different place.

The fight scenes expose the craft problem in its sharpest form. Stage combat is a technology of distance and angle, built for a house that watches from one side; a surrounding crowd collapses the angles, sees the pulled blow from behind, and stands within the margin the choreography reserves for error. Whitman's reservation about Houses, fights that looked too choreographed, records the audience's side of the same difficulty: at arm's length, illusion cannot hide, so the violence must either be stylized openly or danced, the settlement SONDERimmersive reached by becoming dance theatre outright. The intimate scenes invert the problem. The potion speech played to a ring of fifty standing strangers, close enough to be touched, forbids the actor every resource of the large house, the projected climax, the rhetorical turn to the gallery, and demands the conversational truth that film acting made familiar. Critics of the small promenade productions praise exactly that quality when they find it, naturalism that dissolves the sense of performance, and its rarity in the record measures the demand. The immersive actor must be athlete, guide, witness and confidant in rotation, and the companies that sustain the form are the ones that rehearse the rotations as skills in themselves rather than discovering them in performance.

To the rotations belongs one more office, the host's. A promenade audience is close enough to speak, and sooner or later, in every run, someone does: a question at the ball, a joke offered to Mercutio, a child answering the Chorus in earnest. The seated actor is protected from the house by convention and darkness; the promenade actor works unprotected, inside a crowd licensed to move and murmur, and must absorb the intervention without breaking the fiction or punishing the spectator. Iris's performers, speaking of the garden seasons, describe an environment that can never be predicted and is different every single day, with the surroundings themselves influencing the playing, and the description holds for every site in the survey. The skill is closer to the original companies' platea craft than to anything the modern rehearsal room teaches: direct address maintained as a living negotiation, the crowd read as a partner, the text held firmly enough to survive being answered. Companies that cast for it thrive in the form; companies that cast only for the voice discover, at the first rainy Saturday, that the voice was the smaller half of the job.

## The Practical Ledger: Focus, Access, Bodies, Weather

The challenges of the mode are not footnotes to its aesthetics; they are the same problem in administrative dress. Focus first: a moving crowd must be re-gathered and re-hushed at every station, and each re-gathering costs minutes and momentum, which is why promenade texts compress even when companies swear fidelity. Audibility second: verse spoken in open air, in gardens, garages and warehouses reaches the back of a standing crowd unevenly, and the spectators worst served are those the walking has tired most. Sightlines third: a promenade spectator's view depends on arrival order and height, so the form distributes its best experiences unequally, a fact in tension with its democratic publicity. Mobility fourth: the form as standardly practised excludes or burdens spectators who cannot stand for two hours or walk a route, and the honest companies, New York Classical with its seated week, Iris with its garden seating at stations, build the exception into the run rather than apologizing for the rule. Weather, licensing, neighbourhood noise and capacity complete the ledger: an outdoor promenade plays under permits a seated house never needs, to a crowd capped by the width of its narrowest path, which is why the form's economics belong to small companies and festivals rather than to the repertory mainstream, a constraint it shares with the older mobility of [touring and regional productions](/2028/04/21/touring-regional-productions/), which solved the same arithmetic by moving the play instead of the audience.

Behind the ledger's public entries lies a rehearsal reality that seated production never faces: the route itself must be rehearsed as choreography, and the company that will move six hundred people through a garden or a park needs a front-of-house operation closer to crowd management than to ushering. Stewards must be cast, costumed or badged, briefed on sightlines and pinch points, and empowered to hold a scene's start against a straggling tail, because a promenade scene that begins before its audience has arrived plays its exposition to a path. Rain is not an inconvenience but a dramaturgical event, changing the ground, the sound, the crowd's temper and the tomb scene's sky. Companies build wet-weather variants of routes the way touring companies build understudy plots, and the variants, like the understudies, tend to be less loved and more revealing: a promenade that cannot survive its own weather was a fair-weather argument. These logistics rarely appear in reviews and never in manifestos, but they decide productions as surely as any interpretation, and the survey's survivors are, without exception, companies that treated the movement of their audience as a discipline to be staffed rather than a metaphor to be announced.

Money runs through the ledger as well. The survey displays the form's two economies in their purest states. At one pole, New York Classical's promenade was free, seven hundred performances' worth of civic subsidy and philanthropy buying the walking crowd its parks; at the other, Secret Cinema's immersion was a premium product, forty-nine pounds a head at five thousand heads a night, the audience's membership of Verona sold as a luxury good with bars and rides inside it. Iris Theatre's churchyard economy sits between, a charity's modest ticket multiplied by ten thousand summer spectators. The economics are not external to the aesthetics. Free promenade can afford fidelity, since nothing but the play must be sold; premium immersion must sell the world as well as the evening, and the world, unlike the play, can be photographed, merchandised and repeated at scale. Alston's audit of the experience economy is, at bottom, a reading of these two ledgers, and the survey's textual outcomes follow the money with embarrassing fidelity: the freer the ticket, the fuller the text.

A further entry in the ledger concerns the youngest spectators, because promenade has become one of the principal doors through which school audiences enter this play. New York Classical reports playing to nearly two thousand children under eighteen each season, and the logic is plain: a form that moves the body recruits attention that a seat, a darkened room and a third hour of verse would tax past breaking, and the play's public scenes, its fights, feasts and chases, are precisely the scenes a young audience reads first and best. The educational settlement cuts both ways, and honesty requires the cut to be recorded. What the school promenade teaches supremely is the play as civic event, the feud as a thing crowds do; what it teaches least is the play as verbal event, since the chamber scenes, where the poetry thickens into philosophy, are the scenes the form serves worst and shortens first. A generation meeting the tragedy on its feet will know Verona better than any seated generation, and may know the verse less. Whether that is a bargain depends on whether the feet lead back to the book, and the companies that programme study materials beside their routes are voting, with their budgets, that the walk and the reading belong to one education.

None of this is an argument against the mode. It is the argument for judging it as a set of trades rather than a flavour of modernity. Every cost in the ledger purchases a named gain elsewhere in the play: the walk that tires the audience is the walk that makes the feud's geography felt; the standing that burdens the duet scenes is the standing that makes the crowd scenes true. A production earns its promenade when the gains land on the public architecture and the losses are managed, by proximity, pacing and compression honestly admitted, at the chamber door.

## The Documentation Problem: Reviewing and Remembering a Moving Play

One difficulty shadows the whole survey and belongs in the open: this form is hard to document, and the difficulty biases the record. A seated production leaves a stable object behind it, a promptbook, a design, a set of performances that differ nightly only in temperature. A promenade production leaves a route, a set of permissions, and the testimony of witnesses who each saw the event from a moving position inside it. A free-roam production leaves something weaker still, thousands of partial evenings no two alike, from which reviewers assemble verdicts with the candour, or without it, that the form demands. The reviews quoted in this article should be read with that caution attached. Whitman walked Houses as its spectators walked it and reports the experience of a participant, which is the only honest report the form allows. The Utah Review's account of the drive-in Verona is the testimony of one car among many, parked in one of three territories, nearer some pairs than others. Movie Marker's three stars for Secret Cinema measure an evening its reviewer concedes was shaped by crowd, cost and weather as much as by art. In each case the critic is a witness before being a judge, and the survey has weighted the testimony accordingly, preferring dated mechanics, who stood where, what moved, what was cut, to verdicts about evenings no second person can have shared.

The archival consequence matters for the play's long history. Performance history is written from records, and the promenade record is thin by construction: small companies, local reviewers, company websites, planning documents. The Friar's warning to Romeo, "Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast" (2.3), might stand as the archivist's motto for the whole mode: the faster the audience moves, the less of the event survives to be examined. This article's method has been to build only on what could be checked, company records, dated reviews, licensing papers, published scholarship, and to leave the form's many rumoured productions, the one-night warehouse Veronas and festival walkabouts that no dated notice preserves, outside the survey's rows. The rows that remain are few. Their fewness is itself the finding that the next section carries into the verdict: for a play performed somewhere in the world every week, the documented immersive record is a handful of small rooms, two parks, a churchyard and a festival of sixty thousand, which is to say that the mode's reputation in connection with this play runs far ahead of its archive.

## Verdict: Citizens, Not Explorers

The record supports a clear verdict. Immersive staging offers this play one great gift, the restoration of its audience as citizens of Verona, participants in the crowd whose behaviour the tragedy exists to examine, and the gift is delivered reliably by guided promenade with the text intact, at churchyard scale and park scale alike. The same staging, pursued as free-roam exploration or surrounding spectacle, takes back more than it gives: the language thins to narration, paraphrase or a film, the chamber scenes scatter, and the audience's celebrated freedom resolves into consumption of a world. Levenson's edition supplies the standard by which the choice is made: the play's effects are verbal events, a sonnet exchanged hand to hand, an argument with the lark, a speech that talks a girl through terror, and any staging of Romeo and Juliet is finally a device for delivering those events to hearers. Machon is answered on the public scenes, where co-presence is knowledge. Alston is answered on the whole map, where the terms of participation must be audited scene by scene. Gurr is answered most fully of all: the yard at the Globe held a crowd that stood inside the event and heard every word, because it stood still. The promenade productions that deserve the play are the ones that remember both halves of that inheritance. They move their citizens through Verona's streets so that the feud can happen around them, and then, at the orchard, the chamber and the tomb, they do the oldest thing in theatre. They gather them, and hush them, and let the verse across the last few feet.

The test for any future production follows from the record, and it can be stated as plainly as the survey states its rows. Ask what the audience is given to be: citizens under orders, guests at a feast, jurors at an audit, and the production is working with the play's grain. Ask whether the audience can still hear a whispered duet from where it stands, and the production has priced its chamber scenes honestly. Ask what reaches the audience besides the world, a summons, a colour, a photograph that cannot be taken, and the answer will confess whether the text survived its setting. The play will go on being offered to the immersive stage, because its crowds are an open invitation no generation of directors will refuse. The productions that deserve it will be the ones that accept the invitation on the play's own terms, which are written, as they always were, in the verse.

That is the whole of the matter, and it is enough. Immersive staging is neither the play's future nor its betrayal; it is a lens that magnifies one half of the work and dims the other, and lenses are judged by the honesty with which their distortion is declared. The churchyard, the park, the warehouse and the garage each declared it in their practice, gathering their crowds for the verse after walking them through the world. The festival that declared it least, wrapping a film in a city, still proved the crowd scenes' appetite for citizens. Between those poles the record is complete enough to judge, and the judgment stands: give this play an audience that can be assembled, implicated and finally hushed, and the oldest staging condition in its history turns out to be the one its newest directors keep reinventing, because the play was built for it from the first Prologue.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch immersive survey, and what does it record about each production?

The InsightCrunch immersive survey is this article's table of the documented immersive and promenade stagings of the play, and it records for each production the company, place and dates, the form the staging took, what the audience physically did, and what happened to Shakespeare's text. Those five columns are the survey's argument in miniature. Reading down them shows the pattern the article defends: productions that kept the audience together and the text intact, the churchyard promenade and the park promenade, delivered the play whole, while productions that freed the audience to roam or surrounded it with spectacle delivered a retelling, a digest or a film. The survey also records the form's notable absences, including Punchdrunk, whose immersive Shakespeare is Macbeth.

### Q: What is the difference between promenade staging and free-roam immersive staging?

The difference is who controls the audience's path. In promenade staging the whole audience moves together, following the actors from one playing place to the next, so every spectator receives the same scenes in the same order and the play remains a single shared event. In free-roam immersive staging, spectators are released individually into a built environment where scenes proceed simultaneously in different rooms, and each person assembles a partial version of the work by choosing where to go. The distinction decides what a production can be: promenade is a staging method for a text, testable against the text line by line, while free-roam is an environment derived from a source, judged by atmosphere. Romeo and Juliet, a play of sequential speeches, survives the first and dissolves in the second.

### Q: What does Panoramic Theatre mean in New York Classical Theatre's practice?

Panoramic Theatre is the name New York Classical Theatre gives its own promenade method: the audience follows the actors from place to place around a park, relocating about every twenty minutes, so that the landscape supplies a sequence of stages and the crowd's movement supplies the scene changes. The company, founded by Stephen Burdman in 2000, built its free outdoor Shakespeare practice on the method across New York's parks. Its discipline is the point. A Panoramic audience never wanders and never settles; it is led, gathered and re-gathered on a timetable, which is why the method can carry a complete text where freer forms cannot. The company's 2018 Romeo and Juliet is the survey's clearest demonstration of the method at work on this play.

### Q: How many actors performed New York Classical Theatre's 2018 Romeo and Juliet?

Six actors performed the whole play. Ian Antal played Romeo and Connie Castanzo played Juliet, while four colleagues doubled across the rest of Verona: Vivia Font played Lady Capulet and Benvolio, Ian Gould played Tybalt, the Nurse and Friar Laurence, Nick Salamone played Capulet and Mercutio, and Clay Storseth played the Prince, Montague, Paris and the Apothecary. The doubling was an interpretive instrument and not only an economy. An audience that watches the same faces change households learns the feud's arbitrariness in its own memory of the evening: the enemy is a neighbour in another coat. The company carried the production from Central Park to four further parks in the summer of 2018, playing to more than four thousand people in Central Park alone, with every performance free.

### Q: What was Take a Seat week in the 2018 New York Classical Theatre run?

Take a Seat week was the company's deliberate accessibility variant, staged during the Rockefeller Park engagement in late June 2018, in which the audience remained in one place for the entire hundred-minute performance instead of following the actors. It was designed for spectators with physical challenges for whom the walking form is a barrier, and the company encouraged blankets and portable chairs for those performances. Its importance for the survey is theoretical as well as practical. By translating its own promenade production into a seated form, New York Classical conceded that mobility is the method's means rather than its meaning: the meaning, an audience placed inside the public world of the play in a real public place, survived the removal of the walking.

### Q: Which play did Iris Theatre choose for its first outdoor promenade summer show?

Iris Theatre chose this play. The company, founded by Daniel Winder in 2007 as the resident company at St Paul's Church in Covent Garden, opened its first outdoor promenade summer season in 2009 with Romeo and Juliet, Laura Wickham playing Juliet and Sam Donnelly playing Romeo, performed through the church's gardens with the audience following the action from station to station. The choice suited the site with unusual precision: a churchyard offered consecrated ground for the Friar's cell, the secret wedding and the burial, and garden paths made the feud's territorial division a matter of yards the audience crossed on foot. The production founded a durable summer institution, and the company went on to play to some ten thousand spectators a summer across its promenade seasons.

### Q: Where was Secret Cinema's Romeo + Juliet held, and how many people attended?

Secret Cinema's Romeo + Juliet was held outdoors in Gunnersbury Park in west London in August 2018, where the company built Verona Beach, the world of Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film, across the park. The scale set it apart from every other production in the survey: about five thousand spectators attended each night, and more than sixty thousand attended across fifteen showings, with tickets starting at forty-nine pounds. Spectators were assigned to the Montague or Capulet house before arrival, issued identities, and released into a festival of bars, fairground rides, rival territories, a gospel choir and a mansion ball. The evening ended with the audience seated on the grass before a screening of the film, with actors performing scenes beneath the screen.

### Q: Why is Secret Cinema's Romeo + Juliet a boundary case in the immersive survey?

Because its immersion surrounded the play rather than staging it. The audience's membership of Verona Beach was genuine and effective: assigned houses, rival colours, a ball to dance at, streets to roam. But Shakespeare's words reached that audience overwhelmingly through Luhrmann's film, screened at the evening's end, while the live performance supplied atmosphere, episodes and social membership around it. The survey therefore files the event as a boundary case, an immersive first act wrapped around a film. The classification is not a dismissal. The event demonstrated, at a scale no theatre company could match, that the play's public architecture of feast and feud can carry thousands of participants inside the fiction. It also demonstrated the limit: the chamber scenes, where the play actually lives, cannot be supplied by a world.

### Q: What changed when SONDERimmersive's Through Yonder Window returned in 2021?

The audience stopped walking and watched from cars. Through Yonder Window, SONDERimmersive's dance-theatre interpretation of the play directed and choreographed by Graham Brown, had first played in 2020 inside a parking garage at the Gateway complex in Salt Lake City, where roughly a thousand spectators moved among its stations. The expanded version of April 2021, presented with Utah Presents, moved to the upper deck of the Alsco parking garage, where each party watched from its own vehicle. The experiment isolated the form's components with unusual clarity. Verona's geography survived intact, arranged in three territories of church, street and households, while the audience's defining immersive act, physical movement through the fiction, was removed entirely and replaced by distribution across a viewpoint.

### Q: How did the drive-in audience hear Through Yonder Window in 2021?

Through the car radio. The 2021 production carried its narration and Wachira Waigwa-Stone's composed score to each vehicle by radio, while eight performers played the story in movement across the parking deck's three zones. The radio did the work that promenade staging usually assigns to proximity: it unified a dispersed audience into a single act of attention, and it restored a private channel between the work and each spectator that the drive-in format had seemed to abolish. The arrangement also completed the production's demonstration of the form's limits. Narration can guide a scattered audience through a story, but it replaces the verse it summarizes, and the play's duets and soliloquies, which need a gathered hush rather than a broadcast, were the element the format could not recover.

### Q: Why is Punchdrunk's immersive Shakespeare Macbeth rather than Romeo and Juliet?

Because free-roam immersion and verse drama exclude each other, and Punchdrunk's practice is honest about the exclusion. Sleep No More, the company's Macbeth-derived event at New York's McKittrick Hotel from 2011 to 2025, placed masked, silent spectators in a hundred rooms of simultaneous action and carried its story through choreography, design and one-to-one encounters, with virtually none of Shakespeare's text performed. A plot of corridors, secrets and apparitions survives that treatment; a plot carried by a shared sonnet, an argument about a lark and a girl reasoning herself through terror does not. The company's production record accordingly holds no staging of Romeo and Juliet. The absence is the survey's most eloquent row: the form's defining company looked at the canon and chose the tragedy whose meaning can be walked through rather than heard.

### Q: What were Secret Cinema's spectators given to make them members of a house?

They were given an allegiance, an identity and a costume. Before arrival, each spectator was assigned to the house of Montague or the house of Capulet, issued an identity and a police summons in the fiction's terms, and instructed to dress in the assigned house's colours, Hawaiian shirts and angel wings for some, red and black or blue and white for others. Inside Verona Beach the assignment had social consequences: rival territories to drink in, a mansion ball to enter, gangs whose brawls a guest watched as a partisan rather than a tourist. The device is the survey's largest proof that the play's public architecture is participatory by design. The feud requires only sorting to become experience; five thousand strangers needed no rehearsal to feel it, because the text's crowd scenes had already written their parts.

### Q: How is the orchard scene played when the audience follows the actors?

It is played close, inside a ring, with the audience stationed as overhearers. The orchard scene (2.2) is written as concealment and listening: Romeo below the window, Juliet above, each hearing the other imperfectly. Promenade productions gather their crowd around and beneath the playing place, slow the evening's rhythm, and let the scene's small acoustic work at close quarters, a solution as old as the platea, where the soliloquy was a confidence shared with standing neighbours. The scene exposes the form's tax more clearly than any other. Spectators who arrive late from the previous station, or who stand at the ring's edge in open air, lose the asides and half-lines on which the duet's tension runs, so the productions that serve the scene best are the ones that narrow the crowd's freedom precisely there.

### Q: What work does the Chorus do in a promenade production?

The Chorus does the usher's work in verse. Shakespeare's Chorus is a guide figure who belongs half to the fiction and half to the audience, contracting with the crowd, promising a city and asking the hearers to supply its defects with their thoughts. Promenade productions need that function in practical form: someone must lead the audience from station to station, settle it, and frame what it is about to see, and companies distribute the office among actors doubling the Chorus, stewards at the route's edges, or the leading performers themselves. The practical necessity illuminates the text. The Chorus was never decorative; it was the mechanism by which a standing, mobile audience in an open yard was organized into attention, and promenade staging, in needing the mechanism again, recovers the reason the speeches exist.

### Q: Why do promenade productions tend to run shorter than seated ones?

Because walking consumes the evening. Every relocation of a crowd costs minutes of movement, re-gathering and re-settling, and a production that spends those minutes must recover them from the text, from the pace, or from the scene count. New York Classical Theatre's complete Romeo and Juliet ran about a hundred minutes against the two hours and more of a seated full text, an economy achieved by tempo and doubling. SONDERimmersive compressed the play to about an hour of narration and dance. The pattern across the survey is consistent: mobility is paid for out of the text, and the productions differ mainly in how honestly they acknowledge the exchange. The shortening is not a scandal in itself. It becomes one only when the marketing promises the whole play and the route quietly delivers a digest.

### Q: Can spectators with limited mobility attend a promenade performance?

Yes, where the company has designed for them, and the survey's record shows the design taking several forms. New York Classical Theatre built a complete seated variant, Take a Seat week, in which the whole performance played to a stationary audience. Iris Theatre's garden format seats spectators at its stations. Riot Act's Houses offered seating on request for spectators who could not stand through its two hours. The underlying point is structural: a form that defines itself by audience movement must decide whether movement is its meaning or its method. The companies that treat movement as method can translate the experience for every body; the companies that treat it as meaning exclude by definition. The play itself, written for a yard where standing was one option among several, supports the translators.

### Q: What does the audience do during the ball scene in an immersive production?

It attends the ball. In promenade and immersive stagings the audience is recruited as Capulet's guests: standing or dancing at the feast's edge, masked where the production masks it, watching the dancers and becoming the crowd within which the lovers' meeting must hide. The recruitment is textual rather than decorative. The scene's machinery requires guests, because the shared sonnet the lovers exchange (1.5.92-105) is a private form performed in public, and its danger, Tybalt's recognition, its cover and its witnesses are all supplied by the room. A seated audience observes that machinery; a standing audience is part of it. The ball is consequently the scene in which immersive productions most often earn their claims, and the one cited most warmly in reviews of the churchyard, warehouse and park versions alike.

### Q: Why does the tomb scene demand stillness from a promenade audience?

Because the last scene is a funnel of solos and duets over two bodies, and its effects are all effects of concentration. Paris's vigil, Romeo's address to the dead, Juliet's waking and the Prince's closing audit follow one another without crowd cover, in speeches that assume a room of hearers looking at one small space for an unbroken stretch. An audience still arriving, choosing positions or murmuring through those speeches spends the capital the whole evening has saved. Productions that serve the scene therefore choreograph its approach: the route slows, the crowd is gathered into a ring and hushed before the vault, and the walking body of the audience becomes the monument's architecture. The demand is the play's, not the form's invention; promenade merely has to earn, by discipline, the stillness a seated house gets free.

### Q: Is theatre in the round an immersive form?

No, on the taxonomy this article defends. Theatre in the round surrounds a fixed playing area with seated spectators who do not move, choose no path and enter no fiction; its intensities are the older intensities of the arena stage, closeness and shared visibility among the audience. The immersive label is nevertheless borrowed for such staging with growing looseness, because the word markets well. The borrowing has a cost: it empties the category the survey depends on. If every close staging counts as immersive, the genuine experiments in audience movement, the churchyard promenade, the panoramic park, the garage stations, lose their distinct record and their distinct problems. Precision about the word is what allows the real form, with its real gains for this play's public scenes, to be argued for at all.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch immersive survey to argue that the play's public scenes gain more from immersion than its private scenes?

An essay should read the survey's form and text columns against each other and let the correlation carry the claim. The productions that moved a guided crowd through the whole text, Iris in the churchyard and New York Classical in the parks, gained most exactly where the play is public: the brawl witnessed from inside the crowd, the ball staffed by audience-guests, the street fight received by implicated citizens. The productions that freed or dispersed the audience, Secret Cinema's Verona Beach and the drive-in Verona, kept the public architecture, geography, feast and feud, and surrendered the private architecture to narration, choreography or film. Set out in rows, the survey shows the trade the essay needs: immersion buys the crowd scenes with the chamber scenes, every time, and the price list is the evidence.
