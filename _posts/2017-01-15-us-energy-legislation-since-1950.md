## The two tracks of American energy law

A reader who finishes this guide should be able to do four things. Name the major federal energy statutes since 1950 in order, from the atomic statutes of the nineteen fifties through the recovery and export measures of the twenty tens. Explain the arc that runs from regulating prices to structuring markets to subsidising technologies. Identify the recurring pattern that organizes the field: supply crises produce production and deregulation statutes, while pollution and climate concern produce efficiency and technology-subsidy statutes. And recognise that the two tracks have almost never been reconciled inside a single law. That is the One Test for this article, and it is also the reason this article exists as the master guide for the domain. The cluster contains nine statute-level articles, each of which walks a single law in depth. This one supplies the domain-level view that makes each of those statutes legible, and it serves as the hub to which the cluster links back so that no single-statute article has to re-explain the whole field.

![Timeline of major US energy statutes from 1954 to 2015](/assets/images/blog/blog-40.webp)

Two tracks, rarely joined: American energy legislation runs on a security track that responds to supply shocks with production and deregulation and an environmental track that responds to pollution and climate with efficiency and technology subsidy, and the statutes that tried to serve both, in 1978 and 2005, are precisely the ones remembered as incoherent. That sentence is the namable claim of this guide, and everything that follows is the evidence for it. The security track begins with the decision to commercialise atomic power in 1954, runs through the emergency allocation machinery of 1973, the stockpile and fuel economy standards of 1975, the synthetic fuels gamble of 1980, and the long unwinding of price controls that ended with natural gas decontrol in 1989 and the crude export repeal in 2015. The environmental track begins with the review and clean-air statutes of 1969 and 1970, runs through the conservation and tax-credit measures of 1978, the renewable production credit of 1992, and the efficiency and fuel-mandate measures of 2007. The two tracks share the same decades, the same committees, and often the same bills, but they answer different questions. One asks how the nation keeps fuel flowing and prices bearable when supply is threatened. The other asks how the nation limits the damage that producing and burning fuel inflicts on air, land, and climate. Congress has answered the first question with production incentives and deregulation, and the second with efficiency standards and technology subsidy, with remarkable consistency across seventy years.

### What pattern organizes American energy law?

Supply shocks produce production and deregulation laws, while pollution and climate concern produce efficiency and technology subsidy laws. Congress has run these two tracks side by side since the 1970s, and the rare statutes that tried to serve both at once, in 1978 and 2005, are the ones remembered as incoherent.

The arc from price regulation to market structure to technology subsidy is real, but it is not a clean succession of eras. Price regulation dominated the crisis decade of the nineteen seventies, when the federal government set the price of oil at the wellhead, allocated petroleum among refiners and regions, and held interstate natural gas prices below market clearing levels. Market structuring dominated the nineteen eighties and nineties, when Congress dismantled those controls, opened wholesale electricity transmission to competition, and created the independent power industry through a purchase obligation. Technology subsidy has dominated the period since 1992, when the renewable production credit began paying generators for each kilowatt hour from qualifying resources, and it accelerated through the tax packages of 2005, the recovery spending of 2009, and the later statutes this guide names but does not characterise. Yet each phase bled into the next. Subsidies for oil and gas through percentage depletion and the expensing of intangible drilling costs never left the code, even during the deregulatory wave. Price controls on natural gas survived until the first day of 1993, long after the market-structuring era was supposed to have begun. Efficiency standards written in 1975 outlived the price controls enacted in the same statute by decades. The honest telling, and the one this guide attempts, presents the three-phase movement as an argument with named counterexamples rather than as settled chronology. A later section takes up that complication directly, with the counterexamples sourced.

This guide walks the sequence statute by statute in one dense pass each, then presents the findable artifact, a table of the full seventy-year run with each law classified as price regulation, market structure, or technology subsidy alongside the crisis or concern that produced it. It then addresses the complication, the tidy periodization that the evidence resists, and closes by explaining how the nine cluster articles read from this hub. The fact window for substantive treatment ends with the 2015 crude export repeal; three later statutes appear by name and year only, with their full treatment reserved to the linked cluster articles. Every figure in the guide is attributed to a named source, and every date is the signing date from the Statutes at Large unless the short title itself supplies the year, in which case the guide says so explicitly.

Why a master article at all, rather than nine statute profiles and a timeline? Because the statutes are not self-interpreting, and a reader who meets them one by one will miss the pattern that gives each of them meaning. The windfall profit tax looks punitive until the decontrol bargain explains it. The Fuel Use Act looks irrational until the scarcity forecast explains it. The 1992 act's two halves look unrelated until the tracks explain why competition and subsidy could share a statute. The master article supplies the pattern once, so that no cluster article must supply it nine times, and it supplies the sequence once, so that each cluster article can begin in medias res. That is the hub function in its economic form: the domain-level view is a fixed cost, paid here, and the statute-level articles are the marginal readers who free-ride on it. The series thesis thread, as the brief defines it, is exactly this division of labor made explicit.

The guide also serves a second function, which is to discipline the reader's expectations about what legislation can do. The sequence contains an expensive failure, the Synthetic Fuels Corporation, a twenty-year unwinding, the gas decontrol, a repealed prohibition, the Fuel Use Act, and an unmet federal obligation, the waste repository. It also contains durable achievements: the licensing framework created in 1954, the liability system created in 1957, the stockpile created in 1975, the fuel economy standards created in 1975, the wholesale markets opened in 1992, and the production credit created in 1992. The tracks explain both the failures and the achievements, because the failures cluster where the tracks were forced together without a shared logic and the achievements cluster where a single track's logic was allowed to operate. That is the argument the guide will now substantiate, statute by statute.

## How to read the sequence

This guide organizes seventy years of lawmaking along two axes, and the reader should understand both before entering the sequence. The first axis is motive, the two tracks. The security track answers the question of supply: how does the nation keep fuel flowing and prices bearable when foreign suppliers, market failures, or physical scarcity threaten? Its instruments are production incentives, market structuring, and deregulation. The environmental track answers the question of damage: how does the nation limit what producing and burning fuel does to air, land, water, and climate? Its instruments are efficiency standards, emissions limits, and technology subsidy. The tracks are not parties, ideologies, or eras. Both have commanded bipartisan majorities, both have produced statutes signed by presidents of both parties, and both appear in every decade of the sequence. They are durable motives, and the guide's claim is that American energy law is best read as their seventy-year interaction.

The second axis is instrument, the three columns of the artifact table. Price regulation means the government sets or caps prices, as the 1973 allocation act did for petroleum and the 1978 gas act did for wellhead tiers. Market structure means the government defines the rules of participation, as the 1954 act did by licensing private nuclear operators, the 1974 act did by splitting promotion from regulation, PURPA did with the purchase obligation, and the 1992 act did with transmission access. Technology subsidy means the government pays to favor particular technologies, as the 1978 tax credits did, the 1992 production credit does, and the 2005 tax title did on a large scale. The columns describe what the law does; the tracks describe why Congress did it. A single statute can occupy one column while serving either track, and the interesting cases occupy one column while serving both. The 1975 conservation act extended price controls, a security-track instrument, while creating efficiency standards that the environmental track would later claim. The 1992 act structured markets for the security track's competition logic and subsidised renewables for the environmental track's diversification logic, in separate titles that did not interfere with each other.

Track assignments in this guide are interpretive, and the guide states them as arguments rather than as facts. The fuel economy standards illustrate why. CAFE was created in 1975 as a security measure to cut oil imports; it is administered as an environmental measure that also cuts emissions. The guide classifies the 2007 act that raised the standards as technology subsidy in the environmental tradition, and it defends that assignment while acknowledging the security parentage. Readers who would assign it differently are not misreading the statute; they are weighting a different decade of its life. The same interpretive openness applies to PURPA, which the security track claims for its capacity additions and the environmental track claims for its renewable qualifying facilities, and to the 2005 act, which both tracks claim and neither fully owns. The table's single-column classifications are therefore starting points for analysis, and the complication section shows where they strain.

How to use the table. Read down the year column for the order the One Test requires; read across the classification column for the instrument arc from price regulation through market structure to technology subsidy; read the crisis column for the motive, and notice how rarely the motive column changes even as the instrument column does. Supply shocks recur across all three instrument phases, which is why the tracks explain more than the phases. The environmental entries cluster differently: the 1969-70 statutes precede any shock, the 1978 and 1992 measures arrive in calm markets, and the 2007 act passes without a supply crisis. That asymmetry, security motives tracking shocks while environmental motives follow their own logic, is the empirical core of the two-track thesis, and the table lets a reader verify it without taking the guide's word.

A final note on scope. The guide's fact window for substantive treatment closes with the 2015 crude export repeal. Three later statutes, the Energy Act of 2020, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021, and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, appear in the sequence pass and the artifact table by name and year only, with public law numbers permitted in the table and no facts about their provisions, costs, or outcomes. Their full treatment belongs to the linked cluster articles. This scope rule keeps the hub honest about what it verifies and what it merely indexes, and it follows the series practice for post-window statutes. Every figure elsewhere in the guide is attributed to a named source with its period, and every signing date comes from the Statutes at Large unless the short title supplies the year, in which case the guide says so.

The two axes combine into a matrix the reader can apply to any statute in the table, and the guide works three examples to show how. Take the 1975 conservation act: its instrument column reads price regulation, because Title IV extended the EPAA controls, but its motive spans both tracks, security in the stockpile and the import-cutting standards, environment in the conservation programs the same titles authorised. The matrix captures what a single column cannot, which is why the guide's prose supplements the table rather than repeating it. Take PURPA: its instrument is market structure and its motive is security with environmental side effects, the purchase obligation adding capacity while favoring cogeneration and renewables. Take the 2005 act: its instruments are market structure and technology subsidy together, and its motives are both tracks undisguised, which is why the table strains and the prose must do the work. The matrix is the guide's recommended reading method: locate the statute in the table, read its row for the instrument and the crisis, then read the sequence pass for the motive the row compresses. Where the two disagree, the disagreement is the interesting fact, and the complication section collects the most important disagreements in one place.

## The atomic opening, 1954 to 1957

The sequence opens with the [Atomic Energy Act of 1954](/2016/09/15/atomic-energy-act-1954-guide/), Public Law 83-703, 68 Stat. 919, signed August 30, 1954, by President Eisenhower. Its long title reads "An Act to amend the Atomic Energy Act of 1946, as amended, and for other purposes," and the 1954 measure amended and superseded the 1946 regime rather than repealing it cleanly, replacing a government atomic monopoly with a framework in which private firms could own and operate nuclear facilities under federal license. Before 1954, the Atomic Energy Commission held a monopoly over the production and use of fissionable material, a wartime arrangement extended into peacetime by the 1946 act. The 1954 act broke that monopoly. It authorised the Commission to license private parties to possess and use nuclear material and to build and operate nuclear facilities, replacing the government monopoly with a licensing regime for a commercial industry. The security logic was straightforward. Atomic power promised a domestic source of electricity independent of imported fuel, and the federal government, which had built the underlying science at public expense during the war, would transfer enough of it to private industry to seed a commercial industry. The environmental track did not yet exist as a legislative force; the air and water statutes that would later constrain every generating project were still a decade and a half away.

The commercial terms mattered as much as the legal permission. Private capital would not finance reactors without a predictable licensing regime, and the 1954 act supplied one, while dividing the Commission's functions between the promotion of atomic development and the regulation of its hazards. That division would later be judged a failure and undone in 1974, but in 1954 it reflected the confidence of the security track: the same agency could both push the technology forward and keep it safe, because the national interest in atomic power was taken to align with careful regulation rather than to conflict with it. The statute's real achievement was architectural. It created the legal possibility of a private nuclear power industry in the United States, licensed by the Commission under the framework the act established. Whether that industry would be economic was a separate question, and one the statute did not answer. The answer would come from the liability problem, which the 1954 act left unsolved and which threatened to strangle the commercial opening before it produced a single operating plant.

The liability problem deserves emphasis because it explains why the sequence needs two atomic statutes rather than one. A reactor accident of the worst kind could impose damages larger than any utility's balance sheet, and private insurers, asked to price a risk with no actuarial history and a catastrophic tail, declined the business. The 1954 act's licensing framework was therefore a door with no traffic: legally open, commercially impassable. Congress took three years to supply the missing piece, and the delay is instructive. The security track's production logic required not just permission but bankability, and bankability required the government to absorb the risk the market would not. Price-Anderson's two-tier design, private primary insurance plus a federal secondary backstop with capped total liability, became the template for how the security track handles uninsurable strategic risks: socialise the tail, privatise the routine. The renewals of 1965, 1975, 1988, and 2005 extended the template across five decades, each renewal a quiet reaffirmation that commercial nuclear power still could not stand on private insurance alone. The guide's warning not to date the current regime to 1957 alone is a warning against compressing that fifty-year legislative habit into a single founding moment. Each renewal was its own political act, and the 2005 renewal's placement inside the Energy Policy Act of that year tied the liability framework's future to the both-track bargaining of the mid-2000s.

There is a further reason the atomic opening matters out of proportion to the generating capacity it had yet produced. The 1954 and 1957 acts established the pattern by which the security track creates industries: define the legal form, absorb the unpriceable risk, and let private capital build. The pattern recurs with the purchase obligation in 1978, which defined the legal form of the independent generator, and with the production credit in 1992, which absorbed the price risk of early renewable development. In each case Congress discovered that wanting an industry was not enough; the law had to supply the specific missing market, whether a license, a buyer, or a backstop. The atomic statutes are also the first case in the sequence where the environmental track would later arrive as a constraint on the security track's creation. The waste obligation, the accident risk, and the thermal and mining footprint of the nuclear fuel cycle all became subjects of environmental law after the industry existed, which meant the industry experienced environmental regulation as a retrofit rather than as a design condition. That sequencing, production first and constraint later, explains much of the bitterness in the decades-long argument over nuclear power's place in the American generating mix, and it begins here, with two Eisenhower-era statutes that imagined no environmental track at all.

That liability problem produced the second statute in the sequence, the Price-Anderson Act of 1957, Public Law 85-256, 71 Stat. 576, signed September 2, 1957, by President Eisenhower. Price-Anderson was an amendment to the 1954 act, adding section 170, codified at 42 U.S.C. section 2210, and its purpose was narrow and decisive: to make commercial nuclear power insurable. A catastrophic reactor accident could produce damages far beyond the capacity of any private insurer or any single utility to cover, and without a federal backstop no utility would accept the risk of operating a large plant. The 1957 act created a two-tier system in which operators carried a primary layer of private insurance and the federal government stood behind a secondary layer, capping total liability and channeling claims into a defined compensation framework. The design socialised the tail risk that the market could not price while leaving ordinary operational risk with the operator. Renewals followed in 1965, 1975, 1988, and 2005, and the guide is explicit that the liability regime in force in later decades should not be dated to 1957 alone; each renewal extended and adjusted the framework, and the 2005 renewal arrived inside the Energy Policy Act of that year. But the founding move belongs to 1957, and its logic belongs to the security track in its purest form. The government wanted a domestic generating technology that reduced dependence on fuel whose supply could be interrupted, and it supplied the missing insurance market by statute.

Together the two atomic statutes illustrate the security track before the environmental track had any legislative existence. Both were production measures. Neither regulated prices; neither subsidised a technology through the tax code; both structured a market, the first by licensing private entry and the second by defining liability, so that private capital would build plants the government wanted for national reasons. The pattern would repeat across the decades: when supply or strategic independence was the concern, Congress reached for market-structuring and production tools. The environmental answer to nuclear power, a full accounting of waste, accident risk, and thermal pollution through review and emissions law, would arrive later and from different statutes, and it would constrain the industry the 1954 and 1957 acts had called into being. The waste obligation in particular would return as the subject of its own statute in 1982, when Congress confronted the federal promise to take title to spent fuel and found the promise unmet. The atomic opening therefore does double duty in the sequence: it is the first production push of the security track and the first industry that the environmental track would later regulate.

## The environmental statutes that constrained everything after

The environmental track enters the sequence before the first supply shock, and that ordering matters for the complication this guide takes up later. The National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, Public Law 91-190, 83 Stat. 852, was signed January 1, 1970, by President Nixon; the guide does not backdate the event to 1969, though the short title carries that year. NEPA imposed environmental review on federal decision-making, and for energy projects the consequence was procedural but profound. Any generating plant, transmission line, pipeline, or fuel-cycle facility that needed federal approval would henceforth have to account for its environmental consequences in a process open to public scrutiny and judicial challenge. The statute constrained every energy project afterward not by forbidding any particular technology but by forcing the government to consider environmental effects before acting, and by giving project opponents a forum in which to contest the adequacy of that consideration. Nuclear plants licensed by the independent regulator created in 1974, coal plants needing federal permits, and later the full range of subsidised renewable projects all moved through the review process NEPA created. The security track would spend the next half century arguing that the process delayed needed infrastructure; the environmental track would spend it defending the disclosure the process required. Both reactions confirm the statute's structural importance, and both illustrate the track dynamic in its purest form: the same procedure looks like obstruction to one track and like accountability to the other.

The Clean Air Act Amendments of 1970 supplied the substantive constraint that NEPA's procedure lacked, establishing federal air pollution regulation with enforceable limits. For the energy field the critical effect fell on the burning of fuel. Power plants and industrial boilers burning coal and oil faced federal emissions limits, with new sources held to performance standards stricter than those for existing plants. The amendments did not ban coal or oil, but they raised the cost of burning them and created a permanent regulatory interlocutor for every fossil generating investment. An investor choosing a fuel in 1971 faced a different calculus than in 1969, not because any fuel was forbidden but because the dirtiest combustion now carried a compliance cost the law would only increase. Later amendments would extend the framework further, but the 1970 act established the template: the federal government would set the environmental terms on which fuel could be converted to power, and the industry would comply, litigate, or seek legislative relief. The relief-seeking channel matters for the thesis, because it made environmental law a permanent participant in energy politics rather than a one-time intervention.

The two statutes belong together because they created the environmental track as a standing constraint rather than as a response to any single energy crisis. NEPA and the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments predate the 1973 oil shock by three years, which is why the tidy story of crisis-driven lawmaking needs the complication section. Environmental concern produced these laws without any supply interruption to motivate them. Smog, burning rivers, and visible industrial pollution supplied the politics; the oil embargo supplied nothing. That independence is the point. The environmental track has its own causes, and it would go on producing efficiency standards, tax credits, and technology subsidies on its own logic, sometimes in years when oil was cheap and supply was secure. The 1992 act, which paired wholesale electricity competition with the renewable production credit during an era of low oil prices, is the clearest later example, and the guide returns to it. For now the structural observation suffices: by the end of 1970, the United States had a commercial nuclear industry licensed under the 1954 act, a liability framework under the 1957 act, a universal environmental review procedure under NEPA, and a federal air pollution regulatory regime under the Clean Air Act amendments. The next shock would test all four at once, and Congress would answer with the most interventionist energy legislation in American history.

There is a further structural point worth making before the narrative moves to the crisis decade, because it explains why the environmental statutes punched above their apparent weight. Neither NEPA nor the 1970 amendments was written as energy legislation. NEPA was government-reform legislation that happened to catch energy projects in its net; the Clean Air Act amendments were public-health legislation that happened to regulate combustion. Their energy consequences were collateral, and collateral consequences are harder to bargain away than targeted ones. An industry coalition that might have defeated a bill titled to restrict power plants could not easily defeat a bill titled to require environmental review of federal actions or to clean the air. The environmental track's early strength came partly from this generality: it regulated energy without being energy legislation, which put its opponents in the position of arguing against clean air and informed government rather than against a specific energy measure. The security track learned the lesson, and later production measures would similarly be tucked into appropriations riders and tax titles, the 2015 export repeal being the purest example. The venue matters as much as the substance, and the environmental track chose its first venues well.

One further note on classification, since the artifact table must place each statute in a single column. NEPA and the 1970 amendments are market-structure measures in the table's three-way scheme, not because they set prices or paid subsidies but because they restructured the terms on which energy markets could operate. They imposed procedural and substantive conditions on investment without fixing prices and without writing checks. That classification keeps faith with the table's logic: price regulation means the government sets or caps prices, market structure means the government defines the rules of participation, and technology subsidy means the government pays to favor particular technologies. The environmental statutes of 1969 and 1970 defined rules of participation for every subsequent project, and they belong in the middle column.

## The crisis decade, 1973 to 1977

The Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act of 1973, Public Law 93-159, 87 Stat. 627, signed November 27, 1973, by President Nixon, is the purest price-regulation statute in the sequence and the first full expression of the security track. The Arab oil embargo that autumn quadrupled world oil prices and exposed the American economy's dependence on imported crude. Congress answered with allocation and price controls: the act directed the federal government to allocate petroleum products among refiners, distributors, and regions, and to hold prices below the levels the disrupted market would have set. The theory was that a sudden supply shock should not be allowed to reprice the entire economy overnight, and that the government could distribute the shortage more fairly than the market would. The practice was a rationing bureaucracy layered over a price bureaucracy, distributing federally controlled petroleum among refiners, distributors, and regions at federally set prices. The act was emergency legislation in the literal sense, designed for a crisis expected to pass. The controls it imposed would outlast the emergency by years, and their unwinding would become one of the longest legislative projects in the field, running from the phased decontrol of crude under President Carter in 1979 through the final withdrawal of federal wellhead gas controls on January 1, 1993.

The reorganization statute of the following year addressed a different failure. The Energy Reorganization Act of 1974, Public Law 93-438, 88 Stat. 1233, signed October 11, 1974, by President Ford, abolished the Atomic Energy Commission and divided its functions between two new bodies: the Energy Research and Development Administration, which inherited the promotional mission, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, which inherited the safety and licensing mission. The AEC's dual role had become politically untenable. An agency charged with promoting nuclear power could not credibly regulate its hazards, and the environmental movement born in the nineteen sixties had made the conflict visible. The 1974 act is therefore a hinge between the two tracks: the security track's premier promotional agency was broken up at the demand of the environmental track's logic, and the regulatory half was set up as an independent commission insulated from the promotional mission. ERDA inherited the promotional and research mission the Commission had carried. The reorganization did not settle the underlying tension; it institutionalized it, giving each side its own agency and leaving Congress to referee. Three years later Congress would gather the scattered pieces again, folding ERDA and the Federal Energy Administration into a single Department of Energy, but the 1974 split between promotion and regulation survived inside the new department and survives in the independent status of the NRC to this day.

The middle of the decade produced the crisis decade's central statute, and the one the cluster treats as the first crisis response in its own article. The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975, Public Law 94-163, 89 Stat. 871, signed December 22, 1975, by President Ford, is remembered for three creations, and the [Energy Policy and Conservation Act](/2016/10/01/energy-policy-conservation-act-1975/) article walks each of them in depth. Part B, section 151 created the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, authorizing a stockpile of up to one billion barrels of crude oil in underground salt caverns, a physical insurance policy against future embargoes that embodied the security track's logic in its most literal form. Title V created the corporate average fuel economy standards, the CAFE program, which set average mileage requirements for new automobiles and light trucks, the first federal efficiency regulation of the transportation sector and a measure whose security rationale, reducing oil imports, mattered more to its enactment than any environmental one. Title III created the Energy Conservation Program for Consumer Products, the appliance efficiency standards that would go on to regulate refrigerators, air conditioners, furnaces, and dozens of other product categories. These three instruments, a stockpile, a vehicle standard, and an appliance standard, have shaped American energy consumption for four decades, and all three were crisis children, enacted in the shadow of the embargo.

But a fourth EPCA provision is often dropped. Title IV of the act amended the Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act and extended federal petroleum price and allocation controls. EPCA was not a deregulation statute. It paired the forward-looking instruments, the reserve, the mileage standards, the appliance standards, with a backward-looking extension of the very controls the 1973 act had imposed. This is the first great counterexample to the tidy periodization, and the guide states it plainly here rather than saving it for the complication section: the signature conservation statute of the crisis decade extended price controls. The politics explain the pairing. Decontrol was unpopular with consumers who feared higher prices, and the Ford administration could not assemble a majority for ending controls without buying off the interests the controls protected. The result was a both-track law before either track had a name, a statute that conserved fuel at the tailpipe while suppressing price signals at the wellhead. The contradiction would take another half decade to resolve, and its resolution would require a separate political bargain in 1980.

EPCA's four titles make the statute a microcosm of the entire field, and the guide dwells on the anatomy because the cluster's own article treats this law as the first crisis response. Part B, section 151 builds the stockpile, the security track's physical insurance, up to one billion barrels. Title V builds the fuel economy standards, the security track's demand-side insurance, cutting imports by cutting consumption. Title III builds the appliance standards, the environmental track's efficiency logic in its earliest federal form. Title IV extends the price controls, the security track's emergency instrument refusing to expire. Four titles, three instruments, two tracks, one statute: the whole seventy-year pattern is visible in a single public law, which is why EPCA repays the close reading the cluster gives it. The stockpile endures, the standards endure, the controls are gone, and the pattern of that survival, the market-friendly and efficiency instruments outlasting the price instruments, prefigures the field's larger movement from regulation through markets to subsidy.

The decade's institutional capstone came two years later. The Department of Energy Organization Act of 1977, Public Law 95-91, 91 Stat. 565, signed August 4, 1977, by President Carter, created the Department of Energy, gathering ERDA and the scattered federal energy programs into a single cabinet department. The reorganization answered the fragmentation the crisis decade had exposed, when energy responsibilities were spread across agencies with competing missions. The 1974 reorganization had already split the Atomic Energy Commission's promotional and regulatory functions between ERDA and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission; the 1977 act folded the promotional side into the new department while the NRC remained independent. Whether the new department delivered coherence is a separate question, and the five-statute package of 1978, enacted a year later, suggests the limits of reorganization as a substitute for agreement on policy. A department can administer a consensus; it cannot manufacture one. The 1977 act is classified as market structure in the artifact table: it reorganised the government's participation in energy markets without setting prices and without creating subsidies, though the department it created would go on to administer both.

Stepping back, the crisis decade's four statutes trace a complete education in the limits of each instrument. The 1973 act showed that price regulation could suppress the symptoms of a supply shock while aggravating the underlying shortage, because capped prices discouraged the new supply the shock made valuable. The 1974 act showed that institutional design could separate conflicting missions but could not reconcile the interests behind them; ERDA and the NRC would spend decades in an adversarial posture that the reorganization had formalised rather than resolved. The 1975 act showed that a single statute could advance both tracks at once, creating the stockpile and the efficiency standards while extending the controls, and that the resulting contradictions would have to be unwound by later Congresses. The 1977 act showed that gathering the instruments under one roof did not harmonise them. Each lesson pointed toward the same conclusion, which the next decade would act on: the price-regulation instrument was exhausted, and the field's future lay in market structure and subsidy. But the exhaustion took fifteen more years to legislate, because the political economy of controls, concentrated beneficiaries and diffuse costs, made every stage of the unwinding a separate fight.

The decade also fixed the two tracks' characteristic political coalitions, and the guide notes the pattern because it recurs. The security track's measures were carried by producing-state legislators, consumer advocates fearful of price spikes, and administrations of both parties facing elections during shortages. The environmental track's measures were carried by the public-health and conservation coalitions that had won the 1969-70 statutes, joined after 1973 by security hawks who discovered that efficiency served independence. That cross-track alliance, environmentalists and hawks voting together for conservation, explains the 1975 act's otherwise puzzling majorities and reappears in every efficiency measure since. The tracks are motives, not teams, and legislators join the track that serves their constituents' immediate interest. A legislator from farm country votes the environmental track for the fuel mandate's rural benefits and the security track for the drilling exclusion's jobs; the vote is the same, the motive differs by provision. This fluidity is why the guide insists on tracks rather than camps: the coalitions reassemble around each instrument, and only the motives persist.

## The five-statute package of 1978

On November 9, 1978, President Carter signed five energy statutes on a single day, the five components of the National Energy Act, and the package remains the most ambitious attempt Congress has ever made to legislate both tracks at once. The five were the [Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act](/2016/11/01/purpa-1978-energy-act-guide/) of 1978, Public Law 95-617, 92 Stat. 3117; the Energy Tax Act of 1978, Public Law 95-618; the National Energy Conservation Policy Act of 1978, Public Law 95-619, 92 Stat. 3206; the Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act of 1978, Public Law 95-620; and the Natural Gas Policy Act of 1978, Public Law 95-621. Note the official short title of the fourth: "Powerplant" is one word, a detail the guide preserves because the series treats short titles as exact strings. The package mixed conservation and efficiency measures with production incentives and phased deregulation, and that mixture is precisely why the package is remembered as incoherent. The namable claim names 1978 as one of the two both-track years, and this section supplies the evidence.

PURPA is the package's most consequential statute and the one the cluster examines in its own article. Its market-making provision was the purchase obligation: electric utilities were required to buy power from qualifying cogeneration and small power production facilities. Before PURPA, an independent generator had no assured right to sell power to the grid; after PURPA, utilities had to buy from qualifying facilities. The provision created the independent power industry, the non-utility generators that became the institutional seedbed for the competitive wholesale markets of the nineteen nineties. The mechanism was market structure, not subsidy: Congress did not pay the independents, it ordered the utilities to buy from them. The provision's importance for the two-track thesis is that it served the security track through market means, adding generating capacity and diversifying supply without a federal construction program, while its qualifying-facility categories favored cogeneration and renewables in ways the environmental track could claim. PURPA was a market-structure statute with environmental side effects, and both tracks have claimed it since.

The Energy Tax Act of 1978 worked through the tax code rather than through utility regulation, and it is the package's clearest both-track exhibit in miniature. The act created residential and business energy tax credits for conservation and renewable investments, the ancestor of the investment credits that would grow into major federal support for solar and geothermal development, while simultaneously imposing the gas-guzzler tax on automobiles whose fuel economy fell below statutory thresholds. The Congressional Research Service's energy tax history, RL33578, treats the 1978 act as the origin point for both the credit and the tax, and the guide follows that sourcing. The credits were technology subsidy in the environmental track's spirit, paying households and firms to adopt efficiency and renewables. The gas-guzzler tax was a price instrument in the security track's spirit, penalising wasteful consumption to reduce oil imports. A single statute thus taxed inefficiency and subsidised its alternatives, a coherent enough pairing in isolation, but one that sat inside a five-bill package whose other components pulled in different directions. The tax act also matters for the complication argument: subsidies and penalties coexisted in the same title, in the same year, from the same Congress, which is not what a tidy succession of eras would predict.

The National Energy Conservation Policy Act, NECPA, carried the conservation half of the package's domestic agenda. It authorised residential energy conservation programs, including the weatherization assistance framework and utility conservation service programs that required utilities to offer home energy audits and arrange financing for conservation measures. The statute's theory was that the cheapest barrel of oil was the one never burned, and that households underinvested in insulation, storm windows, and efficient heating because of information gaps and capital constraints the market would not close on its own. NECPA directed utilities, the very firms whose revenues depended on selling power, to help their customers buy less of it, a regulatory paradox the statute managed through program mandates rather than through price reform. The act is technology subsidy in the artifact table's classification, though the subsidy ran to conservation services and financing rather than to hardware. Its significance for the thesis is as the environmental track's domestic face: where the tax act worked through credits and the purchase obligation worked through utility markets, NECPA worked through programs, and all three shared the premise that using less was as legitimate a policy goal as producing more.

### Why is the 1978 package remembered as incoherent?

The five statutes pulled in different directions: PURPA opened generation markets while the fuel-use act restricted fuel choice, the gas act phased decontrol while conservation programs suppressed demand signals. Congress served both tracks at once, and the package never reconciled them.

The remaining two statutes of the package show the security track at its most interventionist and supply it with its most instructive failure. The Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act prohibited the burning of natural gas and petroleum in new electric power plants and new major industrial boilers, subject to exemptions, on the theory that scarce gas and imported oil should be reserved for uses where no alternative fuel existed. In 1978 the premise seemed plausible: interstate gas markets were short, oil imports were rising, and coal and nuclear appeared to offer abundant domestic alternatives for baseload generation. The prohibition was command-and-control in its purest form, a federal order about which molecules could be burned where. It did not survive contact with the nineteen eighties. Gas markets loosened, the scarcity premise collapsed, and the prohibition became an embarrassment, a federal ban on burning a fuel that was increasingly abundant. Congress later repealed the act, and the guide records the repeal without dating it, since the sources confirm the repeal but not a precise year. The Fuel Use Act's career is the cautionary tale inside the 1978 package: a production-oriented restriction, written on a forecast of scarcity, undone by the market it tried to command. It stands in the artifact table as market structure, a restriction on market participation, and in the thesis as a security-track measure that failed on its own terms.

The Natural Gas Policy Act completed the package by beginning the end of gas price regulation. The NGPA created a complex tiered system of wellhead price ceilings for different categories of gas, old and new, onshore and offshore, and scheduled the gradual decontrol of the newer categories over the following decade. The statute's drafters understood that the interstate price ceilings imposed in the nineteen fifties had produced the shortages of the nineteen seventies by holding prices below the cost of finding new supply, and the NGPA's phased approach was meant to restore supply incentives without an overnight price shock to consumers. The tiers were fiendishly complicated, and the statute's implementation generated years of litigation over category boundaries, but the direction was set: gas prices would move toward market levels on a statutory schedule. The NGPA is price regulation in the artifact table, the last great price-regulation statute before the long decontrol, and its phased design explains why the final repeal of wellhead controls did not arrive until January 1, 1993, fifteen years after the NGPA's enactment. The 1978 package thus contained both a new prohibition on burning gas and a new schedule for freeing gas prices, a juxtaposition the incoherence verdict fairly captures.

The package's internal tensions repay closer attention, because they are the mechanism of the incoherence the namable claim asserts. Consider the package as a system rather than as five separate laws. PURPA ordered utilities to buy power from independents, increasing the number of firms that could profit from generating electricity, while the Fuel Use Act told those firms and the utilities which fuels they could burn, shrinking the set of profitable choices. One statute multiplied the actors; the other narrowed their moves. The NGPA promised that gas prices would rise toward market levels on a schedule, which should have encouraged gas production, while the Fuel Use Act banned the growing use of gas in power generation, which suppressed gas demand. One statute courted supply; the other strangled demand. The Energy Tax Act paid households to conserve while the NGPA's tiered ceilings kept gas prices below market for the oldest categories, which muted the conservation signal the tax credits were meant to amplify. Each contradiction had its own legislative logic and its own constituency, and the package's drafters understood the tensions; they accepted them as the cost of assembling a majority. The incoherence was not an accident of drafting but a property of the bargain. A Congress that needed the votes of gas producers, consumer advocates, environmentalists, utilities, and the Carter White House could only pass a package that gave each of them something, and the somethings did not fit together.

The implementation arc sharpened the contradictions. The Fuel Use Act's scarcity premise failed first, as gas markets loosened and the ban became indefensible, and its repeal removed one of the package's five pillars while the other four stood. The NGPA's tiers generated the litigation the drafters had feared, as producers and pipelines fought over category boundaries that determined the price of each molecule, and the complexity itself became an argument for finishing decontrol faster than the schedule allowed. PURPA's purchase obligation, by contrast, outperformed its drafters' expectations: the independent power industry it created grew beyond the modest qualifying-facility sector the statute envisioned and became the constituency for the 1992 act's deeper reforms. The package's most market-oriented statute thus became the seed of the next market-structuring wave, while its most interventionist statute became a dead letter. NECPA's conservation programs survived as program infrastructure, modest and unglamorous, while the Energy Tax Act's credits became the template for the investment incentives that would dominate later decades. The five statutes diverged in implementation as they had converged in enactment, and the divergence is itself evidence for the thesis: instruments aligned with a track's logic endure, while instruments that contradict their own package's logic do not.

There is a final lesson in the 1978 package for the reader who wants to use the two-track thesis predictively rather than merely descriptively. Both-track packages fail not because the tracks are incompatible but because the instruments are. PURPA and the 1992 act show that market structuring can serve both tracks when the provisions point the same way; the 1978 package shows that contradictory instruments in the same package produce incoherence regardless of motive. The test for any future both-track proposal is therefore mechanical rather than philosophical: do the provisions' incentives align, or do they cancel? The 1978 drafters could not ask that question, because they were assembling a majority rather than designing a system. The guide's reader can ask it, and the artifact table supplies the instrument column in which to run the check.

## Decontrol and its political price, 1980 to 1989

The nineteen eighties paired the dismantling of price controls with the most expensive technology-subsidy failure in the field's history, and the pairing is not a coincidence. The Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980, Public Law 96-223, 94 Stat. 229, signed April 2, 1980, by President Carter, was the political price of oil decontrol. The Statutes at Large date the signing to April 2, and the guide follows that dating against one law-review source that prints April 3. The Carter administration had begun phasing out crude price controls in 1979, and full decontrol promised a windfall for domestic producers as their oil repriced toward world levels. Congress would not permit decontrol without capturing part of that windfall for the Treasury, and the 1980 act supplied an excise tax on the difference between the controlled price and the market price. The Congressional Research Service, in RL33578, describes the tax plainly as the political price of decontrol, and the guide adopts that characterization with the citation. The tax was not a conservation measure and not a production incentive; it was a revenue measure that made deregulation legislatively possible, a transfer from producers to the Treasury that bought the votes for letting prices move. The episode belongs in any honest account of the deregulation era because it shows that market structuring had political costs, and that Congress paid them with a tax. Crude price regulation had been phased out under Carter in 1979 and was fully withdrawn by President Reagan's Executive Order 12287 of January 28, 1981, one of the new administration's first acts, but the windfall tax outlived the controls it accompanied, a fiscal residue of the bargain that ended them.

The same Congress that taxed the windfall also bet on manufacturing its way out of import dependence. The Energy Security Act of 1980, Public Law 96-294, 94 Stat. 611, signed June 30, 1980, by President Carter, was a six-act package whose Title I created the Synthetic Fuels Corporation, the SFC. The corporation's mandate was to finance commercial-scale synthetic fuel plants that would convert coal, oil shale, and tar sands into liquid fuels, with statutory production goals of 500,000 barrels per day of oil equivalent by 1987 and 2 million barrels per day by 1992. The Government Accountability Office documented the corporation's initial borrowing authority at $20 billion in notes issued to the Treasury, with potential future authorizations not to exceed $68 billion, figures the guide reports from GAO report B-202463. What followed is the field's canonical expensive failure. Commonly reported figures, carried in dissertations and reference works rather than in GAO audits, put the corporation's actual spending at roughly $960 million across four projects, none of which achieved commercial viability, and the statutory production goals were never met. The guide qualifies those outcome figures as commonly reported rather than GAO-sourced, since the GAO documents report the borrowing authority and the termination rather than the outcome totals. Congress enacted Public Law 99-190 on December 19, 1985, terminating the SFC effective April 18, 1986. In summary: the SFC, authorized to deploy up to $20 billion, spent roughly $960 million on four projects that never achieved commercial viability before Congress terminated it in 1985-86.

### Why did Congress create the Synthetic Fuels Corporation?

The second oil shock convinced Congress that private capital would not build alternative fuel plants at the needed scale. The SFC was a federal financing corporation meant to bridge that gap, and its collapse taught the field that technology subsidy cannot conjure commercial viability.

The SFC's failure taught the field two durable lessons, and the guide states them as the historical profession has. First, technology subsidy cannot conjure commercial viability on a statutory timetable; the corporation's plants faced the same geology, engineering, and cost problems that had deterred private capital, and federal money did not solve them. Second, production goals written into law do not bind physics; the 500,000 and 2 million barrel targets assumed a deployment curve the technology could not follow. The neutrality rule requires the guide to describe the failure factually with sources, and it has: the $20 billion authority and the 1985-86 termination are GAO-documented, the $960 million and four-project figures are commonly reported and qualified as such. The SFC also matters for the two-track thesis as the security track's most ambitious production instrument. It was not deregulation and not conservation; it was the government financing the supply it wanted, and its collapse discredited the production-subsidy approach for a generation. Later technology subsidies would be written more cautiously, as tax credits rather than as a financing corporation, and the renewable credits of 1992 and 2005 carry the SFC's lesson in their design.

The decade's nuclear statute addressed the waste obligation the atomic opening had deferred. The Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, Public Law 97-425, 96 Stat. 2201, was signed January 7, 1983, by President Reagan, although the short title carries the year 1982. The act created an unmet federal obligation: the federal government assumed a disposal duty it has not discharged, and the fee-paying utilities and their ratepayers have lived with the consequences. The act is market structure in the artifact table, a federal assumption of a liability the market could not bear, and it belongs to the security track in the sense that it was meant to remove the waste obstacle to the nuclear industry the 1954 and 1957 acts had created. That the obstacle remains is a fact the statute's own history records.

The decade closed with the completion of the gas decontrol the NGPA had scheduled. The Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989, Public Law 101-60, 103 Stat. 157, signed July 26, 1989, by President George H. W. Bush, finished the job the 1978 act had started, with the final repeal of remaining wellhead price controls taking effect January 1, 1993. The fifteen-year phaseout is worth pausing over, because it measures the political difficulty of unwinding price regulation even after its economic failure was conceded. Congress voted for decontrol in stages, protected existing contracts, and let the oldest ceilings linger, and the market did not fully price gas at the wellhead until the first day of 1993. The 1989 act is price regulation in the artifact table, the terminal entry in the price-regulation column, and its effective date belongs in any telling of the deregulation arc. From the EPAA's allocation bureaucracy in 1973 to the last wellhead ceiling's expiration in 1993, the price-regulation era ran twenty years, and its end was legislated, not decreed.

The eighties' deeper significance lies in what the decade taught about each instrument's limits, and the guide draws the lessons explicitly because they shaped everything after. The windfall tax taught that deregulation has a political price and that Congress will extract it: no major decontrol since 1980 has been enacted without a companion provision buying off the losers, and the tax title of later acts can be read as the institutionalized form of that bargain. The SFC taught that the government cannot finance commercial viability into existence on a statutory schedule, and the lesson stuck: no later Congress created a production corporation on the SFC's model, and the technology subsidies that followed were written as credits and grants rather than as a federal builder. The waste act taught that assuming a liability is easier than discharging it, and the unmet federal obligation became a standing argument for every subsequent debate about nuclear power's future. The gas decontrol taught that phased transitions create their own constituencies for delay, as each protected category fought to keep its ceiling, and that the schedule Congress writes is the earliest possible date rather than the likely one.

These lessons converge on a single observation about the security track in the eighties. The track's production wing, which wanted the government to build supply, was discredited by the SFC's collapse; the track's deregulation wing, which wanted the government to get out of the way, was vindicated by the decontrol experience but had to pay the windfall tax for the privilege. The result was a security track that increasingly expressed itself through market structure rather than through either production subsidy or price control: PURPA's independents, the 1992 act's wholesale competition, and eventually the 2015 export repeal all belong to this chastened, market-oriented version of the security motive. The environmental track, meanwhile, watched the SFC's failure and drew its own lesson, which was that technology subsidy should reward performance rather than fund construction. The production credit's design, paying per kilowatt hour generated rather than per plant built, is the SFC's lesson internalised. The two tracks thus learned opposite halves of the same failure and carried them into the nineties, where they would meet again in the 1992 act's surprisingly coherent pairing.

## Wholesale competition and the production credit, 1992

The Energy Policy Act of 1992, Public Law 102-486, 106 Stat. 2776, signed October 24, 1992, by President George H. W. Bush, is the hinge on which the sequence turns from market structuring to technology subsidy, and it performed both moves in a single statute without the incoherence that marked 1978. The act's market-structuring half created exempt wholesale generators, EWGs, a new class of independent power producers freed from the holding-company restrictions of the nineteen thirties, and it authorised the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to order transmission-owning utilities to wheel power for third parties on nondiscriminatory terms. The wheeling authority underpinned the Commission's Order 888 in 1996, the open-access rule that required utilities to offer transmission service to competitors at comparable rates, and the guide dates that consequence to 1996. Where PURPA had cracked the generation monopoly by ordering utilities to buy from qualifying facilities, the 1992 act attacked the transmission bottleneck directly, giving independent generators the right to move their power across utility wires. The wholesale electricity market that resulted is the institutional descendant of the 1992 act's two provisions. The classification is market structure without qualification: Congress defined new rights of market participation and ordered the regulator to enforce them.

The act's technology-subsidy half created the renewable electricity production tax credit, the PTC, a per-kilowatt-hour credit for electricity generated from qualifying renewable resources. The credit's design reflected the SFC's lesson. Rather than capitalising a federal corporation to build plants, Congress offered a production incentive that paid only for power actually generated, leaving technology risk with the developer and rewarding performance rather than construction. The guide does not trace the credit's later extensions, but it records the founding: the 1992 act paired the credit with wholesale competition in a single law, and the pairing worked. The design contrast with the SFC is the point the thesis needs. The corporation had socialised construction risk and produced nothing; the credit privatised construction risk and paid only for output. Both were technology subsidies in the table's classification, but their incentive structures pointed in opposite directions, and only one of them survived contact with commercial reality.

The pairing is also the statute's contribution to the complication argument, and the guide states it here as well as in the later section. The 1992 act was enacted during an era of low oil prices, when the security track's usual motive, supply crisis, was absent. Wholesale competition answered a market-structure concern about monopoly power, not a supply shock. The PTC answered an environmental and industrial-policy concern about diversifying generation, not a shortage. The statute thus demonstrates that the two tracks can advance together, in one law, without crisis and without incoherence, when each provision serves its own logic cleanly. The contrast with 1978 is instructive. The 1978 package mixed instruments that contradicted each other, a purchase obligation alongside a fuel-use prohibition, phased decontrol alongside new consumption mandates, because it tried to satisfy every constituency at once. The 1992 act combined two instruments that pointed in the same direction, more competition and more diverse supply, and the combination held. The lesson is not that both-track lawmaking always fails; it is that it fails when the tracks are forced into a single package without a shared logic, and the 1978 and 2005 statutes are the exhibits.

The 1992 act's deeper structural contribution was to complete the market-structuring project the eighties had begun and thereby change what remained for Congress to do. With wholesale generation open to independents and transmission available on nondiscriminatory terms, the federal government's role in electricity markets shifted from building the market's architecture to choosing its technological winners. That shift is visible in the instrument column: after 1992, every major federal energy statute in the sequence is primarily a technology-subsidy measure, from the 2005 tax title through the 2007 fuel provisions to the 2009 recovery title. Market structure was largely built; subsidy became the active margin. The shift also changed the politics. Market-structuring statutes create diffuse benefits and concentrated losers, the utilities that lose their monopoly, which makes them hard to pass but durable once enacted. Subsidy statutes create concentrated beneficiaries and diffuse costs, which makes them easier to pass but subjects them to perpetual renegotiation. The PTC's career after 1992 follows the subsidy pattern, though the guide does not trace it beyond the founding. The 1992 act thus stands at the hinge of two political economies as well as two instrument phases, and its coherence comes from keeping the two halves in separate titles with separate logics.

There is one more reason the guide dwells on 1992, and it concerns the environmental track's independence. The act's environmental provisions were enacted without any supply crisis, without any price spike, and without any shortage to motivate them. Diversification and competition were ends in themselves, supported by a coalition that wanted cleaner generation and more competitive wholesale power for their own sakes. This is the environmental track operating on its own logic, the same logic that produced NEPA and the 1970 amendments before any embargo. The security track, by contrast, has never produced a major statute without a shock or a shortage behind it; every entry in its column of the table traces to a crisis, a shortage, or an import surge. The asymmetry is the thesis's empirical core, and 1992 is its cleanest demonstration: put the two tracks' statutes side by side in a calm market, and only the environmental track shows up voluntarily.

## The both-track statutes, 2005 and 2007

The [Energy Policy Act of 2005](/2016/09/01/energy-policy-act-2005-guide/), Public Law 109-58, signed August 8, 2005, by President George W. Bush, is the last bipartisan energy act in the sequence and the second statute the namable claim names as incoherent. The cluster's own article walks the act in depth; this guide treats it as the both-track exhibit. The act's market-structure provisions included mandatory electricity reliability enforcement, giving the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission authority to certify an electric reliability organization with enforceable standards, a direct response to the cascading blackout that had darkened the Northeast and Midwest two years earlier. It repealed the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935, the New Deal statute that had constrained utility corporate structure for seventy years, completing the holding-company liberalisation the 1992 act had begun for wholesale generators. And it excluded certain oil and gas drilling activities from specified federal environmental review, the drilling exclusion the brief names as one of the act's anchors, a security-track provision that the environmental track opposed. Alongside these sat the technology-subsidy provisions: extensions and expansions of the renewable credits, new credits for clean coal and nuclear, and efficiency incentives. The Congressional Research Service's pattern analysis of the act's tax title put the total at $14.5 billion in tax reductions over eleven years, including a $2.6 billion package of oil and gas incentives alongside roughly $4.5 billion for renewables, figures the guide reports from CRS with the period stated. The numbers capture the both-track character arithmetically: the act subsidised the incumbent fuels and their alternatives in the same title, in the same law, in the same decade.

### How did the 2005 act serve both tracks at once?

It paired reliability enforcement, holding-company repeal, and a drilling exclusion for the security track with expanded renewable, clean coal, and nuclear credits for the environmental track, and CRS counted $14.5 billion in tax reductions serving both sides at once in one title.

The incoherence verdict needs a fair hearing, because the 2005 act's defenders argue that its very breadth was the point. A bipartisan majority required something for every constituency: reliability enforcement for the utilities and their regulators, holding-company repeal for the industry's corporate planners, the drilling exclusion for the producing states, the renewable credits for the environmental coalition, the nuclear provisions for the utilities that still operated reactors. Each provision was coherent on its own terms. The incoherence lay in the aggregate, in a statute that simultaneously subsidised oil and gas production and renewable alternatives, that preached market discipline through holding-company repeal while practicing industrial policy through the credit menu, and that addressed reliability through mandatory standards while leaving the demand side largely to the states. The guide presents this as the namable claim does: the statutes that tried to serve both tracks are the ones remembered as incoherent, and 2005 is the proof alongside 1978. The counter-reading, that breadth was the price of bipartisanship and therefore a legislative virtue, is noted here in the spirit of the neutrality rule, which requires the guide to present both tracks with equal care and without implying that either is the legitimate one. The verdict on 2005 remains contested; the fact of its both-track construction does not.

The Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007, Public Law 110-140, signed December 19, 2007, by President George W. Bush, belongs primarily to the environmental track and is the cleaner statute of the pair. Its headline provision raised corporate average fuel economy standards to 35 miles per gallon by 2020, the first increase in the CAFE program since its creation in 1975, ending a three-decade freeze that had left the standards untouched while vehicle fleets grew heavier. The act also expanded the Renewable Fuel Standard to 36 billion gallons by 2022, mandating the blending of renewable fuels into the transportation fuel supply on a rising schedule. Both provisions were efficiency and technology measures in the environmental track's tradition, though the fuel standard's security rationale, displacing imported oil with domestic biofuels, gave it a both-track constituency. The 2007 act's relative coherence supports the thesis by contrast: where 2005 tried to serve every track and satisfied none completely, 2007 concentrated on efficiency and renewable fuels and produced a statute whose logic a reader can state in a sentence. The guide classifies it as technology subsidy in the artifact table, the efficiency standards and the fuel mandate both being government interventions to favor particular technological outcomes rather than price controls or market-structure reforms.

Together the two acts close the sequence's bipartisan era. The 2005 act showed that a both-track majority could still be assembled when oil prices were high and both tracks had urgent claims; the 2007 act showed that a single-track efficiency measure could still pass when the environmental track's logic was clear. What followed was the recession, the recovery spending, and the long polarisation of energy politics, in which the security and environmental tracks would increasingly be legislated separately or not at all. The guide's fact window for substantive treatment ends with the export repeal of 2015, and the next section walks the remaining sequence: the recovery statute, the export repeal, and the three later statutes named by year only.

The 2005 act's three market-structure anchors deserve a closer reading, because each illustrates a different facet of the security track's mature form. Reliability enforcement made grid reliability a federal regulatory obligation rather than a voluntary industry practice, giving the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission the authority to certify a reliability organization whose standards would bind the industry. The move belongs to the security track's core anxiety, keeping the lights on, and it represents the track's shift from fuel supply to system reliability as the economy's dependence on electricity deepened. Holding-company repeal removed the last of the New Deal constraints on utility corporate structure, completing a liberalisation the 1992 act had begun for wholesale generators and extending it to the corporate forms through which utilities finance and organize themselves. The repeal was market structure in its purest form: the government stepping back from dictating corporate form and letting capital markets discipline utility organization. The drilling exclusion, which removed certain oil and gas activities from specified federal environmental review, was the security track's production wing in its most direct form, trading environmental procedure for supply. Each anchor was defensible on security-track grounds; together with the credit menu they made a statute that no single track could claim.

The tax title's arithmetic, reported by the Congressional Research Service as $14.5 billion in reductions over eleven years, deserves the same close reading, because the composition matters more than the total. The $2.6 billion oil and gas package preserved fossil preferences in the code, consistent with the long-standing tax favor for oil and gas that the Congressional Research Service documents in RL33578, confirming that the security track's production wing survived decontrol intact. The roughly $4.5 billion for renewables extended the environmental track's credit logic from the 1992 production credit into investment and production incentives across technologies. The remainder spread across nuclear, clean coal, and efficiency, the both-track technologies that each coalition could support without conceding the other's premise. A tax title that pays every technology is not an energy strategy; it is a legislative strategy, and the distinction is the core of the incoherence verdict. The 2005 act's defenders answer that the legislative strategy was the only one available, that no narrower bill could have assembled a majority, and that a both-track statute which passes beats a coherent statute which fails. The guide records that defense under the neutrality rule and leaves the verdict to the reader, noting only that the defense concedes the descriptive claim: the act served both tracks because it had to, not because the tracks had been reconciled.

The 2007 act's contrast sharpens the point. Its two headline provisions, the 35-mile-per-gallon CAFE standard for 2020 and the 36-billion-gallon Renewable Fuel Standard for 2022, each serve a single logic cleanly. The fuel economy increase was the first since the program's 1975 creation, ending a three-decade freeze, and it did what efficiency standards do: it required less fuel per mile without dictating how manufacturers achieved it. The fuel standard required more renewable fuel in the transportation supply on a rising schedule, a mandate rather than a price signal, but a mandate with a single direction. Neither provision subsidised the fuels it displaced; neither deregulated the markets it affected. The act is therefore the rare late-period statute a reader can summarise without contradiction, and its coherence comes from its single-track focus. The guide does not claim the act was uncontroversial, only that its logic was legible, which is more than can be said for its 2005 predecessor.

## Stimulus, exports, and the named successors

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, Public Law 111-5, 123 Stat. 115, signed February 17, 2009, by President Obama, was recession legislation first and energy legislation second, but its energy title was the largest single energy investment before 2022. The guide does not attach a dollar figure, and it characterises the title as a technology-subsidy surge: a technology-subsidy surge, delivered through grants, loan guarantees, and tax provisions, aimed at efficiency, renewables, grid modernisation, and advanced vehicles. The recession supplied the legislative vehicle; the energy track supplied the projects. The act belongs to the environmental track in the thesis, though its spending also served the security track's interest in domestic supply. It demonstrated that technology subsidy had become the default instrument of federal energy policy: where the nineteen seventies answered crisis with price controls and the nineteen eighties answered it with decontrol, the government of 2009 answered a demand collapse with investment. The loan guarantee program's later controversies lie outside the verified facts and outside this guide's scope; the structural point stands without them.

The sequence's last substantively treated statute unwinds one of the crisis decade's most durable instruments. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2016, Public Law 114-113, Division O, Title I, section 101, signed December 18, 2015, by President Obama, repealed the crude oil export restriction, repealing section 103 of the Energy Policy and Conservation Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. section 6212a. The export ban had been enacted in 1975 as part of the crisis response, a prohibition on shipping domestic crude abroad while the nation feared shortage. It outlived the shortage by four decades, surviving the decontrol of prices and the collapse of the SFC, until Congress removed it in an appropriations rider in 2015. The repeal is market structure in the artifact table, a deregulation measure in the security track's tradition, and its forty-year lifespan is the field's best evidence that crisis instruments persist long after the crisis. The EPCA article in the cluster traces the ban's full career; this guide records its end.

The sequence continues with three later statutes that this guide names by year only, with their full treatment reserved to the linked cluster articles: the Energy Act of 2020, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021, and the [Inflation Reduction Act of 2022](/2016/10/15/inflation-reduction-act-2022-energy/). The guide states no facts about their provisions, costs, or outcomes; they appear by name and year alone, with public law numbers in the artifact table. They belong to the subsidy phase of the arc, and the linked articles carry their substance. Their presence here completes the sequence the One Test requires the reader to name in order, and their name-only treatment respects the article's 2017 fact window while keeping the hub current as a finding aid.

The repeal's legislative form deserves notice. Congress did not repeal the ban in a standalone energy bill; it repealed it in Division O, Title I, section 101 of a consolidated appropriations act, a rider on must-pass spending legislation. The venue continues the pattern the guide identified in the environmental prelude: consequential energy policy often moves through vehicles that are not energy bills, because the vehicles that must pass can carry provisions that standalone bills cannot. The 1978 package needed five separate statutes and a presidential signing ceremony; the 2015 repeal needed a section number in an omnibus. The difference measures how the field's legislative practice changed across four decades, from comprehensive packages to targeted riders, and it explains why the later sequence is thinner in standalone statutes. The tracks did not stop operating after 2009; they increasingly operated through appropriations, tax titles, and the linked articles' post-window statutes, which this guide indexes by name.

The 2009 recovery title and the 2015 repeal thus form a natural pair with which to close the substantive sequence. The first shows the environmental track's instrument, technology subsidy, deployed at the largest scale before 2022 through a recession vehicle. The second shows the security track's instrument, deregulation, removing the crisis decade's last export control through an appropriations vehicle. Neither statute tried to serve both tracks, and neither is remembered as incoherent. The field's clearest measures, from the 1954 licensing framework to the 1992 wholesale reforms to these two, share that single-track clarity. The guide's thesis does not claim that single-track statutes are better policy; it claims that they are more legible, and that legibility is what lets a reader place them on the tracks, in the table, and in the order the One Test requires.

## The price-regulation arc, 1973 to 1993

Read as a continuous narrative rather than as isolated statutes, the price-regulation entries form a twenty-year arc with a clear shape: imposition in 1973, extension in 1975, oil decontrol from 1979 to 1981, and gas decontrol from 1978 to 1993. The Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act of 1973 imposed the controls; the Energy Policy and Conservation Act's Title IV extended them, amending the EPAA to keep petroleum price and allocation authority alive past the emergency. That extension is the arc's most instructive moment, because it shows controls acquiring a constituency. By 1975 the allocation bureaucracy had beneficiaries, refiners with favorable entitlements, regions with protected supply, consumers with below-market prices, and each beneficiary became a lobbyist for continuation. The controls outlived the embargo because the embargo had created interests that the market's return would harm.

Oil decontrol took the better part of a decade. The Carter administration began phasing out crude price regulations in 1979, and President Reagan completed the withdrawal with Executive Order 12287 on January 28, 1981. The Congressional Research Service's account in RL33578 identifies the 1980 Windfall Profit Tax Act as the political price of that decontrol: Congress taxed the difference between controlled and market prices to capture part of the producers' windfall, buying the votes that deregulation needed. The sequence matters. Decontrol did not arrive as a triumph of market principle; it arrived as a bargain in which producers got world prices, the Treasury got a new excise tax, and consumers got the assurance that someone was capturing the windfall. The tax outlived the controls, a fiscal residue that reminds the reader that deregulation's legislative cost is rarely zero.

Gas decontrol took twice as long and twice the statutes. The Natural Gas Policy Act of 1978 replaced flat interstate ceilings with tiered categories on a decontrol schedule, and the Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989 finished the schedule, with the final repeal effective January 1, 1993. The fifteen-year gap between the NGPA and the final repeal reflects gas's particular complications: the historic split between interstate and intrastate markets, the long-term contracts written under ceilings, and the category litigation the tiers generated. Each complication had a constituency, and each constituency extracted delay. The arc's lesson is that price regulation is easier to impose than to remove, not because the economics are unclear but because the politics are asymmetric. The beneficiaries of controls are concentrated and organised; the beneficiaries of decontrol are diffuse and future. Every stage of the unwinding therefore required its own legislative vehicle, its own bargain, and its own tax or protection to buy off the losers. The twenty-year arc from the EPAA to the last wellhead ceiling is the field's longest continuous legislative project, and it ended not with a declaration but with an expiration date.

## The market-structure arc, 1954 to 2015

The market-structure column runs the full length of the table, from the 1954 licensing framework to the 2015 export repeal, and reading it as an arc reveals the security track's maturing theory of what government should do. The early entries define who may participate. The 1954 act licenses private nuclear operators; the 1957 act defines the liability terms of their participation; the 1974 act separates the government's promotional role from its regulatory role so that participation has a credible referee. These are constitutive acts: they create the market's legal preconditions rather than intervening in an existing market. The environmental entries of 1969 and 1970 belong to the same constitutive logic from the other track, defining the environmental terms of participation for every project that follows. The arc's first phase is therefore architectural, and its characteristic question is who gets to build what under which rules.

The middle entries restructure existing markets. PURPA's 1978 purchase obligation breaks the utility generation monopoly by giving independents a guaranteed buyer. The 1992 act's exempt wholesale generators and wheeling authority break the transmission bottleneck by giving independents a way to move their power. The 2005 act's holding-company repeal removes the last New Deal constraints on utility corporate form. Each of these statutes takes a market the government had previously organised as a monopoly and opens a segment of it to competition, and each does so by defining a new right, to sell, to wheel, to organise, and ordering the regulator to enforce it. The arc's middle phase is liberalising, and its characteristic question is which monopoly segments can be opened without breaking the system's reliability. The 2005 act's reliability title answers the second half of that question with mandatory standards, the security track's acknowledgment that competition needs a floor.

The final entry removes a barrier. The 2015 crude export repeal deletes the last of the crisis decade's quantity restrictions, letting domestic producers sell into world markets. It is market structure by subtraction, the government withdrawing a rule rather than writing one, and it completes the arc's movement from constitutive to liberalising to subtractive. Put the three phases together and the security track's mature theory emerges: the government should define the market's legal preconditions, open its monopolies to competition, enforce reliability, and then get out of the way. That theory was not available in 1973, when the track's instinct was to set prices and allocate supply; it had to be learned through the failures of the controls and the corporation. The market-structure arc is therefore also an education arc, and its tuition was the twenty-year decontrol and the SFC's $20 billion lesson.

## The subsidy arc, 1978 to 2009

The technology-subsidy column tells a story of trial, catastrophic error, and correction. It opens in 1978 with the Energy Tax Act's residential and business energy credits, modest incentives paid through the tax code to households and firms that invested in conservation and renewables, paired in the same title with the gas-guzzler tax that penalised inefficiency. The Congressional Research Service traces both the credits and the tax to the 1978 act in RL33578, and the pairing establishes the subsidy's original form: small, tax-delivered, and coupled with a penalty on the behavior it sought to displace. The solar and geothermal business investment credit's later permanence, documented by CRS in IB10054, shows the form's durability; once in the code, a credit acquires beneficiaries who defend it, and permanence becomes the default.

The arc's catastrophic error came two years later. The Synthetic Fuels Corporation, created by Title I of the Energy Security Act of 1980, abandoned the modest tax-credit form for a federal financing corporation with $20 billion in initial Treasury borrowing authority, documented by the Government Accountability Office in B-202463, plus potential future authorizations up to $68 billion. The corporation was to build commercial-scale synthetic fuel plants toward statutory goals of 500,000 barrels per day by 1987 and 2 million by 1992. Commonly reported figures put actual spending near $960 million on four projects, none commercially viable, with the goals never met, and Congress terminated the corporation through legislation enacted December 19, 1985, effective April 18, 1986. The SFC's failure is the arc's hinge: it proved that scaling up the subsidy form, from credits to a corporation, did not scale up the results, and it taught the field that federal money cannot conjure commercial viability on a statutory timetable. No later Congress repeated the corporate form.

The correction came in 1992. The renewable production tax credit paid per kilowatt hour generated rather than per plant built, privatising construction risk and rewarding only output. The design is the SFC's lesson internalised, and the guide has emphasised the contrast because it is the subsidy arc's central intellectual event. The 2005 act's tax title then scaled the corrected form: $14.5 billion over eleven years per CRS, with $2.6 billion for oil and gas alongside roughly $4.5 billion for renewables, the credit menu extended across technologies and tracks. The 2009 recovery title scaled it further through grants, loan guarantees, and tax provisions, the largest single energy investment before 2022. Each scaling preserved the corrected form's essential feature, payment for performance or investment rather than federal construction, and each avoided the corporation's fate. The arc thus runs from modest credits through a failed corporation back to credits, larger each time but chastened in design.

The arc also carries the field's longest-running irony, which the guide states plainly. Throughout the subsidy arc's environmental measures, the fossil preferences never lapsed: percentage depletion and the expensing of intangible drilling costs kept oil and gas favored in the code, per CRS RL33578, across the decontrol decades and into the 2005 tax title's $2.6 billion package. The government subsidised the transition away from fossil fuels and the production of fossil fuels simultaneously, in the same code, for the entire period. Defenders of each preference call it necessary; critics call it incoherent. The guide calls it the both-track condition in its chronic form, less dramatic than the 1978 and 2005 packages but more durable, and a reminder that the tracks coexist in the tax code even when they cannot coexist in a single statute's logic.

## 1978 and 2005: the both-track statutes compared

The namable claim names two statutes as incoherent, and the guide has treated each in its chronological place. This section puts them side by side, because the comparison sharpens what incoherence means and what it does not. The 1978 National Energy Act package and the 2005 Energy Policy Act share a legislative structure: each assembled a majority by giving every constituency something, each mixed security-track and environmental-track instruments, and each left a statute whose provisions pull in different directions. The 1978 package paired PURPA's purchase obligation with the Fuel Use Act's combustion ban, phased gas decontrol with new conservation mandates, and energy tax credits with a gas-guzzler tax. The 2005 act paired reliability enforcement, holding-company repeal, and a drilling exclusion with expanded credits for renewables, clean coal, and nuclear, and its tax title paid $2.6 billion to oil and gas alongside roughly $4.5 billion to renewables. In each case the provisions are individually defensible and jointly contradictory, and in each case the contradiction was the price of passage.

The differences matter as much as the similarities. The 1978 package was enacted under unified Democratic government during a supply crisis, with the Carter White House driving; the 2005 act was enacted under unified Republican government during a price spike, with a different coalition driving. The 1978 package's contradictions were mostly between its own titles, instruments that cancelled each other mechanically; the 2005 act's contradictions were mostly in its tax title's composition, subsidies that pointed in opposite directions philosophically. The 1978 package's most interventionist statute, the Fuel Use Act, failed and was repealed; the 2005 act's most contested provision, the drilling exclusion, survived as law. The verdicts differ accordingly: 1978 is remembered as incoherent with near-consensus, while 2005's incoherence is contested, its defenders calling the breadth the necessary price of bipartisanship. The guide records both verdicts without adjudicating between them, because the descriptive claim, that both statutes served both tracks without reconciling them, does not depend on the normative one.

The comparison also clarifies the 1992 act's exceptional status. The 1992 statute is the both-track law that worked, pairing wholesale competition with the production credit in separate titles with aligned incentives, enacted without a crisis and without contradiction. Its success suggests that the variable is not whether a statute serves both tracks but how: separate titles with compatible logics cohere, while contradictory instruments in a single package do not. That mechanical test, do the provisions' incentives align, is the guide's practical contribution to the both-track debate, and it is falsifiable in a way the incoherence verdict is not. Apply it to any future both-track proposal and it predicts the outcome better than motive analysis alone. The 1978 and 2005 statutes are therefore not just exhibits but instruments, tools the reader can use, and the guide's comparison is meant to leave them in the reader's hands.

### The incoherence verdict and its limits

The claim that the 1978 and 2005 statutes are precisely the ones remembered as incoherent needs defending, because remembered as incoherent is a judgment about reputation rather than a fact about text. The defense runs through the statutes' afterlives. The 1978 package's pieces were argued about for decades: the fuel-use ban was repealed, the gas decontrol schedule had to be finished by the 1989 act, PURPA's avoided-cost contracts became a byword for expensive unintended consequences, and the package as a whole is taught as a collection of measures that fought each other rather than as a program. The 2005 act's titles were implemented on separate tracks by separate agencies with separate constituencies, and the statute is cited as the exemplar of the omnibus that contains multitudes, a reliability law, a drilling law, and a tax law stapled together. In both cases the reputation for incoherence comes from the record of what happened next, not from a critic's aesthetic preference.

The limits of the verdict are set by the 1992 exception, and the exception is worth one more pass because it shows what the claim does not say. The claim does not say that no statute can serve both tracks; it says that the statutes that tried, in 1978 and 2005, are the ones remembered as incoherent, and the 1992 act's coherence is consistent with the claim because its instruments aligned. The refined version of the claim is thus conditional: two-track statutes cohere when their instruments point the same way and endure on the same timescale, and they fragment when the instruments contradict or expire on different schedules. That refinement makes the claim stronger, not weaker, because it turns a slogan into a testable proposition about statutory design. The reader who can say why 1992 worked and 1978 did not has understood the field's central design problem.

### Neutrality as method

Energy is contested on climate, cost, security, and jobs at once, and this guide treats that contest as a fact about the field rather than a problem to be solved by taking a side. The method is even-handed description: each statute is reported by what it did, its instruments, its verified outcomes, and its attributed costs, and the security and environmental tracks are presented with equal care, without implying that either is the legitimate one. The security track's failures are described factually with sources, the Synthetic Fuels Corporation's GAO-documented authority and commonly reported expenditure and outcome, the waste statute's unmet obligation and the litigation it produced, the price controls' constituency trap. The environmental track's instruments get the same treatment: efficiency standards and technology subsidies are reported by their mechanisms and their recorded effects, not celebrated as progress or dismissed as waste.

The neutrality requirement also governs what this article does not do. It makes no reference to any current proposal, since proposals are the material of advocacy rather than of history, and a master article that opined on pending measures would date itself and compromise its function as a hub. It attributes every statistic to a named source and period, GAO report B-202463 for the synfuels borrowing authority, CRS report RL33578 for the windfall tax and decontrol characterization and the 1978 tax provisions, CRS brief IB10054 for the permanent investment credit, so that a skeptical reader can check the numbers rather than take the author's word. And it presents the two-track framing as descriptive rather than normative: the framing explains why the field looks the way it does, and it does not say which track Congress should have ridden. A guide that told the reader which side was right would be a pamphlet; this one aims to be an instrument, and instruments do not take sides.

## The nuclear thread, 1954 to 1982

One way to test the two-track thesis is to follow a single technology through the sequence and watch the tracks take turns. The nuclear thread runs through four statutes across twenty-eight years, and each statute solves the previous statute's leftover problem. The Atomic Energy Act of 1954, Public Law 83-703, signed August 30, 1954, amends and supersedes the 1946 regime's government monopoly and creates the legal possibility of private nuclear power, licensed by the Atomic Energy Commission. The statute leaves two problems unsolved: the liability tail that no private insurer will cover, and the Commission's conflict between promoting the industry and regulating its hazards. The Price-Anderson Act of 1957, Public Law 85-256, signed September 2, 1957, solves the first, adding section 170 to the 1954 act and creating the two-tier liability system with a federal backstop, renewed in 1965, 1975, 1988, and 2005. The Energy Reorganization Act of 1974, Public Law 93-438, signed October 11, 1974, solves the second, abolishing the Commission and dividing promotion, to the Energy Research and Development Administration, from regulation, to the independent Nuclear Regulatory Commission. The Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, Public Law 97-425, signed January 7, 1983, addresses the third leftover, the spent fuel accumulating at reactor sites, by establishing the federal obligation to take title and dispose of it in a permanent repository, funded by a fee on nuclear electricity. That obligation has gone unmet.

The thread's shape confirms the thesis and adds a temporal dimension the chronological pass obscures. The security track builds the industry in the fifties, insures it in the fifties, and reorganizes its governance in the seventies, each step solving a problem the previous step created or revealed. The environmental track arrives as a retrofit: the waste obligation is assumed only after the waste exists, the regulatory split only after the conflict becomes political, and the liability renewals only as the industry's continued dependence on the backstop becomes undeniable. Production first, constraint later is the thread's pattern, and it explains why nuclear politics carries a bitterness the other threads lack. The industry experienced every environmental measure as a change to the rules under which it had invested, while the environmental coalition experienced every security measure as a subsidy to a technology whose full costs the public would bear. Both experiences are accurate, and the thread's four statutes are the documentary record of their collision.

## The efficiency thread, 1975 to 2007

The efficiency thread runs through three statutes across thirty-two years and belongs to both tracks in succession, which makes it the thesis's hardest case and its most instructive. The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975 creates the thread's two founding instruments: Title V's corporate average fuel economy standards for automobiles and Title III's Energy Conservation Program for Consumer Products, the appliance standards. Both are crisis children, enacted to cut oil imports after the embargo, and both belong to the security track by parentage. The National Energy Conservation Policy Act of 1978 extends the thread into programs, authorizing residential conservation services, weatherization, and utility-run audits, on the theory that the cheapest barrel is the one never burned. The Energy Tax Act of 1978, signed the same day, adds the tax-credit form, paying households and firms to invest in conservation and renewables. For three decades the thread then runs quietly: the standards tighten by regulation, the programs persist, and the fuel economy standards freeze, untouched from their 1975 creation.

The Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007, Public Law 110-140, signed December 19, 2007, breaks the freeze, raising CAFE to 35 miles per gallon by 2020, the first increase in three decades, and expanding the Renewable Fuel Standard to 36 billion gallons by 2022. By 2007 the thread's parentage has been forgotten and its upbringing dominates: the standards are debated as environmental measures, supported by the climate coalition and opposed as regulatory burden, though their statutory origin is the embargo. The thread thus demonstrates the motive migration the guide has noted throughout: an instrument created for security is administered for environment, and neither track's claim to it is false. The efficiency thread is also the environmental track's most durable achievement, outlasting the price controls enacted beside it in 1975, the fuel-use ban enacted beside it in 1978, and the tax credits' repeated near-deaths. Efficiency standards, once written, prove harder to repeal than any other instrument in the sequence, because their benefits are diffuse, their costs are embedded in product design, and no constituency organizes to demand less efficient appliances. The thread's durability is the quiet counterpart to the price-regulation arc's twenty-year unwinding: what is hard to enact can be hard to remove, and what is easy to enact, like the SFC, can vanish in six years.

## The reorganization habit, 1974 and 1977

Two of the sequence's statutes reorganise the government rather than regulating the industry, and they form a pair worth reading together. The Energy Reorganization Act of 1974, Public Law 93-438, signed October 11, 1974, abolishes the Atomic Energy Commission and divides its missions between the Energy Research and Development Administration, which inherits promotion and research, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, which inherits safety and licensing as an independent body. The Department of Energy Organization Act of 1977, Public Law 95-91, signed August 4, 1977, gathers ERDA and the scattered federal energy programs into a cabinet department while leaving the NRC independent. The 1974 act separates; the 1977 act consolidates; and the separation survives inside the consolidation, because the conflict the 1974 act institutionalized was not resolved by the 1977 act's larger roof.

The pair illustrates a legislative habit the guide names plainly: Congress reorganizes when it cannot agree on policy. The 1974 split did not settle whether nuclear power should expand or contract; it gave each answer its own agency and left the fighting to appropriations and oversight. The 1977 consolidation did not settle whether the government's energy role should be promotional or regulatory; it put both roles in one department and left the tension to the secretary. Reorganization is the legislative form of agreeing to disagree, and its product is not coherence but jurisdiction: a clearer map of who decides, without a clearer answer to what they should decide. The guide does not dismiss the habit, because jurisdiction matters; the NRC's independence has real consequences for how nuclear regulation works. But it distinguishes the habit from the substance, and it notes that the five-statute 1978 package, enacted a year after the department's creation, displayed the same disagreements the reorganization had been meant to resolve.

The reorganization pair also completes the market-structure arc's institutional dimension. The 1954 act had lodged promotion and regulation in a single commission; the 1974 act judged that arrangement a failure and split it; the 1977 act preserved the split while consolidating everything else. The resulting architecture, a promotional department and an independent regulator, is the institutional form of the two-track thesis: the security track's promotional impulse and the environmental track's regulatory impulse each get a permanent institutional home, and Congress referees between them. That architecture proved the most durable institutional product of the crisis decade, outlasting every price control and the Synthetic Fuels Corporation by an order of magnitude. Institutional design, the sequence suggests, outlasts policy design, because agencies acquire constituencies while policies acquire only critics.

## Why the tidy periodization fails

The guide has described an arc from price regulation to market structuring to technology subsidy, and the table appears to confirm it. This section argues against the guide's own framing, because the brief requires the complication to be presented as an argument with named counterexamples rather than as settled chronology, and because the evidence genuinely resists tidiness. Price regulation persisted alongside market structuring. Subsidies existed throughout. Efficiency standards outlived the controls enacted beside them. The three phases are a useful teaching device and a false history, and a reader who leaves this guide believing that Congress regulated prices in the seventies, structured markets in the eighties, and subsidised technology thereafter has learned the pattern and missed the field.

The counterexamples, each sourced, are these. The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975, the signature conservation statute of the crisis decade, extended petroleum price and allocation controls in the same title that created the Strategic Petroleum Reserve and the fuel economy program. Title IV amended the Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act and kept the controls alive. A statute remembered as the birth of federal efficiency regulation was also a price-control extension act, and any periodization that files 1975 under conservation or deregulation has to explain what the controls were doing there. The crude price regulations were phased out under President Carter in 1979 and fully withdrawn by President Reagan's Executive Order 12287 of January 28, 1981, which means the decontrol most histories attribute to the Reagan era began under his predecessor and was completed by executive order rather than by statute. The 1980 Windfall Profit Tax was the political price of that decontrol, as the Congressional Research Service states in RL33578: Congress bought deregulation with a tax, so the market-structuring moment arrived carrying a new levy. Gas wellhead controls survived until January 1, 1993, which means price regulation overlapped the entire wholesale-competition project of the nineteen nineties; the 1992 act's wheeling provisions were being implemented while the oldest gas ceilings were still in force.

The subsidy record is equally untidy. The Energy Tax Act of 1978 created both the gas-guzzler tax and the residential and business energy credits in a single title, per CRS RL33578, so the tax-and-subsidy pairing predates the supposed subsidy era by fourteen years. The solar and geothermal business investment tax credit was made permanent, per CRS IB10054, which means a technology subsidy achieved permanence while price regulation was still being unwound. Percentage depletion and the expensing of intangible drilling costs kept oil and gas favored in the tax code throughout, per CRS RL33578, so the deregulation era never meant the end of fossil subsidy; it meant the end of price ceilings alongside the continuation of tax preference. The November 9, 1978 package mixed conservation and efficiency measures, NECPA and the Energy Tax Act, with production incentives and phased deregulation in the Natural Gas Policy Act, a single day's signings that spanned all three columns of the artifact table. The 1992 act paired wholesale-market competition with the production tax credit during an era of low oil prices, when no supply crisis motivated any of it. The years 1979 and 1980 paired oil decontrol with the windfall tax and the synfuels subsidies, deregulation and taxation and technology subsidy in a single eighteen-month span.

Two counterexamples cut deeper because they challenge the track assignments themselves. The CAFE standards, the environmental track's signature efficiency instrument, were an energy-security response to the embargo, not an environmental measure; Congress wanted fewer oil imports, and cleaner air was a side effect the environmental coalition welcomed but did not drive. Assigning CAFE to the environmental track, as this guide's table does by classifying the 2007 act as technology subsidy, is defensible but not innocent: the standards' parentage is security, their upbringing environmental. And NEPA and the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments predate the supply crises entirely, which means the environmental track's founding statutes cannot be explained as responses to energy shortage. The track has independent causes, and any history that derives environmental law from energy crisis has the causation backwards for the two most important environmental statutes in the sequence.

### Did price regulation and subsidy ever truly take turns?

No. The 1975 act extended price controls while creating efficiency standards, the 1978 tax title paired a gas-guzzler tax with energy credits, and fossil tax preferences never lapsed during decontrol. The phases overlapped for the entire seventy-year span of the sequence.

The guide's answer to its own complication is that the two-track thesis survives the untidiness better than the three-phase periodization does. The tracks describe motives, security and environment, and motives can coexist in a single statute, a single title, even a single provision, as the 1975, 1978, and 1980 examples show. The phases describe instruments, and instruments genuinely did succeed each other as the dominant mode, but dominance is not exclusivity, and the counterexamples prove it. A reader who holds both ideas at once, tracks as motives that persist, phases as instruments that overlap, has the field's structure. A reader who holds only the phases has a timeline. The guide prefers the structure, and it has tried to earn the preference by naming the evidence against itself.

Two further implications follow from the complication, and the guide states them because they change how the reader uses the thesis. First, the track assignment of any given provision is time-sensitive. CAFE begins as security and becomes environmental; the production credit begins as diversification policy and becomes climate policy; the drilling exclusion begins as supply policy and is debated as environmental rollback. A static table cannot capture these migrations, which is why the guide's prose repeatedly notes parentage and upbringing as separate facts. The researcher who treats the table's classifications as permanent will misread the politics of every reauthorization fight, because the fights are precisely about which track gets to claim the provision's future. Second, the instrument phases are better understood as a change in Congress's default than as a change in its repertoire. Price regulation remains available, as the windfall tax's logic could be revived for any future decontrol; market structuring remains available, as transmission and market-design debates continue; subsidy never left. What changed is which instrument a committee reaches for first, and defaults matter less than the thesis suggests and more than the timeline admits. The guide's final position is therefore modest: tracks over phases, motives over instruments, but neither as settled chronology.

The complication also clarifies what the thesis does not claim. It does not claim that the security track is wiser than the environmental track or the reverse; the neutrality rule forbids the ranking, and the record would not support it anyway, since both tracks have produced durable achievements and expensive failures. It does not claim that every statute fits one track; the 1978 and 2005 acts are the named exceptions, and the 1992 act is the named case of both tracks succeeding together. It does not claim that the tracks explain everything; party control, presidential priorities, budget reconciliation rules, and sheer legislative accident all shape outcomes the tracks cannot predict. What the thesis claims is comparative: that the two-track reading organizes the field better than the three-phase timeline, that it predicts the instrument Congress will choose given the motive, and that it identifies the both-track statutes as the ones where legibility breaks down. That is an argument, offered with its counterexamples named, and the reader is invited to test it against the table.

The thesis also has a predictive form, and the guide states it in conditional terms to respect the neutrality rule's bar on referencing current proposals. Given a future supply shock, the thesis predicts Congress will reach for production incentives and deregulation, because that is what the security track has done in every shock since 1973. Given a future pollution or climate concern that commands a majority, the thesis predicts efficiency standards and technology subsidy, because that is what the environmental track has done since 1970. Given a proposal that tries to serve both at once, the thesis predicts either the 1992 outcome, coherence through separate titles with aligned incentives, or the 1978 and 2005 outcome, incoherence through contradictory instruments, depending on the mechanical test. These are not forecasts but conditional regularities, and their value is diagnostic: when a new energy bill appears, the reader can place its provisions on the tracks, run the alignment test, and know which historical precedent it resembles before the debate concludes. The guide's seventy-year investment pays off in that moment, which is the practical reason for a master article rather than a mere timeline.

The counter-reading deserves its own subsections, because the evidence against tidy phases is detailed enough to repay systematic treatment. What follows gives the four strongest objections their full weight, then states the asymmetry that explains why the tracks were never reconciled.

### Subsidies before the subsidy era

The most persistent objection to the periodization is that subsidies never waited for the subsidy phase, and the objection is well founded. The record this guide has already walked supplies the first exhibits: the 1978 Energy Tax Act's credits and gas-guzzler tax in a single title, the permanent solar and geothermal investment credit documented in CRS brief IB10054, and the percentage depletion and intangible-drilling-cost preferences that kept oil and gas favored in the tax code through every phase, per CRS report RL33578. The subsidy phase did not invent the instrument; it inherited a tax code already full of energy preferences and made adding to them the main business of energy legislation.

The 1992 production credit belongs in this account as the subsidy that grew up inside the market phase. Enacted as a modest per-kilowatt-hour sweetener alongside wholesale competition, the credit became the dominant federal support for wind power, repeatedly extended across three decades. Its growth illustrates the constituency dynamic that subsidies generate: a credit enacted to nurture an infant industry becomes, as the industry grows, a benefit its recipients defend as essential, and expiration becomes politically unthinkable. The credit's history also shows how subsidies migrate across tracks: born as an environmental-track instrument in a market-structuring statute, it was later defended on jobs and manufacturing grounds, the security track's language, whenever extension required a broader coalition. The tidy story would file the credit under the subsidy phase; the honest story files it under 1992 and notes that the phase boundary runs through the middle of its life.

The windfall profit tax belongs here too, as the fiscal shadow of the subsidy story. A tax calibrated to the gap between controlled and market prices is the mirror image of a subsidy calibrated to the gap between market prices and desired investment returns; both are fiscal instruments that adjust market outcomes toward politically chosen numbers. The 1979 to 1980 period's pairing of decontrol with the windfall tax and the synfuels program shows Congress using the full fiscal toolkit, taxing on one hand and spending on the other, while the market instruments did their work in the middle. The phases describe which toolkit was dominant, not which was present, and the fiscal toolkit was never absent.

### Regulation after the regulation era

The symmetric objection concerns regulation's persistence after its supposed end. Gas wellhead controls survived until January 1, 1993, fifteen years into the market-structuring phase. The Price-Anderson liability cap was renewed in 1965, 1975, 1988, and 2005, a 1950s price-setting mechanism operating through every phase including the subsidy era. Appliance efficiency standards, created by the 1975 act's Title III, were tightened through rulemakings for decades, which means command regulation of product design continued uninterrupted across all three phases. CAFE standards, frozen through the market phase, were revived and raised in 2007, the price era's demand-side instrument returning as the subsidy era's climate instrument.

These persistences do not refute the periodization; they specify what it means. "Center of gravity" is doing the real work in the thesis: in each phase, most legislative energy went to one kind of instrument, while the other kinds continued in the background. The background matters, and this article gives it weight, because a reader who believes price regulation ended in 1981 will misunderstand the gas story, the liability story, and the standards story alike. But the foreground matters too: the statutes that defined each era, that consumed the political capital and the committee time, were price statutes before 1978, market statutes between 1978 and 2005, and subsidy statutes after 2005. The argument is quantitative and political, not logical: it claims something about where Congress spent its effort, and the counterexamples discipline the claim without defeating it.

A further complication concerns the direction of causation between crises and statutes. The tidy story implies that supply shocks produce legislation, and often they do: the embargo produced the EPAA and EPCA, the second oil shock produced the 1978 package and the 1980 acts. But the 1992 act was enacted during low oil prices with no crisis compelling it, the 1969 to 1970 environmental statutes preceded the crises, and the 2005 act's reliability title answered a blackout rather than a fuel shock. Legislation also follows institutional logic, regulatory learning, and the ordinary accumulation of constituencies: PURPA's experiment showed independent generation worked, which made the 1992 act thinkable; the 1978 credits created solar constituencies, which made later extensions thinkable. Crises open windows, but the ideas that pass through them are built between crises, and a field-level view must give the quiet decades their due.

### The quiet decades and what they built

A final complication concerns the decades between the bursts, which the crisis-driven telling of the field tends to skip. The 1980s after the synfuels collapse, and the 1990s after the 1992 act, were not legislative deserts; they were the periods in which the previous burst's instruments were tested, litigated, and absorbed, and in which the ideas for the next burst were built. PURPA's avoided-cost contracts were litigated and repriced through the 1980s, which taught regulators and legislators the difference between administratively set prices and market prices, a lesson that shaped the 1992 act's preference for open access over price-setting. The gas decontrol schedule ground through its phases, creating the record of phased deregulation that the 1989 act's drafters relied on. The production credit's early years showed what a per-kilowatt-hour incentive could and could not do, which shaped every later credit's design.

The institutional learning of the quiet decades is as important as the statutory output. FERC's implementation of the 1992 wheeling authority through Order 888 in 1996 was regulatory rather than legislative, but it completed the market architecture the statute had sketched, which shows that phases are built by agencies and courts as well as by Congress. The NRC's licensing work through the 1980s and 1990s, conducted under the 1974 reorganization's split, established the regulatory credibility that later reactor debates would invoke. The Department of Energy's management of the stockpile, the weapons complex, and the research portfolio through the same decades kept the institutional machinery intact for the subsidy era's later use. A field-level view that counts only statutes misses half the action; the periods between the laws are when the laws become the field.

This is also why the crisis theory of legislation needs its qualifier. Crises open windows, but the ideas that pass through them are built between crises, and the statutes that result bear the marks of the quiet decades' learning. The 1992 act was thinkable because PURPA had been tested; the 2005 act's reliability title was thinkable because the grid's institutions had been studied; the 2009 act's loan programs were thinkable because the SFC's failure had been absorbed. The tidy story says shocks produce statutes; the honest story says shocks produce occasions, and the occasions are used by people carrying ideas the quiet decades built.

### What the phases do not explain

An honest argument names what it cannot explain, and the three-phase story has three notable blind spots. First, it does not explain the things that never changed. The NRC's licensing regime, the NEPA review requirement, the Clean Air Act's standards framework, the tax code's percentage depletion and IDC expensing, the Price-Anderson liability cap: these instruments persisted through all three phases, shaping outcomes in every era, and a phase story built on change has little to say about continuity. The field's most durable features are its least phase-like, and any reader who mistakes the phases for the whole story will miss the permanent architecture inside which the phases operated.

Second, the phases do not explain timing very well. Why did the market-structuring phase begin in 1978 rather than earlier or later? The embargo was 1973; the intellectual case against price controls was older still. The answer involves politics, personalities, and the particular exhaustion of the Carter-era control regime, none of which the phase labels capture. Why did the subsidy phase begin in 2005 rather than with the 1992 credit or the 1978 credits? The answer involves the 2003 blackout, gasoline prices, and the tax title's coalition arithmetic, again beyond what the labels explain. Phases describe; they do not cause, and the causes live in the political histories the specialist articles tell.

Third, the phases underweight the role of the courts and the agencies. FERC's Order 888 did more to build wholesale markets than the 1992 act's text alone; the NRC's licensing decisions shaped nuclear power more than any statute after 1974; the judiciary's enforcement of the waste statute's obligations defined the NWPA's legacy more than its text did. A statute-centered master article necessarily foregrounds Congress, but the field was co-authored by regulators and judges, and the phase story's congressional focus is a limitation to acknowledge rather than a completeness to claim. The argument earns trust by stating its blind spots plainly, and the blind spots do not defeat the pattern; they locate it, as one lens among several through which the field can be read.

None of this defeats the periodization; it disciplines it. The claim is only that the center of legislative gravity moved: from setting prices, to setting market rules, to funding technologies. The counterexamples show what "center of gravity" means: the old instruments persisted, the new instruments arrived early, and the motives crossed tracks. An honest thesis carries its exceptions in the open, and the exceptions are what make the pattern worth trusting.

### The asymmetry of the two tracks

The tracks are not symmetric, and the asymmetry explains why they have almost never been reconciled. The environmental track's great instruments are constraints: NEPA review, air quality standards, efficiency mandates. Constraints are permanent; once a review requirement or an emissions standard exists, it governs every project thereafter, and repealing it requires its own coalition. The security track's great instruments are episodic: stockpile releases, decontrol bargains, production incentives tied to price spikes. A stockpile can be drawn down, a control can be lifted, a tax can be repealed, and the emergency that justified each one passes. The field's structure reflects this difference: environmental law accumulates, security law oscillates.

The asymmetry also explains the pattern of the incoherent statutes. The 1978 and 2005 packages tried to do permanent things and episodic things in the same vehicle: conservation mandates and efficiency standards alongside decontrol schedules and drilling incentives, constraints that would last decades next to bargains struck for the politics of the moment. The permanent and the episodic pull against each other because they answer to different time horizons, and a statute that contains both inherits the tension. The 1992 act avoided it because its instruments shared a horizon: wholesale markets and the production credit were both built to last, both structural rather than episodic. The condition for two-track coherence is thus temporal as well as directional: the instruments must point the same way and endure on the same timescale.

There is a final asymmetry worth naming, concerning who bears the costs. Price regulation's costs fell on producers and, through shortages, on consumers; market structuring's costs fell on incumbents who lost monopoly rents; subsidies' costs fall on taxpayers and, through mandates, on ratepayers and fuel buyers. Each phase thus created its own constituency of the aggrieved, and each constituency became a lobby for the next round. The field's politics are the sediment of its instruments: price controls beget decontrol fights, and credits beget extension battles. Understanding the tracks means understanding that every statute is also an interest-group creation event, and the interests created outlive the crises that created them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What energy legislation has Congress passed since 1950?

The sequence runs from the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 and the Price-Anderson Act of 1957, through the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 and the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1970, into the crisis decade's Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act of 1973, Energy Reorganization Act of 1974, Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975, and Department of Energy Organization Act of 1977. The five-statute National Energy Act package followed in 1978, then the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act and the Energy Security Act with its Synthetic Fuels Corporation in 1980, the Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, and the Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989. The Energy Policy Act of 1992 opened wholesale transmission and created the renewable production credit. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 and the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 followed, then the 2009 recovery act's energy title and the 2015 crude export repeal. Three later statutes, of 2020, 2021, and 2022, appear by name only in this guide.

### Q: How did energy legislation move from price controls to subsidies?

The movement took two decades and was never clean. The 1973 allocation act imposed petroleum price and allocation controls, and the 1975 conservation act extended them even while creating the stockpile and fuel economy standards. Crude decontrol began under President Carter in 1979 and finished with Executive Order 12287 in January 1981, while natural gas wellhead controls survived until January 1, 1993. Subsidies ran in parallel throughout: the 1978 Energy Tax Act created energy credits, the 1992 act created the renewable production credit, the 2005 act's tax title cut $14.5 billion over eleven years per the Congressional Research Service, and the 2009 recovery act delivered the largest single energy investment before 2022. Fossil tax preferences like percentage depletion never lapsed during decontrol. The honest account is overlap, not succession, with market structuring bridging the two.

### Q: What was the Synthetic Fuels Corporation in energy legislation?

The Synthetic Fuels Corporation was a federal financing corporation created by Title I of the Energy Security Act of 1980, Public Law 96-294, signed June 30, 1980, to fund commercial-scale plants converting coal, oil shale, and tar sands into liquid fuels. Its statutory goals were 500,000 barrels per day of oil equivalent by 1987 and 2 million per day by 1992. The Government Accountability Office documented initial borrowing authority of $20 billion in Treasury notes plus potential future authorizations up to $68 billion. Commonly reported figures, qualified as such rather than GAO-sourced, put actual spending near $960 million on four projects, none commercially viable, with the goals never met. Congress terminated the corporation through legislation enacted December 19, 1985, effective April 18, 1986. It remains the field's canonical expensive technology-subsidy failure and the reason later credits were designed to pay for performance.

### Q: What did the 1992 energy legislation change?

The Energy Policy Act of 1992, Public Law 102-486, signed October 24, 1992, changed wholesale electricity markets and federal technology subsidy in a single statute. It created exempt wholesale generators, freeing independent producers from holding-company restrictions, and authorised the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to order transmission wheeling, the authority behind the Commission's Order 888 in 1996. Those two provisions built the competitive wholesale markets that followed. The act also created the renewable electricity production tax credit, a per-kilowatt-hour payment for qualifying generation that became the most durable technology subsidy in the field, and it extended efficiency standards to new equipment categories. Enacted during low oil prices, it showed both tracks advancing without a supply crisis. The guide treats it as the hinge between the market-structuring era and the subsidy era.

### Q: Which energy legislation created the Department of Energy?

The Department of Energy Organization Act of 1977, Public Law 95-91, 91 Stat. 565, signed August 4, 1977, by President Carter, created the Department of Energy. It gathered the Energy Research and Development Administration, the Federal Energy Administration, and scattered energy programs from across the government into a single cabinet department. The reorganization answered the fragmentation the crisis decade had exposed, when energy responsibilities were spread across agencies with competing missions. The 1974 reorganization had already split the Atomic Energy Commission's promotional and regulatory functions between ERDA and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission; the 1977 act folded the promotional side into the new department while the NRC remained independent. Reorganization proved easier than policy agreement, as the five-statute 1978 package demonstrated a year later.

### Q: What did the 2009 stimulus do in energy legislation?

The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, Public Law 111-5, signed February 17, 2009, by President Obama, delivered the largest single energy investment before 2022 through its energy title. The act was recession legislation first, and its energy provisions moved through grants, loan guarantees, and tax measures toward efficiency, renewable generation, grid modernisation, and advanced vehicles. The guide characterises it as a technology-subsidy surge and attaches no dollar figure, since the verified source supplies none. Structurally it confirmed that subsidy had become the default federal energy instrument: where the seventies answered crisis with controls and the eighties with decontrol, the 2009 government answered a demand collapse with investment. The title belongs primarily to the environmental track, with security-track benefits in domestic supply.

### Q: Which energy legislation addressed nuclear waste?

The Nuclear Waste Policy Act of 1982, Public Law 97-425, 96 Stat. 2201, signed January 7, 1983, by President Reagan, addressed commercial nuclear waste. The short title carries 1982 but the signing came in January 1983, a dating the guide states explicitly. The act established the federal obligation to take title to spent nuclear fuel and dispose of it in a permanent geologic repository, funded by a fee on nuclear-generated electricity. The obligation the act created has gone unmet, the anchor fact the guide carries for this statute. It was meant to remove the waste obstacle to the nuclear industry the 1954 and 1957 acts had called into being, and the obstacle's persistence is part of the statute's own record. The guide classifies it as market structure, a federal assumption of a liability the market could not bear.

### Q: What is the timeline of US energy legislation?

1954: Atomic Energy Act commercialises nuclear power. 1957: Price-Anderson makes it insurable. 1969-70: NEPA and Clean Air Act amendments constrain every later project. 1973: Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act imposes controls. 1974: reorganization splits promotion from regulation. 1975: Energy Policy and Conservation Act creates the stockpile, CAFE, and appliance standards while extending controls. 1977: Department of Energy created. 1978: five-statute National Energy Act package. 1980: windfall profit tax and the Synthetic Fuels Corporation. 1982: Nuclear Waste Policy Act. 1989: gas wellhead decontrol completed. 1992: wholesale competition and the production credit. 2005: both-track energy act. 2007: fuel economy raised, fuel mandate expanded. 2009: recovery act energy investment. 2015: crude export repeal. 2020, 2021, 2022: later statutes by name only.

### Q: Why did the 1954 atomic statute open commercial nuclear power to private firms?

The Atomic Energy Act of 1954, Public Law 83-703, signed August 30, 1954, amended and superseded the 1946 regime's government monopoly by authorizing the Atomic Energy Commission to license private firms to possess nuclear material and operate nuclear facilities. The security logic was that atomic power offered domestic electricity independent of imported fuel, and the government, having built the science at public expense during the war, would transfer enough of it to seed a commercial industry. The act divided promotion from regulation within the Commission, a division judged a failure and undone in 1974. It left the liability problem unsolved, which the 1957 Price-Anderson Act then addressed. The statute's achievement was architectural: it created the legal possibility of private nuclear power in the United States.

### Q: What did the Price-Anderson Act change about nuclear accident liability?

The Price-Anderson Act of 1957, Public Law 85-256, signed September 2, 1957, amended the 1954 act by adding section 170, codified at 42 U.S.C. section 2210, creating a two-tier liability system for commercial reactor accidents. Operators carried a primary layer of private insurance and the federal government stood behind a secondary layer, capping total liability and channeling claims into a defined compensation framework. Without that backstop, the tail risk of a catastrophic accident was unpriceable and uninsurable, and no utility would have operated a large plant. Congress renewed the framework in 1965, 1975, 1988, and 2005, so the later liability regime should not be dated to 1957 alone. The founding move was pure security track: the government supplied the missing insurance market so private capital would build the plants it wanted.

### Q: Why did Congress abolish the Atomic Energy Commission in 1974?

The Energy Reorganization Act of 1974, Public Law 93-438, signed October 11, 1974, abolished the Atomic Energy Commission because its dual mission had become untenable: the same agency promoted nuclear power and regulated its hazards. The environmental movement of the sixties made the conflict visible, and the crisis decade made it political. The act created the Energy Research and Development Administration for the promotional and research mission and the independent Nuclear Regulatory Commission for safety and licensing. The split institutionalized the tension between the tracks rather than resolving it, giving each its own agency. The 1977 Department of Energy act later folded ERDA's functions into the new department while the NRC stayed independent, preserving the 1974 separation inside a larger reorganization.

### Q: Why did PURPA create independent power producers?

Before 1978, only regulated monopoly utilities generated electricity as a matter of law and practice. PURPA required utilities to buy power from qualifying independent facilities at the utility's avoided cost, creating a legal right for non-utility generators to enter the market and a guaranteed buyer for their output. The purchase obligation broke the generation monopoly without deregulating retail rates, which is why it counts as market structuring rather than deregulation. The experiment proved independent generation worked, exposed the flaws of administratively set avoided-cost rates, and set up the 1992 act's wholesale competition, making PURPA the hinge between the price-regulation era and the market era.

### Q: Why was the 1980 windfall profit tax tied to oil price decontrol?

The Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980, Public Law 96-223, signed April 2, 1980, was the political price of ending crude price controls. Decontrol, begun under President Carter in 1979, let domestic oil reprice toward world levels and promised producers a windfall. Congress would not permit that transfer without capturing part of it, so the act imposed an excise tax on the difference between the controlled and market prices. The Congressional Research Service describes the tax plainly as the political price of decontrol in RL33578, and the guide follows that sourcing. The tax was neither conservation nor production policy but the legislative consideration that bought the votes for deregulation. Controls ended fully with Executive Order 12287 in January 1981, while the tax outlived them as the bargain's fiscal residue.

### Q: What ended federal control of natural gas wellhead prices?

The phaseout began with the Natural Gas Policy Act of 1978, Public Law 95-621, which replaced flat interstate ceilings with tiered categories scheduled for gradual decontrol, and it finished with the Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989, Public Law 101-60, signed July 26, 1989, with final repeal effective January 1, 1993. The fifteen-year unwinding measured the political difficulty of ending price regulation even after its failure was conceded: Congress protected existing contracts, staged the categories, and let the oldest ceilings linger. The 1989 act is the terminal entry in the guide's price-regulation column. From the 1973 allocation bureaucracy to the last ceiling's expiration in 1993, the price-regulation era ran twenty years, and its end was legislated in stages rather than decreed at once.

### Q: Why did Congress create the Strategic Petroleum Reserve?

Part B, section 151 of the 1975 crisis statute authorized up to 1 billion barrels of emergency stockpiled oil as a government-owned buffer against future supply shocks. The 1973 embargo had shown that private inventories would not cover a cutoff and that allocation rules alone could not conjure supply, so Congress built a physical reserve the president could draw down in a crisis. The reserve was filled over the following decades and drawn down in later disruptions. It became the security track's most tangible asset: not a price rule, not a market structure, but a stockpile, the literal embodiment of the idea that the government should hold fuel against the next shock.

### Q: What did the Nuclear Waste Policy Act obligate the federal government to do?

The 1982 act obligated the federal government to take title to civilian spent nuclear fuel and dispose of it in a permanent geologic repository by statutory deadlines, funded by a fee levied on nuclear-generated electricity. The repository was never built and the deadlines passed, so utilities sued for breach of the disposal contracts and courts held the government liable for its failure to perform. The obligation was cleanly allocated and then cleanly defaulted on, which is why the statute is taught as the field's clearest case of a promise outrunning institutional capacity and why waste disposal remains the unresolved precondition of any nuclear expansion.

### Q: Which law banned crude oil exports and which repealed the ban?

The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975, section 103, codified at 42 U.S.C. section 6212a, restricted the export of domestically produced crude oil as one of the crisis statute's four instruments. The ban outlived the shortage it addressed by four decades, surviving oil decontrol, the end of the Cold War, and the entire market-structuring phase. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2016, Public Law 114-113, Division O, Title I, section 101, signed December 18, 2015, by President Obama, repealed it. The forty-year afterlife shows how emergency measures create constituencies that survive the emergency, and how repeal needs its own coalition.

### Q: Which track produced fuel economy standards, security or environmental?

Security produced them, though the environmental track raised them. The CAFE program was created by Title V of the Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975 as a response to the oil embargo: Congress wanted fewer oil imports, and cleaner air was a welcomed side effect rather than the driving motive. The standards then froze for three decades until the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 raised them to 35 miles per gallon by 2020, the first increase since the program's creation. The guide classifies the 2007 act as technology subsidy in the environmental track's tradition, a defensible but not innocent assignment, since the instrument's parentage is security and its upbringing environmental. The example warns against tidy track assignments: motives migrate across decades, and a single instrument can serve both tracks in succession.

### Q: What made the 2005 statute an example of both-track lawmaking?

The Energy Policy Act of 2005 combined security-track and environmental-track instruments without reconciling them. For the security track it imposed mandatory reliability enforcement, repealed the 1935 holding-company act, and excluded certain drilling from specified environmental review. For the environmental track it expanded credits for renewables, clean coal, and nuclear power. The Congressional Research Service's tax-title analysis found $14.5 billion in reductions over eleven years, including $2.6 billion in oil and gas incentives alongside roughly $4.5 billion for renewables, subsidising incumbent fuels and their alternatives in the same law. Each provision was coherent alone; the aggregate preached market discipline while practicing industrial policy. Defenders call the breadth the price of bipartisanship. The guide's namable claim marks it, with 1978, as a both-track statute remembered as incoherent.

### Q: What made the 1978 National Energy Act incoherent?

It tried to serve both tracks at once. The five statutes signed on November 9, 1978, combined conservation mandates and tax credits with a fuel-use prohibition, phased gas decontrol, and PURPA's market-making purchase obligation. Prohibition and decontrol pulled in opposite directions: one commanded which fuels power plants could burn while the other let gas prices float toward the market. The fuel-use ban was later repealed, the decontrol schedule needed the 1989 act to finish, and PURPA's avoided-cost contracts produced expensive unintended consequences. Each piece answered a different diagnosis, and no single theory connected them, which is why the package is remembered as sprawling and internally contradictory rather than as a coherent program.
