## The Requirement Before a Transfer May Be Completed

Before a licensed dealer may hand over a firearm to a buyer who holds no license, a federal background check must occur. The transfer does not rest on the judgment of the dealer alone or on a representation by the buyer alone. Federal law interposes a system outside the transaction, and it requires the licensee to consult that system before the transfer is completed. The firearm may change hands only after the consultation has produced the result the statute specifies, or after the waiting interval the statute specifies has elapsed without a notification that receipt would be unlawful, and only after the licensee has verified the identity of the person at the counter through a photo identification document. The sequence matters as much as the substance, because the check belongs to the interval between agreement and completion, while the licensee retains the power to stop the transaction altogether.

![Licensed dealer initiating a NICS background check while a buyer completes the federal firearms transaction record - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The operative question the provision answers is therefore narrow and concrete. The question is whether a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer may complete a transfer of a firearm to a person who is not a licensee, and if so, what must happen first. The answer is structured as a prohibition with conditions. The licensee may not transfer unless the stated steps occur before completion. Every later feature of the system, from the databases consulted to the responses returned to the records retained or destroyed, exists to serve that single gating function. A check that produces no bar permits the transfer to proceed under the terms of the provision. A check that produces a notification of unlawfulness closes the gate. A check that produces neither outcome within the statutory interval leaves the gate in the position the statute defines for that circumstance. The provision does not create a general licensing scheme for buyers. The duty attaches to a defined class of transferors, acting in a defined kind of transaction, before completion rather than after.

The formal identity of the law explains both the design and the chronology. The check requirement was created by the [Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act](/2021/04/15/brady-act-1993-guide/), cited as Public Law 103-159 and printed at 107 Stat. 1536, signed on November 30, 1993 by President Clinton. The operative check provision was codified at 18 U.S.C. 922(t), within the federal firearms provisions that govern licensees and prohibited receipt. Implementing regulations for the system appear at 28 C.F.R. Part 25. The statute did not move from enactment to a permanent national instant system in a single step. Between signature and the permanent arrangement, the law passed through an interim phase with a different mechanism, a different decision maker, and a different relationship between time and permission. Where the interim arrangement used elapsed time and a local official as the checking mechanism, the permanent arrangement used a national system and a consultation measured in responses rather than in a waiting period imposed on every covered purchase. The mandate in subsection (t) belongs to the permanent arrangement.

During the interim phase, from February 28, 1994 to November 29, 1998, handgun transfers carried a waiting period of up to five business days while the chief law enforcement officer of the locality of the buyer ran the check. The structure placed the burden of inquiry on a local official rather than on a national system, and it placed a defined interval between purchase and delivery for handguns as the ordinary course. The permanent system replaced that arrangement on November 30, 1998, when the National Instant Criminal Background Check System, known by the acronym NICS, launched under the operation of the Federal Bureau of Investigation. From launch, the check covered handguns and long guns alike. The operating unit is the NICS Section of the Criminal Justice Information Services Division of the FBI, located in Clarksburg, West Virginia.

### What must happen before a licensed dealer transfers a firearm?

Before a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer transfers a firearm to a person who is not a licensee, the licensee must contact the National Instant Criminal Background Check System, obtain a unique identification number or allow three business days to pass without a denial notification, and verify identity through photo identification.

That short answer contains the whole mandate, and each element carries weight that a summary can obscure. The duty holder is the licensee. The trigger is a transfer to a person who is not a licensee. The timing is before completion of the transfer. The first act is contact with the system. The second element is disjunctive, meaning that either of two outcomes satisfies it: the system provides a unique identification number, or three business days have elapsed since contact without a notification that receipt would violate the governing prohibitions. The third element is independent of the system response and concerns the person in front of the licensee: identity verified by examining a valid photo identification document. A licensee who performs only two of the three elements has not performed the mandate, and a licensee who performs all three after the firearm has been delivered has performed them too late.

The class of persons bound by paragraph (1) of subsection (t) is stated in the text with precision. The paragraph addresses a licensee who is a licensed importer, a licensed manufacturer, or a licensed dealer. Those three categories exhaust the duty holders named in the provision. The other party to the covered transaction is described by a negative: a person who is not a licensee. Where both parties hold licenses, the paragraph by its terms does not supply the check requirement for that transfer, because the transferee falls outside the described class. Where the transferor holds no license, the paragraph likewise does not supply the requirement. The federal check requirement therefore attaches to transfers by licensees, a boundary that defines both the reach of the mandate and its limit. The [licensee framework and the prohibited categories that the check enforces](/2021/04/01/gun-control-act-1968-complete-guide/) come from the broader federal firearms structure within which subsection (t) sits, and the check operates as a screening point inside that structure rather than as a freestanding prohibition of its own. Subsection (t) supplies the procedure, and the substantive bars on receipt that the procedure tests are stated in subsections (g) and (n).

The first element, contact, sounds administrative and carries the operative force of the provision. Before completion of the transfer, the licensee contacts the system. Contact is the act that opens the inquiry and starts every clock the paragraph contains. The three business day interval in the second element runs from the contact, not from the agreement to sell and not from the arrival of the buyer. Without contact, no interval runs and no number issues. The provision therefore gives the licensee no route to compliance that bypasses the system and relies on elapsed time alone. Time counts only after the system has been consulted. That ordering prevents the waiting interval from becoming a substitute for the check itself. The interval is a fallback inside a check that has begun, available when the system has not returned the identifying response and has not notified the licensee of a violation.

The second element deserves close attention because its two branches produce different kinds of assurance. In the first branch, the system provides a unique identification number. Under paragraph (2), that number is assigned where receipt would not violate subsection (g) or (n) or State law, and the number functions within the transaction as the system marker that the inquiry returned no bar. In the second branch, three business days have elapsed since the licensee contacted the system and the system has not notified the licensee that receipt would violate subsection (g) or (n). The statute defines a business day for this purpose as a day on which State offices are open in the State in which the transfer takes place, so the interval is measured by the administrative calendar of the State of transfer rather than by a uniform federal calendar. The second branch rests on silence after a defined interval rather than on an affirmative number, and the distinction matters for the record duty and for the meaning of permission.

Permission under the second branch is framed by the structure of a prohibition. Paragraph (1) states that the licensee may not transfer unless the conditions are met. Once three business days have elapsed after contact without a denial notification, the condition stated in that branch is met, and the paragraph no longer bars the transfer on that ground. The statute permits in that circumstance; the paragraph does not command the licensee to transfer. A licensee confronted with an unresolved inquiry retains the choice to wait or to decline, because satisfaction of a condition that lifts a prohibition is not an order to complete a sale. State law may impose a longer wait in some states, and such a State requirement operates alongside the federal condition rather than being displaced by it. Paragraph (2) itself frames the affirmative number by reference to State law as well as to subsections (g) and (n), which confirms that the check sits inside a layered set of prohibitions rather than above them.

The third element, identity verification, anchors the records inquiry to a physical person. The licensee must have verified the identity of the transferee by examining a valid photo identification document. A records check run against a name supplied in conversation, without examination of a document that ties that name to the person present, would leave the third element unperformed. Conversely, examination of a document without contact with the system would leave the first element unperformed. The two acts answer different risks. Contact tests the identifying information against records of prohibition held in the system. Document examination tests whether the person completing the transaction is the person whose identifying information was tested. The mandate requires both tests before completion, and the provision assigns the examination to the licensee rather than to the system, because only a person present with the buyer can perform it.

Paragraph (4) adds a record duty that attaches at the moment the affirmative branch is used. When the system notifies the licensee that the information available to the system does not demonstrate that receipt of a firearm by the transferee would violate law, and the licensee transfers a firearm to the transferee, the licensee includes the unique identification number in the record of the transfer. The duty is specific. The number, and not a narrative of the inquiry, is the item the paragraph requires in the record. The requirement links the completed transfer to the system response that preceded it, so that the record of that transfer carries the identifier assigned to that inquiry. A transfer completed on the elapsed time branch presents a different record posture, because no unique identification number has been provided in that branch, and the paragraph by its terms addresses the case in which the system has notified the licensee and provided the number. On the licensee side, paragraph (4) preserves a single connecting identifier inside a record that remains with the licensee.

Paragraph (5) states the consequences when a licensee knowingly transfers a firearm in violation of paragraph (1). The mental state element is part of the provision: the paragraph addresses a licensee who knowingly transfers in violation, rather than imposing its sanctions on every defective transaction regardless of knowledge. After notice and an opportunity for a hearing, the sanctions are suspension of the license for not more than six months, or revocation of the license, and a civil fine of not more than $5,000. The combination reaches both the permission to operate and money. Suspension interrupts the licensed business for a bounded period, revocation ends the license, and the fine adds a monetary penalty capped at the stated amount. The procedural condition matters as well: a knowing violation is not answered by an automatic forfeiture stated in the text, because the paragraph builds notice and a hearing opportunity into the enforcement mechanism itself, before the listed consequences are imposed.

Paragraph (6) addresses a different exposure, on the government side of the system. Governments and their employees who are responsible for providing information to the system are not liable for damages because of a failure to provide information to the system that would prevent an unlawful transfer, and are likewise not liable for damages because information in the system prevented a transfer that would have been lawful. The immunity runs in both directions of error. A gap in the information supplied to the system, followed by an unlawful transfer that complete information might have prevented, does not create damages liability for the government or employee responsible for providing information. An entry in the system that blocks a transfer that would have been lawful likewise does not create damages liability on that ground. The paragraph reflects a structural choice about the information supply chain, because the system depends on information provided by governments at several levels. The immunity is bounded by its subject: the paragraph speaks to liability for damages for the provision or effect of information in the system, and it does not alter the licensee duties in paragraph (1), the record duty in paragraph (4), or the sanctions in paragraph (5).

The mandate, stated in full, is therefore a compact allocation of acts, timing, and consequences. A defined licensee must act before completion. Contact opens the inquiry. A number closes it affirmatively, or three business days of silence close the federal condition by interval, with a business day counted by the calendar of State offices in the State of transfer. A photo identification document examined by the licensee ties the inquiry to the person present. A number received and used is entered in the transfer record. A knowing violation exposes the licensee, after notice and a hearing opportunity, to suspension bounded at six months, to revocation, and to a civil fine bounded at $5,000.

What the mandate does not do is equally part of its meaning. The paragraph does not impose the check on transfers outside its defined pairing of a licensee transferor and a nonlicensee transferee, and it does not erase State law, which paragraph (2) treats as an independent source of bars and which in some states adds waiting time beyond the federal interval.

With the mandate fixed in those terms, the operation of a single check becomes the natural ground of inquiry: how identifying information moves from the licensee to the system or to a State contact point, how the system searches its records and returns proceed, denied, or delayed responses, and how the interval, the record, and the response interact inside one transaction from first contact to completed or abandoned transfer.

## The Check in Motion From Form to Determination

A NICS background check begins inside a licensed business, at a counter, with paper, before any database is searched and before any firearm changes hands. The sequence matters because the statute at 18 U.S.C. 922(t) places the duties in a particular order. A licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer may not transfer a firearm to a person who is not a licensee unless, before completion of the transfer, the licensee contacts the system, receives the response the statute requires, and verifies the identity of the transferee by examining a valid photo identification document issued by a government. The check is therefore not a separate errand that a buyer completes in advance and carries from store to store. The check belongs to a specific attempted transfer, initiated by the licensee who would make that transfer, and anchored to the records that licensee creates at the premises where the transaction occurs. Every later step, the transmission of descriptors, the automated search, the review by an examiner, and the assignment of a transaction number, flows from that starting point at the counter.

### The Paper Record at the Counter

The buyer completes ATF Form 4473, the Firearms Transaction Record, at the premises of the licensee. The form records identity and eligibility answers, and the entries on the form supply the factual foundation for the check that follows. Identity in this setting is not established by assertion alone. The licensee verifies the identity of the buyer against a government photo identification document presented during the transaction, comparing the person and the information offered for the transfer with the document that the statute requires the licensee to examine. The examination serves two connected purposes within the design of 18 U.S.C. 922(t). The examination confirms that the individual standing at the counter is the individual named in the transaction record, and the examination ties the identifying information transmitted to the system to a document issued through a government process rather than to a name supplied without corroboration.

The placement of Form 4473 at the beginning of the sequence also explains the division of responsibility that runs through the entire check. The buyer supplies the information and the answers recorded on the form. The licensee controls the process that follows, because the statutory duty to contact the system rests on the licensee, and because the licensee is the party prohibited from completing the transfer until the conditions in paragraph (1) of subsection (t) have been satisfied. The form remains part of the licensee record of the transfer rather than a filing sent to and retained by the agency that operates the check. That separation between the transaction record held by the licensee and the inquiry conducted through the system recurs at every stage, in the limited information transmitted for the search, in the number assigned to a valid inquiry, and in the responses that return to the licensee without any transfer of the underlying firearm description to the system.

### Transmission to the Federal Bureau of Investigation or the State Point of Contact

After the form has been completed and identity has been verified against the photo document, the licensee transmits the identifying information of the buyer to the system. Transmission occurs through one of two channels. A licensee may use NICS E-Check online, or a licensee may transmit the information by telephone. The destination depends on the arrangement in force in the state where the transfer takes place. In a state with a point of contact, the inquiry goes to the designated state agency, which conducts the check. In a jurisdiction without a state point of contact for the transaction, the inquiry goes directly to the Federal Bureau of Investigation. In either arrangement the operative event is the same. The licensee contacts the system before completion of the transfer, and the system receives a defined and limited set of information for the purpose of searching records for a prohibition.

The information transmitted consists of the name of the buyer and descriptors of the buyer. The descriptors named in FBI operations materials include date of birth, sex, race, height, weight, and place of birth. A Social Security number may be included, and inclusion is optional. The optional character of that number reflects the structure of the inquiry, which rests on the combination of name and descriptive identifiers rather than on a single government number. Descriptors allow records held in criminal history and other searched systems to be compared against the person described in the inquiry, so that a record belonging to a different person who shares a name can be distinguished from a record that matches the buyer across multiple identifiers, and so that a possible match returned by an automated search can be tested by a human examiner against the full set of descriptors supplied by the licensee.

Equally significant is the information the licensee does not transmit. The system is told whether the firearm involved in the transaction is a handgun or a long gun. The system receives no make of the firearm, no model of the firearm, and no serial number of the firearm. The inquiry therefore identifies a person for record searching and identifies only the broad category of firearm for purposes of the check, while the specific firearm remains described in the records of the licensee rather than in the inquiry sent to the government system. That boundary is built into the transaction itself, at the moment of transmission, and it holds regardless of the channel used. An inquiry by telephone conveys the same category information and withholds the same firearm details as an inquiry submitted through E-Check, and an inquiry routed through a state point of contact follows the same principle as an inquiry handled directly by the Federal Bureau of Investigation.

Each valid inquiry receives a NICS Transaction Number, abbreviated as NTN. The number identifies the transaction inquiry within the system and provides the reference that connects the inquiry, the response returned to the licensee, and the record of the transfer maintained by the licensee. Under 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(4), when the system notifies the licensee that the information available does not demonstrate that receipt of the firearm would violate subsection (g) or subsection (n), and the licensee transfers the firearm, the licensee includes the unique identification number provided by the system in the record of the transfer. The number thus performs a practical function at the counter and a documentary function afterward. At the counter, the number confirms that a particular inquiry was accepted and answered. In the licensee record, the number preserves evidence that the check required before transfer was conducted for that transaction, tied to the date and the inquiry rather than to a general claim that a buyer had been checked at some earlier time for some other purchase.

### The Automated Search and the Work of the Examiner

Submission of the inquiry sets an automated search in motion. The system searches records available to NICS for information bearing on whether the buyer is prohibited under federal law or under an applicable state prohibition from receiving the firearm. Where the search returns no matching record, the transaction is proceeded automatically, without individualized review by an examiner. The automated character of a clear result explains the speed of the majority of checks. A name and a set of descriptors that produce no match require no record interpretation, no comparison of conflicting entries, and no judgment about the legal effect of a prior event, and the system can return a proceed response through the same channel by which the inquiry arrived.

A possible match changes the character of the check entirely. When records returned by the search may correspond to the buyer and may establish a prohibition, the inquiry moves from automated matching to human review. A NICS examiner, holding the title Legal Instruments Examiner and working within the NICS Section of the Federal Bureau of Investigation at Clarksburg, West Virginia, reviews the records associated with the possible match. The examiner compares the identifying information in the records against the name and descriptors transmitted by the licensee, examines the substance of the underlying record, and determines whether a federal prohibition or an applicable state prohibition exists for the person described in the inquiry. The distinction between a possible match and a prohibition is central to the operation of the system. A similar name, a partial descriptor match, or a record with an unclear outcome does not by itself establish that receipt would be unlawful. The examiner function exists to resolve that gap between a record that surfaces in a search and a legal conclusion that a particular buyer may not receive a firearm.

In a point of contact state, the state agency conducts the check and applies the same basic division between automated clearance and review of a possible match, with the addition of state records and state prohibitions within the scope of the state search. The route of the inquiry therefore affects the records consulted and the prohibitions considered, while the logic of the response remains consistent. A transaction with no matching record proceeds. A transaction with a possible match requires examination of the record before a determination can be made. A transaction in which review establishes a prohibition is denied. A transaction in which review cannot resolve the record within the initial examination is delayed, a response that signals continued research rather than either approval or refusal.

### How Long Does a NICS Background Check Take?

Most checks resolve during the initial automated search. FBI operations reporting placed the immediate determination rate at an average of about ninety percent since two thousand three, falling to eighty five point three percent in two thousand twenty. Remaining checks require review by a NICS examiner before a final response issues.

The figures in the direct answer require careful statement, because the rate measures determinations made immediately within the processing of an inquiry, and FBI operations reporting has described an average immediate determination rate of about 90 percent since 2003. The Attorney General asked the NICS Section to maintain a rate of 90 percent, and the reported average across the period beginning in 2003 corresponded to that level. Performance in a single year can depart from the long period average when transaction volume or record complexity changes the proportion of inquiries that return a possible match. In 2020, a year of exceptionally high check volume in which the system processed 39,695,315 checks initiated through the system in combined federal and point of contact counting, the immediate determination rate reported by the Federal Bureau of Investigation was 85.3 percent. The two figures describe the same measure at different scales. The figure of about 90 percent describes the average reported since 2003, and the figure of 85.3 percent describes the rate for calendar year 2020 alone.

Availability of the channels through which an inquiry can be made also shapes the practical experience of the check, separate from the processing time of an individual search. Telephone service for NICS has customarily been available 17 hours a day, seven days a week, except Christmas. E-Check has been available at all hours. A licensee using the online channel can therefore submit an inquiry outside the hours during which telephone service operates, while a licensee using the telephone channel depends on the staffed hours of that service. In either channel, availability of submission and speed of determination remain distinct questions. An inquiry submitted promptly through an available channel can require examiner review if a possible match appears, while an inquiry that returns no matching record can receive an automatic proceed response without waiting for individualized attention from the NICS Section or from a state point of contact agency.

### The Three Responses Returned to the Licensee

The check concludes, at the level of the response communicated to the licensee, in one of three forms, and each form carries a different legal and practical consequence for the transfer. The first response is proceed. A proceed response follows where no matching record is returned by the automated search, or where examination of a possible match establishes that no federal or applicable state prohibition applies to the buyer. The response signifies that the system has provided the unique identification number contemplated by 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(1), and the licensee may complete the transfer, recording the NICS Transaction Number in the record of the transfer as required by paragraph (4). Proceed is a determination about the information available to the system concerning prohibitions on receipt. The response rests on the records searched and on the identifying information transmitted, and the response attaches to the inquiry that produced the number recorded for the transaction.

The second response is denied. A denial follows where review of the records establishes that a federal prohibition or an applicable state prohibition applies to the buyer, so that receipt of the firearm would violate subsection (g) or subsection (n) of section 922 or applicable state law considered through the check. A denial is not generated by a possible match alone. Denial requires the examiner determination, after review of the records associated with the match, that a prohibition exists for the person identified in the inquiry. The licensee who receives a denial may not transfer the firearm in that transaction. The difference between denial and the other responses lies in finality at the point of response. Proceed communicates that the check has been satisfied. Denial communicates that the check has identified a bar. Both rest on a determination, either the automatic determination that no record matches or the examiner determination that a record establishes a prohibition or that a record does not establish a prohibition after review.

The third response is delayed. A delay communicates that the automated search or initial examiner review has produced a potential match or a record question that cannot be resolved immediately, and that further research is required before a final determination of proceed or denied can be made. Delay is therefore an interim response rather than a finding about eligibility. The response reflects the condition of the records as much as the history of the buyer. A record may lack a disposition, a record may require retrieval from a court or another agency, or a record may require additional comparison against the descriptors in the inquiry before an examiner can decide whether a prohibition exists. During a delay, the licensee has received neither a proceed determination with the clearance contemplated by the statute nor a notification that receipt would violate subsection (g) or subsection (n). The transaction remains open within the system while examiners pursue the missing or unclear information, and the legal treatment of the passage of time during that open period is governed by the separate delayed transaction provisions of 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(1)(B)(ii).

The distinction among the responses also governs what the licensee records and what the licensee may do next without any further contact with the system. A proceed response supplies the number that enters the transfer record. A denial supplies no clearance number for a completed transfer, because no transfer may follow from that inquiry. A delay supplies an open status, preserving the inquiry while research continues, without supplying either clearance or a final bar. That administrative difference mirrors the legal difference among the outcomes. Each response answers the same statutory question, whether the information available through the check demonstrates that receipt would violate federal or applicable state law, and each response answers that question at a different level of resolution and finality for the transaction in question.

Read together, the three responses describe a sorting mechanism built into a single inquiry. Proceed clears the transaction through an automatic no match result or through examiner review that resolves a possible match in favor of no prohibition. Denied closes the transaction on an examiner determination that a prohibition exists. Delayed holds the transaction open when a possible match cannot be resolved on the records immediately available. The sorting occurs anew for each transaction, because a check attaches to a transaction rather than to a person. A purchaser who buys more than one firearm in a single transaction is checked once, and a single inquiry with a single NICS Transaction Number covers the firearms included in that transaction, regardless of the number of firearms involved. Conversely, the clearance produced by one transaction does not function as a standing clearance carried by the person into a later purchase. A later transaction requires a later inquiry, a later search against the records available at that time, and a later response of proceed, denied, or delayed returned to the licensee conducting that transfer. The transaction based design keeps the inquiry, the number, the response, and the licensee record aligned as a single unit, from the completion of Form 4473 at the premises through the determination that ends the check.

## The Databases Searched

A background check under the National Instant Criminal Background Check System is a search for a record, and the character of the search depends on the character of the records placed within reach of the system. When a licensee contacts the system, the identifying information supplied for the buyer is compared against records held in three named databases, each built for a different purpose, each holding a different kind of information, and each answering a different part of the statutory question. That question is defined by subsection (g) and subsection (n) of section 922, which together describe the circumstances under which receipt or possession of a firearm would be unlawful. The databases do not create that definition. The databases supply the material from which the system determines whether a record demonstrates a prohibition, whether a record suggests a possible prohibition that requires further review, or whether no matching record is returned at all.

The three databases are searched together, but the combined search should not be mistaken for a single collection of criminal history. One database holds documented criminal justice information assembled for law enforcement use. A second database functions as an index into criminal history records maintained by the states. A third database was created for the background check system itself and holds records of persons prohibited on grounds that the other two systems do not hold in usable form. The differences explain why all three are necessary and why the absence of a record in one database says little about the contents of the others. A felony conviction, a protection order, an arrest warrant, a mental health adjudication, and a dishonorable discharge arise in different institutions and enter federal searching through different channels. A court enters a protection order, a state repository holds a criminal history, a federal agency contributes a discharge record, and a tribal agency may contribute a record within its knowledge.

### The National Crime Information Center

The National Crime Information Center, known by the initials NCIC, supplies the first part of the search, and its contribution reflects a purpose broader than firearm transfers. The NCIC is a source of documented criminal justice information, including wanted persons and protection orders. Wanted persons in this setting means persons who are the subject of arrest warrants, and protection orders means court orders entered for the protection of another person and recorded within the criminal justice information available to the system. A search of the NCIC therefore asks, in part, whether the identifying information supplied for a buyer corresponds to a person sought under a warrant or a person named in a protection order held in that system. Either kind of record can bear directly on the statutory inquiry, because the circumstances described in subsection (g) include matters that arise from court action and from outstanding criminal process as well as from completed convictions. The NCIC gives the background check access to that kind of information without requiring the examiner conducting the check to query each issuing court or each law enforcement agency separately.

The value of the NCIC to the check lies in reach as well as in content. Criminal justice information of the kind held in the NCIC originates in agencies and courts across the country, and a buyer may be the subject of a warrant or a protection order entered far from the place of purchase. A search confined to conviction records would miss such information, because a warrant can exist before any conviction has occurred and a protection order can exist without a criminal conviction at all. The NCIC search addresses that gap by bringing documented criminal justice information into the same inquiry that examines conviction history. The search has a defined boundary, however. The background check draws on the information documented within the NCIC rather than on the underlying files of every agency that might possess relevant records elsewhere. Where a warrant or a protection order has not been placed in the NCIC, that portion of the search cannot return it, although the same kind of record may appear in the NICS Index when an agency has contributed the record there. The NCIC provides the lead that an order or a warrant exists, and the examiner reviewing a possible match works from the underlying record, whose terms and status determine whether a prohibition exists, rather than from the identifying information alone.

### The Interstate Identification Index

The Interstate Identification Index, known by the initials III, supplies the conviction history at the center of many checks, and its name describes its method. The III is the index of state criminal history records. Rather than functioning as a single warehouse that displaces state repositories, the III points into records maintained through the states, and those state records are the source for felony and misdemeanor conviction records used in the check. A name based inquiry begins with identifying descriptors, and names, dates of birth, and other descriptors can be shared by more than one person or recorded with variations. The index structure directs the search toward the state criminal history that corresponds to the person, and review of that history supplies the detail needed to determine whether a conviction exists, what the conviction was for, and whether the conviction carries a prohibition under federal law or under applicable state law.

Felony conviction records form the most familiar part of the III contribution. A felony conviction recorded in a state criminal history gives the background check a concrete event to evaluate against the statutory language, because a conviction is a judgment entered by a court after proceedings in a criminal case, and the record of that judgment can identify the offense and the disposition. Misdemeanor conviction records are searched through the same channel, and one class is singled out for express mention, namely misdemeanor crimes of domestic violence. A misdemeanor label alone does not answer the statutory question, because the nature of the offense can matter to whether the conviction falls within the prohibition described by the statute. The state criminal history allows an examiner to look beyond the label attached to an offense in a summary and toward the conviction itself. Without access through the III to state held histories, a federal check would depend on states volunteering individual conviction records into a separate federal collection for every case, and the index avoids that duplication by drawing on the repositories where criminal histories are maintained in the ordinary course. A conviction that was never posted to the relevant state record, or a state record that was never reflected through the index in a form the search can reach, cannot supply a match.

### The NICS Index

The NICS Index supplies the third part of the search, and the index differs from the other two databases in origin and in reason for existence. The NICS Index holds records contributed by federal, state, local, and tribal agencies on persons federally prohibited from receiving or possessing firearms where the information is not in the NCIC or the III. The contributors matter because the events that create many prohibitions are known first, and sometimes only, to the agency or government that handled the underlying matter. A federal agency may hold a record arising under federal authority, a state agency may hold a record arising under state process that has federal consequences for firearm possession, a local agency may hold information generated through local contact, and a tribal agency may hold a record arising within tribal jurisdiction. The NICS Index gives each level of government a place to contribute such records directly to the background check system, rather than leaving the record to be discovered, if at all, through a criminal history index built for a different class of information or through a general criminal justice database that may never have received the record.

The contents of the NICS Index are defined by that gap filling purpose. The index includes protection orders and active warrants not located in the NCIC. The index includes felony convictions posted to a state record and not reflected in the III. The index includes persons under a court order not to possess a firearm. The index includes records showing illegal or unlawful alien status. The index includes persons adjudicated as a mental defective or committed to a mental institution, using the statutory terminology carried in the records described for the system. The index includes persons dishonorably discharged from the armed forces. The index includes persons who renounced United States citizenship. The index includes unlawful users of controlled substances. Each item represents a record that an agency has contributed because the underlying fact can establish a federal prohibition and because the record was not held in the other two systems in a form available to the check. The list should be read as a description of a records collection, not as a substitute statement of the prohibitions themselves. A record enters the index because an agency has information about a prohibited person, and the examiner reviewing a possible match must determine from the record whether the prohibition in fact applies.

Several features of the contents explain the architecture of the search. Protection orders and warrants absent from the NCIC show that overlap is intentional and that duplication at the margins serves completeness, because an agency that knows a record has not reached the NCIC can contribute the record to the NICS Index so that a check can find it. State record felony convictions not reflected in the III serve a parallel function, giving the check a second path to a conviction that exists in a state record even though the index does not reflect it. The remaining contents reach beyond criminal justice records altogether. A dishonorable discharge is a military personnel outcome, a renunciation of citizenship is recorded through federal processes concerned with nationality, and a commitment to a mental institution follows a process distinct from a criminal prosecution. None of those events would be expected to appear as a conviction in the III, and none depends on a warrant or a protection order of the kind held in the NCIC.

### Why does the NICS background check search a separate NICS Index?

The NICS Index exists because federal prohibitions extend beyond convictions to court orders, adjudications, discharges, immigration status, citizenship renunciation, and unlawful drug use, and conviction histories in the other systems do not contain reliable records of those events for examiners to find during a check.

The fuller explanation begins with the structure of subsection (g) and subsection (n) of section 922. Those provisions describe prohibited status by reference to a series of circumstances, and only part of that series rests on a criminal conviction. Other circumstances rest on a court order, on an adjudication, on a commitment, on a discharge from the armed forces under dishonorable conditions, on a formal renunciation of citizenship, on immigration status, or on unlawful use of a controlled substance. A search designed to answer the statutory question must therefore look for more than convictions. Conviction records alone cannot answer the statutory question for a straightforward reason. A conviction record proves the judgment it records and is silent about everything else. The absence of a felony conviction in the III says nothing about whether a court has ordered the person not to possess a firearm, nothing about whether the person has been committed to a mental institution following an adjudication, nothing about whether the person left the armed forces under a dishonorable discharge, and nothing about whether the person has renounced citizenship. Silence in a conviction index is evidence only about convictions.

The NCIC answers part of the wider question, because documented criminal justice information includes warrants and protection orders, but the NCIC was not created as a comprehensive repository for every federal firearm prohibition. Protection orders and warrants can be missing from the NCIC, and state felony convictions can be missing from reflection in the III, as the contents of the NICS Index expressly recognize. Beyond those gaps, an entire set of disqualifying events has no natural home in either system. Mental health adjudications and commitments are recorded through courts and institutions concerned with treatment and competency, not through criminal history repositories. Dishonorable discharges are recorded through military personnel systems. Renunciations of citizenship are recorded through the processes that document nationality. Unlawful alien status is recorded through immigration systems. Unlawful use of controlled substances may be known to an agency through records that never resulted in a conviction available to the III. A purpose built index gathered those scattered records into the check without pretending that the records were criminal histories.

### The Additional Immigration Query for a Noncitizen Buyer

A further query extends the search in one defined circumstance. For a noncitizen buyer, the databases of United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement are also queried to determine lawful presence. The additional query reflects the place of immigration status within the statutory scheme and the location of the records that can establish that status. The three principal databases, even taken together, were not described as the complete source for determining whether a person who is not a citizen is lawfully present, and immigration enforcement records supply the additional source for that determination. The query is tied to the buyer rather than applied as a general addition to every transaction regardless of citizenship. The NICS Index itself can contain records showing illegal or unlawful alien status contributed by agencies, so immigration status can reach the check through the index as well as through the separate query. A contributed record reflects information an agency placed in the index for firearm check purposes, while the query seeks a determination of lawful presence from the agency charged with immigration enforcement and holding the relevant systems.

### Record Completeness and the State Contribution

Completeness is the operating condition that qualifies every statement about what the databases contain. The system answers from the records governments have supplied, and that sentence states a fact about the design rather than a judgment about the performance of any agency. A court must create the record of a judgment or an order, an agency must retain and transmit or contribute the record through the appropriate channel, and the record must carry identifying information that permits the search to associate the record with the buyer whose information was submitted. A break at any point in that chain means that the searched systems hold no corresponding record for the check to find. The consequence follows without regard to the seriousness of the underlying prohibition. A prohibition with no record in any searched system produces no match, because the system has no separate means of discovering an unrecorded event during the brief interval in which a point of sale check is conducted. The names of the databases identify where the search looks. Record supply determines what the search can find there.

Most underlying records originate outside the FBI, and that origin shapes both completeness and correction. State agencies and courts are the sources of most underlying records, including the criminal histories reached through the III, many protection orders and warrants, and many of the state contributed records held in the NICS Index. Federal, local, and tribal agencies contribute records within their own knowledge, but the volume of courts entering judgments and orders across the country is a state and local phenomenon. The background check therefore depends on the record keeping of institutions that have no role in selling firearms and no presence at the transaction. When a record is missing, unclear, or incomplete, the gap ordinarily traces back to the creation, posting, or transmission of the source record rather than to the act of searching, and examiners seek missing or clarifying material from the courts and agencies that hold the source records.

The point of contact arrangement adds a further layer to that evidentiary base. In a point of contact state, the licensee contacts the state agency rather than dealing directly with the FBI for the check, and the state agency searches its own state record systems in addition to the three federal databases. The addition is substantive, not merely administrative. State record systems can hold records that are available to the state search in a form or to an extent beyond what the federal search reaches, and the state agency applies state prohibitions alongside the federal inquiry. For that reason, processing experience and denial outcomes can differ from state to state, and the same buyer information can be examined against a wider set of records in a point of contact state than in a jurisdiction where the FBI conducts the check through the three federal databases and the additional immigration query when the buyer is not a citizen.

Taken together, the databases searched describe a division of labor organized around the kinds of facts that can make receipt of a firearm unlawful. The National Crime Information Center contributes documented criminal justice information, including wanted persons and protection orders. The Interstate Identification Index contributes access to state criminal history records, including felony and misdemeanor conviction records and, within the misdemeanor records, convictions for misdemeanor crimes of domestic violence. The NICS Index contributes the records that federal, state, local, and tribal agencies have placed in a purpose built collection for prohibitions the other two systems do not hold, ranging from protection orders and warrants absent from the NCIC and felony convictions absent from reflection in the III to court orders against possession, unlawful alien status, mental health adjudications and commitments, dishonorable discharges, renunciations of citizenship, and records of unlawful users of controlled substances. The immigration query for a noncitizen buyer adds the databases of United States Immigration and Customs Enforcement for the determination of lawful presence. Every part of that apparatus depends on records supplied by the governments that created them.

## The Delayed Lane and the Default Proceed

Most transactions through the National Instant Criminal Background Check System ended in an immediate determination, and the delayed transaction represented the exception rather than the ordinary course. The delay arose when the search returned information pointing toward a possible prohibition but not establishing the prohibition with the clarity required for a denial. A denial required a determination that receipt would violate subsection (g) or subsection (n) of section 922 of title 18 of the United States Code or applicable state law.

The delayed lane reflected a problem of records rather than a problem of identity alone. The licensee had transmitted the identifying information supplied on the Firearms Transaction Record, the system had assigned a NICS Transaction Number, and the automated search had located a record that required human review. A Legal Instruments Examiner took responsibility for the file. The examiner considered whether the record belonged to the transferee and whether the underlying event carried a federal or applicable state prohibition. In many delayed transactions the match was genuine, but the disposition was missing or unclear. A disposition was the final outcome of an arrest or charge, such as a conviction, a dismissal, or an acquittal. Without that outcome an examiner could not know whether an arrest on a felony charge had ended in a felony conviction, in a conviction for a lesser offense, or in a dismissal that left no prohibition at all. The examiner sought the missing records from courts and agencies, and the transaction remained delayed while the search continued.

### Why a Transaction Enters the Delayed Lane

A transaction entered the delayed lane because the databases consulted during the check were assembled from many contributing sources, and the completeness of any single record depended on the court or agency that created the record and transmitted the record onward. A search across the National Crime Information Center, the Interstate Identification Index, and the NICS Index could retrieve an entry created at the start of a case rather than at the end of a case. An arrest generated a booking record promptly in many jurisdictions, while the later judgment depended on a court clerk entering the outcome and on that outcome reaching the state repository and then the federal index. Where that chain broke or lagged, the search returned the beginning of a story without the ending.

Ambiguity produced delay as readily as absence. A record might state a disposition in abbreviated or coded form that did not translate cleanly into the categories of federal law. A state offense name might not reveal whether the offense, as defined under the law of the convicting jurisdiction, matched a federal prohibition. A date might be missing, a charge might be listed without a level, or two entries for the same event might conflict. An examiner confronting such a record faced a legal determination rather than a clerical one. An unclear record did not demonstrate a prohibition, and an unclear record did not demonstrate the absence of a prohibition, so a proceed response on the strength of an unresolved record would have treated uncertainty as eligibility. The delay preserved that distinction. The transaction was neither approved nor denied on incomplete information. Response times varied, since a court with digitized records could answer quickly while a court whose older cases rested in paper files required a manual search.

### How the Three Business Days Are Counted

The governing text appeared at section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii) of title 18 of the United States Code. Under that provision a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer was barred from transferring a firearm to a nonlicensee unless, before completion of the transfer, the licensee had contacted the system and one of two paths had been satisfied. The first path was the provision of a unique identification number by the system. The second path, the path that governed delayed transactions, required that three business days had elapsed since the licensee contacted the system and that the system had not notified the licensee that the transfer would violate subsection (g) or subsection (n). The statute supplied its own definition within the same clause. A business day meant a day on which State offices were open in the State in which the transfer took place. The count therefore followed the working calendar of state government in the state of transfer, rather than the federal calendar, the licensee business calendar, or a simple count of calendar days.

The practical effect of that definition was direct. A Saturday or a Sunday on which State offices were closed did not count toward the three days. A state holiday on which State offices were closed did not count, even if the same date was an ordinary working day in another state and even if the licensee premises were open for trade. A weekday on which State offices were open counted, regardless of whether a federal office observed a holiday on the same date, because the definition looked to State offices in the State of transfer. Two transactions initiated on the same calendar date in different states could therefore end the period on different calendar dates. A contact made late in a week produced a count that paused while State offices were closed and resumed when State offices reopened, so elapsed calendar time frequently exceeded three calendar days even though the statutory measure was three business days.

The starting point was equally precise. The three business days ran from the licensee contact with the system, not from completion of the Firearms Transaction Record alone, not from a later internal review by an examiner, and not from the moment a court or agency received a request for a missing disposition. Contact meant initiation of the check through the prescribed channel, whether through the FBI directly in a non point of contact jurisdiction or through the designated state agency in a point of contact state. An examiner who needed additional time received no extension from the text, and the legal consequence of the passage of the three business days operated independently of whether the research had finished.

### What Happens When Three Business Days Pass Without a Denial?

After three business days have elapsed since the licensee contacted the system without notification that receipt would violate federal law, the statute permits the licensee to transfer the firearm. The permission does not require transfer, and a licensee may wait for a determination or decline the transaction entirely.

The trigger for that permission was the passage of time combined with silence from the system on the question of prohibition. The system had not provided the unique identification number that accompanied a proceed determination, and the system had not notified the licensee that receipt would violate the law. The transaction occupied an unresolved status at the moment the period expired. The statute allowed the transfer to go forward without deeming the transferee eligible and without converting the delayed response into a proceed determination. The distinction between permission to transfer and a determination of eligibility was central. Eligibility remained undetermined at the moment of a default proceed transfer, the examiner file remained open, and a later determination could establish that the transferee had been prohibited all along, with consequences addressed through the delayed denial process.

The authority was permissive rather than mandatory. Once the three business days had elapsed under section 922(t)(1)(B)(ii), the licensee was no longer barred by that paragraph from completing the transfer, but no provision compelled completion. A licensee who preferred to wait for a final determination was free to wait. A licensee who followed a policy of transferring only on an affirmative proceed response was free to follow that policy. The federal text set a floor for the waiting obligation rather than a duty to sell. A description of the licensee as required to hand over the firearm after three business days therefore misstated the provision. The law removed a federal barrier after the period; the law did not create a federal obligation to transfer.

The federal period also had limits. In some states a statute or regulation imposed a waiting period longer than the federal three business days for some or all firearm transfers, and a licensee in such a state was bound by the longer state period even after the federal period had expired. A transferee whose federal period had run in a state with a longer state wait had satisfied only one of two applicable legal clocks. The federal default proceed described the point at which federal law ceased to bar the transfer on timing grounds, and no more than that point.

### Research After the Third Business Day and the Ninety Day Limit

Expiration of the three business day period did not end the work of the NICS Section. The FBI continued researching a delayed transaction after the third business day, pursuing the same court and agency records sought during the initial period. A determination reached on the fourth business day or in a later week could prevent a transfer that a licensee had chosen to hold voluntarily, sparing a later retrieval. A determination reached after a transfer had occurred could trigger the referral process for retrieval and could establish the correct outcome for the record even when the firearm had changed hands. The research therefore served a preventive function for transactions that remained pending by licensee choice and a corrective function for transactions completed under the default proceed.

The continuation had a defined outer limit for retention of identifying information. Under section 25.9(b) of title 28 of the Code of Federal Regulations, if no final determination was reached on a delayed transaction, the identifying information associated with the transaction was purged when the transaction reached ninety days. The unresolved delayed transaction was retained across a ninety day span and then purged if the research produced no determination. After the purge the date and the NICS Transaction Number were retained. An examiner therefore worked against two clocks: the three business day clock governed the licensee right to transfer, and the ninety day clock governed retention of the identifying information needed to act on a determination.

### The Delayed Denial and Referral for Retrieval

A delayed denial described the outcome in which research, after a transfer had been completed under the default proceed, established that the transferee had been prohibited from receiving the firearm. The transaction had been delayed for a missing or unclear disposition, three business days had elapsed without a denial notification, the licensee had exercised the statutory permission and had transferred the firearm, and the examiner had then obtained a record establishing a prohibition under subsection (g) or subsection (n) or under applicable state law incorporated in the check. The transfer, lawful for the licensee to complete at the moment of completion under the elapsed period rule, had placed a firearm with a person whom the law barred from receiving the firearm.

The institutional response was divided between two federal bureaus according to function. The FBI, which operated the check system and made the determination, notified the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives of the delayed denial. The notification was a delayed denial referral, and responsibility for retrieval of the firearm rested with the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives rather than with the FBI. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives administered the licensing system and maintained the field structure through which a retrieval was carried out. A NICS examiner did not retrieve firearms. Retrieval meant the process by which agents of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives sought to recover a firearm from a person determined to have been prohibited at the time of receipt, after the bureau assigned the referral within a field division for investigation and action under bureau procedures and priorities.

Retention rules made that corrective function possible. A delayed transaction left the question of eligibility open, and the system retained identifying information for the ninety day period in order to match a late arriving disposition to the correct transaction and to support a referral if the late determination was a denial. Without that retention a determination that could not be connected to the transfer and the transferee could not be referred for action. The Government Accountability Office report GAO-18-440, issued in June 2018 and addressing fiscal year 2017 federal transactions processed by the FBI, placed delayed denial retrievals within the larger enforcement picture that followed denials generally, in which the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives referred a portion of denial matters to field divisions and United States Attorneys prosecuted a small number of referred cases. The report documented declination guidelines applied by federal prosecutors and the difficulty of proving that a buyer knew of the prohibition.

### Two Readings of the Default Proceed

The default proceed drew sustained disagreement, and the disagreement turned less on mechanics than on the judgment the mechanics embodied. Both readings accepted the same statutory facts. The check was required before transfer, a delayed response signaled an unresolved possible match, three business days measured the interval during which the federal bar operated in the absence of a determination, and after that interval the licensee could transfer while research continued and a later denial produced a referral for retrieval. The readings diverged on whether that arrangement struck a defensible balance or created a foreseeable route by which a prohibited person obtained a firearm through the licensed market.

Officials defending the design described the provision as a deliberate statutory balance between a screening system and the right of a person against whom no prohibition had been established to complete a lawful purchase without indefinite administrative delay. On that reading the period gave the government a defined opportunity to establish a prohibition from public records before a transfer, while placing a limit on the burden that incomplete government records could impose on a transferee who might have no disqualifying history at all. A check that could not conclude, defenders argued, could not hold a lawful transfer indefinitely, because an open ended hold would convert a records failure by a court or an agency into a denial in effect, imposed without the determination required for an actual denial and without the challenge process that accompanied an actual denial. The permission after three business days was, on that account, the protection of the buyer against administrative inaction. Defenders also pointed to the permissive character of the transfer and to continued research and retrieval referral as the corrective mechanism.

Officials and organizations urging a longer period described the same arrangement as a structural gap in the screening system. On that reading the delay itself was the relevant signal, because a delay indicated that a record of potential disqualification existed and had not been resolved, so expiration of three business days bore no relationship to resolution of the underlying question. A prohibited person whose records were slow, whose case file rested in a court with a backlog, or whose disposition had not reached the databases received the firearm through a licensed dealer in the ordinary course of business, proponents of a longer period argued, and the system learned of the prohibition only after the transfer that the check existed to prevent. Retrieval was, on that account, a remedy after the fact rather than a screening measure, dependent on field resources, on locating the recipient, and on recovering the firearm, and the remedy could not restore the position that a completed check would have secured. Proponents therefore proposed lengthening the period before a default proceed transfer was permitted, or requiring an affirmative determination before transfer in delayed cases.

The balance reading placed the risk of an unfinished government search on the government after a defined interval, on the ground that the alternative placed the cost of record failures on persons who had not been shown to be prohibited, including persons whose delays reflected a common name, an old arrest with a dismissal that had never been transmitted, or a record belonging to another person. The gap reading placed the risk on completion of the transfer, on the ground that a delayed transaction was a subset in which a possible prohibition had already surfaced, so the probability that the transferee was prohibited was higher than in the group cleared immediately, and the consequence of an error was a firearm in the hands of a prohibited person rather than a postponed purchase. The dispute concerned the weight assigned to each class of case and the remedy each side preferred.

The pathway from transaction record to response runs through a small number of stages, each with a defined actor, a defined search, a defined clock, and a defined fate for the record created at that stage.

## The Record Rule and What Happens to the Information

A background check produces information before it produces an answer. A purchaser supplies a name and descriptors on a form at a licensed dealer's premises, a licensee transmits identifying information to a federal system or to a state point of contact, and a response returns as proceed, denied, or delayed. Every stage creates a record in the ordinary administrative sense, even if the record exists only for hours inside a computer system. The question that drew sustained legislative attention was never whether information would be created, because creation was unavoidable if the check was to function. The question was what would happen to the information after the answer had been given, how long any part of it would survive, who would hold whatever survived, and whether survival would amount in practice to something the governing statutes forbade. For an approved transaction, the answer is destruction, rapid and nearly complete, leaving a number and a date and nothing that identifies a person or a transfer.

The design divides custody. The federal system keeps almost nothing from an approved transaction, while the licensee keeps a detailed record for a long period at the place of business. Neither half makes sense without the other. A reader who looked only at the federal destruction rule might conclude that no record of an approved purchase existed anywhere, and a reader who looked only at the licensee's file might conclude that a complete record sat in government hands. Neither conclusion follows. The detailed record is retained for not less than twenty years with the dealer, unless the dealer discontinues business and delivers records to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives under a separate provision, and two statutory provisions prohibit the centralization that a central file would require.

### The Duty Written Into the Check Itself

The starting point is section 922(t)(2) of Title 18 of the United States Code, which provides that if receipt of a firearm would not violate subsection (g) or (n) of section 922 or State law, the system shall assign a unique identification number, provide that number to the licensee, and destroy all records of the system with respect to the call, other than the identifying number and the date the number was assigned, and all records of the system relating to the person or the transfer. The system assigns a number because the licensee must enter it in the record of the transfer under section 922(t)(4), and then destroys the records because the inquiry has reached the result the statute contemplated. The exception preserves an identifier for the transaction and a date, stripped of the identifying content, such as a name or descriptors, that gave the inquiry its purpose.

That structure places destruction inside the original statutory bargain rather than treating destruction as an administrative preference adopted later by the agency operating the system. The Federal Bureau of Investigation operates the system through its Criminal Justice Information Services Division at Clarksburg, West Virginia, and the regulation at Part 25 of Title 28 implements the rule, but neither the Bureau nor the regulation invented the underlying duty. Congress wrote a destroy command into the same paragraph that authorized an approval to proceed by number. The command also explains why an approval differs in kind from the other responses. A denial rests on a determination that a prohibition applies, and such a determination can be challenged through processes that extend beyond the moment at the counter. A delay rests on an unfinished inquiry, and an unfinished inquiry cannot be completed if every trace disappears within a day. An approval rests on a completed inquiry with a lawful result, and retention beyond delivery of the number would serve purposes the statute did not assign to an approved check, such as building a historical list of persons who passed a check.

Precision about the object of destruction prevents a common misunderstanding. The duty in section 922(t)(2) reaches records of the system. The duty does not reach the licensee's own record, which exists under a different provision and serves a different function. The duty does not reach records in the underlying databases that were searched, such as a criminal history record held through the Interstate Identification Index or a protection order record held through the National Crime Information Center, because those records belong to the source systems and to the agencies that contributed them, and a single check neither created them nor consumed them. The duty reaches the system's records of the call and the identifying information assembled for that inquiry. Destruction therefore leaves the architecture of criminal justice information intact while removing the transaction layer that connected a named purchaser to a particular attempted purchase on a particular date. After destruction, the source databases contain whatever they contained before the inquiry, the licensee contains a completed form and a number, and the federal system contains a number and a date without a name.

### Where the Twenty Four Hour Deadline Comes From

For all of the clarity in section 922(t)(2), the authorizing statute does not itself state the twenty four hour deadline that became the most contested feature of the record rule. The deadline comes from appropriations law. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2004, signed on January 23, 2004, requires destruction of identifying information on an approved transaction, described in operations material as a proceeded transaction, not more than twenty four hours after the licensee has been notified of the approval. The requirement has been carried in appropriations acts since the fiscal 2004 act, and the regulation at section 25.9(b) of Title 28 implements the requirement. The distinction between an authorizing statute and an appropriations provision is more than a civics refinement. An authorizing statute creates a program, defines its duties, and sets its permanent legal framework. An appropriations act supplies money for a period and frequently attaches conditions on the use of that money, conditions that operate with full legal force for the funds and period covered and that Congress may carry forward from act to act. A condition repeated across successive appropriations acts can govern an operating system for years without being written into the authorizing title as permanent law. The twenty four hour rule has exactly that character. An appropriations provision, not the authorizing statute, sets the system's most contested feature, and any account that attributed the deadline to the Brady Act itself, or to a regulation an agency adopted on its own authority, would misstate the legal source.

The appropriations history deepens the point. Riders in the late 1990s and early 2000s first required retention of system records for audit purposes for periods of months, on the basis that a system making millions of determinations could not be evaluated unless records survived long enough for auditors to sample transactions and compare inputs against outcomes. The fiscal 2004 act reversed course and compressed the timetable to twenty four hours after notification to the licensee, a period too short for retrospective audit sampling in identifying form. Congress moved from a model in which accountability through later inspection justified temporary retention, to a model in which prompt destruction took priority and accountability had to be achieved through other means, such as aggregate reporting and system controls.

### What Remains After an Approval, a Delay, and a Denial

Categories decide outcomes under the record rule, and the regulation treats each response category differently because each presents a different administrative situation. An approved transaction presents a closed file. A delayed transaction presents an open file, in which examiners are searching for a disposition or another record that will resolve a possible match. A denied transaction presents a closed file of a different kind, one that records an adverse determination with consequences for the purchaser, for any challenge the purchaser brings, and for enforcement agencies that receive referrals. Approved transactions receive the shortest retention of identifying information, delayed transactions receive an intermediate period tied to the ninety day life of an unresolved inquiry, and denied transactions receive retention measured in years. Any summary that assigned a single retention period to the system as a whole would erase the distinctions on which the regulation depends.

For an approved transaction, identifying information is destroyed within twenty four hours after the licensee has been notified of the approval, under the appropriations requirement implemented at section 25.9(b). The audit log, the system's internal record of its own activity, does not escape the purge. Audit log residue for an approved transaction is purged so that only the transaction number and the date remain, and any remaining information connected with the approval is purged within ninety days, so that the audit log holds no identifying information for approved transactions. The two stages deserve separate attention. The twenty four hour destruction addresses the identifying information of the proceeded transaction, the material that connects a person to the inquiry. The ninety day outer limit addresses residue, the fragments an operating system generates around a transaction and that might linger in a log after the principal record has been destroyed. A rule that destroyed the principal record while leaving identifying residue untouched in a log would accomplish less than its text promised, because reconstruction from residue could defeat destruction in substance. The regulation closes that path by requiring the log itself to be reduced to the number and the date. What survives an approved check on the government side, after both stages have run, is remarkably spare. A number exists, a date exists, and a name, descriptors, and the connection between a person and a firearm transfer do not exist in the system file.

For a delayed or open transaction, the timeline stretches because the work stretches. A delay means the databases returned a potential match whose disposition was missing or unclear, and examiners needed records from courts and agencies to determine whether a prohibition applied. The statute permits a licensee to transfer after three business days have elapsed without a denial notification, although the statute does not compel a transfer, and the federal inquiry can continue beyond that point. Retention follows the inquiry. Identifying information for a delayed or open transaction is destroyed after ninety days, and the date and the NICS Transaction Number are retained. Destroying identifying information within twenty four hours for a transaction whose central question remained unanswered would end the inquiry by destroying its subject. Retaining identifying information indefinitely for a transaction that never produced a denial would convert an unfinished inquiry into a permanent file on a person against whom no prohibition had been established. Ninety days steered between those outcomes, allowing examiners a defined period to obtain records and reach a determination while placing a fixed end on the file if no determination arrived.

For a denied transaction, the regulation adopts a markedly longer period. Records of a denied transaction are retained in the audit log for ten years, after which the records are transferred to an electronic database maintained by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. An approval file disappears within a day in identifying form and within ninety days in residual form, while a denial file persists for a decade and then moves to another federal database. A denial is an adverse determination that a person is prohibited from receiving a firearm, and the person may challenge it under section 25.10 of Title 28, directing the challenge to the Federal Bureau of Investigation or, in a point of contact state, to the state agency, with an entitlement to the reason for the denial on request. A review requires a record to review, a referral for investigation requires a record to investigate, and retention also allows the system to recognize a repeat attempted purchase by a person previously determined to be prohibited. Whether ten years strikes the correct balance has been contested, but the structural reason is plain: approval ends a lawful transaction, while denial records a determination of ineligibility, and determinations of ineligibility generate proceedings that unfold over time.

### Does the Government Keep a Record of Approved Gun Purchases?

For an approved transaction, the federal government keeps no record identifying the purchaser or the transfer. The system destroys identifying information within twenty four hours, and the audit log retains only the NICS Transaction Number and the date the number was assigned. The detailed record remains with the licensee.

The direct answer rests on the two registry bars as well as on the destruction rule, and the bars must be read alongside the purge periods rather than as slogans detached from a text. Section 926(a) of Title 18 provides that no rule or regulation may require that licensee records be centralized or transferred to a facility owned, managed, or controlled by the United States or a State, or establish any system of registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions or dispositions. Section 103(i) of the Brady Act provides a bar specific to the check system, stating in substance that the system may not be used to establish any system of registration, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. Destruction then removes the data. A government that destroyed identifying information within twenty four hours, retained only a number and a date, and operated under a statutory bar against using the system to establish registration would lack both the legal authority and the factual material for a list of approved purchasers, and claims that such a list exists are answered by the statutory structure, as a [separate examination of federal gun law myths](/2021/08/15/federal-gun-law-myths/) also considers in testing registry claims against the governing texts. The only government side residue of an approved check is a number and a date, and a number and a date, without more, identify no person and describe no firearm.

### The Licensee Record and the Work of Tracing

The other half of the design sits in a dealer's files rather than in a federal database. The buyer completes Form 4473, the Firearms Transaction Record, at the licensee's premises, the licensee verifies identity against a valid photo identification document, and the completed form remains with the licensee after the transfer. Section 478.129(b) of Title 27 requires a licensee to retain each Form 4473 for not less than twenty years after the date of sale or other disposition, and the corresponding acquisition and disposition record is kept under the same regulation family. Twenty years is a long retention period, and the length reflects the function the form performs in a decentralized system. If a firearm is recovered years after a sale and law enforcement seeks to identify the first retail purchaser, the form created at the sale may be the only document that completes the final link in the chain. A short period would break that link for older firearms, while a long period preserves the link while leaving custody with the dealer, rather than transferring custody to a central government repository at the time of sale.

Custody changes when a business ends. Section 923(g)(4) of Title 18 and the regulation at section 478.129 provide that if a licensee discontinues business, the records are delivered to the Out of Business Records Center of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, or to a Bureau office, unless a successor licensee takes the records within the terms of the regulation. The exception follows from necessity rather than from a change of philosophy about centralization. A record that must survive for twenty years cannot vanish because a shop closed in year three, and a former dealer who has left the business cannot serve as a permanent custodian. Delivery to the federal center at the end of a business preserves continuity for records that already existed in decentralized form. The transfer occurs at discontinuance, not at the time of each sale, and the distinction preserves the structural difference between dealers holding records during their business lives and a government facility accumulating records transaction by transaction as sales occur.

Tracing demonstrates the consequences. The Bureau traces a recovered firearm through the chain of distribution, from the manufacturer or importer through any distributor to the dealer and the first retail purchaser shown on the dealer's Form 4473, moving from holder to holder requesting the record that identifies the next holder. Tracing is therefore a manual, record by record process, dependent on the completeness of records created by separate businesses over periods that can span decades, and it ends at a defined point, because later transfers outside the licensed chain sit outside that form unless another law created another record. Defenders of the structure treated the resulting friction as the price of preventing a registration system, while administrators and law enforcement treated the same friction as a practical burden. Both descriptions refer to the same mechanism.

### The Tension Between Destruction and Completeness

The tension arising from the record rule arises because two legitimate administrative values depend on opposite treatments of the same information. Destruction protects the privacy of a person who passed a check and prevents a federal file on lawful purchasers, and destruction works only if identifying information genuinely disappears on schedule. Completeness supports audit, error correction, enforcement, and tracing, and completeness works only if records survive long enough, and in sufficient detail, for a reviewer or an investigator to reconstruct what happened. Every additional day of retention strengthens later review and weakens the guarantee that an approved purchaser leaves no identifying trace, and every acceleration of destruction does the reverse. The movement from months of retention to twenty four hours was the policy dispute expressed in recordkeeping form.

The completeness concern takes concrete shapes. An auditor examining whether determinations were made correctly needs transactions to examine, and an approved transaction purged within twenty four hours cannot be sampled in identifying form months later. A person repeatedly delayed by a similar name or an incomplete source record cannot point to a retained federal file from an earlier successful check, because no such file survives. On the licensee side the concern runs in the opposite direction. Federal law imposes a twenty year duty on dealers precisely because the dealer record carries the long term evidentiary burden that the federal system does not carry for approved transactions, so if dealer records are incomplete or discarded early, the decentralized design loses the record on which tracing depends, and no central backup exists. Trust is therefore placed in a federal system to destroy on schedule and without residue beyond a number and a date, and in licensees to create accurate forms, store them safely for two decades, produce them for lawful trace requests, and deliver them intact when a business closes.

Taken together, the provisions describe a settlement rather than a simple rule. Section 922(t)(2) orders destruction and preserves a number and a date. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199 imposes a twenty four hour clock for approved transactions and reverses an earlier audit retention model. Section 25.9(b) sorts transactions into approved, delayed, and denied categories and assigns each category a fate measured in hours, in ninety days, or in ten years followed by transfer. Sections 926(a) and 103(i) of the Brady Act bar the use of the system and the centralization of licensee records for registration. Section 478.129(b) and section 923(g)(4) place the durable record with the licensee for not less than twenty years and move that record to a federal center only when a business discontinues. A number and a date in a federal log, a detailed form in a dealer's file, a denial record retained for a decade, a delayed file destroyed after ninety days, and a trace that moves from holder to holder form a single structure, built from choices about privacy, accountability, and the limits placed on a federal system that was required to answer a question at a sales counter without becoming a registry of the people who received a lawful answer.

## The Federal and State Division, Permit Alternatives, and Appeals

The National Instant Criminal Background Check System was designed as a national check, and the statute at 18 U.S.C. 922(t) placed operation of the system with the Federal Bureau of Investigation. Administration, however, never followed a single channel in every jurisdiction. Federal law permitted a division of labor under which a state agency could serve as the point of contact between licensees in that state and the federal system, and states took up that option in different degrees. The result, under the arrangement in force at June 2021, was a layered structure in which some checks began and ended with a state agency, some checks were divided between a state agency and the FBI according to the kind of firearm or the kind of permission involved, and many checks went directly to the FBI with no state intermediary at all. That division matters for more than routing. A point of contact agency did more than forward a name. The agency ran the federal check and added state records and state prohibitions, so the check performed in one state could draw on a different body of information than the check performed in another state. Permit alternatives added a second form of variation, because a qualifying state permit could satisfy the law in place of a fresh check at the counter. Appeals and challenges added a third, because a denial was not the final word, and the record rules governing approved transactions shaped the way a mistaken denial or a repeated delay could return in a later transaction.

### The Point of Contact Architecture Described in Federal Operations Reports

The counts describing the architecture come from the FBI NICS Operations Reports for 2018 and 2019, and the arrangement described in those reports was the arrangement in force at June 2021. Thirteen states were full point of contact states: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Florida, Hawaii, Illinois, Nevada, New Jersey, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Utah, and Virginia. In a full point of contact state, licensees contacted the state agency for all firearm checks, including permit checks. The state agency stood between the licensee and the federal system for every covered transaction within its borders, whether the transaction concerned a handgun or a long gun and whether the inquiry arose from a sale at the counter or from a permit process administered through the state. The licensee's first call, or the licensee's first electronic submission, went to the state, and the state carried responsibility for conducting the check through to a response.

Seven additional states were partial point of contact states, divided into two distinct groups according to the work each group performed. Four states were partial point of contact states for handguns: Maryland, New Hampshire, Washington, and Wisconsin. In each of those four states, the state ran handgun checks and the FBI ran long gun checks. The dividing line was the category of firearm. Three states formed a different partial category, partial point of contact states for handgun permits: Iowa, Nebraska, and North Carolina. In those states, the state ran checks for handgun purchase permits, and the FBI ran other checks. The dividing line in that group was the permit function rather than the category of firearm transferred at retail.

In the remaining thirty states, the District of Columbia, and the five territories, licensees contacted the FBI directly. Federal reporting described that group as thirty six non point of contact jurisdictions on the FBI map, a figure that combined the thirty states with the District of Columbia and the five territories. No single description of a NICS transaction therefore captured every transaction. A complete description had to begin with geography, namely where the transfer took place, and then with category, namely what kind of firearm or permit the transaction involved, because the answers determined which agency received the inquiry and which records the inquiry would reach.

### What a Point of Contact Agency Does Differently

A point of contact agency received the query from the licensee, and that receipt changed the path of the check from the first step. In a direct federal jurisdiction, the licensee transmitted the buyer's identifying information to the FBI by the NICS E-Check service online or by telephone. In a point of contact state, the licensee transmitted the same kind of identifying information to the designated state agency instead, and the state agency conducted the inquiry. The identifying information itself did not change with the channel. The buyer completed ATF Form 4473 at the licensee's premises, showed government photo identification, and supplied the name and descriptors recorded for the check, such as date of birth, sex, race, height, weight, and place of birth, with a Social Security number optional. The system was told whether the firearm was a handgun or a long gun, and the system received no make, model, or serial number of the firearm. Each valid inquiry received a NICS Transaction Number. The difference lay in the hands that received the inquiry and in the records those hands searched.

The state agency ran the federal check, which connected the transaction to the three federal databases: the National Crime Information Center, the Interstate Identification Index, and the NICS Index. A point of contact state then added a layer that a direct federal check did not carry in the same form. Point of contact states searched state record systems in addition to the three federal databases, and point of contact agencies added state records and state prohibitions to the federal inquiry. A record held in a state system, or a prohibition imposed by state law, could therefore enter the determination through the state's own search, and examiners working the inquiry assessed whether a federal prohibition or an applicable state prohibition existed.

The consequence for a reader of federal statistics followed directly from that design. Processing times varied by state, and denial rates varied by state, because the systems producing the figures were not identical. One jurisdiction might route every inquiry through a state agency searching state records alongside federal records, another might route only handgun inquiries through the state, another might route only permit inquiries through the state, and another might route every inquiry straight to the FBI. National figures aggregated across those arrangements combined unlike systems into a single total. A denial grounded in a state prohibition discovered through a state record search sat in an aggregate alongside a denial grounded in a federal record returned by the FBI. Two buyers presenting identical information in different states could therefore have inquiries travel through materially different administrative paths before a proceed, denied, or delayed response came back.

### How Does a Permit Replace a NICS Background Check?

A permit replaces the check only when the permit qualifies under section 922(t)(3). The permit must allow possession or acquisition, must have been issued within five years by the state of transfer, and state law must require official verification that available information shows no legal bar.

Subsection (t)(3) of 18 U.S.C. 922 supplied an exception to the ordinary sequence, and the exception was written narrowly. Paragraph (1) of subsection (t) set the general rule: a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer could not transfer a firearm to a person who was not a licensee unless, before completion of the transfer, the licensee contacted the system, the system provided a unique identification number or three business days elapsed without a notification that receipt would violate subsection (g) or (n), and the licensee verified the transferee's identity by examining a valid photo identification document. Paragraph (3) stated circumstances in which paragraph (1) did not apply. The permit circumstance was one of those circumstances, and the statutory text attached conditions to the permit itself, to the timing of issuance, to the place of issuance, and to the law of the issuing state. A permit that failed any one condition did not carry the exception, however genuine the document appeared at the counter.

The first condition concerned what the permit did. The permit had to allow the person to possess or acquire a firearm. A document serving some other purpose did not meet the text. The second condition concerned time. The permit had to have been issued not more than five years earlier, a limit that operated as a measure of the freshness of the verification behind the permit and that ran from issuance. The third condition concerned place. The permit had to have been issued by the state in which the transfer took place. A permit from another state, even a permit of the same general kind and even a permit issued within the five year period, did not satisfy the place condition, because the text tied the exception to a permit issued by the state of transfer. The fourth condition concerned the law under which the permit issued, and the condition reached behind the document to the process that produced the document. State law had to provide that the permit issued only after an authorized government official had verified that the information available to that official did not indicate that possession of a firearm by the applicant would be in violation of law. The exception therefore rested on a judgment made before the retail transaction began, and the permit embodied that earlier verification within a defined period and within the same state.

Read as a whole, the permit provision did not abolish the check in the transactions within the exception. The provision relocated the check in time and in institutional hands. Instead of a check run by a licensee through the system at the moment of transfer, the law accepted a verification run by an authorized government official before issuance of the permit, provided that state law required that verification and provided that the permit remained within the five year window and came from the state of transfer. Each condition closed a different gap between the permit in the buyer's hand and the check that the permit replaced.

### The Other Exemptions in the Same Subsection

The permit circumstance was not the only circumstance in paragraph (3). Paragraph (1) also did not apply where the Attorney General had approved the transfer under section 5812 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986. Section 5812 governed transfers under the National Firearms Act, and a transfer that had received the Attorney General's approval through that separate statutory channel stood outside the paragraph (1) requirement. The structure substituted a different federal process, and the background check paragraph did not add a second clearance on top of that approval. Paragraph (1) also did not apply where the Attorney General had certified that compliance with paragraph (1) was impracticable. The certification ground rested on conditions such as sparse law enforcement, remoteness, and the absence of telecommunications facilities. That ground addressed geography and infrastructure rather than the status of the buyer or the character of the firearm. The three circumstances differed in kind. The permit circumstance relied on a prior state verification. The National Firearms Act circumstance relied on a prior federal approval. The impracticability circumstance relied on a federal certification that the ordinary requirement could not be carried out under the conditions on the ground.

### Permit Alternatives in Practice

Statutory conditions alone did not tell a licensee which specific permits qualified, and practice supplied an administrative answer to that question. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives qualified specific permits and published the Permanent Brady Permit Chart. The chart served as the reference through which a licensee could determine whether a particular permit, issued by a particular state, met the federal standard for an alternative to the check. Qualification was permit specific rather than general. A state could have more than one kind of permit, and the federal question was whether the specific permit presented, judged against the statutory conditions and against the state law governing issuance of that permit, had been qualified. The counts for qualifying permits were reported through two different measures, and the measures were kept separate in federal reporting. Per the FBI's 2019 operations report, twenty five states had at least one ATF qualified alternate permit. That figure counted states with at least one qualifying permit of any covered kind. The Bureau of Justice Statistics reported that twenty four states offered ATF qualified exempt carry permits in 2021. That figure counted a narrower category, carry permits carrying the exemption. The two figures described overlapping but different sets, drawn from different sources and framed for different years and categories, and neither figure displaced the other.

At the counter, a transaction using a qualifying permit retained much of the ordinary paperwork. The buyer completed ATF Form 4473 even when a permit supplied the basis for proceeding without contacting the system, and the licensee verified identity against a valid photo identification document in the ordinary manner. The difference appeared in the record of clearance. Instead of recording a unique identification number supplied by the system, the licensee recorded the permit. Acceptance of a qualifying permit also rested within the licensee's discretion. A licensee presented with a qualifying permit could accept the permit and proceed on that basis, and a licensee could instead run the check through the system anyway. The statute permitted reliance on the permit; the statute did not compel reliance. That discretion mattered because the verification behind a permit could be as old as the five year limit allowed, while a fresh check searched the databases at the moment of transfer.

### Appeals and Challenges After a Denial

A denial ended a transaction, and a denial did not end the legal process available to the person denied. Under 28 C.F.R. 25.10, a denied buyer could challenge the denial. A person who had been denied could direct a challenge to the determination, and the person was entitled, on request, to the reason for the denial. A denial communicated only as a conclusion left a buyer with nothing concrete to contest, while a stated reason identified the record or the prohibition on which the determination rested and allowed the buyer to address that record directly, whether by showing that the record belonged to another person, that a disposition had been entered incorrectly, or that the underlying information was incomplete or mistaken.

The destination of a challenge followed the same federal and state division that governed the original check. A challenge could be directed to the FBI, or, in a point of contact state, to the point of contact agency. The routing reflected the origin of the determination. Where the FBI had processed the check and issued the denial, the FBI stood as the agency holding the determination under challenge. Where a state point of contact agency had processed the check, run the federal databases, added state records and state prohibitions, and issued the denial, the state agency stood as a proper recipient of the challenge, since the determination might rest on state records or on a state prohibition that the state agency was positioned to examine. The FBI maintained an appeal services function for challenges within federal responsibility, giving the challenge process a dedicated administrative home.

### Why Does a Denied Buyer Receive No Benefit From an Earlier Approved Check?

An earlier approved check leaves no usable file because federal law requires destruction of its records apart from the transaction number and date. A later denial or delay therefore starts from the databases alone, and a buyer must challenge the denial through the FBI or the point of contact agency.

The structural reason appeals and repeated delays recurred lay in the record rule governing approved transactions. Under 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(2), when receipt of a firearm would not violate subsection (g) or (n) or state law, the system assigned a unique identification number, provided the number to the licensee, and destroyed all records of the system with respect to the call, other than the identifying number and the date the number was assigned, and all records of the system relating to the person or the transfer. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2004, signed on January 23, 2004, required destruction of identifying information on an approved transaction not more than twenty four hours after the licensee was notified of the approval, a requirement carried in later appropriations acts and implemented at 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b). After destruction, the audit log for an approved transaction retained only the NICS Transaction Number and the date, with residual information purged within ninety days. The only government side residue of an approved check was the transaction number and its date.

That design carried a benefit and a cost, and appeals sat at the point where the cost became visible. The benefit was privacy through erasure: a lawful buyer who passed a check left almost no government record of the inquiry, and the system could not grow, transaction by transaction, into a registry of lawful purchasers. The cost fell on a buyer whose records produced a false match or an incomplete disposition. Because the approved transaction file had been destroyed, nothing from an earlier successful check carried forward to a later transaction. A buyer who had been delayed on one occasion and had ultimately proceeded held no file that a later examiner could consult when the same ambiguous record appeared again. Each later check began from the databases as the databases stood at that moment, and if the underlying state or court record remained incomplete, the same delay or the same mistaken denial could recur, transaction after transaction. The challenge therefore turned on the record behind the denial itself, and correction of the underlying record, rather than accumulation of a favorable file, supplied the durable remedy in the ordinary course.

### The Voluntary Appeal File and the Unique Personal Identification Number

The Voluntary Appeal File supplied a consent based exception to the destruction rule, designed for the buyer caught in the recurrence that the destruction rule produced. Under the ordinary rule, the FBI could not retain the identifying information and documentation that would allow a recurring record problem to be resolved once and applied to later checks. The Voluntary Appeal File changed that position for a person who chose to enter the file. A person could consent to the FBI retaining identifying information and supporting documentation so that future checks would resolve faster. Consent was the foundation of the arrangement. Retention that federal law forbade as a general practice became permissible for an individual who volunteered for retention, supplied supporting documentation bearing on eligibility, and accepted that the FBI would hold that material for use in later inquiries. A successful applicant to the Voluntary Appeal File received a Unique Personal Identification Number, known by the abbreviation UPIN. The buyer entered the UPIN on ATF Form 4473 for later transactions, and the number connected the later transaction to the retained file, allowing the examiner working a later check to reach the documentation that explained the earlier record problem rather than beginning the inquiry from the ambiguous database entry alone.

The Voluntary Appeal File did not alter the general record rule for buyers outside the file. Approved transaction records for the general body of buyers continued to be destroyed under 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(2), the twenty four hour appropriations deadline, and 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b), leaving only the transaction number and the date in the audit log. Denied transaction records followed a different retention path, held in the audit log for ten years and then transferred to an electronic database maintained by the FBI under the same regulation. Delayed or open transactions occupied an intermediate position, with identifying information destroyed after ninety days and the date and transaction number retained. Against that background, the Voluntary Appeal File stood as a narrow, voluntary bridge between two commitments that pulled in opposite directions. One commitment destroyed the records of lawful transactions so that the check could not become a registry. The other commitment gave a repeatedly delayed or wrongly denied buyer a workable path through later transactions without relitigating the same record problem at every counter.

## The Limits of the Check and the Denial Record

The National Instant Criminal Background Check System operates at a defined point in a defined kind of transaction, and the limits of the system follow from that placement. The federal requirement does not attach to firearms in general, to possession in general, or to every change of hands. The requirement attaches when a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer transfers a firearm to a person who is not a licensee, and the statutory duty in section 922(t)(1) belongs to the licensee in that transaction. The licensee contacts the system before completion of the transfer, verifies identity by examining a valid photo identification document, and completes the transfer only after the system provides a unique identification number or after the statutory waiting mechanism has run without a notification that receipt would violate subsection (g) or subsection (n). Outside that licensed transfer, the structure changes. A firearm that does not pass through a licensee does not enter the transaction sequence that the check was built to govern, and a buyer who never stands at a licensed counter never generates the inquiry, the transaction number, or the response on which the rest of the system depends. The boundary therefore describes the reach of a checkpoint rather than the reach of federal firearms law itself.

### Where the Federal Requirement Ends

The boundary turns on the status of the transferor. A licensed dealer, manufacturer, or importer transferring to an unlicensed person stands inside the mandate. Two unlicensed persons transferring a firearm between themselves stand outside the federal check mandate when the transfer takes place between residents of the same state, because no licensee participates and section 922(t)(1) imposes its duty on the licensee. No search runs against the National Crime Information Center, the Interstate Identification Index, or the NICS Index for that transfer, no proceed, delayed, or denied response returns, and no NICS Transaction Number is created. Federal law in that setting relies on the substantive prohibitions and on other provisions governing transfers, rather than on a screening step interposed before completion.

The evidentiary difference matters for enforcement. In a licensed transaction, the check creates a moment at which eligibility is tested against records before the firearm changes hands, and the licensee acts as the gatekeeper who must pause, inquire, and honor a denial. In a private intrastate transfer without a federal check requirement, no comparable moment exists under the provision, and a dispute after the fact turns on proof about the parties and the circumstances rather than on a system response generated at the time. Federal law places the mandatory screen at transfers by licensees, where volume, record keeping duties, and regulatory supervision converge, and leaves transfers outside the licensed trade to prohibitions, to other transfer rules, and to whatever requirements a state chooses to impose.

### Does Federal Law Require a Background Check for a Private Sale?

Federal law imposes no background check requirement on a transfer between two unlicensed residents of the same state. The transfer carries no federal checkpoint, no contact with the National Instant Criminal Background Check System, and no transaction number. The federal prohibitions on receipt and possession continue to bind the buyer throughout.

The answer requires precision about what absence means. The absence of a check requirement does not repeal, suspend, or narrow the prohibitions in subsections (g) and (n) of section 922 for a buyer in a private transfer. A person whom federal law forbids to receive or possess a firearm remains forbidden to receive or possess a firearm acquired from a neighbor, a relative, or an acquaintance in the same state, on the same terms that govern a firearm acquired from a dealer. What the private intrastate transfer lacks is the checkpoint, the advance inquiry that tests the buyer against the three databases and returns a denial before delivery. Enforcement outside that channel, where no state requirement supplies a substitute screen, depends on detection after acquisition or on a later proceeding in which possession comes to official attention. The difference is procedural rather than substantive, a difference in timing and mechanism rather than in the legal status of a prohibited buyer.

A transfer free of a federal check requirement is not a transfer free of federal law. The background check provision contributes one instrument to the statutory scheme, a pretransfer records search conducted through a licensee, and the instrument has a defined field of operation. The check reaches every covered licensed transfer, across handguns and long guns, through either a state point of contact or the Federal Bureau of Investigation directly. The check does not reach a private intrastate transfer between unlicensed persons under federal law unless state law adds a requirement of its own, and states have occupied that open terrain in sharply different ways.

### State Requirements Beyond the Federal Floor

State law overlays the federal boundary with requirements that vary in scope, in method, and in the records consulted. Some states impose waiting periods longer than the three business days allowed under the federal delayed transaction mechanism. Some states require a background check for a private transfer, conducted through a licensee or through a state agency, inserting a screening step into a transaction that federal law leaves unscreened. Some states operate permit systems under which a government official verifies eligibility before issuing a permit that reaches private acquisition. Other states impose none of those additions and leave the federal boundary as the operative rule. A transfer completed without any inquiry in one state may require an inquiry, a permit, or a licensed intermediary in another. A [comparison of federal and state firearms law](/2021/08/01/federal-vs-state-firearms-law/) places that division in its broader setting, because the private transfer question represents one instance of a general pattern in which federal law sets a floor for licensed commerce and states decide whether to build above that floor.

Counts of the state overlay depend on the measure applied, and two published counts illustrate the dependence. By the count of the Giffords Law Center, twenty states and the District of Columbia require a background check for transfers of at least some firearms between private parties, a formulation that accommodates states covering all firearms and states covering a category such as handguns. The Center for American Progress, in February 2021, counted twenty-two states and the District of Columbia under a broader measure that included permit to purchase systems reaching private sales, so a state whose permitting requirement subjected a private buyer to a records verification entered the total even where the state imposed no separate check at the point of private transfer. The difference between twenty and twenty-two reflects a methodological difference rather than a factual dispute. One measure asks whether a check is required for a private transfer, and the broader measure asks whether a private buyer passes through a government verification, either at transfer or through a permit. Both counts leave a majority of states without such an addition under either measure.

The overlay also changes the records side of a check where a state acts as a point of contact. Point of contact agencies search state record systems in addition to the three federal databases, and state prohibition sets differ from the federal set, so a check routed through a state agency or a licensee in a point of contact state draws on a wider record base than the federal databases alone, and denial rates and processing times vary by state for that reason, among others. State law decides whether a private transfer is checked at all, and state administration helps decide what a check finds when one occurs.

### The Denial Record in Fiscal Two Thousand Seventeen

The enforcement record behind the check rests most fully on a report of the Government Accountability Office, GAO-18-440, published in June 2018, which examined denials and prosecutions for fiscal 2017. The measure in that report covers federal transactions only, meaning transactions the Federal Bureau of Investigation processed, and transactions handled by point of contact states fall outside the measure. Within that defined universe, the system processed 8,606,286 federal NICS transactions in fiscal 2017, and 112,090 of those transactions ended in denial, about 1.3 percent of the federal transactions measured. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives referred 12,710 of the denial cases to agency field divisions for investigation, and United States Attorneys Offices had prosecuted 12 of the referred cases through June 2018, the point to which the report tracked prosecutions. Each figure in that chain requires the explanations documented in the same report, stated in the same passage, because the chain without the explanations invites readings that the report itself does not support. The denial total is not a count of crimes, and the prosecution total reflects investigative screening, guideline based declination, and the evidentiary demands of the offense as well as the size of the denial pool.

The explanations begin with prosecutorial selection. Federal prosecutors applied declination guidelines to denial cases and weighed those cases against other gun violence cases competing for the same resources, placing a false statement prosecution arising from a denied counter transaction alongside investigations involving firearms already in circulation and offenses involving violence. The guidelines directed attention to factors such as the seriousness of the underlying prohibition, the evidence available, and the prospect of success. The knowledge element deepened the selection problem. A prosecution for a false statement to a licensee under section 922(a)(6), punishable through section 924(a)(3) by imprisonment for up to ten years under the penalty in force for the period covered, requires proof that the buyer made a false statement, and officials described proof that the buyer knew of the prohibition as a genuine evidentiary difficulty. Many denied buyers held old convictions or misunderstood personal status, circumstances in which a buyer might answer eligibility questions incorrectly without the knowledge a prosecution must establish. A record showing a prohibition establishes why the system denied the transaction, but does not establish what the buyer understood when completing Form 4473.

Administrative practice inside the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives formed the third explanation. Field divisions increasingly issued warning notices to denied persons in place of investigation as referral volume grew, and referrals had risen 59 percent over six years covered by the report. A warning notice informs the denied person of the prohibition and of the consequences of further attempts or unlawful possession, without the resource commitment of a full investigation. The fourth explanation concerns the denominator. Many denials were resolved as erroneous or as records problems on challenge, so the 112,090 denials include transactions denied on records that later correction, clarification, or successful challenge displaced. A buyer denied in error committed no offense by attempting the purchase, so only a subset of the total could ever support a false statement prosecution. The figure and the explanations therefore belong together as a single finding. In fiscal 2017 the federal system denied about 1.3 percent of 8,606,286 federally processed transactions, investigators received 12,710 referrals, prosecutors filed 12 cases through June 2018, and the documented reasons for the narrowing were guideline based declination amid competing gun violence cases, difficulty proving knowledge, an agency shift toward warning notices under rising referral volume, and a denial pool that included erroneous and records based denials later resolved.

Delayed denials add a further element. Where a transaction proceeded after three business days without a denial notification and later research established a prohibition, the Federal Bureau of Investigation notified the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, and that bureau bore responsibility for retrieving the firearm. Retrieval differs from a counter denial in every practical respect, because the firearm has left the store and agents must locate the buyer and recover the weapon through direct contact. Government reporting treats retrieval volume as part of the same enforcement picture as denial investigation, since each delayed denial converts a records problem, often a missing disposition that examiners could not obtain within the statutory window, into a field operation.

### Denials and Prosecution at the State Level

The state pattern in the same Government Accountability Office report diverged sharply from any single model. Officials in 10 of the 13 states the office selected for review said their states did not investigate or prosecute denials, citing resource demands and missing state statutes. A state without a statute criminalizing the conduct underlying a denial lacks a state charge to bring, and a state agency facing the same triage pressures as federal investigators may assign denial follow up a low priority. The other three states investigated a high proportion of denials, and one reported about 1,900 referrals for prosecution in 2017 and about 470 convictions. The contrast with 12 federal prosecutions through June 2018 does not yield a simple comparison, because the state figure arises under state law, state charging standards, and a state denial pool, while the federal figure arises from federally processed transactions under federal guidelines. The comparison does establish that denial enforcement is a function of institutional choice and statutory provision rather than a fixed consequence of a denial itself.

The variation also complicates national aggregation. Federal denial figures exclude point of contact state transactions, state denial figures arise under differing prohibition sets and record bases, and state enforcement figures reflect differing statutes and practices, so a combined national statement built by adding those unlike measures together would misrepresent every component. The Government Accountability Office handled the problem by bounding the federal chain to federally processed transactions in fiscal 2017 and by identifying the single state referral and conviction figures as the experience of one state rather than a national rate.

### Two Readings of the Same Record

One reading emphasizes enforcement after the response. Commentators and officials who advance the enforcement gap argument contend that a checkpoint whose violations are almost never prosecuted deters weakly, because a prohibited person who attempts a purchase and suffers only a denial, a warning notice, or no contact at all learns that the attempt carries little consequence beyond failure to obtain that firearm from that dealer. The argument points to the fiscal 2017 chain, 112,090 federal denials producing 12,710 field referrals and 12 prosecutions through June 2018, as evidence that the system identifies prohibited attempts at scale and then declines to pursue the attempters, and stresses the retrieval burden that falls after the fact when a delayed denial requires agents to recover a firearm already transferred. On that reading, a faster and more complete search yields limited returns unless investigation and prosecution follow adverse findings, and the private transfer boundary compounds the problem because a denied buyer who faces no prosecution retains every incentive to seek a firearm through a channel with no checkpoint. Proponents tend to favor funded denial investigation units, state statutes supplying a charge for a false statement or a prohibited attempt, and systematic referral practices modeled on the states that investigated a high proportion of denials.

A second reading emphasizes the administrative burden the system carries and the composition of the denial pool. Commentators and officials who advance the administrative burden argument observe that the system processed millions of transactions, including 8,606,286 federally processed transactions in fiscal 2017 within the Government Accountability Office measure, and that each determination depends on records supplied by courts and agencies across the country whose completeness the Federal Bureau of Investigation does not control. Examiners chase missing dispositions because source records arrive incomplete, and false matches burden lawful buyers who share identifiers with prohibited persons, suffer delay or denial, and must use the challenge process or the Voluntary Appeal File to correct a record the buyer did not create. On that reading, a denial pool that includes erroneous and records based denials later resolved cannot support prosecution at anywhere near the denial rate, the knowledge element screens out cases involving old convictions and genuine misunderstanding, and prosecution resources directed at violent offenses reflect a rational ordering rather than neglect. Proponents tend to favor investment in record completeness at the source and in faster correction mechanisms, presenting the warning notice practice as a proportionate response. Neither reading disputes the reported figures. The disagreement concerns what the narrowing between 112,090 denials and 12 prosecutions signifies, institutional failure to enforce a prohibition or the predictable output of a high volume records system whose adverse findings vary widely in culpability and provability.

### The Provision Mapped Whole

The provision resolves into a small set of working parts. The mandate comes first. A licensee may not transfer a firearm to an unlicensed person without contacting the system before completion of the transfer, verifying identity against a valid photo identification document, and receiving either a unique identification number or the passage of three business days, defined by days on which state offices are open in the state of transfer, without notification that receipt would violate federal or applicable state law. The search comes second. The inquiry, identified by a NICS Transaction Number and carrying the identity of the buyer and the category of firearm without make, model, or serial number, runs against the National Crime Information Center, the Interstate Identification Index, and the NICS Index, with state point of contact agencies adding state records and state prohibitions in states that perform the check. The clock comes third. A possible match produces review by an examiner, an unresolved match produces a delay during which examiners seek missing records, the licensee may transfer after three business days if no denial has issued while remaining free to wait, and a later denial passes to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives for retrieval. The record rule comes fourth. An approved transaction leaves the government only the transaction number and its date, with identifying information destroyed within twenty four hours under appropriations law carried forward from the fiscal 2004 act, delayed transaction information destroyed after ninety days, denied transaction records retained for ten years, the Form 4473 held by the licensee for at least twenty years rather than by an agency, and a statutory bar in section 926(a) and in section 103(i) of the Brady Act against using the system or licensee records to build a registration system. The state division comes fifth. Thirteen states served as full points of contact under the arrangement in force in June 2021, four states ran handgun checks as partial points of contact, three states ran handgun permit checks as partial points of contact, licensees elsewhere contacted the Federal Bureau of Investigation directly, and qualifying state permits issued within five years after a government verification could substitute for the individual check at the discretion of the licensee. The boundary comes last. The mandate governs transfers by licensees and stops there, a private transfer between unlicensed residents of the same state carries no federal check requirement, the prohibitions continue to bind the buyer in that transfer without a checkpoint to enforce them in advance, and states decide for themselves whether to close that space with a check, a permit, a waiting period, or no addition at all. Held together, those six parts describe a records screen at the licensed counter, deliberately denied a registry, divided in administration between a federal bureau and state agencies, timed by a three day clock, and bounded by a private sphere that federal law regulates through prohibitions rather than through screening.

The provision at 18 U.S.C. 922(t) holds together because each part limits the part beside it. The check gives the transfer a factual test, conducted against records held in three databases and resolved through an automated search or through examiner review of a possible match. The clock gives that test a boundary, measured in three business days counted by the calendar of State offices in the State of transfer, after which a licensee may proceed without a denial notification while research continues toward a determination. The destruction rule gives the test an endpoint for the person who passed, because identifying information from an approved transaction is destroyed within 24 hours and the audit log retains a transaction number and a date without a name. A design with a check and no clock could hold a lawful transfer indefinitely on an incomplete record. A design with a check and no destruction rule could accumulate a federal file on lawful purchasers transaction by transaction. The statute instead joins a mandatory inquiry, a bounded interval, and a rapid purge, and the join is the provision. The three responses complete the mechanism, because proceed supplies the number that enters the licensee record, denied closes the transaction on a determination that a prohibition exists, and delayed preserves the inquiry without converting uncertainty into either outcome.

Administration carries the same joined character. A licensee holds the detailed transaction record for not less than twenty years, while the federal system holds almost nothing from an approved check. A state point of contact agency adds state records and state prohibitions to the federal search, while a licensee in a direct federal jurisdiction sends the inquiry to the Federal Bureau of Investigation. A qualifying permit moves verification to the moment of issuance, within a five year window and under a state law that requires an official check before issuance. A denial opens a challenge process, and the Voluntary Appeal File offers a consent based record for a buyer caught by a recurring record problem. Later changes to this framework are treated in the series article on the [Bipartisan Safer Communities Act](/2021/06/01/bipartisan-safer-communities-act-2022/). Each route returns to the same statutory questions: who must act, what information may be searched, how long an answer may take, and what record may remain after the answer arrives.

Careful study of the provision rewards attention to sequence and custody rather than to slogans attached to the check in public argument. The mandate comes before completion of transfer. The contact comes before the clock. The number comes before the record entry. The destruction comes after approval, and retention for denied and delayed transactions follows a separate schedule set by category. That order also explains the federal and state division, because the same sequence reaches a state agency in a point of contact state and the federal bureau elsewhere without changing the responses or the record fate attached to each response. A student or practitioner working through that sequence can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) and can [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) while tracing each rule to the paragraph or regulation that carries the rule. Held in that order, the check, the clock, and the destruction rule read as one machine with three motions: inquiry before transfer, permission bounded by time, and erasure after approval for the transaction that produced no bar.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does a NICS check actually work?

The buyer completes ATF Form 4473 at the premises of the licensee and shows government photo identification, and the licensee verifies identity against that document. The licensee then transmits the name and descriptors of the buyer by NICS E-Check or by telephone to the FBI or to the state point of contact. Each valid inquiry receives a NICS Transaction Number. The system searches the National Crime Information Center, the Interstate Identification Index, and the NICS Index. Where no matching record is returned, the transaction is proceeded automatically. Where a possible match appears, a NICS examiner reviews the records and determines whether a federal or applicable state prohibition exists. The response returned is proceed, denied, or delayed.

### Q: What databases does NICS search?

The search runs against three named databases. The National Crime Information Center holds documented criminal justice information, including wanted persons under arrest warrants and protection orders. The Interstate Identification Index is the index of state criminal history records and supplies felony and misdemeanor conviction records, including misdemeanor crimes of domestic violence. The NICS Index holds records contributed by federal, state, local, and tribal agencies on persons federally prohibited where the information is not in the other two databases, including protection orders and active warrants absent from the first database, felony convictions not reflected in the index, court orders against possession, unlawful alien status, mental health adjudications and commitments, dishonorable discharges, renunciations of citizenship, and unlawful users of controlled substances. For a noncitizen buyer, Immigration and Customs Enforcement databases are also queried.

### Q: Does NICS create a gun registry?

The system is barred from becoming a registry by text and by destruction. Under 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(2), an approved transaction receives a unique identification number, and the system destroys all records of the call other than the identifying number and the date the number was assigned. Appropriations law, through section 617 of Public Law 108-199 and acts carried forward from the fiscal 2004 act, requires destruction of identifying information on an approved transaction within 24 hours after the licensee is notified of approval. The audit log retains only the NICS Transaction Number and the date. Section 926(a) forbids centralization of licensee records or a registration system, and section 103(i) of the Brady Act forbids use of the system to establish registration. The detailed Form 4473 remains with the licensee.

### Q: What happens if NICS does not respond in three days?

Silence after a defined interval has a defined effect. Under 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(1)(B)(ii), once three business days have elapsed since the licensee contacted the system and the system has not notified the licensee that receipt would violate subsection (g) or (n), the licensee may transfer the firearm. A business day is a day on which State offices are open in the State of transfer. The statute permits the transfer and does not compel the transfer, so a licensee may wait or decline. State law may impose a longer wait in some states. The FBI continues researching the delayed transaction after the third business day, and a later determination of denial after transfer produces a referral to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives for retrieval.

### Q: Which states run their own NICS checks?

Under the arrangement in force at June 2021, described in the FBI NICS Operations Reports for 2018 and 2019, thirteen states were full point of contact states: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Florida, Hawaii, Illinois, Nevada, New Jersey, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Utah, and Virginia. Licensees in those states contact the state agency for all firearm checks, including permit checks. Four states were partial point of contact states for handguns: Maryland, New Hampshire, Washington, and Wisconsin, where the state runs handgun checks and the FBI runs long gun checks. Three states were partial point of contact states for handgun permits: Iowa, Nebraska, and North Carolina, where the state runs permit checks and the FBI runs other checks. In the remaining thirty states, the District of Columbia, and the five territories, licensees contact the FBI directly.

### Q: Can a state permit replace a NICS check?

A permit replaces the transaction check only under 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(3). The permit must allow the person to possess or acquire a firearm, must have been issued not more than five years earlier by the State in which the transfer takes place, and state law must provide that the permit issues only after an authorized government official has verified that available information does not indicate possession would violate law. The buyer completes Form 4473 in any event, and the licensee records the permit instead of a transaction number. Acceptance rests in licensee discretion, and a licensee may run the check anyway. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives qualifies specific permits and publishes the Permanent Brady Permit Chart. Per the FBI 2019 operations report, 25 states had at least one qualified alternate permit, and the Bureau of Justice Statistics reported 24 states offered qualified exempt carry permits in 2021.

### Q: How do you appeal a NICS denial?

A denied buyer may challenge the denial under 28 C.F.R. 25.10. The challenge may be directed to the FBI or, in a point of contact state, to the state agency that conducted the check. The denied person is entitled, on request, to the reason for the denial, and the reason identifies the record or prohibition on which the determination rested. The FBI maintains an appeal services function for challenges within federal responsibility. A challenge can show that a record belonged to another person, that a disposition was entered incorrectly, or that underlying information was incomplete or mistaken. Correction of the underlying record supplies the durable remedy, because records of an earlier approved transaction were destroyed and no earlier file carries forward into a later check.

### Q: What transfers do not require a NICS check?

The federal check requirement attaches to transfers by licensees, and a transfer between two unlicensed residents of the same state carries no federal check requirement. No contact with the system occurs for such a transfer, no databases are searched under the provision, and no transaction number is created. The prohibitions in subsections (g) and (n) continue to bind the buyer in a private transfer, so absence of a checkpoint does not remove the prohibition itself. Separate federal rules govern interstate private transfers and knowing transfers to prohibited persons under other provisions. State law adds a further layer, and some states require a check through a licensee or a state agency for private transfers, impose waiting periods, or operate permit systems reaching private acquisition.

### Q: What information from the Form 4473 is transmitted to NICS?

The licensee transmits identifying information drawn from the transaction record, consisting of the name of the buyer and descriptors of the buyer. Descriptors named in FBI operations materials include date of birth, sex, race, height, weight, and place of birth. A Social Security number may be included, and inclusion is optional. The system is told whether the firearm involved is a handgun or a long gun. The system receives no make of the firearm, no model of the firearm, and no serial number of the firearm. Transmission occurs by NICS E-Check online or by telephone, to the FBI or to the state point of contact, and each valid inquiry receives a NICS Transaction Number that connects the inquiry, the response, and the licensee record of the transfer.

### Q: What does a delayed NICS response mean and how long can it last?

A delayed response means the search returned a potential match whose disposition was missing or unclear, and examiners require records from courts and agencies before a determination can be made. Delay is an interim response rather than a finding of eligibility or ineligibility. Examiners seek the missing records, compare descriptors against the record, and determine whether a federal or applicable state prohibition exists. The FBI continues researching a delayed transaction after the third business day, the point after which a licensee may transfer without a denial notification. If no final determination is reached, identifying information for the transaction is purged when the transaction reaches 90 days under 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b), and the date and the NICS Transaction Number are retained after the purge.

### Q: Can a licensee refuse to complete a transfer after a default proceed?

A licensee may refuse or may wait. The default proceed rests on the structure of 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(1), which bars a transfer unless a stated condition is met. Once three business days have elapsed after contact without notification that receipt would violate subsection (g) or (n), the condition in that branch is satisfied and the federal paragraph no longer bars the transfer on that ground. Satisfaction of a condition that lifts a prohibition is not an order to complete a sale. A licensee who follows a policy of transferring only on an affirmative proceed response may follow that policy, and a licensee who prefers to await a determination may await a determination. State law may also impose a longer wait that binds the licensee after the federal interval ends.

### Q: How long does the FBI keep records of a denied transaction?

Records of a denied transaction are retained in the audit log for 10 years, after which the records are transferred to an electronic database maintained by the FBI, under 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b). The longer period reflects the character of a denial as an adverse determination. A denied buyer may challenge the denial under 28 C.F.R. 25.10 and is entitled on request to the reason, and a review requires a record to review. A referral for investigation likewise requires a record to investigate, and retention allows recognition of a repeat attempted purchase by a person previously determined to be prohibited. The period stands in contrast to an approved transaction, where identifying information is destroyed within 24 hours and the audit log retains only the transaction number and the date.

### Q: What is the Voluntary Appeal File and what is a UPIN?

The Voluntary Appeal File is a consent based exception to the destruction rule for approved transactions. Because records of an approved check are destroyed apart from a transaction number and a date, a person repeatedly delayed or wrongly denied holds no earlier file that a later examiner can consult, and the same ambiguous record can produce the same delay at a later counter. The file lets a person consent to the FBI retaining identifying information and supporting documentation so future checks resolve faster. A successful applicant receives a Unique Personal Identification Number, known as a UPIN. The buyer enters the UPIN on ATF Form 4473 for later transactions, and the number connects the later transaction to the retained file and to the documentation explaining the earlier record problem.

### Q: Who operates NICS and where is it based?

NICS is operated by the FBI, through the NICS Section of the Criminal Justice Information Services Division, located in Clarksburg, West Virginia. The system launched on November 30, 1998, as the permanent arrangement required by the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act, Public Law 103-159, and from launch the check covered handguns and long guns alike. Implementing regulations appear at 28 C.F.R. Part 25. Examiners within the section, titled Legal Instruments Examiners, review possible matches returned by the automated search and determine whether a federal or applicable state prohibition exists. Telephone service has customarily been available 17 hours a day, seven days a week, except Christmas, and NICS E-Check has been available at all hours for licensees submitting inquiries online.

### Q: What is the difference between a full and a partial point of contact state?

In a full point of contact state, licensees contact the state agency for all firearm checks, including permit checks, and the state agency conducts each check. Thirteen states held that status under the arrangement in force at June 2021. A partial point of contact state divides the work. In Maryland, New Hampshire, Washington, and Wisconsin, the state runs handgun checks and the FBI runs long gun checks. In Iowa, Nebraska, and North Carolina, the state runs checks for handgun purchase permits and the FBI runs other checks. In every point of contact arrangement, the state agency runs the federal check and searches state record systems in addition to the three federal databases, adding state records and state prohibitions to the inquiry.

### Q: How many background checks does NICS process in a year?

FBI monthly NICS statistics count checks initiated through the system, with federal and state point of contact checks combined, and the count includes permit checks and rechecks. The count does not measure firearms sold, because one check can cover several firearms in a single transaction and some sales involve more than one check. In calendar 2020 the system processed 39,695,315 checks, the largest annual total since launch. From November 30, 1998 through December 31, 2020, the cumulative total was approximately 372.7 million checks. A figure stated without its measure invites error, because a count of checks initiated, a count of federal transactions in a fiscal year, and a count of firearms transferred describe three different quantities produced by the same system.

### Q: What happens after a denial, and is a denied buyer prosecuted?

Government Accountability Office report GAO-18-440, covering fiscal 2017 federal transactions processed by the FBI only, counted 8,606,286 transactions and 112,090 denials, about 1.3 percent. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives referred 12,710 cases to field divisions, and United States Attorneys had prosecuted 12 cases through June 2018. The report documented the explanations together with the figures: prosecutors applied declination guidelines against competing gun violence cases, proof that a buyer knew of the prohibition presented a genuine evidentiary difficulty where convictions were old or status was misunderstood, field divisions increasingly issued warning notices as referrals rose 59 percent over six years, and many denials were resolved as erroneous or as records problems on challenge. A false statement to a licensee is a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. 922(a)(6), punishable through 924(a)(3) by imprisonment for up to ten years under the penalty in force for the period covered.

### Q: Can a state make a buyer wait longer than three business days?

State law may impose a longer wait, and a licensee in such a state is bound by the longer state period even after the federal interval has expired. The federal rule in 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(1)(B)(ii) sets the point at which federal law ceases to bar a delayed transfer on timing grounds, after three business days counted by days on which State offices are open in the State of transfer. The federal interval operates as a floor for the waiting obligation rather than as a ceiling on state authority. A buyer whose federal period has run in a state with a longer state wait has satisfied one of two applicable clocks, and completion of the transfer awaits satisfaction of the state clock as well.

### Q: What does a licensee do with the Form 4473 after the sale?

The Form 4473 remains with the licensee rather than with an agency. Under 27 C.F.R. 478.129(b), a licensee must retain each Form 4473 for not less than 20 years after the date of sale or other disposition, and the corresponding acquisition and disposition record is kept under the same regulation family. If the licensee discontinues business, the records are delivered to the ATF Out-of-Business Records Center or to an ATF office, under 18 U.S.C. 923(g)(4) and 27 C.F.R. 478.129, unless a successor licensee takes the records within the terms of the regulation. The retained form supports tracing, because ATF traces a recovered firearm through the chain of distribution and ends at the first retail purchaser shown on the Form 4473 of the dealer, in a manual record by record process.

### Q: What should a student know about the NICS check provision for an exam?

Examination answers on the provision rest on five elements stated in statutory order. The mandate sits at 18 U.S.C. 922(t)(1): a licensee may not transfer to a nonlicensee before contact with the system, an identification number or the passage of three business days without a denial notification, and verification of identity through photo identification. The three responses are proceed, denied, and delayed. The clock is three business days counted by State office days in the State of transfer, and the default proceed permits rather than compels a transfer. The record rule destroys identifying information for an approved transaction within 24 hours, retains denied records for ten years, and leaves Form 4473 with the licensee for twenty years. The state division assigns checks to state point of contact agencies or to the FBI according to state status and transaction kind.
