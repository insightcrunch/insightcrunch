## The Verdict First: Fewer Coinages Than Legend, More Craft Than the Myth Allows

Shakespeare's coinages and rare words in Romeo and Juliet are the most exaggerated exhibit in the whole case for the playwright as inventor of English, and the exaggeration conceals the genuine achievement. The verdict defended across this article is double. Against the legend: the play coined very few words outright, and most of the items on its popular lists are first surviving records, older formations, or rarities that no evidence can attach to one man's invention. For the play: its distinction is real but differently located. It minted compounds with unusual freedom, stretched old terms into new senses, raided the vocabularies of law, falconry, medicine, and the racecourse for erotic and violent work, and placed rare diction at the moments of highest pressure, where a strange word lands like a bell. The credit belongs less to invention than to concentration, placement, and the compound style. A language is not enriched only by the words a writer creates. It is enriched by the uses a writer proves a word can bear, and this play's proofs have been cited ever since.

![Shakespeare's coinages and rare words in the play, the notable terms of the coinage ledger - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The method matters more than any single item, so the terms of the audit come first. A coinage, strictly, is a word formed by a writer that had no prior existence in the language. A first record is something else entirely: the earliest surviving written attestation a dictionary can find, which may postdate generations of speech. A rare use is a word with a thin trail in the written record, whether old or new. A new sense is an old form doing fresh work. A compound is a formation built on the spot from existing parts by a rule every speaker of the language possessed. Popular writing about this play collapses all five categories into the single glamorous word invented, and the collapse is the myth. The sections that follow keep the categories separate, grade the play's notable terms one by one in a ledger, and reach a verdict on what the vocabulary adds. Quotations are kept brief and are taken from René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, cited by act, scene, and line; the textual caution that the play survives in a shorter quarto of 1597 and the fuller quarto of 1599 applies throughout, since a word's first record can shift with the edition that preserves it.

The play announces the whole method at its own front door. The Prologue is a sonnet of fourteen lines, and inside those fourteen lines sit two welded compounds of fate, "star-crossed" and "death-marked" (Prologue), one rare adjective spun from a noun, "misadventured" (Prologue), and one common word, "civil," made to carry three senses at once (Prologue). A listener who understood the next two hours needed no more instruction than that: the novelty would be in the welding, the rarity would be in the record, and the deepest effects would come from old words loaded past their ordinary weight. The tragedy then performs, at full length, exactly the programme its first fourteen lines advertised, which is one reason the play serves so well as the audit's specimen. Its verbal signature is not hidden in obscure corners. It stands at the entrance, in the most memorised passage of the text, where any reader can test the claims this article makes against the lines themselves.

### How many words did Shakespeare invent?

No honest number exists. Every figure in circulation counts first citations in a dictionary, and a first citation records the earliest surviving print a reading programme happened to gather, not the moment of invention. Words lived in speech and in lost plays long before any lexicographer found them, so the total is unknowable.

Every confident total is a measure of the archive, not of the man. The scale of the correction can be put plainly. Older reference works and the popular tradition that feeds on them credit the playwright with somewhere near seventeen hundred inventions, a figure repeated in classrooms, quizzes, and programme notes. Lexicographers who recount with stricter rules arrive at far smaller numbers, and the recounts keep shrinking individual claims as earlier attestations surface in digitised archives of early print. What survives every recount is not a tally of inventions but a pattern: the plays, this one among them, are dense with first records, rare forms, compounds, and extended senses, and the density is a fact about the texts even when the invention story fails. The play under examination is an early work, written when its author was near thirty, and its vocabulary already shows the habits that the myth later misread as manufacture: an ear for the compound, a taste for the technical term carried across domains, and a willingness to let a word's grammar bend under emotional pressure.

None of this diminishes the play for a reader who came looking for wonders. It relocates them. A first record in a much-read text does real work in a language: it fixes a form in print, gives it the prestige of a famous line, and supplies later writers with a precedent they can cite and imitate. A word that enters the written language through the Prologue of Romeo and Juliet has a different future from the same word muttered once in a lost pamphlet, even if the pamphlet came first. The ledger method honours that distinction instead of erasing it. Where the evidence supports only a first record, the ledger says first record. Where a formation is genuinely hard to parallel, the ledger says so and shows its working. Where an old word is doing new work, the ledger credits the work rather than the word. The result is a smaller pile of coinages and a larger, more interesting account of verbal craft.

The first audience's experience is the lost evidence in all of this, and imagining it honestly disciplines the claims further. That audience heard no footnotes. A formation like "star-crossed" arrived in a crowded yard, spoken once, at speed, and it was understood, which means the novelty the audience experienced was never the novelty of opacity. The period had a taste for hard words and mocked it in the same breath, on stages where pedants and gallants choked on inkhorn terms to general laughter; the play under audit does not ask its hearers to admire a vocabulary. Its formations are built to be caught on the wing, its rarities are planted in contexts that gloss them as they pass, its strangest word, the runaways of the summons, is strange precisely because the context refuses to settle it. Verbal novelty in this play is a branch of hospitality: the guest is given something new in a form the house rules make welcome. That hospitality, rather than any tally of inventions, is what a modern reader still registers as freshness when the lines are heard, and it is a quality of use, not of manufacture, from the Prologue's first compound to the tomb's last bargain.

## First Recorded Is Not Coined: How the Dictionary Builds a Myth

The myth has a mechanism, and the mechanism is the dictionary itself. The Oxford English Dictionary, the archive on which nearly every coinage claim rests, was built by readers who excerpted quotations from the books they read, and the books they read most closely were the canonical ones. Jürgen Schäfer demonstrated the distortion in his study of the dictionary's documentation: for the period around 1600 the readers drew disproportionately on a narrow shelf of prestigious authors, with Shakespeare excerpted more thoroughly than any contemporary, so his works supply first citations for hundreds of words that were simply the first the programme found in print. A word spoken in every market town could wait decades for its first surviving record if no reader happened to transcribe the cheap print where it appeared, while a word in a famous play was captured at once. The dictionary's Shakespeare is therefore partly an artifact of the dictionary's method, and the coinage lists built on it inherit the bias.

David Crystal, the most widely read modern defender of the playwright's lexical importance, accepts much of this and still argues that the novelty is real. In his accounts the count of first uses is high because the writer was genuinely venturesome: ready to convert nouns to verbs, to compound freely, to borrow and naturalise foreign stock, and to trust an audience to follow a formation it had never met. Crystal's Shakespeare is not a manufacturer working from nothing but a powerfully productive user of the language's own rules, and on that description the two camps nearly agree. The disagreement that matters is with the stronger popular claim, and its sharpest critic is the lexicographer Julie Coleman, whose work on slang and on the dictionary's sources insists that citation counts measure the reading habits of Victorian excerptors at least as much as the habits of Elizabethan writers. Where a word belongs to speech, to a trade, or to a region poorly served by surviving print, its absence from earlier records proves nothing about its age.

The adjudication this article reaches is that Coleman wins on method and Crystal wins on scale. On method: no first citation, standing alone, can carry the weight the word invented puts on it, and any claim built from citation counts must be discounted by the known bias of the archive. On scale: even after the discount, the density of first records, fresh compounds, and extended senses in this single early play is remarkable, and the pattern of the density, examined item by item below, shows a writer exploiting the productive machinery of English with unusual confidence. The editor's evidence arbitrates between the two. Weis, editing from the early quartos, notes form after form whose spelling, hyphenation, or very presence differs between the 1597 and 1599 texts, which means some portion of the play's supposed verbal novelty belongs to transmission, to compositors and scribes, rather than to any author. A responsible account holds all three findings at once: the archive is biased, the writer was bold, and the text itself is not a perfectly stable witness.

### Why does a dictionary date a word by its first surviving print rather than by its first speaking?

Because print is what survives to be checked. Speech leaves no dated evidence, lost plays leave none, and a lexicographer can cite only documents that exist. The first surviving print is therefore a floor, not a birth date: the word is at least as old as the citation and may be far older.

Every coinage claim that forgets the distinction mistakes the archive's earliest receipt for the word's first breath in the language. The practical consequence for this play is a discipline of phrasing. The careful verbs are recorded, attested, preserved, extended, and given currency. The careless verb is invented, which asserts a fact about origins that the evidence almost never supplies. The distinction is not pedantry, because the two claims point at different achievements. Invention is an act of solitary manufacture. First preservation in a durable and beloved text is an act of transmission, and transmission is how words actually enter a language's permanent stock: not at the moment of coining, whenever that was, but at the moment a form becomes findable, quotable, and reusable by strangers. Judged by that standard the play's contribution grows rather than shrinks, since its lines have been findable and quotable without interruption since the 1590s.

One further mechanism completes the picture: the feedback loop between fame and citation. A word preserved in a famous passage attracts glosses, and the glosses attract citations, and the citations harden into the claim that the famous passage created the word. School editions annotate the striking term, dictionaries quote the annotated line, popular lists harvest the dictionary entry, and the harvest is presented as a census of invention. Breaking the loop requires returning to the primary question the ledger asks of every item: what, exactly, does the surviving evidence show? Sometimes it shows a formation with no earlier parallel. Sometimes it shows a racing term, a legal term, or a medical term that the play borrowed with everyone else. Sometimes it shows a word so rare that the honest verdict is that the evidence is too thin to grade. The ledger that follows is built to display those differences rather than flatten them.

The archive, to its credit, keeps correcting itself, and the corrections run in one direction. The great dictionary's supplements and revisions through the twentieth century antedated thousands of first citations as more early print was read, and the digitising of the period's surviving books has accelerated the process beyond anything the slip-readers could have imagined: a sermon, a pamphlet, a mayor's accounts, searchable in an afternoon, demote another supposed coinage to a first record that is not even first. Lexicographers welcome the demotions, since a dictionary's business is the record and not the legend, but the popular lists are never revised at the same speed, and the figure of seventeen hundred inventions persists in print decades after the scholarship that produced it has been dismantled piece by piece. The lag is itself a finding about how verbal fame works: a claim attached to a beloved name has a survival advantage over its own refutation, which is the survival mechanism of the ledger's eighth section operating on the scale of scholarship.

## Counting the Word-Hoard: What the Totals Can and Cannot Show

Before the ledger is read, the counting itself deserves an audit, because every popular claim in this territory begins with a number and most of the numbers are softer than they look. A concordance of a play must first decide what a word is. Marvin Spevack's Harvard concordance to the complete works, the standard instrument for such counting, tallies graphic forms: each distinct spelling and inflection stands in its own entry, so a verb in its several persons and tenses swells the total without adding a single item to the vocabulary a speaker commands. Count by dictionary headword instead, gathering the inflections under one lemma, and the same text yields a far smaller figure. Neither method is wrong. They answer different questions, and confusion between them is the seed of most pub-quiz lexicography, in which the playwright is said to have used tens of thousands of words, more than any writer before him, as though the count were a census of possessions rather than an artifact of the counting rule.

The play's own total, by the forms method, runs into the low thousands of distinct items, a large stock for a single play and a thoroughly professional one for a repertory dramatist writing at speed for a settled company. Set beside the whole canon, whose totals range by method from the high teens of thousands of headwords to near thirty thousand distinct forms, the play looks like what it is: an early work by a writer whose hoard was already wide and growing. None of these figures measures invention. A hoard can be wide because its owner reads widely, listens in markets and law courts, and retains what registers offer; width is evidence of appetite and memory, not of manufacture. The figures that carry information for the present audit are ratios, not totals: how many items in a thousand running words are first records, fresh compounds, technical raids, or stretched senses. By that measure the play scores high, and scoring high on density is a claim the text can be made to prove, passage by passage, in a way that no total can be.

Two further cautions belong to the counting house. Proper names inflate every total while adding nothing to the vocabulary question, and this play is rich in names that are themselves small lessons in transmission: Capulet and Montague arrive from Italian sources through French and English intermediaries, wearing the marks of every border they crossed. And spelling, in a period before standardisation, splits single words into rival forms on the page, so that a forms-based count of an old-spelling text overstates the hoard while a modernised edition quietly merges the variants and understates it. The ledger therefore makes no argument from totals at all. It grades items, and items can be checked. A reader who wants a single sentence to carry away from the counting house can take this one: the play's vocabulary is large, its marked density is larger, and its inventions, strictly so called, are the smallest part of either figure.

The counting tradition has a history of its own, and it explains where the large soft numbers came from. Alfred Hart's statistical studies of the canon, published in the 1940s, gave the vocabulary question its first rigorous treatment, tallying forms across the plays and estimating the total stock at figures far above any contemporary's, estimates that depended, as Hart himself acknowledged, on decisions about compounds and variants that another counter could reasonably make the other way. Later concordance work refined the tallies without settling the decisions. The lesson the counting house teaches, across a century of instruments, is that the totals move with the method while the marked items stay put. A compound is a compound under every counting rule; a first record survives every recount until an antedating displaces it; a stretched sense can be pointed to in the line. The ledger therefore builds on the stable stratum and lets the totals go, which is why its findings can be stated as verdicts rather than as estimates.

## The Dictionary's Author: How Shakespeare Became the Record's Favourite

The feedback loop described earlier has identifiable engineers, and naming them completes the mechanism. The first is Samuel Johnson, whose Dictionary of 1755 rested its authority on quotations from the best writers and drew those quotations from Shakespeare more than from any other single source. Johnson's Shakespeare is the standard of the language because Johnson needed a standard and chose the most quotable author available; two centuries of readers then met the playwright's lines as the very illustrations of what English words mean, and the association of the man with the language's ownership began in earnest. When the Oxford English Dictionary undertook the historical record a century later, its readers inherited both Johnson's canon and the habit of reaching for it, and Schäfer's analysis of the resulting documentation showed the consequence in figures: for the crucial decades the programme's coverage of Shakespeare ran far ahead of its coverage of the period's other print, so first citations gathered on his pages the way iron filings gather on the strongest magnet in the tray.

Charlotte Brewer's history of the Oxford dictionary's making adds the institutional detail: fascicle by fascicle, the editors worked under constraints of space, labour, and inherited slips, and the Shakespearean density of the quotation base was never a policy so much as an accumulation of conveniences, the famous author being the easiest to excerpt, the best edited, and the most likely to be sitting open on a reader's desk. The modern recounts, Crystal's among them, begin from that accumulated base and differ chiefly in how much of it they discount. The disagreement adjudicated earlier in this article stands, but its terms can now be sharpened. Coleman is right that the base is biased; Crystal is right that a residue of genuine novelty survives the discounting; and the editor's quartos remind both parties that the base is not even a stable text. What neither the bias nor the residue licenses is the popular figure, which survives because it is round, large, and attached to a beloved name.

There is a quieter consequence worth booking before the ledger opens. Because the dictionary's Shakespeare is partly an artifact, other writers of the decade are systematically under-credited, and the play's own minor speakers are under-credited within it: the kitchen English of the Nurse, preserved almost by accident, stands for a whole unrecorded stratum of the language that no reading programme could have captured even with the best will in the world. The ledger's rare-use column is where that stratum surfaces. Reading it with the mechanism in mind converts the article's scepticism into something more useful than debunking: a redistribution of credit, away from a solitary inventor and toward the language community, the printing houses, the excerpting readers, and the one writer whose ear and placement made his text the place where the community's words were best preserved.

Read whole, the ledger teaches its own lesson before any commentary touches it. The coinage column, strictly graded, is nearly empty. The compound column is full. The first-record and rare-use columns hold the famous items, and several rows, the racing phrase above all, are borrowings that popular lists have misfiled as inventions for generations. The distribution across the acts is also instructive: the rarities thicken in Juliet's summons to night, in the Friar's cell during the banishment crisis, in Capulet's rage, and in the tomb, which is to say they gather where feeling is at full stretch and ordinary diction is being asked to do extraordinary work. The sections after the ledger take the columns in turn: compounds first, since they are the play's true mint; then the extended senses and grammatical bendings; then the rare words and the footnotes they attract; then the speakers who spend them; then the question of why some survived.

A column-by-column reading sharpens the point. The first-record column is the column of accidents, its contents determined jointly by the writer's choices and by which papers happened to survive a century of fire, flood, and waste-paper recycling before the excerptors arrived. The rare-use column is the column of registers, its contents marking the boundaries of the trades and households the play visits. The new-sense column is the column of art, the only one whose contents can be credited to the text without remainder, since a sense stretched in a line is stretched whether or not any earlier pamphlet is ever found. And the compound column is the column of the language itself, the shared machinery running at full visibility. A reader who keeps the four columns distinct holds the whole argument of this article in one view: accidents, registers, art, and machinery, in proportions the myth reverses.

## The Compound Mint: Where the Play's New Words Are Actually Made

Compounding is the one word-making process in English that belongs to every speaker. Join two familiar parts by a living pattern and the result is understood at first hearing, which is why the compound column of the ledger is full and the coinage column is bare. The play's compounds are not inventions in the strong sense; they are exercises of a common liberty performed with uncommon aim. Juliet summoning the night performs the liberty three times in a dozen lines. The sun's horses are "fiery-footed" (3.2), their hooves carrying the fire of the element they draw. Night is asked to spread a "close curtain" as "love-performing night" (3.2), the office of the darkness welded into a single adjective. The same night arrives as a matron "sober-suited" (3.2), dressed by the compound, and a moment later as "loving, black-browed night" (3.2), the brow doing the work of a whole description of temper. Each formation is transparent. No listener in the first audience needed a gloss, and no reader since has stumbled over the sense, yet the density of the welding is the style: four compounds in one speech, each fitted to the pentameter like a stone to an arch.

The Prologue spends the same liberty at the level of fate. "Star-crossed" and "death-marked" (Prologue) are compounds of the marked-by family, and their transparency is their power: the audience receives the whole astrology and the whole ending in two welded adjectives before a character has spoken. In the tomb the liberty turns inward, when Romeo calls his flesh "world-wearied" (5.3) and imagines the self as a "sea-sick weary bark" (5.3) driven on the rocks, the compound in each case compressing a biography into a word. Capulet's rage compounds downward into insult, "green-sickness carrion" and "tallow-face" (3.5), where the welding is contemptuous: a daughter priced in the materials of the sickroom and the chandler's shop. Even the wit-duel compounds for sport, when Romeo caps the jesting with "single-soled" (2.4), a shoemaker's measure of thin leather converted into a scoring system for jokes. The pattern across all five sites is constant. When the play needs a word that does not quite exist, it does not import one and does not manufacture one from foreign parts. It welds two words that do.

One family of the welds deserves a note of its own, the fate compounds built on the pattern of a power plus a passive participle: star-crossed, death-marked, and their cousins across the canon. The pattern is the grammar of the play's astrology compressed into morphology, since the first element names the agent and the participle names what the agent has done to the person, and the person himself disappears from the word entirely, present only as the surface on which the crossing or the marking happened. That is why such compounds cluster at the play's thresholds, in the Prologue that assigns the lovers to their stars before they speak, and why they thin out as the action proceeds: the later acts are busy showing the agents at work in words of other kinds. The compound family is thus a small map of the tragedy's determinism, filed in the ledger under first records, where the honest grading puts it, rather than under inventions, where the myth would frame it.

### Why are hyphenated compounds so easy to understand at first hearing?

Because the parts arrive already known and the pattern of joining is one the language uses everywhere. A listener who has never met fiery-footed knows feet, knows fire, and knows what a hyphenated adjective of that shape describes. The novelty is in the pairing, not in the materials, so comprehension is instant.

The pleasure is in the fit rather than in decoding. This is also why the compound evidence cuts against the invention myth from inside. A coiner of words, in the popular picture, is a smith at a private forge, striking forms no one has seen. The play's actual practice is the opposite: public materials, public rules, private aim. The distinction matters for how credit is assigned. Shakespeare did not need to invent English's compounding habit, which descends from the oldest stratum of the language and was exercised by every pamphleteer and preacher of the decade. What the play does is aim the habit at exactly the objects that need it: fate in the Prologue, desire in the summons, self-disgust in the tomb, contempt in the quarrel, sport in the duel. Judged as compounding, the work is superb and the claim is modest, and the modesty is not a demotion. It is the difference between crediting a writer with the language and crediting him with its use, and only the second credit survives the evidence.

A word on the hyphens themselves, since editors mediate here too. The early quartos print such formations inconsistently, sometimes joined, sometimes spaced, sometimes hyphenated, and modern editions regularise according to house practice. Weis's text, followed here, prints the welded forms where the sense demands a single adjective, but the underlying fact is that compounding in the period was a gradient, not a switch: two words could lean together for one line and part again in the next. The ledger's compound rows therefore grade the formation, not the punctuation. Where a popular list claims a hyphen as a birth certificate, the honest claim is narrower and stronger: the pairing, in that place, doing that work, is first found here or found here at its most memorable.

## Old Words, New Work: Sense Extension and the Functional Shift

If compounding is the play's mint, sense extension is its bank: old capital made to yield new income. The ledger's most instructive rows are the ones where nothing about the word's shape is new and everything about its employment is. "Civil" is the compact example. The Prologue's line "civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue) sets the word's civic sense, citizens of one city, against its courteous sense, the civility that bloodshed cancels, and lets a third sense, war among citizens, gather behind both. When Juliet later summons "civil night" (3.2), the courteous sense stands alone and serene, a darkness asked to behave with decorum. One word, three poles of meaning, and the play's whole politics of the feud is latent in the spread. No coinage was required; the extension of an old term across the play's own scenes did the work, and the work is greater than most inventions achieve.

The Friar's "philosophy" is the doctrinal example. Philosophy in the period named a field of study, and in the cell it is prescribed like a drug: "Adversity's sweet milk, philosophy, / To comfort thee" (3.3), an armour offered against a word. The sense on offer is philosophy as a practiced discipline of consolation, nearly the ancient sense of philosophy as medicine for the soul, revived and narrowed to a single use: a technique a desperate man can be taught in an afternoon and fail to practice by nightfall. Romeo's answer measures the extension by refusing it, since exile experienced is not exile argued. The word's new employment fails inside the fiction and succeeds outside it, giving audiences a portable term for the gap between knowing a consolation and being consoled.

Grammatical bending, the functional shift, is the boldest form of the same thrift. Romeo's tirade in the cell handles "banished" as a physical object: the word is repeated, weighed, cursed, and begged to be renamed, "death" offered as the honest label the sentence hides (3.3). A participle has become a noun, the name of a thing that can be carried, and the shift enacts the scene's psychology, in which a word is experienced as the blow itself. Capulet, raging, bends grammar the other way: "Thank me no thankings, nor proud me no prouds" (3.5) verbs an adjective and plurals a courtesy in a single line, the syntax of refusal improvised under temper. These are the moments the invention myth is reaching for when it credits the playwright with making English, and they deserve the credit, correctly assigned: not new words, but new permissions, demonstrations that the grammar itself could flex where feeling required it. Even the tomb's "paramour" (5.3), a word centuries old, is extended by reassignment, the lover's title handed to Death, who keeps Juliet "in dark" as a mistress is kept; the horror is an old word in a new household.

### How can an old word gain a new sense without changing its shape?

By being used, in a strong context, at the edge of its established meaning, so that hearers stretch the meaning to fit the occasion. The context does the teaching: a term for study placed beside milk and armour is heard as a discipline of comfort, and a participle handled like an object is heard as a thing.

Repetition in famous lines then fixes the stretched sense where later speakers can find and reuse it. The lesson for the ledger is that the status column must have room for work as well as birth. A list that only asks when a word first appears will rank the racing slang and the legal rarity beside the genuine formations and miss the play's finest verbal acts entirely, since "civil" and "philosophy" and "banished" are unremarkable as records and remarkable as employments. The play's vocabulary earns its reputation in these rows, and the reputation survives the correction of the myth precisely because employment, unlike invention, can be verified from the text itself. Any reader can test whether "civil" carries three senses in the Prologue, whether the cell's consolation is a practice rather than a subject, whether the participle is handled as a thing. The evidence is on the page, in the lines, and it does not depend on the survival of any pamphlet or the diligence of any Victorian reader.

## One Word Under the Microscope: Engrossing

The method of this article can be watched at full magnification in a single item, the tomb's "engrossing" (5.3), which the ledger grades a rare use and which repays a slower reading. The word arrives in English from the phrase in gross, wholesale, by the great quantity, and by the 1590s it carried three live senses. In trade law, to engross was to buy up the whole stock of a commodity in order to resell at a forced price, an offence against the market that statutes and common law pursued alongside forestalling and regrating; the engrosser was a monopolist, a cornerer, a man who made scarcity for profit. In the law's other house, engrossing named the fair writing of a deed or instrument in a large formal hand, the clerk's craft of making a document final and unalterable. And in ordinary use the word was gathering its third sense, to absorb wholly, to take up the whole of a thing or a person, the sense in which later English would speak of an engrossing book or an engrossing grief.

Romeo's line spends all three senses at once and tells the audience so by its neighbours: "seal with a righteous kiss / A dateless bargain to engrossing death" (5.3). A bargain is struck, the language of the market; a seal is set, the language of the instrument; and Death is imagined as the engrosser in every register, the monopolist who has cornered the supply of Juliet and will resell nothing, the clerk whose fair hand makes the contract unalterable, the absorber who takes up the whole of two lives into his single keeping. One word, three professions, and the line's chill depends on all of them sounding together. This is what the ledger's modest grade, rare use, conceals and what the article exists to recover: the play's characteristic act is not the manufacture of a word but the convergence of a word's whole history in a single placement. No dictionary's first-citation column can represent that act, which is why an audit of vocabulary that never leaves the dictionary will always understate the play even while it overstates the inventions. The microscope also shows why the falsity of the coinage claim, item by item, is compatible with the truth of the larger claim readers have always wanted to make. Something extraordinary is happening to English in these lines. It is happening at the level of sense, placement, and pressure, and it can be shown, word by word, to anyone willing to look through the instrument.

The bargain's other adjective completes the instrument's lesson. "Dateless" (5.3) is the conveyancer's negative: a bargain with no date of expiry, no term after which the seal lapses, set against every earthly contract the play has shown, the marriage settlement Capulet kept redrafting, the banishment with its implied pardon, the Friar's potion with its forty-two hours. Romeo drafts a deed that cannot run out, in the vocabulary of the office that drafted deeds, and the rarity of both words is the signature of the register: the tomb speech is a legal instrument disguised as a love speech, and its two rarest words are its clauses. A coinage hunter files the pair as curiosities. The ledger files them as the play's method caught in the act, professional language conscripted to make a feeling irrevocable.

## The Verbing of English: Conversion and the Nonce Formation

The functional shift, met already in the cell and the quarrel, deserves a section of its own, because it is the process by which the play's boldest verbal events occur and the one the invention myth most consistently misdescribes. Conversion, the grammarians' term for using a word in a new word-class without changing its form, was English's cheapest growth mechanism in the period: any noun could be tried as a verb, any adjective as a noun, and the trial cost the hearer nothing, since the syntax announced the new employment before the sense had to be computed. The play converts constantly and casually. A participle becomes the name of a sentence in the cell. An adjective becomes a verb of defiance in Capulet's mouth. Thanks acquire a plural body. Each event is a small proof that the grammar, not the dictionary, was the period's real mint, and that a writer's originality could consist entirely in the timing of conversions every speaker was licensed to attempt.

At the far end of conversion sits the nonce formation, the form built for one occasion with no expectation of adoption, and the play owns a beauty: Mercutio, greeting the transformed Romeo of the wit-duel, cries "O flesh, flesh, how art thou fishified!" (2.4), welding the noun fish to the mock-learned suffix of Latinised making. Fishified is everything the myth wants a coinage to be: unattested before, irresistible in place, a whole argument about a lover dried into a herring compressed into one ridiculous word. It is also, by the ledger's discipline, the exception that grades the rule. Formations of its kind are rare in the play, rarer still in the ledger, because the play's genius runs the other way, toward the transparent compound and the stretched common word. The nonce word is a firework; the play prefers lamps. Noticing the preference is the difference between an audit and a celebration, and it is why fishified, the strongest single coinage candidate in the text, earns its section here rather than a headline: one swallow of pure invention does not make a summer of manufacture, and the honest account of the vocabulary is the account of the lamps.

## Borrowed Tongues: Imports, Learned Stock, and the Professions' Stores

A further column of the play's verbal wealth is neither native welding nor native stretching but import, and the imports matter to the audit because the invention myth depends on a picture of the playwright minting in a private English. The play's English is in fact cosmopolitan by theft, as literary English then was. "Paramour" (5.3) had travelled from the Old French phrase par amour, by love, worn down over centuries from an adverbial phrase into a noun for the person loved illicitly; the tomb speech inherits the whole journey and spends the arrival. "Perjury" arrives by the Latin road through French law and devotion, and Juliet spends it as a commonplace of the lover's religion: "At lovers' perjuries, they say, Jove laughs" (2.2). Even the ledger's Latinate adjectives, "inauspicious" and "unsubstantial" (5.3), are hybrid manufactures, English negative prefixes bolted onto Latin stems, a process the period's grammarians watched with the same mixture of pride and alarm that attended every enlargement of the tongue.

The most theatrical imports are Mercutio's, and they are aimed. His anatomy of Tybalt's fencing (2.4) is a litany of foreign terms, the passado, the punto reverso, the stoccata, the new Italianate science of the rapier set out in the fencing manuals of the decade, and the litany's foreignness is the characterisation: Tybalt is priced as an imported article, a duellist by the book, his vocabulary as studied as his footwork. The play thus uses imports the way it uses compounds, as instruments of judgement, and the distribution is the same: the terms concentrate where a speaker is being measured. Around these showpiece imports runs a quieter trade in the professions' stores, which the ledger samples across its rows: the law's demesnes and engrossing, medicine's green-sickness and canker, the falconer's hood and bating, the racecourse's wild-goose chase, the shoemaker's single sole, the seaman's bark. Each profession kept a vocabulary as a shop keeps tools, and the play's speakers keep breaking into the shops.

The period knew it was doing this and argued about it in print. The inkhorn controversy, defenders of plain English against the importers of learned terms, had its purists, who wished the language to grow only from native stock, and its pragmatists, Richard Mulcaster among them, who answered that English was enriched by every tongue it could plunder and that usage, not pedigree, naturalises a word. History ruled for Mulcaster, and the play is evidence for his side of the case: its most admired effects are achieved impartially by native welding, by sense-stretching, and by goods frankly imported, with no hierarchy among the three. A verdict on the vocabulary that honoured only the native processes would misdescribe the text. The playwright's distinction was never purity of mint. It was freedom of movement among all the stores the language kept, native and foreign, high and low, at a moment when the stores were unusually full.

## Rare Words and the Footnote: Why Parts of the Play Need a Glossary

Rarity is a different phenomenon from novelty, and the ledger keeps it in its own column for a reason. A rare word may be ancient, dialectal, technical, or simply unlucky in its paper trail. The Nurse's "ladybird" (1.3), her endearment for Juliet, sits near the head of the word's surviving record in any sense, which tells a reader almost nothing about how long nurses had been saying it in kitchens where no one wrote it down. Her "bump" (1.3), the swelling on the child's brow "as big as a young cockerel's stone," supplies the dictionaries with their first citation for the swelling sense, and again the kitchen caveat applies: toddlers fell in every house in England, and the word for the result cannot have waited for a play to be born. What the play provides is not the word's origin but its preservation, in a speech so circumstantial, the wormwood, the dove-house, the broken brow, that the term is fixed in its domestic habitat forever.

Technical rarity works differently, because technical terms have owners. Mercutio's conjuration spends "demesnes" (2.1), the lawyer's word for lands held in one's own possession, inside a catalogue of a mistress's body, and the rarity is the joke's seasoning: a term of conveyancing made to survey anatomy. The double senses at work in that catalogue belong to the bawdy gloss tradition, treated in its own study; the concern here is the record, and the record shows a playwright raiding a profession's cupboard for a word whose precision is itself comic. Juliet's summons raids another profession's stores. Around the compounds of 3.2 sits a small falconry cluster: night is asked to "hood" her "unmanned blood, bating in my cheeks" (3.2), where hood, unmanned, and bating are the falconer's terms for covering a hawk's eyes, for a hawk not yet trained to the lure, and for the flutter of a tethered bird straining to fly. A girl pricing her own desire reaches for the vocabulary of bird-training, and the rarity of the terms in a love speech is the measure of the speech's daring.

The strangest exhibit is "runaways" (3.2), in the same summons: night is to spread its curtain "that runaways' eyes may wink" while Romeo leaps to her arms. Editors have glossed the runaways as deserters from the day, as the stars fleeing before darkness, and as eyelids, and the disagreement is itself the evidence: the word's sense here is underdetermined by its other occurrences, too rare in the period to settle. Weis prints and glosses with a caution that amounts to an admission, and the admission is worth more than a confident note would be, because it marks the exact point where lexicography runs out and interpretation begins. Rare words, honestly handled, teach the limits of the record. They are the ledger's humility column.

### Why do the rarest words in a play this famous still need footnotes?

Because fame preserves the lines, not the world the lines were spoken into. The trades, sports, medicines, and household practices that supplied the play's rare terms have vanished or changed their names, so the words arrive without the experience that once explained them. A footnote rebuilds a fragment of that vanished world.

The rebuilding is specific: what a falconer did with a hood, what a lawyer meant by demesnes, what a nurse saw on a child's brow. Without it, the rarest passages read as decoration; with it, they read as precision.

The footnote has its own discipline, and the best editors practice it visibly. A gloss that names a sense and stops is a different instrument from a gloss that confesses a dispute, and Weis's handling of the hard words in this play, from the runaways of the summons to the legal flavour of "engrossing" (5.3), a term of monopoly patents and of drafting instruments, extended at the climax to death's "dateless bargain," shows the second instrument at work. "Dateless" itself (5.3) is the fitting emblem of the column: a bargain without date or term, sealed with a kiss, in a word that never entered common use however famous the line became. Rarity and fame, the ledger notes, are independent variables. A word can ride the most quoted speech in the language and still die, and a word can live for centuries on the strength of a single preservation. Which fate a given item met is the subject of the survival section below; which speakers spend the rarities, and under what pressure, comes first.

For most readers across the last two centuries, the footnote was also their first lexicography, met at school, in editions whose notes were written for the classroom and carried a classroom's virtues and limits. Such notes had to decide quickly, and on rare words they often decided by compression: a term became its nearest modern equivalent, a disputed sense became a settled one, and a first record became, in the margin's shorthand, an invention. The compression was understandable and its effects were long, since a gloss memorised at fourteen outlives the scholarship read at forty. The modern scholarly note, Weis's above all, is best understood as a reform of that inheritance: the same small space at the foot of the page, used to display the evidence instead of replacing it. A reader who compares a school gloss and an Arden gloss on the runaways of the summons is watching the whole history of the coinage question turn, in a single word, from assertion to audit.

## The Gloss as Genre: Four Centuries of Explaining the Play's Words

Between the first audiences, who needed no notes, and the modern reader, who is offered hundreds, stands the gloss, and the gloss has a history that bends the coinage question one more turn. The earliest editions of the play printed the words bare, trusting a shared world. The eighteenth-century editors, Lewis Theobald and William Warburton among them, began the annotation in earnest, and their work set the pattern that still governs: a hard word attracts a note, the note proposes a sense, and the proposal, repeated from edition to edition, hardens into the word's biography. Theobald's fame rests on emendation, the correction of passages where the transmitted text seemed corrupt, and the lesson of his career for the ledger is double-edged. Some of his corrections recovered sense that printing had mangled, proving that a strange form in the text may be a stranger's error. Others rewrote what was merely difficult, proving that editors, like myth-makers, prefer a text that confesses to one that resists.

The school editions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries industrialised the gloss and, in doing so, manufactured a share of the coinage myth at the chalk-face. A note had to be short; "a word coined by Shakespeare for this passage" is shorter than the truth and more memorable, and generations of pupils copied the formula into essays where it still circulates. The modern scholarly editions reversed the direction. Weis's Arden notes are models of the audited style: attestations are cited, parallels are weighed, and uncertainty is printed in the same size of type as knowledge, so that a reader can watch the evidence run out at exactly the place a school note would have invented a certainty. Levenson's Oxford notes practice the same discipline with a different emphasis, attending to what the words do in performance. The contrast between the two editorial centuries is the contrast of this whole article in miniature. Where the gloss asserts, the myth grows; where the gloss shows its working, the ledger becomes possible. The play's rare words have had both treatments for four hundred years, and a reader weighing any claim about them should ask first which century's manners the claim was written in.

## The Printing House Share: Quartos, Compositors, and the Editor's Hand

One share of the play's supposed verbal novelty belongs to neither the writer nor the language, but to transmission, and an honest ledger must book it. Romeo and Juliet survives in two early quartos of very different character: the short quarto of 1597, a compressed text that bibliographers since W. W. Greg have generally treated as reported or memorially reconstructed, and the fuller quarto of 1599, printed from copy far closer to the author's papers. A word attested in the fuller text alone may be the author's; a word attested in the shorter text alone may be an actor's memory, a reporter's paraphrase, or a compositor's guess. The ledger's first records are therefore records of particular documents, and the documents differ in authority, which is one more reason the status column grades evidence rather than origins.

Spelling widens the uncertainty. English spelling in the 1590s was a practice, not a system: the same word could appear in two or three shapes within a single page, compositors followed their own habits, and a dictionary counting forms across such texts inherits ghosts, words that look rare because they are one spelling of a common item caught in an unusual dress. Modernised editions, Weis's among them, quietly heal most of this, regularising spelling so the reader meets the word and not the accident. The healing is a service, but it changes the evidence base: discussions of the play's verbal novelty conducted on old-spelling texts and on modernised texts are not counting the same objects, and claims migrate between the two without noticing. Hyphenation is the smallest case of the same condition. Compostors hyphenated by eye and by line-length, so a compound's welded look in a quarto may record a justification decision in the printing house, and an editor who regularises the hyphens is making, item by item, the very judgement the ledger exists to display: whether two words are leaning together for a line or have fused into a formation.

Even the capital letters conspire. Early texts capitalised nouns freely, and the personified Death who stalks the tomb speeches owes part of his typographic stature to that freedom, a share an editor must apportion when a modern text decides which abstractions to dress as persons. The point for the audit is not that the text is untrustworthy; the fuller quarto is a good witness, and Weis's edition rests on it. The point is that novelty is a property a form can acquire in the printing house as well as in the study, and any census of the play's verbal inventions that fails to deduct the printing house share overstates the author by exactly the amount the compositors contributed. The ledger's caution, grade the evidence and name the witness, is the only stable position in a territory where the ground itself was set in type by other hands.

## Who Speaks the Rare Words: Rarity Follows Pressure

Lay the ledger over the dramatis personae and a pattern emerges that no list of inventions ever shows: the rare and newly formed words are not distributed evenly across the cast, and they are not distributed by rank or by learning. They cluster by pressure. Mercutio, dying and jesting, is the play's great raider of registers. His wit-duel runs on the doubleness of "counterfeit" (2.4) and scores itself with "single-soled" (2.4); his conjuration spends the lawyer's "demesnes" (2.1); his vocabulary reaches the racecourse for the "wild-goose chase" (2.4) and the fencing school for its arithmetic. His advice to a lover is built from the same stores: "If love be rough with you, be rough with love" (1.4) is a proverb bent in the speaking, and his invitation "we must have you dance" (1.4) sets the social surface his rarities keep breaking. The mechanics of his punning belong to the study of the quibble; what the ledger records is the range of his thefts, sport, law, shoemaking, and stable-yard carried into a gentleman's mouth, where the rarity of each term certifies the speed of the mind spending it.

Juliet spends her rarities in a single direction: upward, into summons and prayer. The compounds of 3.2 are hers, fiery-footed, love-performing, sober-suited, black-browed, and so are the falconry terms and the disputed runaways, a whole technical register conscripted to describe a bride's impatience in the decorous form of an address to night. No other speaker in the play is granted such a concentration of verbal novelty in so few lines, and the concentration is characterisation: the sheltered girl has the play's boldest idiolect precisely because her feeling has no public language and must build one from compounds, trades, and prayer. Capulet's rarities sit at the opposite pole of age and power. His are the compounds of contempt, green-sickness and tallow-face, and the grammatical violence of "proud me no prouds" (3.5), the mint of a man accustomed to being obeyed, whose inventions are all acts of pricing: a daughter valued in the materials of sickness and cheap fat. The Friar's rarity is doctrinal, canker and philosophy, the garden and the schoolroom carried into counsel. His first speech is the herbal register at full stretch, "baleful weeds and precious-juiced flowers" (2.3) growing in one sentence, the compound precious-juiced doing in the garden what Juliet's compounds do in the summons, while "the infant rind of this weak flower" (2.3) prices a plant in the vocabulary of childhood. The Friar is the one speaker whose rarities come from books and practice equally, a professional whose trade terms are his thinking, and the ledger marks the difference: where Mercutio raids registers for display and Juliet for need, the Friar's technical diction is simply his native tongue, which is why his failures in the play are never failures of vocabulary. The Nurse's is domestic, bump and ladybird and the idiom of the man of wax, the words of the nursery preserved nowhere else because nurseries kept no records. Her self-description, "I am the drudge and toil in your delights" (2.5), is ordinary diction, and the ledger's point about her is the reverse of invention: her speech is where the archive's blindness is most visible, since a whole register of women's household English survives chiefly because a playwright wrote down a nurse.

Romeo's rarities are saved for the end. For four acts his diction runs on the common stock of Petrarchan love, the ready-made figures that the study of [metaphor and the lovers' shared vocabulary](/2025/02/21/metaphor-lovers-vocabulary-analysis/) traces as they pass between the lovers and are remade in the passing. At the tomb his register changes: inauspicious, unsubstantial, engrossing, dateless, world-wearied, sea-sick, a run of legal, commercial, and welded terms spent over a supposed corpse. The pattern completes the play's law of verbal pressure. Where a speaker is merely performing a received feeling, the diction is common. Where feeling outruns every received form, in summons, in rage, in dying, in grief, the diction welds, raids, bends, and rarifies. The vocabulary's novelty is therefore an index of sincerity inside the fiction, which is a finer thing for a play to achieve than a long list of first citations, and it is verifiable scene by scene.

Paris is the control case that proves the law from the negative side. The County's diction never leaves the courtly common stock: his suit is conducted in the formulas of compliment, his grief at the tomb in the formulas of strewing and weeping, flowers, night, and tears in their received arrangements. He is not a fool, and the play does not mock his language; it simply never lets his feeling outrun the forms supplied for it, and so never lets him weld, raid, or bend. He dies speaking correctly. Set beside Juliet's summons or Romeo's tomb speech, the correctness is the tragedy's quiet judgement on a whole way of loving: the received language, perfectly kept, is the one instrument in the play that never tells the truth at full pressure. The ledger has no Paris rows, and the absence is among its most eloquent entries.

Against these spenders the play sets its official voices, and the contrast sharpens the law. The Prince speaks the diction of edict and sentence, common words in their public senses, and his authority depends on the plainness: law cannot afford rarities, since a proclamation misunderstood is a proclamation failed. Lady Capulet, negotiating, reaches for a single sustained figure, Paris as a book to be read, a volume that "only lacks a cover" (1.3), and her one excursion into technical vocabulary, the binder's and the glossator's terms, is the measure of a mind conducting a transaction in the language of property. The Chorus, framing all, spends the Prologue's compounds and then falls silent, as if the official voice had exhausted its novelty in the doorway and left the rarities to the lovers and their world. Rarity, in short, belongs to feeling and to craft inside the fiction, and plainness to power, which is the tragedy's linguistic politics in one distribution.

## The Common Stock at the Centre: What the Play Refuses to Coin

The distribution has a second half, and it may be the most telling finding in the audit. At the emotional centre of the play, where feeling is greatest and the stakes are mortal, the diction is not rare at all. The most famous lines in the tragedy are built from the commonest words in the language. Romeo's self-verdict in the square, "I am fortune's fool" (3.1), spends four ordinary words and a possessive. Juliet's discovery at the feast, "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5), is a sentence a child could parse, its power carried entirely by the chiasmus of only and only, love and hate. The parting is conducted in the nursery's own monosyllables, "Good night, good night" (2.2), repeated until repetition itself becomes the meaning, and the last act closes on the plainest sentence in the play: "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3), six words, none of them remarkable, every one of them permanent.

The pattern inverts the myth's expectation. A coiner, in the popular picture, would spend the inventions at the climaxes, where the audience is listening hardest. The play spends the common stock at the climaxes and the rarities at the thresholds: in summonses that prepare a meeting, in rages that prepare a breach, in curses, conjurations, and consolations that surround the central acts of love and death without being those acts themselves. The reason is artistic and exact. Novelty draws attention to the word; the climaxes require attention on the thing, and so the diction there effaces itself into transparency, letting love, hate, night, kiss, and die carry the scene without a single strange term competing for the ear. The rarities are the play's frame and weather. The common stock is its events. Any account of the vocabulary that totals the frame and ignores the events has mistaken the play's decoration for its substance, which is the final and most consequential error the ledger exists to correct.

## Survival and Loss: Why Some of These Words Lived

The ledger's last question is actuarial: which items entered the language's permanent stock, and what decided the matter? The clearest survivor is the Prologue's compound. "Star-crossed" escaped the play and now serves any doomed enterprise, its astrology forgotten, its fatalism intact. The phrase's career as a fate-formula is traced in the study of the lovers' epithet; the lexicographical point is narrower. A compound survives when it names something the language had no compact name for and when the vehicle carrying it is quoted without pause for four centuries. The Prologue is the most excerpted fourteen lines in English drama, and its adjectives rode the excerpting. Survival, in other words, is partly a property of the word and partly a property of the fame of its first famous home, and the dictionary's habit of citing the home then completes the circle by calling the survivor a coinage.

The control group is the tomb. "Dateless" and "sober-suited" are as well formed as "star-crossed," as transparent, as memorable in place, and neither entered common use. Their lines are famous; the words stayed rare. The difference is need. English had work for a compact adjective meaning thwarted by fate and no equally compact rival; it had less work for a bargain without expiry outside the one speech, and none for a matron's sober dress as a description of night. Words live by employment, and employment is granted by later speakers solving their own problems, not by the merit of the formation. The same law explains the racing phrase: "wild-goose chase" survived because the thing it names, a futile pursuit in another's wake, recurs in every generation's experience, and it survived as a phrase rather than a word, which is how most of the play's genuine bequests to English actually travel. The bequest list, honestly drawn, is headed by phrases and figures, the star-crossed lovers, the wild-goose chase, the parting's sweet sorrow, and only after them by single words.

There is a corollary the popular lists never print. For every item in the ledger that later citation made famous, the play contains formations that editors cannot even stabilise, the runaways of the summons chief among them, where the record is too thin for certainty about what word, in what sense, the first audiences heard. Textual criticism and lexicography meet at that point: Weis and Levenson both flag the crux rather than resolve it, and the flag is the honest artifact. A language's history written only from its survivors flatters the survivors' maker. Written from the ledger whole, survivors and casualties together, the history credits the play with something truer and more useful: a demonstration, at unusual density, of how English of the 1590s actually grew, by welding, borrowing, stretching, and bending, in the mouths of speakers under pressure, some of whose words the future kept and most of whose words it let fall.

The survival machine has one more gear, the schoolroom. Anthologies for young readers excerpt the same dozen speeches in generation after generation, the Prologue, the balcony, the summons, the tomb, and a word that lives inside those speeches is re-encountered by every schoolchild in the language, while a word of equal merit in an unexcerpted scene waits for the readers of whole plays, a smaller company in every age. The ledger's map of rarities is therefore also a map of the excerpting habit, and the two maps explain each other: the summons and the tomb are dense with rarities partly because their speakers are at full stretch, and the rarities there are famous partly because the speeches are anthologised. Fame and density chase each other down the centuries, and the dictionary, arriving late with its citations, records the position of the chase and calls it a birthplace.

## The Casualty Column: Formations That Never Travelled

An audit that studies only survivors will always acquit itself too easily, so the ledger's casualties deserve their own post-mortems. "Single-soled" (2.4) died with its occasion: the jest's scoring system required the shoe trade's measures and the duel's conventions in the same head, and when both faded the compound lost the world that made it legible, surviving only inside its scene like an insect in amber. "Sober-suited" (3.2) died of unemployment: night's matron costume fitted one speech exactly and no later need arose that the word answered better than its plainer rivals. "Tallow-face" (3.5) died with the material economy that priced faces: tallow was the cheap fat of common candles against the wax of the rich, and a culture that stopped burning tallow stopped hearing the insult's arithmetic. "Thankings" (3.5) was never a candidate for travel, a one-line grammatical event rather than a form seeking adoption, and its staying home is no failure at all. "Dateless" (5.3) achieved the strangest afterlife of the set, immortality as quotation without currency as a word: the bargain is on every anthology page, and the adjective seals it there, used by no one. Its neighbour in the same speech, "unsubstantial" (5.3), shares the fate and sharpens its irony: Romeo addresses Death as unsubstantial in the act of granting him appetites, a keeper of brides in the dark, and the paradox is the speech's engine, yet the adjective itself never naturalised, remaining a citation rather than a possession. Two of the most scrutinised lines in English drama thus carry two words the language declined to adopt, which should end any lingering idea that proximity to greatness is a word's guarantee. The language keeps what it can use, and it is no judge of scenes.

The pattern across the casualties is the control group the survival argument needed. Formation quality is roughly constant across the ledger; fate varies wildly; therefore fate is not explained by formation. What explains it is need meeting quotation: an item travelled when later speakers had recurring work for it and a famous vehicle kept it in circulation while the work was found. The conclusion disciplines the credit assigned to the survivors. If the same hand, in the same play, made "star-crossed" and "sober-suited," and the world kept one and dropped the other, then survival cannot be the measure of the making, and a criticism that counts a writer's contribution by his words' survival is measuring the world's needs and calling them the writer's genius. The ledger's fuller picture, survivors and casualties filed together, gives the truer measure: a maker whose formations were uniformly strong, of which the language, for its own reasons, adopted a selection. The casualties are therefore not the play's failures but its evidence, the control group without which the survivors' fame would prove nothing at all about the hand that formed both the kept and the unkept alike.

## Among the Rivals: The Decade's Common Practice

The final control on the verdict is comparative, and it is the control the myth never runs. Set the play's ledger beside the output of its decade and the profile stops looking singular. Thomas Nashe's pamphlets of the same years coin, compound, convert, and raid with a freedom that matches or exceeds anything in the play, and Nashe worked in prose pamphlets, precisely the ephemeral print the dictionary's programme under-read, so his first records understate his novelty by the same mechanism that overstates the playwright's. Christopher Marlowe's plays, a few years earlier, show the same density of classical import and welded epithet. Ben Jonson, a few years later, would build comedies whose dialogue is a museum of trade vocabularies, cant, and learned coinage, and would theorise the practice in his criticism. The 1590s were a decade of verbal expansion across every genre that used the language ambitiously, driven by print's growth, by translation, by the professions' rising literatures, and by a grammar in a phase of unusual flexibility.

What distinguishes the play within that company is therefore not process but preservation and placement, and the distinction can be stated as the audit's closing comparative finding. Nashe's coinages died with his pamphlets because the pamphlets died; Marlowe's imports lived chiefly where later writers quoted the plays; the play under audit entered a repertory, then a print tradition, then a school tradition, and its words were carried by all three conveyors without interruption. Had the survival of texts been distributed differently, the dictionary's first citations would wear different names, and the myth would belong to another author. This is not an argument that the play is ordinary. It is the argument that its extraordinariness is of the kind that survives auditing: density, aim, and an unmatched vehicle. A criticism that needs the playwright to have manufactured the language will always be disappointed by the evidence. A criticism that asks what the language, at its most expansive moment, could be made to do in a single play will find the answer here, and the answer does not shrink on inspection.

## Talking About Coinage Responsibly: The Verdict on the Vocabulary

The verdict can now be stated in full. Romeo and Juliet did not stock English with invented words. It did something better evidenced and, for a reader, better worth studying: it subjected the existing resources of English, its compounding rule, its stock of technical registers, its convertible grammar, to a pressure test across five days of fictional time, and the record of that test, preserved in a text the culture never stopped reading, fixed dozens of forms and senses in the written language. The cliche says the playwright invented the language. The ledger restores the careful truth: he is among its greatest recorded users at the moment of its fastest growth, and the play is a prime exhibit because its verbal boldness can be checked line by line, which the myth's claims cannot.

Responsible talk about all this has rules, and they are simple enough to state. Say first recorded where the evidence is a first record, and name the dictionary whose record it is. Date claims to texts, not to intentions: the play of the 1599 quarto, not the mind of its author, is what the evidence describes. Expect antedatings, and treat every coinage label as provisional, a report on the archive at a moment, not a fact of nature. Distinguish the careers of words from the careers of phrases, since the play's durable bequests are mostly phrases. And keep the writer's genuine distinctions in view while the myth's are surrendered: density of first records, freedom of compounding, range of registers raided, grammatical flexibility under pressure, and a placement of rare diction so exact that the rarities function as a map of the tragedy's stresses.

The same audit, applied across the canon, returns the same distribution, which is the last piece of evidence the verdict needs. Play after play, the popular lists dissolve under grading into compounds, phrases, first records, and stretched senses, with outright inventions scarce everywhere and the phrases carrying the durable fame: the pattern is not an accident of this text but the signature of a practice. That consistency is why a single play can stand as the audit's specimen for the whole question. Romeo and Juliet is not the canon's most inventive text by any count; it is among its most read, and the reading is what converted its records into claims. A less loved play with the same ledger would have generated no myth, which proves the myth's true subject: not the words, but the fame that preserved them and the archive that fame attracted.

The rules can be tested against the three claims about this play that circulate most widely, and the regrading is quick. The claim that the playwright invented "star-crossed" regrades to a first record of a transparent compound, a distinction of preservation. The claim that he invented the "wild-goose chase" regrades to a borrowing, a racing term spent in a wit-duel, with the play as its preserver and populariser rather than its source. The claim that the play contains hundreds of his coinages regrades to the finding that the play contains some thousands of words, among them a handful of formations that may be his, dozens of first records, scores of rarities and extensions, and a density of verbal work that no tally of inventions was ever needed to establish. Each regraded claim is less thrilling than its original and more interesting, because each points at a mechanism a reader can verify, and verification, unlike thrill, compounds.

### Which verbs should replace invented in careful writing about the play's words?

Recorded, attested, preserved, and extended are the working verbs, with given currency for the play's role in fixing a form in print. Each verb names exactly the act the evidence shows: a dictionary recorded the word here first, the text preserves it, the line extends its sense, the passage's fame gave it currency.

Invented names an act no archive can witness, and careful writing retires it except where a formation's novelty is the argued conclusion rather than the opening assumption. The balcony scene, of all places, supplies the play's own warning against word-worship, when Juliet asks what a name holds and proposes that a rose by any other name would smell as sweet. The question behind [the balcony's inquiry into what a name contains](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/) is the lexicographical question turned inward: the lovers are destroyed in part by names, Montague and Capulet, that carry no substance beyond their records, while the words the play is praised for inventing turn out to carry records rather than inventions. The parallel is exact enough to end on. A culture that credits a writer with manufacturing its language is making Juliet's error in reverse, mistaking the record for the thing. The thing itself, the play's verbal life, needs no manufacture to be extraordinary.

## The Sound Test: Coinages and Rare Words Read Aloud

A final test is available to any reader, and it is the oldest: the ear. The play's formations were built for voices in a yard, and their design assumes hearing rather than inspection. Compounds land in a single stress-pattern, fiery-footed, sober-suited, black-browed, each a small rhythm that a tongue can fire without rehearsal, while the extended senses announce themselves by friction, the lawyer's demesnes in a lover's conjuration, the merchant's engrossing at a deathbed, the falconer's hood and bating in a bride's prayer. That friction is audible before it is understood, which is why the rarities cluster at the play's pressure points: a strange word in the mouth is an event, and the play spends its events where the feeling is.

The exercise of voicing the verse is governed by its own discipline, and [the guide to reading the play aloud, with meter as a performer's instrument](/2025/08/22/reading-aloud-meter-performance/), sets out how the line's measure carries an actor through an unfamiliar word without breaking stride. The discipline matters to the ledger because so many of its items are metrical citizens: the welded adjective fills a foot exactly, the verbed adjective in Capulet's line keeps the refusal inside one breath, and the tomb's heavy compounds slow the closing lines to the pace of a man lifting a body. How the pentameter itself is built, and why its regularity can host such variety, is the subject of [the account of blank verse in the play and how the measure works](/2024/12/06/blank-verse-romeo-and-juliet/); the present point is only that the coinages, so called, were engineered as sound before they were collected as words, and that any audit of them which ignores the engineering mistakes specimens for the life they were taken from.

Actors feel the ledger in the mouth before scholars see it on the page. A welded compound gives the tongue a single gesture to make, one stress-peak to strike, so the line's energy gathers instead of scattering across a paraphrase. A raided technical term, demesnes or engrossing, lands with a small deliberate clunk, the sound of a foreign object in the sentence, and a good production lets the clunk be heard rather than smoothing it into the surrounding music, because the clunk is the meaning: a mind reaching past its ordinary stock. Even the Nurse's domestic rarities work this way in performance, her circumstantial memories, the wormwood and the dove-house and the broken brow, building a verbal habitat in which a word like bump needs no footnote because the scene around it is the gloss. The stage, in other words, still performs the audit nightly: items that were made for voices survive in voices, and their rarity on the page is partly an artifact of print, which preserves the word and discards the world that explained it.

Heard aloud, the verdict of this article becomes intuitive. The play does not sound like a dictionary being written. It sounds like a language being used at full stretch by speakers who need more than it ordinarily gives them: a nurse whose kitchen words no one else bothered to preserve, a wit spending the vocabularies of four trades in a single afternoon, a girl welding adjectives in the dark because no existing word names what she feels, a father bending grammar because obedience has run out, a friar turning a garden weed and a school subject into doctrines, and a lover at a tomb reaching for the law's and the counting-house's hardest terms because the love-words have all been spent. That is the play's true lexical achievement, and unlike the myth, it can be heard.

The sound test has a final refinement, and it returns the audit to its beginning. The rarities are at their most audible in the mouths of single voices speaking alone or at full stretch, the summons, the rages, the tomb, while the play's choral and official moments, the Prologue excepted, keep to the common stock, and the Prince's closing sentence is as plain as any line in the text: "For never was a story of more woe" (5.3). A listener who tracks nothing else can hear the distribution with the naked ear, the strange words gathering where a single feeling is too large for its language and the plain words resuming command wherever the city speaks as a city. The vocabulary's map and the tragedy's map are the same map. That coincidence, demonstrable in performance any night of the year, is worth more than the whole apparatus of invention, and it is the finding with which the ledger closes: the play did not add a stock of words to English so much as it taught English, permanently and by example, where its words give out and what its speakers do then.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch coinage ledger, and how does it grade the play's notable words?

The InsightCrunch coinage ledger is the vetted list of the play's notable words and phrases set out in this article, each item graded by the state of its evidence. The grades are coinage for a formation with no earlier attestation, first record for the earliest citation the dictionaries print, rare use for a thin trail in the record, new sense or new grammatical use for old forms doing fresh work, and not his for borrowings misfiled as inventions. The grading is the argument: once the grades are separated, the play's genuine verbal achievements stand clear of the invention myth.

### Q: What is the difference between a word Shakespeare coined and a word first recorded in his work?

A coined word is one the writer formed that had no prior existence in the language. A word first recorded in his work is one for which the play happens to supply the earliest surviving written example a dictionary can find. The second fact is documented and checkable; the first is an inference that the documentation cannot support on its own, since speech, lost plays, and unprinted papers leave no trace. The distinction is the hinge of every responsible coinage claim: first records measure the archive's survival, while coinage asserts an origin the archive cannot witness.

### Q: Why do dictionaries credit Shakespeare with so many first uses if he did not invent the words?

Because the great dictionaries were built by readers excerpting the books they valued most, and no author was excerpted more thoroughly than Shakespeare. A word appearing in a much-read play was captured by the reading programme at once, while the same word in cheap print, in speech, or in a lost manuscript waited for a later capture or none. The result is a systematic bias: the canon supplies a disproportionate share of first citations, and the citations harden, in popular retelling, into a census of inventions that the underlying evidence never claimed to be.

### Q: How did the Oxford English Dictionary's reading programme inflate Shakespeare's share of first citations?

The programme recruited volunteers to read chosen books and transcribe quotations onto slips, and the chosen books skewed toward prestige literature. Jürgen Schäfer's study of the dictionary's documentation showed that for the decades around 1600 the excerpting leaned heavily on a narrow shelf of esteemed authors, with Shakespeare read more closely than any contemporary dramatist. Words current in trades, regions, and ephemeral print were under-sampled by comparison. The inflation is thus mechanical rather than fraudulent: the most-read author became the most-cited author, and first citations accumulated where the readers had been looking.

### Q: Why is death-marked a weaker coinage claim than it looks?

Because the formation belongs to a productive family. English of the period freely welded a noun to the participle marked, and death itself served as the first element of many compounds, so a writer producing death-marked was exercising a shared pattern rather than striking a private form. The compound is unattested earlier and the Prologue is its first record, which is a genuine distinction. But transparency cuts both ways: a formation any competent speaker could have produced on the same morning cannot bear the weight of the word invention without further evidence, and none survives.

### Q: If compounding was open to every writer of the period, why do this play's compounds feel distinctive?

The distinctiveness lies in aim and density rather than in the process. Other writers compounded; few compounded so exactly at the pressure points of a scene. The Prologue welds fate into two adjectives, Juliet's summons welds night into a costumed matron across four formations in a dozen lines, Capulet welds contempt into the materials of a sickroom, and the tomb welds a life's weariness into a single word for the flesh. Each compound is fitted to its object and its metre. The process was common property; the marksmanship is what readers are responding to when the formations feel invented.

### Q: What is a hapax legomenon, and why are a play's rarest words the hardest to judge?

A hapax legomenon is a word recorded only once in a body of text, and a play's rarest words approach that condition in the wider record: one or two attestations, often centuries apart. Such words resist judgement from both directions. A single occurrence cannot show whether the form was a daring novelty, a trade term, a dialect word, or a scribe's slip, and the absence of parallels removes the comparisons by which editors normally work. The honest treatment is the one the ledger gives the runaways of Juliet's summons: print the word, report the dispute, and let the status column confess the thinness of the trail.

### Q: Why does the word bump in the Nurse's speech matter to lexicographers?

Because the dictionaries take their first citation for the swelling sense of bump from that speech, where the Nurse remembers the toddler Juliet falling and raising a bump on her brow. The citation matters as a dated, contextualised specimen: the word is caught in its domestic habitat, with its cause and size described, which lets lexicographers fix the sense precisely. It does not show the word was invented for the play. Children had fallen in every household in England for centuries, and the household word for the result is unlikely to have waited for a playwright to give it birth.

### Q: What does Capulet's "proud me no prouds" show about how new uses are born?

It shows a new use born from grammar rather than from vocabulary. In the rage of Act 3 Scene 5, Capulet bends an adjective into a verb and gives a courtesy a plural body in a single breath: "Thank me no thankings, nor proud me no prouds." No new word has been manufactured; the existing stock has been re-deployed by the language's own rules of conversion, under the pressure of temper. Much of what popular lists call Shakespeare's coinages are events of this kind, demonstrations that the grammar could flex, and they are among the play's most repeatable lessons in verbal invention properly understood.

### Q: Why is inauspicious at the tomb graded only a first record and not a coinage?

Because the evidence reaches only as far as the record. Romeo shakes off "the yoke of inauspicious stars" (5.3), and the dictionaries' trail for the adjective begins at about this play in the written record. That makes the line the word's earliest surviving print home. It does not exclude earlier life in speech or in lost writing, and the formation itself, a Latin prefix on a Latin stem, was available to any educated writer of the decade. Grading the item a first record states the whole of what is known and resists the extra step that the evidence cannot pay for.

### Q: How do editors decide whether a strange word in the text is a coinage or a corruption?

They weigh the witnesses and the sense. The play survives in a short quarto of 1597 and a fuller one of 1599, and a strange form found in only one witness invites suspicion of the printing house, while a form in both earns presumption of authority. Editors then test the sense against the word's other occurrences in the period, against the speaker's habits, and against the metre. Where the trail runs out, as with the runaways of Act 3 Scene 2, the responsible edition prints the reading and flags the uncertainty, since a confident gloss on a corrupt or unique form manufactures evidence the record does not contain.

### Q: Why did wild-goose chase enter the language as a phrase while sober-suited stayed a rarity?

Because later speakers had recurring work for the one and not the other. The racing term named a recognisable experience, a futile pursuit following another's erratic course, and every generation found fresh occasions for it. Sober-suited named a costume for night inside a single speech of summons, and no later need arose that the compound exactly fitted. Survival is granted by employment, not by quality of formation: sober-suited is as well made as any item in the ledger. The contrast is the cleanest proof in the play that a word's fate is decided by its users after the playwright, not by its maker.

### Q: What happens to a coinage claim when an earlier example of the word turns up?

The claim collapses to a first record, and the item changes columns in the ledger. Antedating is a routine event in lexicography, and the digitising of early print has made it routine at speed: sermons, pamphlets, and account books keep surrendering earlier instances of words long credited to famous authors. A claim framed responsibly survives the demotion without embarrassment, since it asserted only what the record showed at the time. A claim framed as invention is destroyed by it, which is the practical argument for the ledger's discipline: grades that report the evidence can be revised; grades that assert origins can only be refuted.

### Q: Why does the play put so many of its rarest words in mouths under pressure?

Because rarity in the play is a function of need. Speakers performing a received feeling, Petrarchan longing, formal condolence, official judgement, manage on the common stock. Speakers whose feeling outruns every received form must build: Juliet welds compounds and raids the falconry manual, Capulet bends grammar in rage, the Friar turns a weed and a school subject into doctrine, Romeo at the tomb reaches for the hardest terms of law and trade. The distribution can be checked across the ledger, whose rarities gather in the summons, the cell, the quarrel, and the tomb, the four sites where ordinary diction is asked to do extraordinary work.

### Q: What is the inkhorn context, and why does it shrink Shakespeare's supposed originality?

Inkhorn terms were the learned borrowings, chiefly from Latin, that flooded English writing in the sixteenth century and provoked a famous controversy about whether the language was being enriched or corrupted. By the 1590s the flood was a condition of literary life: every ambitious writer worked in a language visibly expanding by import, conversion, and compounding. Placing the play in that context shrinks the supposed originality because the processes the myth calls invention were the period's common practice. What remains distinctive is not the use of the processes but the judgement with which this play aims them, which is a critical distinction rather than a lexicographical one.

### Q: Why do schoolbooks and quizzes keep repeating the invention myth after scholars have corrected it?

Because the myth is a better story than the correction and a simpler one to print. A number, seventeen hundred words invented by one man, fits a quiz card; the truth, that citation counts reflect a biased archive and that the genuine achievement is a pattern of compounding, extension, and placement, requires a paragraph. The myth also flatters a national story about a single genius refounding the language. Corrections travel slowly against such advantages, which is why the ledger method matters pedagogically: it gives a teacher something as concrete as the myth, a table that can be checked, in place of a slogan that cannot.

### Q: How should a reader treat a footnote that calls a word in this play a coinage?

As a claim about the record, pending evidence, rather than as a fact about origins. The useful questions are mechanical: which dictionary's first citation supports the note, whether the form appears in both early quartos, whether the item is a word, a sense, a compound, or a phrase, and whether earlier attestations have surfaced since the edition was prepared. A footnote that survives those questions has earned provisional trust. Most notes that use the word coined are compressing the first-record finding for space, and the compression is harmless once a reader knows what has been compressed.

### Q: Which speaker in the play stretches English furthest, and what does the stretching consist of?

Mercutio, and the stretching consists of raids rather than inventions. In a single afternoon of stage time he spends the vocabularies of the law courts in his conjuration, of the racecourse in his sporting slang, of the shoemaker's bench in his scoring of jests, and of the fencing school in his anatomy of Tybalt, bending each register into a gentleman's wit. His dying speech then turns the same mobility to curse and measurement. No other speaker commands so many cupboards, and the ledger's Mercutio rows, demesnes, wild-goose chase, single-soled, counterfeit, are all borrowings or formations on common stock, which is precisely the point: the play's most verbally extravagant figure manufactures almost nothing.

### Q: How can the coinage ledger be used in an essay about the play's language without repeating the myth?

By arguing from the grades rather than from the totals. An essay can take one column, the compounds, the extended senses, the grammatical bendings, and show how that column's items cluster by scene and speaker, which converts a vocabulary topic into an argument about the play's design. The ledger's status column supplies the essay's honesty: claims are pegged to first records and formations that any reader can verify in the text, and the invention myth, where it appears, is treated as a reception phenomenon to be explained rather than a premise to be borrowed. The strongest essays on this subject are audits, and the ledger is an audit's natural evidence.

### Q: What did Romeo and Juliet genuinely add to English, if invention is the wrong word for it?

It added durable phrases that still do daily work, the star-crossed lovers and the wild-goose chase among them; it fixed a set of first records in print, where later writers could find and reuse them; it extended the senses of old words, civil, philosophy, paramour, in lines too famous to ignore; it demonstrated, at a density few texts match, the compounding and converting powers of the language; and it preserved whole registers, the nursery's, the falconer's, the lawyer's, that the written record would otherwise have lost. Preservation, extension, and demonstration are the genuine additions, and they are not smaller than invention. They are how languages actually grow.
