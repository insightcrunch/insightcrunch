Three years before Jay Gatsby threw his first party, Sinclair Lewis published a novel about a middle-aged real estate broker in a midwestern city who owns an alarm clock he is proud of. Reading Gatsby vs Babbitt by Sinclair Lewis as a pair looks unpromising at first, since one book is a lyric tragedy about a criminal millionaire on Long Island and the other is a satire about a man who joins clubs. What the pairing produces is the fullest available picture of what the 1920s did to Americans with money, because the two books attack the same money culture from opposite ends of it and in opposite modes, and neither attack is complete without the other.

![Gatsby vs Babbitt by Sinclair Lewis comparing 1920s materialism and conformity in Fitzgerald and Lewis - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Lewis published Babbitt in 1922, and it entered the language almost immediately. To call someone a Babbitt is still to accuse them of a specific American failing: prosperous conformity, opinions borrowed from the club and the trade paper, a self assembled entirely from purchased goods and civic slogans. Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby in 1925 and gave the same decade its elegy. Between them the two novels cover the class range where the money actually sat, and they agree about what the money was doing to the people who had it. They disagree, sharply and productively, about whether the correct response is contempt or grief.

## Gatsby vs Babbitt by Sinclair Lewis: What the Pairing Compares

Accurate accounts of both books first, since most students meet Lewis only through this pairing.

The Great Gatsby follows a self-invented millionaire on Long Island whose illegal fortune exists to recover a woman he loved five years earlier. Its social world is inherited wealth on one shore and new money on the other, its narrator is a bond salesman from Minnesota who rents a small house next to a mansion, and its arc ends in three deaths and a funeral almost nobody attends.

Babbitt follows George F. Babbitt, forty-six, a real estate broker in the fictional midwestern city of Zenith, in the fictional state of Winnemac. He has a wife, three children, a house with modern fixtures, a car whose model signals his exact standing, and memberships in the Boosters' Club, the Athletic Club, and the Elks. He gives speeches about civic progress. He believes what the trade papers tell him to believe. Roughly two thirds of the way through, prompted by the imprisonment of his one real friend, Paul Riesling, he attempts a rebellion: an affair, a bohemian social set, and a flirtation with the liberal politics of a lawyer named Seneca Doane. Community pressure and his wife's sudden illness return him to conformity. The novel closes with his son eloping and Babbitt, who has never once done what he wanted, telling the boy to go ahead.

### What do The Great Gatsby and Babbitt have in common?

Both are 1920s novels arguing that American prosperity has hollowed out the people enjoying it. Each shows a man whose identity is assembled from purchased goods and borrowed social scripts, each treats money as a substitute for a self, and each ends with the central figure having gained nothing that mattered to him.

The shared subject is the manufactured self. Gatsby builds his from a name, a war record, an Oxford claim, a house, a car, a wardrobe, and a library of uncut books. Babbitt builds his from a mortgage, a club roster, a set of opinions he could not defend if questioned, and an inventory of appliances. Both men would be unable to describe themselves without listing possessions and affiliations. Both novels treat that inability as the central American condition of the decade, and both make it visible by showing what happens when the assembled self is put under pressure.

The second shared subject is emptiness experienced as vague dissatisfaction. Neither protagonist can name what is wrong. Babbitt wakes from a recurring dream of a fairy child who thinks he is gallant, then goes downstairs and argues about breakfast. Gatsby, who has everything the decade advertised, stands on his lawn at night reaching toward a light. The two images are more alike than their settings suggest: a man in possession of what he was told to want, reaching after something the possession did not supply.

The third is that both books are about the 1920s while the 1920s were happening. Neither is a retrospective. Lewis wrote inside the boom's opening years and Fitzgerald inside its middle, and neither had the benefit of knowing how the decade ended. That makes their agreement about the hollowness contemporaneous rather than hindsight, which is worth stating in an essay because it removes the suspicion that the critique was assembled after 1929.

## The Genuine Points of Contact

Five contacts hold up under pressure.

The first is the party as a social instrument that produces no connection. Fitzgerald's Chapter 3 is built from this observation. Guests arrive at a house where they were not invited, consume what they did not pay for, invent rumors about a host most have never met, and leave. The description that opens the chapter fixes the whole atmosphere in one image: "in his blue gardens men and girls came and went like moths." Jordan Baker later delivers the diagnosis as a joke: "I like large parties. They're so intimate." Lewis builds the same observation across the dinner party Babbitt gives, where the guests are chosen for standing, the conversation is a recitation of received opinion, and the host measures the evening's success by whether the correct people came. Neither writer needs a villain. The gathering itself is the evidence.

The second contact is the object as a proxy for the person. Fitzgerald's most famous version is the shirt scene in Chapter 5, where Gatsby throws imported shirts onto a table until Daisy cries into them, an emotional response to textiles that the novel presents without comment. Lewis works the same territory continuously and more dryly: Babbitt's relationship with his alarm clock, his car, his cigar lighter, and the fixtures of his bathroom is rendered with the tenderness a novelist usually reserves for people. In both books the goods carry the affect that the human relationships cannot.

### Do the two novels criticize the same money culture?

They criticize the same one from different heights. Lewis targets the middle-class version, where standing is signaled by appliances, club memberships, and correct opinions. Fitzgerald targets the elite version, where standing is inherited and money alone cannot buy entry. Both conclude that the culture leaves its beneficiaries hollow.

The third contact is the borrowed opinion. Babbitt's politics, his views on labor, immigration, prohibition, and civic improvement, are supplied by his associations and abandoned the moment the associations withdraw approval. Tom Buchanan performs an elite version of the identical operation in Chapter 1, where he recommends a book about the rise of the colored empires and delivers its argument as though it were his own thought. Fitzgerald's handling is briefer and colder, and Nick's silence in response is part of the judgment, but the mechanism is the same: a man repeating what he has read to establish that he belongs to the group that reads it.

The fourth contact is the friend who fails and what the failure exposes. Babbitt's rebellion begins when Paul Riesling, the one person he genuinely loves, shoots his wife and goes to prison. The event reveals that Babbitt has built a life with no capacity to absorb real trouble. Fitzgerald arranges an equivalent test in Chapter 9, when Gatsby dies and the hundreds who ate his food do not come to the funeral. Klipspringer telephones about tennis shoes. The party guests vanish. In both novels a crisis is used as an instrument to measure how much of the social world was real, and in both the answer is almost none.

The fifth contact is the wife or woman positioned as an accessory to standing. Myra Babbitt is described largely in terms of her role and her failure to be interesting, and Babbitt's inability to see her as a person is one of the things the novel is quietly savage about. Daisy Buchanan occupies a structurally similar position for Tom, who has a mistress in the ash country and a wife who confers status, and who becomes possessive only when the possession is threatened. Neither novelist is centrally interested in the interior lives of these women, which is a limitation worth naming, but both use the marriages to demonstrate that the men have converted every relationship into an asset.

## The Decisive Differences: Satire Against Elegy

Four differences change the argument.

The first is mode, and it governs everything else. Satire and tragedy are not two flavors of criticism; they are two contracts with the reader. Satire asks the reader to stand outside the target and judge, which requires that the target be at least slightly ridiculous. Tragedy asks the reader to stand with the target and grieve, which requires that the target be at least slightly admirable. Lewis makes Babbitt ridiculous, from the opening pages onward, and the ridicule is affectionate rather than cruel but never in doubt. Fitzgerald makes Gatsby admirable, and does it explicitly, through Nick's declaration that Gatsby possessed "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person." A reader cannot be asked to mock and mourn the same figure at once, and the two novels do not try.

The second difference is the protagonist's relationship to belief. Babbitt believes nothing, which is his condition. His opinions are rented, his enthusiasms are performed, and the brief rebellion collapses because there was never any conviction underneath it to sustain the pressure. Gatsby believes absolutely, which is his condition, and Fitzgerald traces the belief to a specific origin, describing the young man whose "heart was in a constant, turbulent riot" and whose reveries provided "a promise that the rock of the world was founded securely on a fairy's wing." One man is hollow because he has no faith; the other is destroyed because his faith exceeded every possible object. Both are ruined by the same money culture, but the mechanism of ruin is opposite.

### Why does Lewis mock what Fitzgerald mourns?

Because the two men lose different things. Babbitt never had a conviction to betray, so his surrender is comic and his life continues. Gatsby had one and pursued it to death, which makes his failure a loss rather than a joke. Mode follows from what is at stake.

The third difference is survival. This is more consequential than it looks. Gatsby dies; Babbitt does not. Lewis's ending has his protagonist alive, back inside the Boosters' Club, materially unharmed, and quietly aware that he has never done a single thing he wanted. That is a bleaker outcome in one respect, since the punishment for conformity is decades more of it. Fitzgerald's ending removes his protagonist from the system entirely and leaves the system running. An essay that notices this can make a real argument: satire cannot kill its target without becoming something else, so the satirist's only available sentence is continuation.

The fourth difference is the class vantage, which determines what each novel can see. Lewis sees the machinery of aspiration from inside the group performing it, so he can render the club, the speech, the trade paper, the sales technique, and the exact social meaning of a car model. Fitzgerald sees the machinery from a vantage where aspiration has either succeeded or is irrelevant, so he can render what inherited money does to the people who have never had to want anything, and what the barrier feels like to a man on the wrong side of it. Neither writer could have produced the other's material, and that is the argument for reading them together rather than choosing between them.

A fifth difference deserves a sentence. Lewis explains. His narration tells the reader what to think about Babbitt, frequently and without ambiguity. Fitzgerald routes everything through a compromised participant whose judgments are available for suspicion, which makes the reader work. The two techniques produce very different reading experiences from the same critique.

## What Each Novel Illuminates in the Other

Lewis illuminates the ordinariness of Fitzgerald's disease. Read alone, Gatsby can seem to describe an exotic condition affecting bootleggers and heiresses. Babbitt establishes that the same hollowing was operating on a real estate broker in a midwestern city who broke no laws and wanted nothing extravagant. Once the condition is shown to be general, the Long Island version stops looking like an aberration and starts looking like the visible extreme of something universal. That reframing strengthens any argument about [materialism and consumer culture in Gatsby](/2024/08/19/materialism-great-gatsby/), because it removes the escape route of treating Fitzgerald's characters as unrepresentative.

Lewis also illuminates the labor and commerce Fitzgerald deliberately omits. Nobody in East Egg appears to work. Nick's bond business exists chiefly to justify a commute. Gatsby's income is gestured at and never shown. Lewis fills the gap: Babbitt sells, negotiates, cheats slightly, attends conventions, and gives speeches, and the reader learns exactly how prosperity is generated in the decade Fitzgerald's characters are spending it. Reading the two together makes Fitzgerald's silence audible as a choice, and the choice is a class argument, since the world of the parties is sustained by work the novel declines to depict.

Running the other way, Fitzgerald illuminates what Lewis cannot reach, which is longing. Babbitt has appetites and a vague ache; he does not have a dream. Satire has no instrument for rendering a desire so large that its object cannot hold it, and Lewis does not attempt one. Fitzgerald builds an entire novel out of exactly that, and the crucial data point is the reunion in Chapter 5, where possession subtracts rather than satisfies: "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." Nothing in Babbitt performs that operation, and nothing in it needs to, but the pairing shows the reader where each mode's ceiling sits.

Fitzgerald also illuminates the barrier Lewis takes for granted. Babbitt's world is stratified, but nobody in it is trying to enter the old aristocracy, because Zenith has none. Fitzgerald's novel is organized around a boundary that money cannot cross, dramatized in Chapter 7 when Tom, who has done nothing to deserve his position, destroys Gatsby simply by naming where the money came from. The hollowness Lewis diagnoses is real; Fitzgerald adds that above it there is a class the hollow cannot join no matter how much they accumulate. That completes the picture of [the hollowness of the upper class](/2024/08/12/gatsby-hollow-upper-class/) as a structure rather than a mood.

Finally, Fitzgerald illuminates the cost of the culture in a way satire structurally softens. Babbitt survives. So does everyone in Zenith. In Fitzgerald, Myrtle Wilson is killed in the road, George Wilson kills himself after killing Gatsby, and Nick's verdict is that the Buchanans "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money." The money culture in Lewis wastes lives; the money culture in Fitzgerald ends them. Naming that escalation is a strong move in a conclusion.

## Conformity and Self-Invention as Two Answers to One Problem

Both novels ask what an American does with a self that has no inherited content, and they record two opposite answers.

Babbitt's answer is conformity. He solves the problem of who to be by consulting the group, and Lewis is precise about the consultation. Babbitt's opinions on prohibition, unions, immigration, and municipal spending arrive from the Athletic Club and the trade press, and he holds them with real warmth because they are the price of belonging. The novel's cruelty is in showing how little coercion is required. Nobody forces Babbitt to think anything. He wants to be inside, and the inside comes with a set of views attached.

Gatsby's answer is invention. Rather than consult a group, he manufactures a person and then makes the world accept him. Fitzgerald gives the mechanism in a single passage: "James Gatz that was really, or at least legally, his name," followed by the claim that Jay Gatsby "sprang from his platonic conception of himself," that "he was a son of God," and that his obligation was therefore to serve "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty." The last phrase is the one that keeps the passage from being romantic. Fitzgerald names the object of the invention as gaudy and false in the same breath in which he grants it religious weight.

Set the two answers side by side and a finding emerges that neither book states alone. Conformity and self-invention are not opposites. They are two strategies for the same predicament, which is that American identity has to be constructed from available materials because none is supplied at birth. Babbitt takes his materials from the group; Gatsby takes his from advertising, magazines, a schedule written in the back of a book, and a boy's idea of what a gentleman is. Both are assembling a person out of purchased and borrowed parts. The difference is only in the supplier.

The two answers also fail differently, and the difference is instructive. Conformity fails by attrition. Babbitt's self is stable, socially rewarded, and worthless to him, and the failure is a slow one he can survive for decades. Invention fails catastrophically, because an invented self depends on nobody producing the original. Tom Buchanan produces it in the Plaza suite in Chapter 7, naming the drugstores and the bootlegging, and Gatsby's construction collapses within an afternoon. Lewis's man is punished with continuation; Fitzgerald's is punished with exposure.

An essay can push this further. Notice that Babbitt's rebellion, when it comes, is also assembled from available materials: a bohemian set, an affair, a fashionable sympathy for a liberal lawyer. He does not invent a self even when he tries to escape; he shops for a different one. Gatsby by contrast invents with genuine originality, which is what makes him worth mourning and what makes Nick's admiration defensible. That distinction, between shopping for a self and building one, is a claim a student can defend from both texts and is unavailable from either alone.

## Advertising, Goods, and the Bought Self

The 1920s were the decade in which national advertising reached maturity, and both novels register the fact, though only one of them says so.

Lewis is explicit. Babbitt's beliefs about which products confer which standing are reported in detail, and the novel treats brand knowledge as a form of literacy. He can read a car, a house, a suit, and a bathroom fixture for exactly what it says about its owner, and this competence is presented as the main intellectual skill he possesses. Lewis is also explicit about the machinery, since Babbitt's own trade depends on persuasion, and his civic speeches are recognizably advertising copy applied to a city.

Fitzgerald never mentions advertising as an industry, but his novel is saturated with it. The one thing presiding over the valley of ashes is a commercial image: "the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are blue and gigantic," a billboard for an optometrist whose practice has vanished while the sign remains. George Wilson eventually mistakes it for God, which is Fitzgerald's most compressed statement about what has replaced belief in the decade. Gatsby's own self-presentation is advertising in the strict sense: a persona designed to produce a specific impression in a specific buyer.

Reading the two together yields a claim about the direction of influence. Lewis shows a man consuming advertising and being shaped by it. Fitzgerald shows a man who has become advertising, an image produced for an audience of one, complete with a house that functions as a display window and parties that function as circulation. Babbitt is the reader of the campaign; Gatsby is the campaign. That formulation is compact, defensible, and connects directly to the wider argument about [consumer culture and advertising in the 1920s](/2027/01/25/consumer-culture-1920s-gatsby/).

The goods themselves carry the same weight in both books. Fitzgerald's shirt scene is the most quoted instance, and its power comes from the mismatch between the trigger and the response, since Daisy weeps at fabric. Lewis achieves an equivalent effect with less compression and more repetition, giving Babbitt small ecstasies over appliances across dozens of pages until the pattern becomes unmistakable. The techniques differ exactly as the modes differ: Fitzgerald compresses to one unforgettable image, Lewis accumulates until the reader cannot dismiss the evidence.

There is a caution worth including in any essay. It is tempting to say both novels condemn consumption, which is too simple. Neither writer objects to goods. What both object to is the substitution of goods for the content of a life, and both are careful to show that the substitution is not chosen consciously by anyone. Babbitt did not decide to become an inventory; Gatsby did not decide that a house would stand in for a self. The culture supplied the substitution and both men accepted it, which is why neither novel offers a villain.

## The Two Rebellions and How They End

Each novel contains a revolt against the money culture, and comparing the two revolts is the most efficient route to the deciding factor.

Babbitt's revolt is late, conscious, and comprehensive. Prompted by Paul Riesling's imprisonment, he questions his work, his marriage, his politics, and his friendships more or less simultaneously. He takes up with Tanis Judique and her set, he defends a liberal lawyer during a strike, and he refuses for a time to join the Good Citizens' League. Lewis is careful to make the revolt unglamorous. The bohemian set is tedious, the affair is unsatisfying, and the political sympathy is more social than principled. When the pressure comes, through business losses and social exclusion and his wife's sudden illness, Babbitt returns, and the return costs him nothing except the knowledge of what he did not do.

Gatsby's revolt is earlier, unconscious, and total. He does not question the money culture; he attempts to beat it at its own game, accumulating faster and displaying harder than the people who were born into it. His revolt is against a specific verdict, delivered in Louisville in 1917, that a poor officer could not have this woman. Everything since has been an appeal against that ruling. Fitzgerald calls the commitment religious, noting that Gatsby "had committed himself to the following of a grail," and the vocabulary is not decoration but classification.

The endings measure the two revolts precisely. Babbitt's fails and he lives, which means the culture absorbs the revolt without effort and continues. Gatsby's fails and he dies, which means the culture defends its boundary with force when the challenge is real. An essay can convert this into an argument: the money culture tolerates dissatisfaction and punishes ambition. Babbitt was never a threat, because a man who is merely unhappy changes nothing. Gatsby was a threat, because a man who accumulates enough to buy his way into an inherited class is testing whether the class is real, and Tom Buchanan's response in the Plaza is the class answering.

There is a final note in each book that repays comparison. Lewis gives Babbitt a redemptive last gesture, telling his son he has never done a single thing he wanted and to go ahead and do what he likes. It is small, private, and the only free act in the novel. Fitzgerald gives Nick a comparable moment in Chapter 8, when he calls across the lawn, "They're a rotten crowd," and adds, "You're worth the whole damn bunch put together." Nick later says it was the only compliment he ever paid Gatsby, since he disapproved of him from beginning to end. Both novels reserve their one clean human act for a moment when nothing can be done, which is as close as either comes to hope.

## Narration: An Explaining Voice Against a Compromised Witness

The formal difference between the two books is not decoration, and it is where a comparative essay can distinguish itself quickly, because almost nobody develops it.

Lewis narrates from outside his protagonist with a voice that knows more than Babbitt and tells the reader so. The narration reports Babbitt's thoughts and then quietly indicates their absurdity, often within the same sentence, and it does this without a character to blame the judgment on. There is no interpreter standing between the reader and the events whose motives could be questioned. The effect is confidence: the reader is never uncertain about what to think, and the pleasure of the book is largely the pleasure of shared superiority.

Fitzgerald routes everything through a participant with his own class position, his own snobberies, and his own investment in the story. Nick tells the reader on the first page that his father's advice was to "just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had," and that he became "inclined to reserve" judgment, and then spends nine chapters delivering judgments. The contradiction is deliberate and it is one of the novel's engines. Nick admits to enjoying "the consoling proximity of millionaires," which places him inside the economy he criticizes. His verdicts are therefore always available for suspicion, and a reader has to decide how much of the elegy is Gatsby's due and how much is Nick's need to have witnessed something significant.

The consequence for the comparison is precise. Babbitt is a novel about a condition; The Great Gatsby is a novel about a condition and about the telling of it. Lewis's technique cannot produce ambiguity, because ambiguity would undercut the satire, and satire requires the reader to be certain. Fitzgerald's technique cannot produce certainty, because the only access to Gatsby runs through a man who both admires and disapproves of him.

This also explains a difference in how the two books handle their protagonists' interiors. Lewis gives Babbitt continuous access: dreams, resentments, small vanities, and the recurring fantasy of a fairy child who finds him gallant. The reader knows Babbitt better than Babbitt does. Fitzgerald gives Gatsby almost nothing, confining the interior to two or three concentrated passages in Chapter 6 and a reconstructed final morning in Chapter 8. Gatsby remains a rumor even to the man narrating him, which is why he can carry symbolic weight that Babbitt cannot. A fully known man is a case study; a partly known man can be a figure.

An essay can turn this into a claim about what each mode requires. Satire needs its target transparent, because the reader must see every mechanism to enjoy the exposure. Elegy needs its subject opaque, because grief attaches to what cannot be fully recovered. The narrative technique in each book follows from the mode, and the mode follows from whether the man had a conviction, which is the deciding factor named earlier. Traced properly, the whole comparison hangs together from that single point.

## Zenith and West Egg: Two American Places

Both novels invent a place and make the invention carry an argument, and comparing the two settings is more productive than comparing the two protagonists.

Zenith is fully imagined and fully ordinary. Lewis gives it a population, an economy, a skyline, suburbs, a class structure, newspapers, clubs, churches, and a university in the invented state of Winnemac. Nothing about it is remarkable, and that is the point: it is meant to be every prosperous American city of the period at once, so that no reader can escape by deciding the satire is about somewhere else. The name itself is a booster's word, and Lewis uses it straight, letting the irony accumulate.

West Egg and East Egg are barely described by comparison, and what description exists is functional. Fitzgerald gives two peninsulas, a courtesy bay, and one sentence of social geography that does more work than a chapter of Lewis: "Across the courtesy bay the white palaces of fashionable East Egg glittered along the water." Everything the novel needs about the class arrangement is in that image, the water as a barrier and the light as a promise. He then adds the valley of ashes between the money and the city, a place anyone traveling between them must cross, described as "a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat."

The two invented geographies encode the two arguments. Zenith is horizontal. Its distinctions are of degree, not of kind, and a man can rise or fall within it without crossing any absolute line, which is why Babbitt's disappointment is a matter of tedium rather than exclusion. Fitzgerald's geography is vertical and separated by water. The two Eggs are physically similar and socially incommensurable, and the bay is uncrossable in the only sense that matters, which is why Gatsby's house faces the wrong way across it and why the green light stays at a distance the whole novel.

The ash valley completes the design and has no Zenith equivalent. Lewis's world has poorer people in it, but they are not made into a landscape and they are not positioned as the toll paid for the prosperity above. Fitzgerald builds the cost into the map. Anyone driving from West Egg to Manhattan passes through the place where the fuel comes from and the ash goes, and the novel's two working-class characters live there. That is a structural argument delivered as geography, and it is available in no other 1920s novel of comparable length.

A student can convert this into a strong paragraph by noting that both writers invented places rather than using real ones, and that the invention lets each control exactly which social facts the reader can see. Lewis wanted a city with no aristocracy so the satire would be about self-imposed conformity rather than exclusion. Fitzgerald wanted a geography with an uncrossable barrier so the tragedy would be about a ceiling rather than a personality. Neither setting is scenery. Both are the argument in physical form.

## Placing the Pairing in a Wider 1920s Argument

Comparative essays often need a third text, and this pairing slots into a larger case about the decade if the additions are chosen for what they supply rather than for what they repeat.

What the Lewis and Fitzgerald pair already covers is the money culture at two altitudes and in two modes. Lewis supplies the professional middle class, the manufacture of standing, the machinery of civic and commercial life, and the comedy of self-imposed conformity. Fitzgerald supplies the elite, the ceiling above the middle class, the psychology of longing, and the human cost when someone tests the boundary. Any third text should extend the range rather than crowd the middle.

The most useful addition downward is a book that shows the people whose labor supports the arrangement. Fitzgerald gestures at them through the Wilsons but keeps them at the edge of the frame, and Lewis has almost nothing to say about anyone who does not own a house. A text that puts the labor at the center closes the class range and prevents the essay from arguing that the 1920s consisted entirely of people with too much furniture.

The most useful addition sideways is a book about the same money culture from a vantage outside the American middle entirely, whether an expatriate one or a regional one. That kind of text tests whether the hollowness Lewis and Fitzgerald describe was a national condition or an urban and suburban one, and it makes the essay's generalization more careful.

What a student should avoid is adding a third novel that repeats one of the two moves already made. Another satire of middle-class conformity adds nothing Lewis has not supplied. Another elegy for a doomed romantic adds nothing Fitzgerald has not supplied. The test is simple and worth applying explicitly: name the thing the third text proves that neither Lewis nor Fitzgerald can prove. If no answer comes quickly, drop the third text and go deeper on the two.

There is one more consideration specific to this pair. Because Lewis is now the less familiar writer, an essay that adds a third text risks giving Babbitt so little space that the comparison collapses into assertion. Two books handled with real particularity will always beat three handled in summary, and the marker is assessing the quality of the comparative reasoning rather than the number of titles named.

Used well, this pairing makes a distinctive claim about the decade that few other combinations can. Most 1920s critiques examine one class position and one mode, which leaves a reader free to conclude that the problem belonged to a particular set of people or to a particular literary temperament. Lewis and Fitzgerald together remove both escapes. The condition operates on a broker in Zenith and on a millionaire on Long Island, it registers as absurdity to one writer and as loss to the other, and both diagnoses were filed while the money was still arriving. That is the argument the pairing exists to support, and it holds without any claim of influence between two writers who never needed to read each other to reach it.

### Does the pairing work if a student has not read all of Babbitt?

It works with accurate knowledge of the whole shape: Zenith, the clubs, Paul Riesling's imprisonment, the failed rebellion, and the return. What it will not survive is vagueness. Four or five precise particulars, used in service of an argument, outperform a general impression every time.

Markers can tell the difference between a student who has read a text and one who has read about it, and the tell is almost always the same. A reader who knows the book reaches for a small, specific, slightly odd detail: the fixtures in the bathroom, the fairy child in the recurring dream, the exact social meaning attached to a car model. A reader working from summary reaches for the plot's outline and the word satire. The same applies in reverse to Gatsby, where the tell is whether the essay quotes anything beyond the green light and the closing sentence.

This matters practically for a comparative essay because the weighting is usually uneven. On most syllabi Fitzgerald is the set text and Lewis is the comparative one, which means the marker is assessing Gatsby knowledge primarily and Babbitt knowledge as supporting evidence. That permits an approach in which Fitzgerald is quoted densely and Lewis is characterized specifically without matching quotation density. What it does not permit is characterizing Lewis loosely, since a single vague sentence about a satire of a businessman signals immediately that the second text was never opened.

There is a reasonable middle path for a student under time pressure. Read the opening chapters of Babbitt closely enough to hear the narrating voice and to see the goods doing their work, read the sequence around Paul Riesling's crisis, and read the closing exchange between Babbitt and his son. Those three portions supply the mode, the turn, and the ending, which is enough to argue with, provided the essay does not pretend to more coverage than it has. Honesty about scope costs nothing and overreach costs marks.

## Reception, the Nobel, and the Word Babbitt

The two books had very different public lives, and knowing the difference prevents a common error of proportion.

Babbitt was a large commercial success on publication in 1922 and an immediate cultural event. Within a year the protagonist's surname had become a common noun, and it remains one, which is a rare achievement for any novel. Lewis followed it with Arrowsmith in 1925 and Elmer Gantry in 1927, and in 1930 he became the first American awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, with the satirical novels of the 1920s at the center of the case. In his own decade Lewis was the more famous writer by a considerable margin.

The Great Gatsby sold modestly in 1925 and disappointed its author. Fitzgerald's reputation at his death in December 1940 rested more on his earlier commercial success and his magazine work than on the book now taught everywhere. Its rise came afterward, through wartime distribution and postwar critical reassessment, and by mid-century it had begun the ascent that made it the most assigned American novel of its era.

The positions have since reversed almost completely. Fitzgerald's novel is universal on syllabi and Lewis's is comparatively rarely assigned, though the word Babbitt outlived the book that produced it. A student writing this comparison should resist two temptations. The first is to treat the current ranking as a verdict on quality, since the book that survived was the commercial failure. The second is to treat Lewis as a minor figure Fitzgerald has eclipsed, which misrepresents the 1920s, when Lewis was the more prominent diagnostician of American life and Fitzgerald was the promising younger novelist whose third book had underperformed.

There is a plausible explanation for the reversal, offered as an explanation rather than a settled fact. Satire ages against its target. Once the specific social world Lewis skewered had passed, much of the pleasure of recognition went with it, and what remains requires historical reconstruction. Elegy ages differently, because grief for a lost possibility does not depend on the reader recognizing the furniture. Fitzgerald's novel also has the advantage of brevity and of prose that rewards the close reading a literature course is built to teach.

## Common Misreadings the Pairing Corrects

Three misreadings of Fitzgerald's novel weaken when Lewis stands beside it.

The first is that Gatsby depicts an exceptional world with no bearing on ordinary American life. The glamour encourages this, and the criminal fortune reinforces it. Lewis removes the escape by showing the identical hollowing in a man who broke no laws, lived in a midwestern city, and wanted only what everyone around him wanted. Once the condition is general, the Long Island version becomes the visible extreme rather than an aberration, and the novel's critique broadens accordingly.

The second is that Fitzgerald has no satirical intent, that the book is romantic throughout and the criticism is confined to Tom and Daisy. The novel contains a great deal of satire, and reading Lewis sharpens the ear for it. The guest list in Chapter 4 is a satirical set piece. The uncut books in the library are a joke at the expense of a man who bought an intellect. Jordan's line about large parties being intimate is a satirist's sentence. What distinguishes Fitzgerald is that he suspends the satire around Gatsby himself while keeping it operating everywhere else, and noticing exactly where the suspension applies is a strong analytical observation.

The third is that the two decades of American materialism split neatly into a roaring 1920s and a chastened 1930s, with the critique arriving only after the crash. Both these books were written inside the boom. Lewis in 1922 and Fitzgerald in 1925 were describing the hollowness while the money was still arriving, which means the diagnosis was contemporaneous and not hindsight. That fact is worth a sentence in any essay, because it forecloses the objection that the critique is retrospective moralizing.

A fourth caution runs the other way. Having noticed the shared critique, some readers conclude that Fitzgerald and Lewis were doing the same thing in different registers. They were not. Lewis wrote to expose a type and to make the type unattractive, and the book has designs on its reader's opinions. Fitzgerald wrote to describe a loss, and the book has no program at all. Treating elegy as satire in a slower tempo will produce a flat essay, and markers notice.

## The Counter-Reading: Are the Two Too Far Apart in Class to Compare?

The objection deserves a full statement. Babbitt is a middle-class businessman in a midwestern city with a mortgage, an office, and a family. Gatsby is a criminal millionaire on Long Island with a mansion, a fabricated past, and no visible occupation. Their incomes differ by orders of magnitude, their social worlds do not overlap, and their books belong to different modes. Any similarity extracted across that distance will be a generalization about materialism that could equally connect either novel to a dozen others, which makes the comparison a category label rather than an analysis.

The concession is real and should be made early. If the essay concludes only that both novels dislike materialism, it has produced nothing, because that statement is true of a great many books and proves nothing about either. The distance in class and mode is genuine and cannot be argued away.

Three specific findings survive the objection.

First, the two-vantage finding. The critique of 1920s money culture is incomplete from either altitude alone. From the middle, Lewis can show how standing is manufactured, how opinions are supplied, and how the machinery of aspiration operates on people who are inside it. From above, Fitzgerald can show what inherited money does to people who never had to want, and what the ceiling feels like from underneath. Neither writer could produce the other's material, and the decade is only fully described with both.

Second, the mode finding. Because satire and elegy are opposite contracts with the reader, their agreement about the diagnosis carries more weight than agreement between two books in the same mode would. Two satirists agreeing tells us about satire. A satirist and an elegist agreeing tells us about the subject. The methodological opposition is evidence, not an obstacle.

Third, the assembled-self finding. Both novels show a man whose identity is constructed from purchased and borrowed materials, one from group opinion and one from invention, and both show the construction failing under pressure. That correspondence is specific rather than generic, and it produces the claim that conformity and self-invention are two strategies for the same American predicament rather than opposites. No single-book reading generates that claim.

The honest position is that the two novels are not siblings and should not be argued as siblings. They are two measurements of the same culture taken at different heights, and the distance between the measuring points is what makes the reading worth doing.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict: Lewis and Fitzgerald diagnose one money culture from two class vantages and in two modes, and the deciding factor between them is whether the protagonist had a conviction to lose.

Everything follows from that. Babbitt has none, so his hollowness is comic and survivable, so satire is the correct mode, so he lives, so the ending is continuation. Gatsby has one and it is enormous, so his hollowness is not hollowness at all but an excess that the world cannot hold, so elegy is the correct mode, so he dies, so the ending widens out to a continent and a current. The mode is not a stylistic preference either writer happened to have; it is determined by what is at stake in the man.

This also settles the ranking question. Lewis is unmatched on the social manufacture of standing, on how opinions are distributed, and on the texture of American prosperity at the level where most of it was actually lived. Fitzgerald is unmatched on longing, on the ceiling above the middle class, and on what the culture costs when it stops merely wasting lives and starts ending them. An essay that awards a winner has misread the task; an essay that names the deciding factor and shows what each mode can therefore see has understood it.

One consequence worth carrying into a conclusion. Because Babbitt survives, Lewis's novel implies that the culture is a permanent condition to be endured. Because Gatsby dies and the Buchanans drive away, Fitzgerald's implies that the culture is actively defended and lethal to anyone who tests it. The second is the darker claim, and it is easy to miss under the beauty of the prose, which is exactly why reading the two together is worth the effort.

## How to Write the Comparative Essay From This Pairing

Organize by axis, never by book. Three paragraphs on Fitzgerald followed by three on Lewis and a closing observation that both criticize materialism is the shape markers cap on sight. Take one row of the two-vantage table per paragraph and handle both novels inside it.

Build the thesis on mode and vantage rather than on shared theme. A workable formulation: Lewis and Fitzgerald attack one money culture from the middle and from the top, and because Babbitt has no conviction to lose while Gatsby has nothing else, the same diagnosis arrives as satire in one book and as elegy in the other, which is why the decade requires both to be fully described. That commits the essay to an argument and gives every paragraph work to do.

Characterize Lewis with specifics. Vague reference to a satire about a businessman reads as unfamiliarity. Name Zenith, the state of Winnemac, the Boosters' Club, Paul Riesling, Tanis Judique, Seneca Doane, the Good Citizens' League, and the closing exchange with Ted. Four or five precise particulars establish credibility immediately.

Quote Fitzgerald densely, since Gatsby is almost always the set text. The lines that do comparative work here are the moths image from Chapter 3, Jordan's remark about large parties, the Chapter 6 self-invention sequence, the description of the object of Gatsby's devotion as vulgar and meretricious, the Eckleburg billboard, and Nick's verdict on the Buchanans. Lines about the green light or the closing current belong in a different essay, since Lewis offers no counterpart and the argument will drift.

Give one full paragraph to mode. The satire-against-elegy contrast is the most sophisticated available point and very few essays develop it properly. Explain that satire requires distance and a slightly ridiculous target while elegy requires proximity and a slightly admirable one, then show that each novel's ending follows from its mode. Support the claim with the observation that Babbitt survives and Gatsby does not.

Address the class objection in the introduction or the first body paragraph. Markers will be watching to see whether the essay notices that comparing a real estate broker with a criminal millionaire raises a problem. State that the comparison tests one culture at two altitudes rather than equating two men, and the rest of the essay is free to proceed.

## Closing Verdict

Two American novels three years apart, one written to make a reader laugh at a man and one written to make a reader grieve for one, arrive at the same account of what money was doing to Americans in the 1920s. The prosperous broker in Zenith and the bootlegger on Long Island are both selves assembled from purchases, both surrounded by relationships that dissolve under the first real pressure, and both unable to name what is missing. Lewis proves the condition is ordinary. Fitzgerald proves it is fatal when someone takes it seriously enough to test the ceiling above it.

For a reader of Gatsby, the pairing does two things. It removes the excuse of exceptionalism, since the same hollowing operates on a man who never broke a law. And it makes the novel's satire audible, since Fitzgerald is funnier and colder about his minor characters than a first reading suggests, and the guest list, the uncut books, and Jordan's joke about intimacy all belong to Lewis's register even though the book around them does not.

What Fitzgerald adds, and what satire cannot supply, is the ceiling and the cost. Zenith has no aristocracy to be excluded from, so Babbitt's disappointment is horizontal, a life spent among people exactly like him. Gatsby's is vertical, and the class above answers his challenge by naming his origins and letting him take the blame for a death he did not cause. That escalation is where the two books part company and where the argument about the decade completes itself, alongside the wider [American Dream novel tradition](/2028/07/31/gatsby-american-dream-novels/) in which both books sit as contemporaneous witnesses rather than retrospective critics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to Babbitt?

Both are 1920s novels arguing that American prosperity had hollowed out the people enjoying it, and both show a man whose identity is assembled from purchased goods and borrowed social scripts. The differences are structural rather than incidental. Lewis writes satire from inside the professional middle class, so his target must be slightly ridiculous and his protagonist survives. Fitzgerald writes elegy from the vantage of a man barred from the elite, so his protagonist must be slightly admirable and he dies. Lewis foregrounds work, selling, and civic life; Fitzgerald keeps labor almost entirely offstage. Read together they cover the class range where the decade's money actually sat, and their agreement about the hollowness carries extra weight precisely because their methods have nothing in common.

### Q: How does Babbitt satirize middle-class conformity?

Lewis works by accumulation and by letting his protagonist condemn himself. George F. Babbitt's opinions on prohibition, labor, immigration, and municipal spending arrive ready-made from the Athletic Club and the trade press, and he holds them warmly because they are the price of belonging. His civic speeches are recognizably advertising copy applied to a city. His affections attach to appliances, a car model, and a mortgage more readily than to his wife. Crucially, Lewis shows how little coercion is required: nobody forces Babbitt to think anything, and he adopts the views voluntarily because he wants to be inside. The satire is affectionate rather than cruel, which is what keeps it from curdling into contempt, but the verdict is never in doubt.

### Q: What critique of materialism do the novels share?

Both argue that goods have been substituted for the content of a life, and both are careful to show that nobody chose the substitution consciously. Fitzgerald compresses the claim into images: shirts that produce tears, uncut books in a library, a billboard for a vanished optometrist that a grieving man mistakes for God. Lewis produces the same claim by repetition, giving Babbitt small ecstasies over fixtures and brand knowledge until the pattern is undeniable. Neither writer objects to possessions as such. What both object to is a culture in which a person can only describe himself by listing what he owns and which groups he belongs to, and in which the relationships that should carry emotional weight have handed it over to objects.

### Q: How do satire and tragedy differ as modes?

They are different contracts with the reader. Satire asks the reader to stand outside the target and judge, which requires that the target be at least slightly ridiculous and that the reader feel superior. Tragedy asks the reader to stand with the target and grieve, which requires that the target be at least slightly admirable. A novel cannot ask for mockery and mourning at once, which is why Lewis makes Babbitt absurd from the opening pages and Fitzgerald has Nick declare that Gatsby possessed "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person." The modes also determine the endings: satire cannot kill its target without becoming something else, so Babbitt survives, while tragedy requires the loss.

### Q: Are Babbitt and Gatsby too far apart in class to compare?

The distance is real and should be conceded plainly. A middle-class broker in a midwestern city and a criminal millionaire on Long Island differ by orders of magnitude in income and share no social world. If the comparison produces only the observation that both novels dislike materialism, the objection wins. Three findings survive it. The critique of the decade is incomplete from either altitude alone, since only Lewis can show how standing is manufactured and only Fitzgerald can show the ceiling above it. The agreement between two opposite modes is stronger evidence about the subject than agreement within one mode would be. And the assembled-self correspondence is specific rather than generic, producing a claim neither book yields alone.

### Q: How do the novels show the era's emptiness from two class vantages?

Lewis writes from inside the group performing aspiration, so he can render the club roster, the sales technique, the trade paper, the civic speech, and the precise social meaning of a car model. His emptiness is horizontal: a life spent among people exactly like him, none of whom he can say anything true to. Fitzgerald writes from a vantage where aspiration has either succeeded or become irrelevant, so he can render what inherited money does to people who never had to want, and what the barrier feels like from below. His emptiness is vertical: a ceiling that money cannot buy through, defended by people who did nothing to earn their position. Together the two vantages describe the decade completely, which neither does alone.

### Q: Who was Sinclair Lewis?

Sinclair Lewis was an American novelist whose satirical books of the 1920s made him the most prominent diagnostician of American middle-class life in his decade. Main Street appeared in 1920, Babbitt in 1922, Arrowsmith in 1925, and Elmer Gantry in 1927. In 1930 he became the first American writer awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, with the 1920s satires central to the case. He is worth knowing accurately for this comparison because in 1922 and 1925 he was the more famous of the two writers by a wide margin, and Fitzgerald was the promising younger novelist whose third book had underperformed. The current ranking, in which Gatsby is universal on syllabi and Babbitt is rarely assigned, is a later development.

### Q: What is boosterism in 1920s America?

Boosterism was the organized promotion of a town or city by its own business class, conducted through clubs, chambers of commerce, speeches, and civic campaigns, and treated as both a commercial activity and a moral duty. A booster talked up local growth, defended local business against criticism, and measured civic health by population figures and construction. Lewis makes it the atmosphere of Zenith, and Babbitt is a practicing booster whose speeches on civic progress are recognizably sales copy. The relevance to Fitzgerald is that boosterism is the middle-class version of a habit the novel shows at the elite level, where standing is asserted through display and opinions are adopted to signal membership rather than because they have been examined.

### Q: Does Gatsby contain satire as well as elegy?

It contains a great deal, and reading Lewis sharpens the ear for it. The guest list in Chapter 4 is a satirical set piece. The uncut books in Gatsby's library are a joke at the expense of a man who bought an intellect he never used. Jordan Baker's remark that she likes large parties because they are so intimate is a satirist's sentence. Tom Buchanan's recitation of a book he has just read, delivered as though it were his own thinking, is satire of a specific and cold kind. What distinguishes Fitzgerald is that the satire operates everywhere except around Gatsby himself, where the mode switches to elegy. Identifying exactly where the suspension applies is one of the strongest analytical observations available.

### Q: Which figures in Gatsby resemble George F. Babbitt?

Tom Buchanan is the closest match in behavior, since his opinions arrive from books he has just read and are repeated to establish membership in the group that reads them, which is Babbitt's exact operation performed at a higher altitude. Meyer Wolfshiem shares the habit of civic sentiment deployed for effect, offering a maxim about showing friendship to a man while he is alive. The party guests as a body resemble Zenith society: present for standing, uninterested in the host, fluent in rumor. Nick himself has a touch of it, since he keeps a list of names and admits to the consoling proximity of millionaires. Gatsby is the one figure who does not fit, because he invented rather than conformed.

### Q: Why does Fitzgerald mourn what Lewis mocks?

Because the two men lose different things. Babbitt never had a conviction, so there is nothing to grieve; his surrender is comic, and the punishment is decades more of the same life. Gatsby had a conviction so large that no object could hold it, and he pursued it until it killed him, which makes the outcome a loss rather than a joke. Fitzgerald signals the classification directly by giving the pursuit religious vocabulary, noting that Gatsby "had committed himself to the following of a grail." A reader can be asked to mock a man with no faith or to mourn a man destroyed by one, and each mode follows from what is actually at stake in the protagonist.

### Q: What thesis works for a Lewis and Fitzgerald comparative essay?

Build on mode and vantage rather than shared theme. A workable formulation: Lewis and Fitzgerald attack one money culture from the middle and from the top, and because Babbitt has no conviction to lose while Gatsby has nothing else, the same diagnosis arrives as satire in one book and as elegy in the other, which is why the decade requires both to be fully described. A second strong option builds on the assembled self, arguing that conformity and self-invention are two strategies for a single American predicament rather than opposites, since both men construct an identity from purchased and borrowed materials and differ only in supplier. Either commits the essay to something it must prove.

### Q: How do the two works treat conformity against self-invention?

They present them as two solutions to the same problem, which is that American identity has no inherited content and must be constructed. Babbitt consults the group and receives a set of opinions, enthusiasms, and possessions that mark him as belonging. Gatsby manufactures a person from magazines, a schedule, and a boy's idea of a gentleman, then persuades the world to accept the manufacture. Fitzgerald gives the mechanism plainly: "James Gatz that was really, or at least legally, his name." The two strategies fail differently. Conformity fails by attrition, slowly and survivably. Invention fails catastrophically, because it depends on nobody producing the original, and Tom Buchanan produces it in a single afternoon at the Plaza.

### Q: What does each work say about advertising and bought goods?

Lewis says it explicitly. Brand knowledge is presented as Babbitt's main intellectual competence, since he can read a car, a house, a suit, or a bathroom fixture for exactly what it says about its owner, and his own trade depends on persuasion. Fitzgerald never names advertising as an industry but saturates the novel with it. The presiding image over the valley of ashes is a commercial one, since "the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are blue and gigantic," a billboard outliving the practice that bought it, and a grieving man eventually mistakes it for God. The most useful formulation for an essay is that Babbitt consumes the campaign while Gatsby has become one, a persona designed to produce a specific impression in a single buyer.

### Q: How does each central man end up?

Babbitt survives. He returns to the Boosters' Club, resumes his business, loses nothing material, and carries the private knowledge that he has never done a single thing he wanted. His only free act is telling his son to go ahead and live as he likes. Gatsby is shot in his own swimming pool by a man deceived into believing he was the driver, and the funeral is attended by almost nobody who ate his food. The contrast is exact and carries an argument: the culture absorbs dissatisfaction without effort and continues, but it defends its boundary with force when someone accumulates enough to test whether the boundary is real.

### Q: Which Gatsby quotations support the materialism critique?

Six do most of the work. The party opening from Chapter 3, "in his blue gardens men and girls came and went like moths," fixes the social atmosphere. Jordan's line, "I like large parties. They're so intimate," delivers the diagnosis as a joke. The Chapter 6 sequence on self-invention supplies the manufactured self. The description of Gatsby's object of devotion as "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty" prevents any purely romantic reading. The Eckleburg billboard supplies the commercial image replacing belief. Nick's verdict that the Buchanans "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money" supplies the cost. Quoted precisely and analyzed rather than dropped in, those six will carry a full comparative essay.

### Q: Is Tom Buchanan the elite version of a booster?

In several respects, yes, and the observation is worth a paragraph. Tom recites the argument of a book he has recently read as though it were his own conclusion, which is exactly the operation Lewis satirizes when Babbitt repeats the Athletic Club's opinions. Tom's confidence is unearned and group-derived, his hostility to anything outside his set is reflexive, and his sense of his own importance is supplied by his position rather than by anything he has done. The difference is that Babbitt's group can expel him and does try, while Tom's cannot, because he was born inside it. That difference is precisely the ceiling Fitzgerald can show and Lewis cannot.

### Q: Were Lewis and Fitzgerald writing in the same literary moment?

Yes, and the fact is worth stating because it forecloses a periodizing error. Babbitt appeared in 1922 and Gatsby in 1925, in the same publishing culture and addressed to overlapping readers. Both were written inside the boom, before the crash, which means their agreement about American hollowness was contemporaneous diagnosis rather than retrospective moralizing. Treating Lewis as a holdover from an earlier realist era and Fitzgerald as the voice of a new one obscures that simultaneity. No claim of influence between them is needed for the comparison to work, and an unsupported influence claim would invite correction. What matters is that two writers with incompatible methods described the same condition at the same time.

### Q: How should a student handle mode when pairing satire with elegy?

Treat mode as an argument, not a style label. Explain what each mode requires: satire needs distance and a target the reader can feel superior to, while elegy needs proximity and a figure the reader can admire. Then show that the endings follow, since satire cannot kill its target without changing genre, which is why Babbitt survives into more of the same life while Gatsby dies. Then make the strongest available point, which is that agreement between two opposite modes is better evidence about the subject than agreement within one mode. Two satirists agreeing tells you about satire; a satirist and an elegist agreeing tells you about the decade they were both describing.

### Q: Does this pairing change how you read Gatsby's parties?

It does, in two directions. Lewis makes the parties look less exotic, since the Zenith dinner operates on the same principles, guests selected for standing and conversation composed of received opinion, which suggests Fitzgerald is describing a general American habit rather than a Long Island peculiarity. It also makes the parties look colder, because Lewis's version is played for comedy and Fitzgerald's is not. The moths image carries fragility and attraction to a light the guests do not understand, and Chapter 9 supplies the audit when almost none of them attends the funeral. Read after Babbitt, the parties stop being glamour with a melancholy undertone and become a demonstration that the social world was never real.
