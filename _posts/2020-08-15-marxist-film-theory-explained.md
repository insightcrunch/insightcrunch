## Marxist Film Theory in Practice

Marxist film theory is best approached as a set of questions about manufacture, technique, and spectatorship rather than as a hunt for political messages hidden inside stories. A student holding a film and an assignment needs to know, before any theorist is named, that this approach examines how a film was made, what its techniques do to the person watching, and how that person is positioned by the viewing situation itself. Content matters, but content is the last thing the method examines and never the only thing. A melodrama and a musical produced inside the same studio system may share an organisation of labour, a grammar of shots, and a seating of the audience while telling stories that appear to have nothing in common. The tradition insists that the shared elements are as analytical as the differing ones, and often more revealing.

![A darkened auditorium, a projector beam crossing it, and the five-part analysis table, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-11.webp)

Three commitments define the field across its whole history. First, cinema is an industrial mode of production, so financing, studio organisation, the labour process, and distribution belong inside criticism rather than in a separate business history. Second, formal techniques carry effects that can be described independently of what a story says, so editing, framing, and sound are evidence and not decoration. Third, the viewing situation itself constructs a position for the spectator, a claim developed most radically by the apparatus theorists around 1970 and examined at length below. Readers who want the framework within which these commitments sit should begin with the [marxist cultural theory guide](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), which this article presupposes rather than repeats. The theoretical account of belief and recognition that the apparatus argument borrows is treated in the article on [althusser ideology state apparatuses](/2012/03/01/althusser-ideology-state-apparatuses/), and the production side of the argument connects to the account of [culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), which handles the industrial organisation of cultural goods in general.

The article that follows is organised as a working instrument. A definition comes first, then the three historical traditions in the order they arose: Soviet montage, the Brechtian programme, and the apparatus theory of the French and British journals. The classification of films into seven categories by their relation to the dominant ideology is then set out in full, because it remains the most teachable device the field produced. A five-part procedure for analysing any film follows, with a worked example carried through all five steps. Political economy and reception close the survey, since a method that begins with manufacture must end with the audiences who complete, resist, or redirect what manufacture began. Throughout, the verdicts of the tradition are stated with their defeaters attached, meaning the evidence that would reverse each judgement is named beside the judgement itself.

## The Five-Part Analysis Table

The five-part analysis table gathers the whole procedure onto a single page. Each row names one element of a film that a Marxist analysis examines, the evidence appropriate to that element, the shape a finding takes when the examination succeeds, and the error that beginners most reliably commit at that stage. The table is reproduced here near the start because the historical sections that follow keep returning to it: montage belongs chiefly to the second row, apparatus theory to the fourth, and the political economy sections to the first.

| Element | What to examine | Evidence to use | What a finding looks like | Error commonly made |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mode of production | Financing, studio structure, labour, distribution | Trade press, contracts, credits, company records | The organisation of manufacture explains a feature of the finished work | Treating the director as sole origin of everything visible |
| Formal techniques | Editing, framing, camera movement, sound design | The film itself, described shot by shot where necessary | A technique is shown to organise attention in a describable direction | Listing devices without stating what each device does |
| Narrative resolution | Which contradiction the ending settles, and by what device | Plot structure, closing sequences, restored or displaced figures | The closing device is matched to the pressure it relieves | Retelling the story and calling the retelling an analysis |
| Viewer position | The place the film constructs for its spectator | Point of view editing, address, spatial coherence, conditions of projection | A position is specified that particular viewers may occupy or refuse | Assuming every spectator occupies the constructed place in the same way |
| Reception | Who watched, under what conditions, with what responses | Reviews, censorship records, fan writing, exhibition history | A documented divergence between constructed position and recorded response | Substituting the critic's own response for the audience's history |

A table of this kind earns its place only if a student can carry it into a screening and return with the columns filled. The rows are ordered deliberately. Production comes before form because the conditions of manufacture limit which forms were available. Form comes before narrative because the same story can be organised by opposed techniques. Viewer position comes before reception because the recorded behaviour of audiences is only intelligible against the position the film offered them. Reception comes last and revises everything, since audiences who talked back, walked out, or remade a film in fan culture expose the limits of every earlier row.

## A Definition and Its Boundaries

Marxist film theory analyses cinema as an industrial mode of production, as a set of formal techniques with ideological effects, and as an apparatus that positions its viewer, rather than as a body of works to be interpreted for political content. Each clause of that definition excludes a neighbouring activity. The first clause excludes auteur criticism that treats a film as the expression of one sensibility. The second excludes thematic criticism that paraphrases plots and grades their sympathies. The third excludes the sociology of audiences taken alone, because the position a film constructs is a property of the work and its exhibition, distinct from whatever audiences eventually did with it.

Boundaries of this sort matter practically. A review that praises a film for its humane message is not performing a Marxist analysis, whatever the politics of the reviewer. An essay that counts the class origins of characters and stops there has performed only a fragment of the second step of the procedure. Conversely, an analysis of editing rhythms in a film with no overt political subject can belong fully to the tradition, provided it connects technique to the organisation of production and to the place built for the spectator. The definition is a gatekeeper, and students save considerable confusion by applying it early.

### What separates a Marxist account of cinema from a reading of political messages?

A message reading asks what a film says about society and grades the answer for sympathy or hostility. A Marxist account asks how the film was manufactured, what its techniques do, which contradiction its ending settles, and what place it builds for a viewer.

### Why does the tradition begin with manufacture rather than with meaning?

Meaning is cheap to allege and hard to check, whereas manufacture leaves records. Contracts, credits, trade reporting, and company archives allow claims about production to be verified or refuted by other scholars. Beginning there disciplines everything that follows, because a reading that starts from documented conditions cannot drift as freely as one that starts from impressions gathered in the dark.

The neighbouring disciplines should be distinguished with equal care. The account of the culture industry, developed in the 1940s by Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno, treats industrially produced culture as a system for reconciling audiences to the existing order, and it supplies part of the production argument used here. Media theory in the wider sense, treated in the article on [marxist media theory explained](/2012/05/15/marxist-media-theory-explained/), ranges across broadcasting and print as well as cinema, and its reception arguments are borrowed back into the fifth step of the procedure below. The parallel procedure for prose fiction is given in the article on a [marxist reading of a novel](/2020/08/01/marxist-reading-of-a-novel/), whose income ledger corresponds loosely to the production row of the table above: both methods begin by asking where the money was before asking what the work means.

## Soviet Montage: The Founding Moment

The founding moment of Marxist film theory is unusual in the history of criticism because the theory and the practice were the same activity carried on by the same people. In the Soviet Union of the 1920s, a small group of directors worked inside a state-supported film industry, short of raw stock, short of equipment, and charged with making pictures for audiences across an enormous and largely illiterate territory. Their solutions to those material problems became a theory of editing. Sergei Eisenstein, Vsevolod Pudovkin, Lev Kuleshov, Dziga Vertov, and Alexander Dovzhenko each treated the cut, the junction between two strips of film, as the place where cinema actually happens. A single shot, on this view, is raw material. Meaning is manufactured when shots collide or link, and the spectator's mind performs the joining work that the screen withholds.

Scarcity shaped the doctrine in ways its authors acknowledged. Imported pictures were re-edited in Soviet cutting rooms because new stock was unavailable, and the re-editing demonstrated practically that the same footage could be made to say different things by rearrangement. Kuleshov's workshop turned that demonstration into experiments. Eisenstein's early work in theatre, where he had staged productions with the Proletkult organisation, supplied the idea of a montage of attractions: a sequence of calculated shocks, each chosen for its effect on an audience rather than for narrative smoothness. When he moved to film, the attractions became shots, and the calculation became a politics, since the effects to be produced in spectators were understood as the formation of conclusions about history and class.

### What did the Kuleshov experiments appear to show?

Workshop exercises associated with Lev Kuleshov intercut an identical shot of an actor's neutral face with a bowl of soup, a child in a coffin, and a reclining woman. Viewers praised the actor's subtle shifts of expression although the face never changed.

### Why did montage theorists invoke dialectics?

Eisenstein argued that art proceeds by conflict: of lines, volumes, tempos, and ideas. Dialectics offered a model in which opposed elements collide and produce a third thing present in neither, which matched his editing practice exactly. Invoking the doctrine also placed film form inside Marxist philosophy rather than beside it, claiming that the method of the cut and the method of historical analysis were one method.

The Kuleshov material deserves a caution that later scholarship supplied. The original footage is lost, the experiments survive through descriptions by participants, and the neat story of the expressionless face may have been polished in retelling. None of that removes the theoretical point, which does not depend on one workshop exercise. Every editor who has placed a reaction shot after an event knows that audiences assign the reaction to the event, supply an emotion appropriate to it, and credit the performer with an interior state. Montage theory generalised that craft knowledge into a claim about where cinematic meaning resides, and the claim survives even if the founding anecdote is partly legend.

## Eisenstein and Intellectual Montage

Eisenstein pushed the doctrine furthest. Where Kuleshov and Pudovkin treated editing as the construction of sense from neutral pieces, Eisenstein treated it as collision engineered to produce concepts. In Strike of 1925, the cutting of workers by troops is intercut with the slaughter of a bull in an abattoir, and the spectator is meant to conclude something about the character of the violence rather than merely witness it. In Battleship Potemkin of the same year, rhythm and graphic conflict carry sequences that have almost no dialogue to paraphrase. In October of 1928, commissioned for the tenth anniversary of the revolution, the method becomes explicitly intellectual: the provisional government's leader Kerensky is intercut with a mechanical peacock spreading its tail, and a descending sequence of religious images moves from ornate icons toward simpler figures, staging an argument about authority and belief as a chain of visual comparisons.

The essay in which this practice received its philosophical statement appeared in 1929, usually translated as Beyond the Shot or as The Dramaturgy of Film Form. Eisenstein there models the shot on the ideogram, pointing to writing systems in which the combination of two depictable elements yields an undepictable concept: a mouth and a child give crying, a knife and a heart give sorrow. The shot, he argues, is a cell; montage is the explosion that occurs when cells are brought into conflict; and the product of that explosion is an idea that no single image contains. This is the principal theorist of the tradition explicitly modelling his method on dialectics, in an essay whose date and argument students should be able to give from memory, because examination questions on montage return to it constantly.

### How does intellectual montage differ from linkage?

Linkage, associated with Pudovkin, joins shots as bricks build a wall: each piece adds information and the sequence accumulates into a scene the spectator follows. Intellectual montage joins shots as collisions: each piece strikes against the next and the sequence produces a concept the spectator must conclude.

### What is the spectator asked to do in an Eisenstein sequence?

The spectator is asked to complete an inference. Images are arranged so that their combination yields a judgement about the events shown, and the judgement feels like the viewer's own discovery rather than an instruction delivered by an intertitle. Eisenstein considered that activity politically valuable, since a conclusion a person reaches appears to that person as knowledge, while a conclusion merely announced can be shrugged away as exhortation.

Pudovkin's contrasting practice clarifies the stakes. His Mother of 1926, adapted from Gorky's novel, builds its effects by accumulation: the heroine's awakening is assembled from details of a demonstration, melting ice, and faces, each shot a brick in a wall of feeling. Pudovkin's manual of film technique, circulated widely in translation from the end of the decade, codified editing as the foundation of film art and instructed filmmakers outside the Soviet Union for a generation. The disagreement between collision and linkage was genuine but should not be inflated into a schism. Both positions agree on the founding proposition that the shot is material and the cut is manufacture. They disagree about what the spectator's joining work feels like and what it is for.

A second caution concerns the audience the theory imagined. Montage writing often speaks as though effects were guaranteed, as though a collision of images must produce its concept in every viewer. That confidence belonged to a moment when the industry was young, audiences were assumed to be malleable, and the directors were also state employees with a pedagogical brief. Later sections of this article, on reception, supply the corrective from inside the tradition. The montage claim itself, that juxtaposition manufactures meaning, is not damaged by the corrective. What is damaged is the assumption that the manufactured meaning arrives intact in every head.

## Vertov and the Camera as Instrument

A related practice treated the camera as an instrument for revealing what the eye cannot see. Dziga Vertov, who took his working name from the sound of a spinning top, ran the Kino-Pravda newsreel series through the 1920s and completed the argument in Man with a Movie Camera of 1929. His manifestos rejected staged drama, actors, sets, and scripts as borrowings from theatre and literature that kept cinema dependent. Against them he set the kino-eye, the camera eye, which he described in a famous phrase as catching life unawares: the machine can slow a gallop, accelerate a plant, reverse a fall, freeze a crowd, and combine viewpoints no body could occupy, so it perceives a world that unaided vision never meets.

The Marxist content of Vertov's position is easy to miss because it wears the costume of technological enthusiasm. His claim was that ordinary perception is socially organised, that people see what habit and interest train them to see, and that an instrument indifferent to habit can disclose the actual connections among labour, transport, food, and leisure that a city lives by. Man with a Movie Camera is built on that disclosure: a day in a composite city is assembled so that production and consumption, waking and working, machinery and bodies appear as one system. The film also shows its own cameraman and its own editing room, an early refusal of the invisible manufacture that later theorists would criticise in the commercial cinema.

### What does the camera reveal that the unaided eye misses?

Vertov's answer was relations. The eye encounters events one at a time and from one station; the camera can juxtapose a miner, a loom, a loaf, and a table so that the connections among them become visible as a single process. Slow motion, freeze frames, and split screens extend the same claim: the instrument reorganises perception so that a society's workings can be inspected rather than merely inhabited.

### Why did Vertov refuse actors and staged scenes?

Staged drama, in his argument, imports the psychology of the bourgeois theatre into a medium whose strength is the registration of actual life. Actors performing invented feelings teach spectators to attend to private destinies. Vertov wanted attention directed at processes and collectives instead, and he treated the refusal of fiction as a formal decision with political content, not as a preference for one genre over another.

Dovzhenko's Earth of 1930 shows how far the founding generation's methods could travel from agitation toward lyric. Its subject is collectivisation in a Ukrainian village, its images are orchards, wheat, faces, and weather, and its editing binds a death and a birth into one rhythm of continuity. The film was attacked in its own moment for formalism and insufficient clarity, which illustrates a tension the tradition never escaped: the more completely form becomes the argument, the less the argument can be paraphrased by officials, teachers, or examiners, and the more vulnerable the work becomes to the charge of obscurity. That tension returns in every later section of this article, because each tradition within Marxist film theory had to decide how much of its meaning it was willing to leave in paraphrasable content.

## Sound, Synthesis, and the Later Eisenstein

The arrival of synchronised sound at the end of the 1920s threatened the founding doctrine, because dialogue anchored shots to continuous scenes and appeared to return cinema to photographed theatre. Eisenstein, Pudovkin, and Grigori Alexandrov answered in a joint statement of 1928 that treated sound itself as montage material: used contrapuntally, set against the image rather than glued to it, sound would extend collision into a new register instead of dissolving it. The statement is a useful document for students because it shows theorists defending a method by generalising it under pressure, a pattern that recurs when the apparatus thesis met its critics four decades later.

Eisenstein's own later work complicated the early doctrine from within. His unfinished projects of the 1930s, his writings on inner monologue and on the montage of attractions revisited, and the two parts of Ivan the Terrible released in 1944 and after his death moved toward what he called vertical montage, the coordinated movement of image, music, and colour as strands of one construction. Prokofiev's scores were composed in dialogue with the editing rather than applied to it. Scholars disagree about whether the later theory fulfils or abandons the essay of 1929. The safer statement, and the one this article adopts, is that the founding proposition survived the journey: meaning remains something manufactured between elements, whether the elements are shots, sounds, or colours, and the spectator remains the site where the manufacture completes itself.

### What happens to montage once sound arrives?

Sound at first appears to restore the continuous scene that montage had broken apart. The Soviet answer of 1928 was to treat the soundtrack as one more element available for collision, so that a heard element could contradict, ironise, or extend a seen one. Where sound merely duplicates the image, montage weakens; where sound is set against the image, the method gains a register it lacked in silence.

### Is Soviet montage only a historical style?

Montage survives wherever editing is used to manufacture a conclusion rather than to smooth a transition: in trailers that argue by juxtaposition, in political advertising, in the rapid associative cutting of music film, and in essay films that think in images. The style of the 1920s is historical, but the proposition beneath it, that juxtaposition produces concepts, describes a permanent resource of the medium rather than a period mannerism.

The legacy question matters for examinations because it separates two claims that are often run together. The weak claim is that a group of Soviet directors developed a distinctive editing practice in the 1920s, which no one disputes. The strong claim is that their practice embodies a general truth about cinematic meaning, namely that the unit of sense is the relation between shots and not the shot itself. The strong claim is the Marxist one, since it locates meaning in production and relation rather than in the intrinsic content of images, exactly as the wider theory locates value in relations of production rather than in things. A student who can state both claims, and say which parts of each survived, has the montage tradition in usable form.

There is a further legacy in film education itself. Montage theory is foundational in teaching because it gives beginners a vocabulary for the cut: graphic match, rhythmic cutting, tonal and overtonal relations, intellectual combination. Courses with no Marxist commitment whatever teach Eisenstein's categories, usually without the dialectical philosophy that generated them. That uptake is an instance of a pattern this series records repeatedly, in which a device produced inside the tradition circulates far beyond it while its originating argument is left behind. The device travels; the philosophy stays home. Remembering the philosophy matters when the device is asked to do analytical work, because the categories were designed to produce conclusions in spectators, and a purely technical use of them forgets what they were for.

## The Brechtian Tradition: Interrupting Identification

The second tradition descends from theatre. Bertolt Brecht, writing in the 1930s and working in exile and then in Berlin until his death in 1956, developed an epic theatre whose central device is usually translated as the alienation effect or, in the vocabulary this series uses, distanciation. A conventional performance, in Brecht's analysis, absorbs its audience: spectators identify with a protagonist, feel what the protagonist feels, and leave purged of the emotions the plot aroused, with the social arrangements that produced the plot left unexamined. Distanciation interrupts that absorption. Actors demonstrate characters rather than inhabiting them, songs comment on scenes instead of expressing them, placards announce outcomes in advance so that attention shifts from what will happen to how and why it happens, and the machinery of the stage stays visible. The spectator is repositioned as a judge of arrangements that could be otherwise.

Brecht's direct engagement with film was limited and partly frustrated. The lawsuit over the film version of The Threepenny Opera in 1931 produced his essay on the Threepenny process, a remarkable document in which he argues that the film industry, as an apparatus of manufacture and distribution, will absorb and neutralise material entrusted to it regardless of the intentions of authors. That argument anticipates the apparatus tradition by four decades, though its target is the industry rather than the viewing situation. His collaborations in exile, including work connected with the film Hangmen Also Die of 1943, showed him the limits of an author's control inside a commercial studio. The Brechtian film tradition proper was therefore built by others, after his death, using his theatrical principles as a programme for the screen.

### What does distanciation ask a spectator to do?

Distanciation asks the spectator to observe a represented action as one arrangement among possible arrangements, and to judge it. Devices that break absorption, direct address, visible machinery, songs that comment rather than express, titles that spoil suspense, return attention from the fate of a character to the conditions that produced the fate.

### Why does the Brechtian programme refuse absorption?

Absorption, in this analysis, reconciles. A spectator who has wept with a character has discharged the emotion the situation aroused, and the discharge substitutes for judgement about the situation's causes. Refusing the devices of absorption is therefore not an aesthetic preference for coldness.

The filmmakers who pursued the programme in the late 1960s and early 1970s worked explicitly under Brecht's name. Jean-Luc Godard, after a decade inside commercial cinema, formed the Dziga Vertov Group with Jean-Pierre Gorin in 1968, naming the collective after the montage pioneer and treating the borrowing as a declaration: films were to be made politically, not merely to carry political subjects. La Chinoise of 1967 stages discussion as its action; Weekend of the same year interrupts its narrative with intertitles, direct address, and a traffic jam that becomes an essay on consumption; Le Gai Savoir of 1969 reduces the fiction to two figures in a dark studio examining words and images as materials. Jean-Marie Straub and Danièle Huillet, working in Germany and Italy, built a cinema of uninflected delivery, held landscape shots, and texts spoken against their images: Not Reconciled of 1965 compresses a novel into fragments that refuse psychological continuity, and The Chronicle of Anna Magdalena Bach of 1968 presents music as labour, with performers filmed at work in period dress and real time given to the effort of playing. Alexander Kluge in Germany pursued a related practice of interruption and montage of heterogeneous materials, beginning with Yesterday Girl of 1966.

Two features unite these otherwise dissimilar filmmakers. Each treats the spectator's activity as the point of the exercise, so that gaps, contradictions, and unassimilated materials are not faults but the spaces where judgement is meant to occur. Each also accepts the cost: such films address small audiences, depend on festivals, cooperatives, and television commissions rather than on the commercial circuit, and are frequently described as difficult by viewers formed on the absorbed model. The Brechtian tradition thus poses a question that the apparatus tradition would shortly answer in a pessimistic register. If the commercial cinema positions its viewer for absorption by the very structure of its manufacture and exhibition, can counter-devices inserted into individual films defeat a positioning that the situation itself imposes? The apparatus theorists thought the situation was the deeper problem, and their argument is the centre of this article.

A qualification belongs here, because the Brechtian inheritance in film is often overstated by admirers. Brecht himself was ambivalent about cinema, valued entertainment more than his austere followers sometimes do, and wrote that a work which taught its audience nothing to enjoy would fail on his own terms. The films named above are also formally various: Godard's collage, Straub and Huillet's austerity, and Kluge's essayistic mixtures share a refusal rather than a style. Students should therefore treat Brechtian film as a programme defined by the function of its devices, interruption in the service of judgement, and not as a checklist of mannerisms such as placards or direct address copied without their purpose.

## The Apparatus Argument: The Claim at Full Strength

The apparatus tradition, developed in French and British journals around 1970, advances the distinctive claim on which this article turns. Its principal text is Jean-Louis Baudry's essay Ideological Effects of the Basic Cinematographic Apparatus, published in the journal Cinéthique in 1970. Baudry asked a question that content analysis never asks. Suppose every film carried a revolutionary message; would cinema, as a machine for showing, still be doing ideological work of another kind underneath the messages? His answer was yes, and the reasoning runs through the technology itself.

The camera, Baudry argued, inherits the monocular perspective codified in Renaissance painting. A quattrocento picture organises space around a single viewpoint, geometrically coherent, with the visible world arranged as though for one eye stationed at the apex of a visual pyramid. The photographic lens was built to that specification and corrects its images toward it, suppressing the distortions that binocular, moving, embodied vision actually lives with. A projected film therefore offers the spectator a world already organised as a spectacle for a centred subject. Add the conditions of exhibition that the commercial cinema standardised: a darkened hall, an immobile body, a luminous rectangle ahead, the projector behind and unheard, the screen functioning as a kind of mirror in which the spectator cannot see himself yet sees everything arranged for him. The spectator is positioned as a unified, all-perceiving subject who appears to command the visible world while doing nothing but receive it.

Ideology, on this account, is not smuggled into cinema by bad content. It is produced by the apparatus, the basic disposition of camera, projection, and seated viewer, before any content is added, because the position of imaginary mastery it constructs rehearses the spectator in exactly the self-understanding that the dominant ideology requires: the individual as origin of meaning, centred, self-present, mistaking a manufactured arrangement for the world simply seen. Baudry supported the argument with an analogy to Plato's cave, whose prisoners, chained and facing shadows cast by a fire behind them, take the shadows for reality. The cinema, he proposed, perfects the cave and adds a refinement the cave lacked: its prisoners pay for their chains and experience the arrangement as freedom and choice.

### What is apparatus theory in film?

the argument that cinema is ideological in its technology rather than its content, because the camera's inherited perspective, the immobile spectator, and the projection situation together position the viewer as a subject who appears to command what is seen. It is now largely rejected as too deterministic.

### What did Baudry claim the basic apparatus does?

Baudry claimed that the disposition of lens, projector, screen, darkness, and seated body constructs a subject position before any story begins. The spectator is placed at the centre of a coherent visible world, granted an impression of mastery over it, and concealed from the labour and machinery that manufactured the impression.

The distinction Baudry later drew between the basic apparatus and the wider disposition of viewing helps students keep the argument straight. The basic apparatus names the equipment and its necessary arrangement: camera, film stock, projector, screen. The wider arrangement, sometimes given the French term dispositif, includes the architecture of the hall, the social ritual of attendance, the darkness, and the spectator's posture and expectations. Critics who refuted a claim about lenses sometimes believed they had refuted a claim about halls, and defenders who shifted between the two senses made the theory harder to test than it needed to be. Precision about which claim is under discussion is the first skill the apparatus debate teaches, and it is exercised again in the rejection section below.

### Why does Renaissance perspective matter to the argument?

The perspective system matters because it is the historical link between a technology and a philosophy of the subject. A picture organised for one stationary eye presents the world as the property of a centred viewer, and that organisation was codified in the same centuries that produced the modern idea of the autonomous individual. Baudry's claim is that the camera inherited the organisation along with the optics, so an ideology of the subject travels inside an apparently neutral technique of representation.

### What work does the cave analogy perform in Baudry's essay?

The analogy supplies the argument with a model of spectatorship as captivity mistaken for knowledge. Prisoners who face only shadows, and who have been chained since childhood, do not experience their condition as deprivation, because they possess no standpoint outside it. Baudry uses the model to argue that cinema's ideological effect is invisible to its audience for structural reasons, which explains why content criticism, conducted from inside the cave, never notices the chains.

The essay's companion piece, written by Baudry a few years later on the impression of reality, extended the argument toward psychoanalysis, comparing the viewing state to dreaming and to the infant's experience before a mirror. Christian Metz carried that extension furthest. In essays of the mid-1970s, gathered around the notion of the imaginary signifier, Metz argued that the cinema's special power lies in the absence of its objects: what the spectator perceives is present as image and absent as thing, so the spectator's own look becomes the load-bearing element of the whole structure, identifying less with characters than with the camera whose viewpoint organises the seen. Where Baudry emphasised the equipment, Metz emphasised the psychic economy of the look, but the family resemblance is plain. Both locate cinema's ideological force in the situation of viewing rather than in the views expressed, and both treat the spectator as a position constructed by that situation.

### How does Metz differ from Baudry on the spectator?

Baudry's spectator is produced by equipment and architecture, a subject effect of the machine. Metz's spectator is produced by a psychic arrangement in which the absent object on screen makes the viewer's own look the real protagonist, and identification attaches primarily to that look and only secondarily to figures in the story. The difference matters because Metz's version can describe degrees and failures of identification, while Baudry's stronger version struggles to describe any variation at all.

## The Theoretical Source: Althusser and Interpellation

Behind the apparatus argument stands the essay on ideology and ideological state apparatuses published by Louis Althusser in 1970, the theoretical source from which the film theorists borrowed their central mechanism. Althusser argued that ideology represents the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence, and that it works by interpellation, the hailing that constitutes a person as a subject who recognises himself in the call. His example was a policeman calling out in the street and the individual who turns around, by that turning becoming a subject of the address. Ideology, in this account, is not a set of false ideas floating above society. It has a material existence in apparatuses and rituals: schools, churches, families, and media, with their timetables, gestures, and repeated acts of recognition.

The film theorists took from Althusser three things. They took the idea that subjectivity itself is an effect to be explained rather than a starting point to be assumed, which licensed the question of how a viewing situation could manufacture a subject. They took the materiality of ideology, which directed attention to equipment, buildings, and rituals instead of to opinions. And they took a problem they never fully solved: Althusser's account is famously short on failure, since interpellation in his telling always works, and a theory of cinema built on it inherited the same difficulty in explaining the spectator who resists, mocks, or simply falls asleep. The companion article on Althusser's apparatuses essay gives the source argument in full; what matters here is the borrowing, and the weakness that travelled with it.

### Where does Althusser enter the film argument?

Althusser enters as the supplier of the mechanism. His account of ideology as a material practice that constitutes subjects by hailing them gave film theorists a way to describe the auditorium as an apparatus in his sense: a ritual arrangement that produces the spectator it addresses. Without that borrowing, the claim that equipment has ideological effects remains a metaphor. With it, the claim becomes a theory, and inherits the theory's weaknesses as well as its reach.

The British journal Screen became the conduit through which these French arguments entered English-language film study, and the label Screen theory attached itself to the whole formation. Through the 1970s the journal published translations of Baudry and Metz alongside work by British writers such as Colin MacCabe and Stephen Heath, who applied the apparatus framework to questions of realism, narrative, and the classic text. MacCabe's account of the classic realist text, first aimed at the novel and the television drama, argued that a hierarchy of discourses inside a work positions the reader or viewer to accept one discourse as the truth of the others, an argument students will meet again in the literary articles of this series. Heath's work on narrative space analysed how films suture the spectator into their spatial constructions, shot by shot. The tradition at its height was therefore not one essay but a research programme, with journals, conferences, and a generation of teachers formed inside it, which is why its later abandonment, described below, was an event in the discipline and not merely a change of fashion.

## A Necessary Distinction: Visual Pleasure and Its Argument

A separate essay of 1975 on visual pleasure and narrative cinema, published in Screen in the same intellectual context as the apparatus writings, is psychoanalytic and feminist rather than Marxist in its argument, and its relation to Marxism is contextual rather than theoretical. Saying so precisely prevents a common conflation that examinations reward students for avoiding. Laura Mulvey's Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema argued that the commercial cinema organises looking along a sexual division: the camera's look, the characters' looks within the fiction, and the spectator's look are aligned so that woman functions as image and man as bearer of the look, with the female figure's presence threatening a castration anxiety that narrative manages either by investigating and punishing her or by fetishising her into a reassuring surface. The mechanisms named are Freudian and Lacanian: scopophilia, castration anxiety, fetishism, the mirror phase. The essay's famous phrase for the aligned structure, the male gaze, names a psychoanalytic organisation of looking, not a relation of production.

Three tests separate the essay from a Marxist argument. Its explanatory machinery is psychoanalytic, since the effects it describes are derived from the structure of the unconscious rather than from ownership, labour, or class. Its evidence is the formal organisation of classical narrative film read through that machinery, not the industry's records. And its proposed remedy, the destruction of pleasure as a radical weapon, calls for an avant-garde that breaks the codes of looking, a programme closer to the Brechtian tradition than to any analysis of who owns the studios. None of this diminishes the essay, which became one of the most influential pieces of film writing of its century and reorganised feminist criticism. It locates the essay correctly, which is what a student citing it in a Marxist framework needs: the essay may be used alongside Marxist analysis, and often is, but citing it as Marxist theory misdescribes both traditions.

### Why is the male gaze so often mistaken for a Marxist concept?

The male gaze is a psychoanalytic and feminist concept, introduced in an essay of 1975 whose mechanisms are scopophilia, fetishism, and castration anxiety rather than class, labour, or ownership. The essay appeared in the same journal and intellectual milieu as the apparatus writings, which explains the frequent conflation.

The conflation persists for understandable reasons. The essay of 1975 shares with apparatus theory a suspicion of pleasure, a focus on the look, and an account of the spectator as constructed rather than free. Its author worked in the same institutions and debates. Survey courses compress the 1970s into a single week, and compression manufactures kinship. A careful student separates the claims by their mechanisms: ask of any argument what does the causing. Where the cause is the organisation of production and the positioning effected by an apparatus in the service of a dominant ideology, the argument is Marxist in the sense used here. Where the cause is the structure of sexual difference as psychoanalysis describes it, the argument belongs to another tradition that happened to share a journal, a decade, and an enemy in the classical cinema's complacency.

## The Seven Categories: Classification by Relation to Ideology

The most immediately usable analytical device in the field comes from an essay of 1969. Jean-Luc Comolli and Jean Narboni, writing in Cahiers du Cinéma under the title Cinema, Ideology, Criticism, proposed that every film be classified by its relation to the dominant ideology, and they distinguished seven categories, labelled in the essay from (a) to (g). The classification appeared a year before Baudry's apparatus essay and shares its premise, that ideology in cinema is a matter of form and situation and not only of message, but it converts the premise into a working grid that a critic can apply to a programme of films in an afternoon. That convertibility is why the grid, unlike the apparatus thesis, was never abandoned: it survives in teaching because it organises judgement without dictating it.

The first category holds films that are wholly imbued with the dominant ideology and transmit it unknowingly, in form and content together. The editors were explicit that this is the great majority of commercial production, films that reproduce the world as the ruling ideas present it and experience that reproduction as simple realism. The second category holds films that attack the ideology on two fronts at once, working directly on political subject matter while also breaking the representational forms through which ideology normally travels. The third holds films whose content is explicitly political, even oppositional, but whose form remains the traditional one of spectacle and identification, so that the politics is delivered inside a packaging that contradicts it. The fourth holds the mirror case: films whose content appears apolitical or escapist while their form, their handling of space, time, or the look, quietly works against the dominant codes. The fifth category, the famous one, holds films that appear to belong entirely to the first category yet are internally fractured, traversed by tensions they cannot resolve, so that a reading can prise them open against themselves. The sixth and seventh concern documentary practice and the claim of direct access to the real: the sixth holds films that present an unmediated slice of life while silently reproducing the ideology of transparency, and the seventh holds work that reflects on its own conditions of production as it proceeds, making the manufacture visible instead of claiming to abolish it.

### How does the Cahiers classification sort films by their relation to ideology?

The classification of 1969 sorts films by their relation to dominant ideology: works wholly transmitting it; works attacking it in content and form together; political content carried in traditional form; apolitical content whose form works against the codes; works seemingly compliant yet internally fractured; documentary claiming transparent access to the real; and documentary that exposes its own manufacture as it proceeds.

### What distinguishes the fractured category from its neighbours?

A film of the first category transmits the dominant ideology smoothly, its form and content in agreement. A fractured film also appears compliant, but its elements pull against each other: a star performance exceeds the moral scheme, a subplot contradicts the resolution, a visual pleasure survives that the story condemns.

The celebrated demonstration of the fifth category came a year later, when the Cahiers editors published a collective reading of John Ford's Young Mr Lincoln of 1939. The film, a Lincoln biography produced inside the studio system, appeared to be a monument of national ideology. The reading attended to what the film could not settle: the play of looks and blockages around its hero, the strange weight given to scenes the plot does not need, the tension between the Lincoln of law and the Lincoln of violence. Whether or not a reader accepts every step, the exercise established a method. A compliant film is not a closed film. Its ideology must be continuously manufactured against materials that resist it, and the resistance leaves marks that criticism can find. Fredric Jameson's later account of the nostalgia film, treated in the article on [fredric jameson postmodernism](/2020/07/15/fredric-jameson-postmodernism/), extends a related thought: works that appear to offer the past as consumable style can be read for the history their style is managing.

### How are the seven categories meant to be used?

The grid is a diagnostic instrument, not a set of pigeonholes for grading films by political merit. A classification states where a film's ideological work is being done, in its content, its form, its fractures, or its claims of transparency, and therefore where a reading should press. Two critics may place the same film differently and both learn from the disagreement, because the argument about placement forces attention to specific formal evidence.

Several cautions govern responsible use. The categories describe relations, not essences, so a film's placement can change as its exhibition context changes: a work of the first category screened in a seminar, interrupted and discussed, is being made to do different work, which is precisely the Brechtian hope. The grid also presumes that a dominant ideology can be specified for the society under discussion, and where that specification is contested the classification inherits the contest. Finally, the seventh category's valorisation of self-reflexive documentary should not harden into a reflex of its own, since a film can display its equipment as a stylistic signature while leaving every other convention untouched. Used with those cautions, the classification remains what its authors built: the fastest route from a general theory of ideology and cinema to a judgement about a particular film that other viewers can test against the screen.

The classification also clarifies what the apparatus thesis could not do. Baudry's argument, aimed at the basic apparatus, applies to all films equally and therefore distinguishes none of them. Comolli and Narboni, writing in the same journal culture a year earlier, built discrimination into their scheme from the start: films differ, their relations to ideology differ, and criticism exists to register the differences. When the strong apparatus claim collapsed, the classificatory practice survived almost untouched, because it had never depended on the claim that equipment determines effect. It depended only on the weaker and defensible claim that form and content can pull in different directions, and that the pulling is legible.

## The Rejection: The Objection at Full Strength

The apparatus thesis stands largely rejected as technologically deterministic, and an honest article must state the objection at full strength before answering what can be answered. The objection, developed by critics including David Bordwell and Noël Carroll in their campaign against what they called Grand Theory, runs as follows. Deriving ideological effects from the properties of the equipment cannot explain why the same equipment produces films that position viewers in radically different ways: a handheld documentary, a Straub and Huillet landscape film, a home movie, and a studio melodrama all pass through lenses, projectors, and screens, yet no common subject position describes their audiences. A theory that predicts the same effect from the same apparatus regardless of the film is refuted by the variety of films. Worse, the thesis treats the viewer as a position rather than as a person who brings a history to the encounter: a trade unionist and a shareholder, a child and a scholar of cinema, a viewer who has read the reviews and a viewer dragged along by friends are assigned one identical place by the theory, while reception research has repeatedly shown that such differences matter to what a screening does.

Carroll's philosophical criticism added a logical charge. Arguments in the apparatus mode, he held, often proceed by association and punning analogy, from mirror to mirror stage to screen, rather than by premises a sceptic can test, and they immunise themselves against counterevidence by treating disagreement as a symptom of the ideology under discussion. Bordwell's complementary charge was historical and empirical: the theory was asserted at a level of generality where no archive, experiment, or audience record could touch it, and film studies made better progress when it traded such total claims for middle-level questions about how particular devices work on particular viewers under describable conditions.

### What exactly did the critics of apparatus theory deny?

The critics denied that an ideological effect can be deduced from equipment. They did not deny that viewing conditions shape experience, that perspective systems carry conventions, or that institutions organise attention. They denied the inference from a shared apparatus to a uniform subject position, pointing to the diversity of films made with identical machinery and the diversity of responses recorded from actual audiences as the evidence the deduction cannot survive.

### Why did the rejection count as an internal correction?

The decisive evidence against the strong thesis came substantially from scholars working inside or beside the tradition itself: reception researchers formed in cultural studies, journal contributors who had published apparatus arguments, and theorists such as Metz who qualified their own earlier claims.

The defeater discipline this series applies to verdicts can be exercised on the rejection itself. The judgement that the apparatus thesis fails would be defeated by evidence that a formal feature intrinsic to the medium produces a consistent viewer position across films with widely differing content and reception, which is the empirical form the claim would have to take. No such evidence has been produced at the level of the basic apparatus. What the record shows instead is variation at every point where variation can be measured: across genres, across exhibition contexts, across audiences, and across the same film screened for different publics. The verdict therefore stands, and it stands in the defeasible form the discipline requires: not as a slogan about determinism, but as a judgement indexed to the evidence that would overturn it.

What survives the rejection should be stated with equal precision, since students often conclude that the whole apparatus episode was an error to be skipped. The conditions of viewing do shape experience, as anyone knows who has watched the same work in a packed hall and on a telephone in daylight. Perspective conventions are conventions, historically made and therefore open to historical analysis. Institutions organise attention through architecture, programming, pricing, and ritual. Each of these is a descendant of the apparatus argument, weakened from a deduction into a set of researchable claims. The tradition's most original proposition was abandoned; the questions it forced onto the agenda, about technology, situation, and subjectivity, were not.

## Reception Studies and the Active Audience

The empirical arm of the correction came from reception research, much of it conducted within the broader Marxist and cultural studies formation rather than against it. Stuart Hall's essay on encoding and decoding, first circulated in 1973 and published in revised form in 1980, proposed that a televisual or cinematic message is encoded within frameworks of meaning at production and decoded by audiences who may take up three broad positions toward it. A dominant-hegemonic decoding accepts the message within its preferred terms. A negotiated decoding accepts the framework in general while reserving exceptions for the viewer's own situation and experience. An oppositional decoding understands the preferred terms and refuses them, reinterpreting the message inside an alternative framework. The scheme's importance for film theory is that it relocates ideological effect from the apparatus to a social encounter, in which class position, education, and organised experience supply the frameworks that decoding uses.

David Morley's audience research of the late 1970s and 1980 put the scheme under empirical pressure, screening programmes for groups differentiated by occupation and politics and recording how differently the same material was read. The results were untidier than any theory wanted: decodings clustered by social position in some respects and scattered unpredictably in others, and Morley himself revised the early findings as the work proceeded. That untidiness is the finding. Audiences are neither the blank subjects of the apparatus thesis nor the free interpreters of a cheerful pluralism. They are people whose readings are patterned by their situations without being dictated by either text or situation alone.

### How does a negotiated reading differ from an oppositional one?

A negotiated reading accepts the general framework a work offers while carving out exceptions where the viewer's own circumstances override it: the overall account is granted, the application to one's own workplace, family, or nation is refused. An oppositional reading rejects the framework itself and reconstructs the material inside an alternative account of how the world works. The distinction matters because most recorded audience resistance is negotiated, partial, and compatible with continued enjoyment of the work.

### What did reception research show that the thesis could not accommodate?

Reception research showed systematic variation where the thesis predicted uniformity. Groups differently placed in the social order produced divergent accounts of identical material, individuals contradicted the position a text had constructed for them while remaining absorbed in it, and the same viewer occupied different positions across a single evening. A theory of the spectator as an effect of equipment has no variable with which to register any of this, which is why the research programme, and not polemic alone, ended the thesis's authority.

For the five-part procedure, reception is therefore not an optional garnish. It is the step at which the analysis submits its claims about viewer position to the only test available, the recorded behaviour of viewers. A constructed position that no audience has ever occupied is a hypothesis about the film's address, valuable as such, but it must be reported as address rather than as effect. That distinction, between what a work offers and what audiences did, is the permanent deposit of the reception turn inside Marxist film analysis, and examiners probe it because it separates students who have understood the tradition's self-correction from students who have memorised its slogans.

## The Procedure: Five Examinations in Order

The practical procedure assembles the surviving parts of the traditions into a sequence a student can perform. A Marxist film analysis examines five things: the mode of production of the film itself, meaning financing, studio structure, labour, and distribution; the formal techniques and what they do; the narrative's resolution of contradictions; the position constructed for the viewer; and reception. The order is the same as the table's, and the order is an argument. Each step constrains the next, so that by the time the analyst reaches the viewer, the claims made there have been disciplined by evidence about manufacture and form, and the final step then tests the whole construction against the record of audiences.

### What does the first step of the five-part analysis examine?

The production step examines how the film came to exist as a manufactured object: who financed it, under what studio or independent structure, with whose labour under what divisions and credits, and through which channels of distribution and exhibition it reached its audiences.

Begin the production step with the credits, which are a legal document before they are a courtesy. Credits record the division of labour, the hierarchy of decision, and the boundary between the company that owns the work and the people who made it. Continue with the trade press of the production period, which reports budgets in the loose sense of scale and intention, disputes between producers and directors, censorship negotiations, and the expectations attached to a release. Company records, where archives preserve them, settle questions that memoirs leave contested. The finding to aim at is not a list of facts but a connection: some feature of the finished work, its length, its ending, its casting, its rating, its genre mixture, explained by the organisation that produced it. A production step that ends without such a connection has gathered background rather than performed analysis.

### What evidence belongs to the production step?

Evidence for the production step is documentary and checkable: contracts and credits for the division of labour, trade reporting for the circumstances of manufacture and release, censorship and classification records for the pressures applied before exhibition, company archives for decisions and their reasons.

The formal step requires the analyst to watch with a notebook and describe techniques before interpreting them. Editing patterns, the construction of space across cuts, the placement and movement of the camera, the relation of sound to image, the management of looks within and toward the fiction: each must first be established as fact about the work. Interpretation then states what the established technique does. Continuity editing, for instance, constructs a coherent space and a centred viewpoint while concealing its own operations, a description that connects the formal step forward to the viewer position step and backward to the montage tradition's insistence that the cut is where meaning is manufactured. The error to avoid is the catalogue, a list of devices with no account of their work, which examiners recognise instantly as description wearing analysis's clothes.

The narrative step asks which contradiction the story sets running and which device stops it. Stories in the commercial tradition typically open a disturbance, in property, desire, law, or kinship, and close by settling it through marriage, inheritance, exposure, death, departure, or reconciliation. The Marxist question is not whether the settlement is happy but what it costs and what it leaves untouched: which character's claim is quietly dropped, which institution is restored without examination, which coincidence arrives to spare the social order a reckoning. A settlement achieved by a discovered will or a returning fortune differs analytically from one achieved by a change in the arrangements that produced the disturbance, and the difference is the finding.

### How should a student treat the resolution of a narrative?

Treat the resolution as the point where a film's ideology does its most concentrated work, because the ending must reconcile the audience to some state of the world. Identify the contradiction the plot opened, name the device that closes it, and list what the device leaves unresolved or unmentioned. An ending that settles a conflict of property by a marriage has answered a question about feeling and left the question about property exactly where the film found it.

The viewer position step gathers the apparatus tradition's usable residue. The analyst specifies the place the film constructs: where the camera puts the spectator relative to the action, whose look the spectator shares and in what order, what knowledge the spectator holds beyond or below the characters', how the fiction acknowledges or refuses the spectator's presence. Stated at that level of specificity, position claims become testable against the film and arguable between critics. Stated as a universal effect of cinema itself, they repeat the error the tradition corrected. The step ends with a hypothesis, and the reception step exists to test it.

## A Worked Example in Five Steps

The worked example uses a generically described narrative structure, of a kind the studio era produced in quantity, so that no proprietary work is needed and every step can be followed on the description alone. Imagine a melodrama of inheritance. A family firm faces ruin. The founder's will is contested between a dutiful son who has worked in the business and a charming nephew who has not. A clerk in the firm's office, in love with the son, discovers an irregularity in the accounts that implicates the nephew's adviser. The founder dies midway. At the close, the irregularity is exposed in a public scene, the nephew withdraws abroad, the clerk marries the son, and the firm is saved under its heir. Generations of audiences found such structures satisfying, which is what makes the structure worth analysing: satisfaction is an effect with causes.

The production step begins with the kind of object this is. A story of this shape, in the studio period, would typically be an A picture or a prestige programme item, built around contracted stars whose casting precedes the script's final form, shot on standing sets representing the office and the house, and scheduled for a release window in which family melodrama performed reliably. Its division of labour is the studio's: a producer unit assigning a director, writers working in sequence under contract, a music department scoring to a template of cues. The finding the step reaches is that the story's shape answers to manufacture at several points. The public exposure scene exists partly because a set-piece climax was a known quantity in the release pattern. The clerk's prominence reflects the casting of a female star whose contract guaranteed her the moral centre. The ending's restoration of the firm satisfies a censorship environment in which the exposure of wrongdoing had to conclude with order visibly restored and wrongdoing located in individuals rather than in the conduct of business as such.

The formal step describes how the film organises attention. The office is shot in deep, legible space, desks in rows, the hierarchy of the room readable at a glance. The house is shot warmly and shallow, faces close, backgrounds soft. Editing follows the classical pattern: establishing views, exchanges of looks, reaction shots that tell the audience whose feelings govern a scene. The clerk is granted reaction shots out of proportion to her place in the firm's hierarchy, which is the formal mechanism by which the audience is taught to value her judgement before the plot vindicates it. Sound bridges carry dialogue across cuts in the office scenes, smoothing the institution's continuity, while the exposure scene is cut harder, with pauses and isolated faces, so that the institution's smoothness breaks exactly where its secret is spoken.

The narrative step identifies the contradiction and its settlement. The story opens a genuine problem: the firm as a family inheritance and the firm as a going concern obey different logics, and the contest between son and nephew stages that difference as a contest of character. The resolution settles the problem by a device that changes the subject. The exposure of the adviser's irregularity converts a structural question, how should such an enterprise pass between generations, into a moral one, who behaved dishonestly, and the marriage then seals the conversion by uniting the heir with the employee whose honesty saved him. What is left untouched is everything structural: the firm's dependence on one family's ownership, the clerk's own position as an employee whose reward is marriage out of employment, the workforce whose livelihoods hung on the outcome and who appear in the story only as background. The ending is satisfying precisely because the device is efficient, and naming the efficiency is the analysis.

The viewer position step specifies the place constructed for the spectator. The audience is placed with the clerk: it learns the irregularity when she does, shares her looks at documents and doors, and is denied the nephew's private scenes until late, so that suspicion is organised as loyalty. In the exposure scene the camera grants the audience a position superior to every character's, assembling the whole room in legible space while each participant sees a fragment, a modest and pleasurable mastery that the apparatus theorists would recognise. The conclusion the step must state, and the worked example exists to produce, is this: the film constructs a viewer who experiences the restoration of the existing order as the triumph of honesty and love, because the viewer's knowledge, sympathy, and spatial command have been aligned throughout with the characters whose victory is that restoration. The position is not an opinion the film states. It is a seat the film builds, and the building can be pointed to, shot by shot.

The reception step then asks what audiences did with the seat. For a generic structure of this period, the evidence is indirect: the durability of the formula across decades of production is itself reception evidence, since studios repeated what audiences rewarded; reviews of the period praised the warmth of the resolution and the performance of the clerk; fan writing dwelt on the romance rather than the accounts. Nothing in the record suggests that audiences decoded the settlement oppositionally, and the honest conclusion is that the constructed position was, for its first publics, largely occupied. The procedure ends there, with a claim about address supported by a claim about response, each stated at its own level of certainty.

## A Second Worked Passage: Montage as Analysis

The worked example above passes lightly over the formal step, so a shorter passage on a named work of the founding period shows the step at full stretch. Battleship Potemkin, made by Eisenstein in 1925 for a state commission marking an anniversary of the 1905 revolution, retells a naval mutiny and its aftermath in five movements. The commission belongs to the production step: a state studio, a commemorative brief, a director formed in agitprop theatre, and a subject chosen from the prehistory of the revolution the state claimed descent from. The film's formal organisation is the analysis's real object. Its editing does not pursue the smooth legibility of the commercial cinema of its decade. Shots are composed for graphic conflict, diagonals against verticals, masses against individuals, machinery against bodies, and sequences are built so that the spectator assembles a judgement about the relations between sailors, officers, and the city that receives the ship.

The celebrated movement set on the harbour steps in Odessa demonstrates intellectual montage at its most concentrated without needing detailed description here. Its construction alternates the agents of violence with its victims, inserts images that belong to no continuous space, a famous trio of stone lions in successive postures edited to suggest a single beast rising, and stretches a brief event across a duration far beyond its clock time. Each device serves the method of 1929: the spectator is not shown a massacre and left to feel; the spectator is given collisions from which a conclusion about the nature of the regime's violence is assembled as the viewer's own act. The stone lions are the textbook instance, since three static shots of carved animals produce, in sequence, a movement and a meaning, rising in protest, that no one shot contains.

The viewer position constructed by such a film differs sharply from the seat built by the inheritance melodrama, and the contrast is the lesson. The melodrama aligns the spectator with a character and a settlement. Potemkin aligns the spectator with a process and a judgement, offering identification with a collective protagonist, the crew and the city, rather than with a private destiny. The reception step complicates the triumph in the usual way: the film's first domestic release drew modest audiences compared with imported entertainments, its canonisation was substantially the work of foreign critics, exhibitors, and film societies, and its influence on filmmakers vastly exceeded its direct public. A Marxist analysis reports all of that without embarrassment, because the procedure's final step exists to keep the earlier steps honest.

### Can any formal feature be shown to position every viewer?

The honest answer, after the tradition's self-correction, is that no feature has been shown to position every viewer. Features differ in the strength and specificity of the invitation they extend: a point of view structure shared by a whole genre invites more uniformly than an idiosyncratic device in a single work.

## The Political Economy of Film

The first row of the table expands into a subfield of its own, the political economy of film, which studies cinema as an industry: the concentration of ownership, the organisation of labour, the management of risk, and the structure of markets. Its questions are continuous with the production step but pursued at the level of the system rather than the single work. How does the political economy of film work, as a research practice? It begins from the observation that films are expensive to make and cheap to reproduce, a cost structure that rewards scale, favours owners of distribution over makers of individual works, and turns every production into a wager that must be hedged by genre, stars, sequels, and presold properties. From that structure it derives the industry's characteristic institutions and then checks the derivation against the historical record.

The classical studio system of Hollywood between the 1920s and the 1940s remains the subfield's central case because its organisation was so complete. A handful of major companies combined production, distribution, and exhibition, owning the theatre chains through which their pictures were guaranteed an outlet. Talent was held under long contracts, production was organised in units on factory lines, and practices such as block booking required exhibitors to take a studio's output in bundles, strong pictures carrying weak ones. The antitrust decision of 1948 in the Paramount case, which forced the majors to divest their theatres and ended several of the bundling practices, is the subfield's standard example of the state redrawing an industry's structure, and of how slowly the habits formed under one structure die after the structure is removed: the majors retained distribution, and distribution, as the theory predicts, proved to be where the durable power lay.

### What does vertical integration explain about a studio film?

Vertical integration, the ownership of production, distribution, and exhibition by one company, explains why studio era films look like members of families rather than isolated works. A company that owns its outlets can plan output as a slate, assign its contracted talent across the slate, standardise lengths and genres to its theatres' programmes, and treat any single picture's failure as a cost absorbed by the system. The individual film's compromises are often the slate's logic made visible.

### What can the division of labour on a production tell an analyst?

The division of labour tells the analyst where decisions could be made and by whom. A credit block that separates story, screenplay, and dialogue among different hands, a music department rather than a composer engaged for the picture, an editor employed by the studio rather than chosen by the director: each records a distribution of authority. Where authority sits, taste follows, and the analyst who maps the labour process can explain features of the finished work that intention talk leaves mysterious.

Anthropology supplied an early classic of the subfield in Hortense Powdermaker's Hollywood, the Dream Factory of 1950, which treated the studios as a social system with its own kinship, taboos, and myths, and reported the industry's own account of itself with an outsider's ear. Later scholarship, including the work of Douglas Gomery on the industry's business history and of Janet Wasko on financing and distribution, professionalised the approach. The subfield's findings feed directly back into criticism. A scholar who knows how a release was financed, which markets it had to satisfy, and what its producers feared from censors and exhibitors reads its ending differently from a scholar who knows only its director's other films. That is the production step operating at career length, and it is why the five-part table puts manufacture first.

### How does distribution shape what a film can be?

Distribution shapes content in advance, because producers design works for the outlets that exist. A market organised around a small number of wide openings rewards premises that can be summarised in a sentence and advertised in a poster; a market of repertory houses and festivals rewards duration, difficulty, and signature. Exhibition windows, censorship regimes in export territories, and the expectations of buyers at markets all enter the production meeting before a frame is shot, and the analyst can often recover them from the shape of the finished work.

Contemporary industry structures must be described generically in an article designed to last, and the generic description is not difficult, because the underlying pressures persist through changes of technology and ownership. Production remains concentrated in a small number of large firms that combine financing with distribution and increasingly with direct exhibition to households. Risk is managed by reliance on established properties, by international presales, and by the concentration of marketing expenditure on a narrow band of releases expected to perform at scale. Labour remains project based and precarious for most participants, organised through guilds and unions whose agreements set the terms the credits record. Independent production survives in the interstices, financed by patchworks of public funds, broadcasters, and private equity, and oriented toward festivals as its market of first resort. A student equipped with the studio system as a model can analyse these arrangements by asking which functions have been combined, where distribution power sits, and how risk is being hedged, and the answers will date more slowly than any list of company names.

## Labour, Stars, and the Manufacture of Consent to the System

The labour process of filmmaking rewards a closer look than criticism usually gives it, because the set is a workplace before it is a place of art. A studio era production divided work among hundreds of specialists whose contributions were coordinated by schedules and budgets rather than by a single vision, and the director's authority operated inside that coordination the way a conductor's operates inside an orchestra's employment contracts. Credits, again, are the primary evidence, and they repay attention to their order and their absences: the uncredited rewrites, the second units, the editors who restructured performances in the cutting room. A Marxist analysis treats the romantic figure of the solitary author as one of the industry's own products, useful in marketing and in copyright law, where a work must have an owner and an origin, and misleading in criticism, where the question is how the thing was actually made.

The star system concentrates the point. A star is at once a worker, a product, and a piece of capital: a person whose image has been built by investment and whose presence in a picture reduces the risk of the investment that the picture represents. Star studies within the tradition, following the work of Richard Dyer on the star image, analyse the persona as a constructed text assembled from films, publicity, and commentary, and ask what social contradictions the persona manages. A star who embodies both luxury and ordinariness, both rebellion and respectability, performs the kind of ideological work the narrative step examines in plots: the reconciliation, in one figure, of things the audience experiences as opposed. The analysis of stars therefore belongs inside the five-part procedure, distributed between production, where the image is manufactured, and reception, where audiences complete, eroticise, ironise, or refuse it.

Censorship belongs to the political economy sections as well, since it is a structure and not a series of anecdotes. The American Production Code, adopted in 1930 and enforced with real sanctions from 1934 until its replacement by a ratings system in 1968, specified what could be shown and, more consequentially, how stories had to end: crime could not profit, authority had to be vindicated in the settlement, transgression required punishment or repentance. Read through the narrative step of the procedure, the Code is a machine for manufacturing resolutions of a determinate kind, and a generation of Hollywood endings becomes legible as compliance with an industrial rule rather than as the moral taste of individual writers. Other national cinemas worked under state censorship of a more direct kind, and the Soviet directors of the founding generation discovered that a state monopoly of production could encourage formal experiment in one decade and proscribe it in the next, when the doctrine of socialist realism was enforced and Eisenstein's projects were shelved or recut. The lesson generalises: whoever finances and licenses production sets the outer bounds of the sayable, and the interesting critical questions concern the room that artists find inside the bounds.

### Why do endings from the studio era so often restore authority?

Endings restored authority because an industrial rule required it and an industrial structure enforced the rule before release. A censorship code with sanctions, applied at script stage by an office the studios themselves maintained, filtered out settlements in which crime prospered or institutions stood condemned. The restored ending is therefore evidence about manufacture first and about the beliefs of audiences or writers only second, if at all.

## Ideology in Content and Ideology in Form

The distinction between ideology carried in content and ideology carried in the form of viewing is the conceptual hinge of the whole field, and students should be able to draw it without notes. Content ideology is what a work says and shows: its represented world, its distribution of sympathy, its explicit claims. Form ideology is what the work's organisation presupposes and rehearses: the centred spectator of the perspective system, the invisible manufacture of the continuity style, the hierarchy of discourses in which one voice is granted the truth of the others, the closure that presents a settlement as the world's own shape. The apparatus argument was the extreme statement of the form side. The seven categories are the working instrument built on the distinction, since a film can be conservative in content and disruptive in form, radical in content and reassuring in form, or any other combination, and the grid exists to keep the combinations distinct.

### Why must content and the form of viewing be graded separately?

Content ideology concerns what a film represents and endorses: its story, sympathies, and explicit judgements. Form ideology concerns what the organisation of the viewing presupposes: the centred spectator, the concealed manufacture, the hierarchy among voices, the shape of closure. A film may be oppositional in the first and compliant in the second, and an analysis that fails to separate the two will grade the film's speeches while missing its seating.

The separation also protects against a lazy symmetry in which every formal device is assigned a fixed political valence. Continuity editing is not eternally and everywhere an ideological anaesthetic; it is a convention whose effects depend on what a given work does with it, and the Cahiers critics themselves found fractures inside the most classical products. Conversely, fragmentation and direct address are not eternally emancipatory; advertising absorbed both within a decade of their avant-garde prestige. The tradition at its best treats devices as having histories and tendencies rather than essences, which is another way of stating the lesson of the apparatus rejection: grade every claim, specify the evidence, and name what would change the judgement.

A useful classroom exercise follows from the distinction. Take two works of opposed content organised by the same form, and two works of similar content organised by opposed forms, and require the analysis to say where the ideological work sits in each case. The exercise is harder than it sounds, because content is loud and form is quiet, and students discover in themselves the habit the tradition exists to correct: the habit of believing that a work's politics are exhausted by the opinions of its characters. The montage directors knew better in 1925, since their whole method consisted of making form argue. The apparatus theorists radicalised the insight into a claim about the machine. The categories and the procedure are what remain when the radicalisation is given up and the insight is kept.

## Jameson, the Nostalgia Film, and the Waning of History

The tradition's most influential later development inside film study came through Fredric Jameson's account of postmodernism, in which the nostalgia film serves as a central exhibit. Jameson's argument, developed in essays of the 1980s and in his book of 1991, holds that a culture organised by late capitalism progressively loses its sense of history as a lived process and replaces it with a stock of styles and images of the past, consumable as pastiche. The nostalgia film, his term for works that approach a past period through the glossy conventions of its representation rather than through any engagement with its conflicts, exemplifies the replacement: the past returns as a look, and the look is reassuring precisely because nothing in it can make a claim on the present. The full argument, with its account of the waning of affect and the cultural logic it names, is treated in the companion article on Jameson and postmodernism, and students should read the two accounts together.

### What is the nostalgia film, and why does it matter here?

The nostalgia film approaches a past era through the accumulated style of its earlier representations, offering the period as an image to be savoured rather than a history to be understood. It matters to Marxist film theory because it extends the form argument into the analysis of historical consciousness: a cinema that converts history into style performs ideological work at the level of a whole culture's relation to its own past, whatever the content of any single work.

Jameson's intervention also illustrates the tradition's migration. By the 1980s the centre of Marxist film argument had moved from the auditorium's machinery to the culture's stock of images, and from a theory of the subject to a theory of periodisation. The move had costs. Claims about a whole culture's relation to history are as hard to test as claims about the apparatus were, and the same demand for defeaters applies: an account of the nostalgia film should state what a film engaging its period's conflicts directly would look like, and several candidate works complicate any blanket diagnosis. The value of the Jamesonian strand for the student is twofold. It supplies the strongest available version of the claim that form carries ideology at scale, and it demonstrates that the tradition continued to generate new objects of analysis after its most famous thesis was given up.

## Misreadings to Correct

Five misreadings recur in student work and in survey coverage, and each has been corrected in the body of this article, in the sections where the correction belongs. They are gathered here because examiners ask about them directly. The first is that Marxist film theory looks for political messages. It does not; it examines manufacture, technique, resolution, position, and reception, and it treats messages as the least interesting stratum of a work. The second is that the male gaze essay is a Marxist argument. It is a psychoanalytic and feminist argument published in a neighbouring context, powerful on its own terms and misdescribed when conscripted. The third is that apparatus theory states the field's present position. It states a position the field's own members largely abandoned as too deterministic, and citing it without its fate is the surest sign of a survey read too quickly. The fourth is that Soviet montage is only a historical style. The style is historical; the proposition that juxtaposition manufactures concepts describes the medium's permanent resources and is exercised daily by editors who have never heard Eisenstein's name. The fifth is that the tradition ignores the audience. Reception research grew inside the tradition's own institutions, corrected its strongest claim, and holds a permanent place as the procedure's fifth step.

### Do Marxists think films are propaganda?

Some films are propaganda, and the tradition has the vocabulary to say which and why: works commissioned to persuade, whose production, form, and address are organised by that commission. The general claim is different. It holds that all films are manufactured objects whose techniques and situations do ideological work, most of it unintended, and that propaganda is the limiting case where the work is intended.

### How do you analyse a film politically without reducing it to its message?

Follow the procedure and let the message come last. Establish the manufacture, describe the techniques and their work, name the contradiction and its settlement, specify the seat built for the viewer, and check the record of audiences. A political analysis conducted in that order will usually find that the message is the smallest part of the film's politics, and that the larger part was visible all along in the credits, the cutting, and the ending's machinery.

## The Strongest Objection, Answered

The strongest objection to the whole enterprise deserves a section of its own, beyond its appearance in the rejection of the apparatus thesis, because it is aimed at the surviving procedure as well. Stated at full strength, and attributed to the critics who made it most sharply, Noël Carroll and David Bordwell, it runs: film theory in the Marxist and psychoanalytic mode proceeds by applying a prefabricated doctrine to works, finding in each film exactly what the doctrine predicts, and treating the uniformity of its findings as confirmation. A method that cannot be surprised by its object is not analysing the object. It is illustrating the doctrine, and its readings of individual films are interchangeable because the film was never the real subject.

The answer begins with a concession. The charge describes a real failure mode, and every teacher of the method has marked essays that commit it: the production step skipped, the formal description replaced by labels, the ending convicted in advance, the audience never consulted. Against that failure mode the procedure's design is itself the defence, since its steps are ordered to force contact with evidence that can disappoint the analyst: archives that contradict the expected story of manufacture, techniques that refuse the expected description, audiences whose recorded responses break the expected pattern. The worked example above was built to show a reading reaching a conclusion its steps earned, and the reception step's honest report, that the constructed position was largely occupied, is a finding the doctrine did not dictate in advance, since an oppositional record would have been reported with equal readiness.

What the apparatus argument does establish, even in defeat, should be recorded as the objection's residue. Viewing has conditions, and the conditions are manufactured: buildings, machines, conventions of representation, and rituals of attendance are made things, made by identifiable institutions for identifiable reasons, and they are not neutral merely because they are familiar. A criticism that attends only to what films say will never describe the conditions under which saying is heard. The tradition's permanent contribution is to have made those conditions a critical object. Its permanent lesson, taught by its own history, is that claims about the conditions must be graded like any others, which is the defeater discipline once more: the apparatus verdict stands because the evidence that would reverse it, a consistent viewer position produced by a medium-intrinsic feature across differing content and reception, has not appeared, and the day it appears the verdict should be revised in print.

## Where the Argument Stands: A Graded Summary

A student leaving this article should be able to grade the tradition's claims rather than recite them. At the top of the scale, as established as anything in criticism, sits the proposition that films are manufactured objects whose organisation of production shapes their form and content, together with the montage proposition that juxtaposition manufactures meaning. In the middle sits the family of claims about form and ideology: that techniques organise attention and sympathy in describable directions, that endings perform settlements whose costs can be itemised, that a work's elements can fracture against its apparent thesis. These claims are as strong as the evidence of the individual reading, which is why the procedure insists on description before verdict. Lower, as a hypothesis about address rather than a law of effect, sits the viewer position: real as a construction inside works, variable in its occupation by audiences. At the bottom, abandoned by the tradition that proposed it, sits the strong apparatus thesis, the claim that the machine itself guarantees a subject. The claim and its fate, taken together, are the field's most instructive possession, because they show a critical tradition proposing its most original idea, testing it against the world, and giving it up in public. Few schools of criticism can show so clean an instance of self-correction, and students who grasp why it happened understand the field better than students who can list its terms.

### Where should a beginner start with this literature?

Start with the montage writings, where the claims are concrete and the films are available, then read the classification of 1969 and perform it on a programme of contrasting works. Take the apparatus essay of 1970 third, with its critics beside it, and the reception work fourth.

### Is apparatus theory still taught?

The essay of 1970 is taught everywhere as a historical monument and a provocation, and its questions about technology and spectatorship organise syllabuses even where its answer is rejected. What is no longer taught, except as an episode, is the answer itself in its strong form.

## The Industry Described Generically

Because an evergreen article must not be fixed in time by its examples, the contemporary organisation of the industry has been described in this article only at the level of structure, and a final gathering of that description may help students apply it. Production is financed by a handful of major companies, with a long tail of independents behind them. Distribution is the commanding height, whether exercised through theatrical chains, broadcasters, or household delivery systems operated by the same firms that finance production. Labour is project based, guild organised, and divided into the specialisms the credits record. Exhibition is stratified between a narrow band of heavily marketed releases and everything else, which competes for attention in festivals, repertory programming, and catalogues. Each element of that description corresponds to a structure the studio system already exhibited in its classical form, which is why the historical case retains its analytical value: the analyst who understands how the majors combined the functions can identify the same combinations under new technical arrangements, and can ask the political economy questions, who finances, who distributes, who labours, who bears the risk, of any arrangement whatever.

### How should an analyst handle an industry that keeps changing its technology?

Analyse functions rather than devices. Technologies of capture, distribution, and display succeed one another rapidly, while the functions of financing, manufacture, circulation, and exhibition persist and their combination or separation determines where power sits. A claim pitched at the level of functions survives the replacement of the devices that perform them, which is the exact respect in which the apparatus thesis, pitched at the level of one historically specific arrangement of devices, did not.

The generic description also disciplines the critic's temptation to announce revolutions. Each new arrangement of the industry has been greeted, in its season, as the end of the old order's power, and each has reproduced the old order's central features in new dress: concentration at the point of distribution, risk hedged by the familiar, labour kept flexible and cheap. A Marxist analysis is not required to be pessimistic about such arrangements, and the reception evidence forbids any simple story of audiences captured. It is required to be unimpressed by novelty as such, and to ask of every proclaimed transformation the production step's questions. Who owns the new channel? Whose labour builds and maintains it? What does its architecture make easy to watch, and what does it bury? Those questions were first asked of the studio system, and they have not aged.

## Form Histories: Continuity and Its Alternatives

A fuller account of the formal step requires a short history of the dominant style and its challengers, since no technique does its work in a vacuum. The continuity system, codified in Hollywood between roughly 1917 and 1960 and exported worldwide, organises space and time for maximum legibility: an establishing view orients the spectator, eyelines match across cuts, movement is matched on action, the axis of action is respected so that screen direction stays stable, and sound, especially dialogue, binds the fragments into a continuous present. The system's defenders describe it as a neutral craft of clarity. The tradition examined here describes it as a rhetoric: a way of presenting a manufactured world as though it simply unfolded before a witness, with the labour of construction concealed by the smoothness of the joins. Both descriptions can be true of the same cut, and the analyst's task is to specify what the rhetoric does in the work at hand.

The alternatives define the system's edges. The montage cinema of the 1920s broke continuity for argument. The deep focus and long take practices associated with directors such as Jean Renoir in the 1930s and Orson Welles in the 1940s preserved spatial unity within the shot and shifted composition's burden from editing to staging, a shift the critic André Bazin championed as a more respectful relation to reality and to the spectator's freedom of attention. The Brechtian cinemas of the late 1960s broke continuity for judgement. The art cinema of postwar Europe developed its own conventions, of drift, ambiguity, and the unresolved ending, which became a style as codified as the one it opposed. Each alternative began as a refusal and settled into a repertoire, available thereafter to any producer for any purpose, which is why the analysis of form must always be completed by the analysis of production and reception: the same device is a critique in one arrangement and a perfume advertisement in another.

### What does Bazin's preference for the long take teach a Marxist analyst?

Bazin's preference teaches that the critique of editing was never the property of one camp. A critic outside the Marxist tradition argued, on grounds of respect for reality and for the spectator's freedom, against the manipulative cut and for compositions that leave ambiguity intact. The convergence instructs the analyst to evaluate devices by their work in context rather than by their pedigree, since a device praised by a realist and a device praised by a dialectician can be the same device.

Welles's work of the 1940s, Citizen Kane of 1941 above all, is the standard classroom case because it sits at the crossing of the systems: a studio production that used deep focus, long takes, and a fractured narration inside a commercial career, and whose plot, a newspaper magnate's life assembled from conflicting testimonies, performs a kind of montage of discourses without abandoning spatial coherence. The Cahiers tradition could read such a work under the fractured category, and generations of teachers have done so. The case also disciplines enthusiasm: Kane was a prestige production by a major studio's subsidiary arrangements, marketed on its director's celebrity from radio and theatre, and its formal daring was itself a selling point within the system it is said to subvert. Form, production, and reception must be read together or not at all.

## Benjamin and the Reproducible Work

Walter Benjamin's essay on the work of art in the age of its technological reproducibility, written in 1935 and revised over the following years, stands behind the whole field as a founding meditation, and students meet it in the profile of [walter benjamin marxism profile](/2015/08/01/walter-benjamin-marxism-profile/). Benjamin argued that mechanical reproduction dissolves what he called the aura of the unique work, its embeddedness in tradition and ritual, and relocates art's social function from cult to exhibition and politics. Film was his central case: a work designed for reproduction from the start, assembled by an apparatus, tested by audiences whose distracted mode of reception he refused to simply deplore. Where the apparatus theorists of 1970 would see the cinema's machinery as the guarantee of a subjected spectator, Benjamin, writing before the classical system had finished consolidating, saw in the same machinery a training ground for new habits of perception and a possible instrument of politicisation.

The contrast between Benjamin and Baudry is one of the most instructive in the field, because the two arguments share premises and divide on the conclusion. Both hold that the technology is not neutral. Both refuse to locate a work's politics in its stated content. They differ on what the machinery does to its public: Benjamin's spectator is distracted, tactile, testing, a figure with critical potential; Baudry's is regressed, centred, dreaming, a figure produced as ideology's subject. The reception research of later decades can be read as an attempt to arbitrate between the two portraits with evidence, and its untidy findings suggest that each portrait captured a possibility of the medium rather than its law. A student who can stage that arbitration has understood more of the tradition than a student who can date every essay in it.

### Why does Benjamin's spectator differ so sharply from Baudry's?

The two writers observed the cinema at different moments of its industrial history and weighted different features of the situation. Benjamin attended to reproduction, collectivity, and the shock effects of montage in a medium still visibly new. Baudry attended to the perfected classical auditorium, its darkness and immobility, and to a psychoanalytic model of regression. The disagreement is therefore partly empirical and partly a choice of model, which is why later evidence could arbitrate parts of it without settling the whole.

## Comolli and the Ideology of the Visible

Between the Cahiers classification and the apparatus essay's aftermath stands Jean-Luc Comolli's series Technique and Ideology, published in the journal Cahiers du Cinéma across 1971 and 1972, which gave the form argument its most detailed technical development. Comolli examined the history of the camera's own conventions: the pursuit of depth of field, the standardisation of lenses and of the frequency of frames per second, the ideology of the visible that demanded ever more complete and legible images. His central claim was that technical choices in cinema are never merely technical. The demand for depth, for instance, presents itself as a demand for realism, for more of the world in the frame, but the realism it serves is a specific historical code, the code of a world arranged for a mastering eye, and each technical advance celebrated as a conquest of reality is also a refinement of that code.

The series matters to students for its method as much as its conclusions. Comolli read patents, trade journals, and manufacturers' claims alongside films, treating the industry's own technical literature as ideological evidence. That is the production step extended into the history of the equipment itself, and it supplies the strongest available answer to the charge that form analysis floats free of material history. Where Baudry deduced the perspective code from philosophy, Comolli documented its installation, decision by decision, in the workshops and standards committees of an industry. Later critics have qualified his readings of particular episodes, and the qualification is welcome, since it treats his work as history rather than scripture, but the method survives the qualifications.

### What does the demand for depth of field reveal, on Comolli's account?

The demand reveals a code presenting itself as nature. Depth of field was pursued and marketed as an increase in realism, yet the deep, legible image answers to a specific organisation of the visible, one in which the world is arrayed in coherent space before a single viewpoint. Comolli's point is not that depth is a trick. His point is that a preference among possible images was installed as the definition of the real, and that the installation can be documented in the industry's own records.

## The Classic Realist Text and the Hierarchy of Discourses

Colin MacCabe's contribution, developed in essays of the 1970s and aimed first at the novel and then at film and television drama, gave the tradition a way to discuss works whose ideology sits neither in machinery nor in message but in the arrangement of voices. The classic realist text, in his account, contains a hierarchy of discourses: characters speak, events unfold, and one discourse, typically an unmarked narrative voice or a framing perspective, is positioned as the metalanguage that tells the truth about all the others. The reader or viewer is invited to occupy the position of that metalanguage, from which the conflicts among the lower discourses resolve into intelligibility. Ideology, here, is the effect of the hierarchy: the world appears as a place where a correct account exists and is available to whoever takes up the offered seat.

Applied to film, the model illuminates the ordinary prestige drama more readily than the avant-garde, which is its virtue. A courtroom picture distributes discourses among witnesses, counsel, and judge, and grants the verdict the status of truth about the whole proceeding. A biographical picture grants its framing narration authority over the memories it shows. The model also predicts its own fractures: works in which no discourse secures the metalanguage position, in which the framing voice is itself ironised or contradicted by what it frames, fall toward the fifth of the Cahiers categories, and the two schemes lock together into a single analytical practice. MacCabe's later work on particular directors extended the argument into detailed readings, and the concept entered the general vocabulary of film and literary study, taught by many who would not describe themselves as Marxists at all, another instance of the device out-travelling the doctrine.

### How does a hierarchy of discourses position a viewer?

A work that arranges its voices so that one of them silently judges the rest offers the viewer that judging place as his own. Occupying it feels like understanding rather than obedience, which is the mechanism's elegance: the viewer experiences agreement with the work's framing as the exercise of his own intelligence. Analysis breaks the spell by naming the arrangement, showing which voice was granted authority and what that voice declines to examine in itself.

## Applying the Classification: A Programme of Contrasts

The seven categories repay exercise on a constructed programme, and the exercise can be performed on generically described works so that the method, rather than any title, carries the lesson. Take first a glossy adventure picture of the studio era in which explorers restore a rightful ruler to a throne overseas. Content and form agree in celebration of the restoring power: the camera adopts the explorers' viewpoint, the landscape is organised as territory awaiting order, the ending crowns the settlement with a wedding and a salute. Placement in the first category is straightforward, and the reading's interest lies in specifying the mechanisms, the distribution of looks, the geography of the frame, the music's assignment of dignity, rather than in the verdict itself.

Take next a picture of the same era whose story concerns a strike, told sympathetically, with the owners shown as selfish and the settlement granted to the workers, yet photographed and edited entirely in the classical manner, its sympathy delivered through a star performance and a romance that closes the film. The classification places it in the third category, political content in traditional form, and the placement starts the real discussion: whether the form's reassurances, the centred spectator, the individualisation of a collective action into a hero's journey, outweigh the content's sympathies, and for which audiences. The grid does not answer that question. It ensures the question is asked in terms of evidence.

Take third a light comedy of the same period, all mistaken identity and bedroom doors, whose content no censor could fault, but whose construction keeps betraying a fascination with the servants' knowledge of their employers, cutting away at the moments of revelation to the faces of those who already knew. A reading may argue for the fourth category, apolitical content whose form works against the codes of deference, or may judge the cutaways a routine comic device and return the film to the first category with its fractures noted. The argument, conducted at the level of specific shots, is the education. Take finally a western of the classical period that celebrates the founding of a town while staging, in its margins, the cost of the founding to those the town displaces, giving the displaced a gravity of framing and music that the plot's verdict never answers. That is the fifth category's home ground, and the reading will follow the Cahiers precedent on the Ford picture of 1939: locate the elements the film cannot integrate, show the work they do against the film's thesis, and resist the temptation to promote the film into secret opposition, since a fracture is not a programme and a cracked monument is still a monument.

### Why is a fracture not a programme?

A fracture is a tension a work cannot resolve, discoverable in its details and often unintended by its makers. A programme is an organised intention to oppose, pursued through choices of content and form. Promoting every fracture into a programme flatters the critic's ingenuity and dissolves the classification's distinctions, since the first category would then contain nothing that a sufficiently ingenious reading could not rescue. The categories exist to keep unintended tension and intended opposition analytically separate.

Documentary placements complete the exercise. A compilation picture that assembles archive footage under an authoritative commentary, presenting its selection as the past itself, belongs to the sixth category whatever the politics of its compilers, because its ideological work is done by the claim of transparency: the cuts are hidden, the provenance of images unmentioned, the commentator's voice granted the metalanguage position described in the previous section. A documentary that shows its own interviews being set up, lets its subjects dispute the questions, and leaves its funding and commissioning visible on screen belongs to the seventh, and the classification invites the further question of whether the self-exposure is a method or a mannerism, answerable only by examining whether the film's other conventions, its hierarchies of voice and its closure, were subjected to the same scrutiny as its equipment.

## Sound, Music, and the Soundtrack's Ideology

The formal step is incomplete if it treats the soundtrack as accompaniment. The tradition's writers on sound, fewer than its writers on the image, established several durable points. Dialogue in the classical cinema is mixed for intelligibility above all other sound, a hierarchy that subordinates the world's noise to the characters' speech and thereby to the narrative's chain of intentions. Music in the classical score works, in the analysis associated with Claudia Gorbman's study of film music, as an inaudible art: it guides emotion, sutures cuts, and signifies character and place while conventionally escaping the audience's conscious notice, a division of labour where the soundtrack's most manipulative element is the one least available to criticism. Voice-over narration claims the metalanguage position in audible form. Silence, used rarely, exposes the machinery of expectation the rest of the soundtrack maintains.

The 1928 statement on sound, discussed earlier, proposed counterpoint as the montage use of the new resource, and the history of the soundtrack can be written as the victory of the opposite principle, synchronisation in the service of continuity. Yet counter-practice persisted at the margins: Straub and Huillet's direct sound and unmixed locations, Godard's interrupted and layered tracks, the deliberate mismatches of image and commentary in the essay film. For the analyst, the practical rule is to give the soundtrack its own pass through the film, ideally a listening pass with the image ignored, and to ask of each element the formal step's question: what does this do, and for whose benefit is it arranged? The question regularly yields the reading's most original findings, because sound is where classical works keep the conventions their images have learned to hide.

### What does the classical score do that audiences are not meant to notice?

The classical score manages the emotions the images are too discreet or too ambiguous to specify, instructing the audience in how a scene is to be felt while withdrawing from conscious attention. Its inaudibility is a convention rather than an accident: the music is mixed, placed, and composed to be felt rather than heard. Analysis reverses the convention by listening deliberately, and scenes regularly change their meaning when the instruction is noticed and mentally removed.

## Exhibition, Architecture, and the Ritual of Attendance

The apparatus argument drew part of its force from architecture, and the topic survives the argument's fall in a more empirical form. The classical picture palace organised its audience before the film began: a hierarchy of seats priced by distance and comfort, a programme of shorts and newsreels that framed the feature, an interval economy of refreshments, ushers enforcing a code of quiet attention in the dark. Each element is a fact about the social organisation of viewing, recoverable from trade sources and memoirs, and each shaped the encounter that criticism later theorised as a private relation between a spectator and a screen. The drive-in, the art house, the multiplex, the festival screening with its introductions and question sessions, and domestic viewing under conditions of pause and interruption are different rituals again, and the same work is, in a strict sense, a different event in each.

For the procedure, exhibition belongs partly to production, since distributors and exhibitors are links in the chain of manufacture, and partly to reception, since the ritual is where audiences are actually met. The analyst's obligation is modest but firm: never describe an effect of viewing without specifying the viewing arrangement the description assumes. Half the disagreements in the literature dissolve when the parties discover that one is describing a premiere audience in a palace in 1938 and the other a seminar room with a pause button. The apparatus thesis universalised one historically specific arrangement, the classical auditorium at its most perfected, and mistook it for the medium. Specifying arrangements is the corrected form of its insight.

## The Soviet Case as Political Economy

The founding generation's industry deserves the production step's treatment in its own right, because the Soviet cinema of the 1920s is the one historical case in which a Marxist film theory operated inside a film industry organised, at least in intention, on non-commercial principles. Production was a state monopoly, administered through organisations that combined the functions the Hollywood majors combined, with the difference that the surplus sought was political and educational rather than monetary. The arrangement produced real freedoms and real constraints, and the record resists both sentimental and dismissive summaries. Imported entertainments earned the revenue that subsidised domestic experiment. Directors of the montage generation received commissions, equipment, and distribution that no market would have granted to work of such difficulty. The same monopoly that commissioned October could, and within a decade did, withdraw the conditions of experiment, as the doctrine of socialist realism hardened and the industry's administration centralised.

The economics explain features of the films that purely formal accounts leave mysterious. The shortage of raw stock in the early years encouraged the re-editing of imports and the workshop experiments from which montage theory grew. The commemorative commission system explains the anniversary subjects and the historical epics. The vast, multilingual, substantially illiterate audience explains the reliance on visual argument over dialogue and the prestige of a method that claimed to teach through images. Even the theory's confidence, its assumption that correctly constructed images would produce correct conclusions in any audience, mirrors the industry's pedagogical brief. When the brief changed, the theory's institutional home vanished, and montage survived as an export, in film schools and in the practice of directors elsewhere, detached from the industry that had produced it. The case is the tradition's own best demonstration of its first principle: to understand a cinema, begin with the organisation that pays for it.

### What did the state monopoly give the montage directors, and what did it take away?

The monopoly gave commissions, equipment, distribution, and freedom from the box office test that would have ended their experiments in any commercial system. It took away independence of subject and, eventually, of method, since a single employer can enforce a doctrine as no market can. The exchange defines the case's lesson for political economy generally: the source of finance is never neutral, and the analyst's question is always which freedoms a given source grants and which it prices.

## Genres as Settlements

Genre is the level at which the narrative step's analysis becomes comparative, because a genre is a standing agreement between an industry and its audiences about which contradictions will be raised and how they will be settled. The western of the classical period raises the contradiction between law and violence, settlement and dispossession, and settles it by founding violence retrospectively blessed. The gangster picture raises the contradiction between the official ethic of success and the actual routes to wealth, and settles it by the gangster's death, which permits the audience to enjoy the route and endorse the punishment in a single sitting. The melodrama raises the contradictions of the family, property, desire, and respectability in collision, and settles them by sacrifice, recognition, or the removal of the figure who embodies the collision. The musical raises the contradiction between work and play, the regimented and the spontaneous, and settles it in the show, where the collective labour of rehearsal is transfigured into the appearance of effortless joy, perhaps the most elegant imaginary resolution the classical cinema produced, since the film's own manufacture is its subject and its secret at once.

Genre analysis disciplines the reading of individual works in two directions. It prevents the critic from crediting a single film with a settlement its genre performs routinely, and it directs attention to the works that strain their genre's settlement, delaying it, doubling it, or letting its cost show. Those straining works are natural candidates for the fractured category, and the history of genres can be written as the history of settlements decaying under the pressure of the contradictions they manage, until a settlement that once satisfied begins to look like an evasion and the genre either renews itself by irony or retires into nostalgia. Jameson's account of the nostalgia film describes one such retirement across a whole culture's output.

### Why does the musical's settlement deserve its reputation as the most elegant?

The musical's standing contradiction is between labour and spontaneity, the drilled collective effort that performance requires and the effortless joy it must appear to be. The genre settles the contradiction by making rehearsal its story: the work of preparation is shown, suffered, and then transfigured in the final show, where effort reappears as nature. A form that displays its own manufacture while persuading the audience that no manufacture occurred performs, in miniature, the whole classical cinema's characteristic operation.

## Writing the Analysis Up

A procedure is only as good as the essay it produces, and the tradition's teachers have converged on a shape for the written analysis that mirrors the table's order. Open with the film as an object: its manufacture, its moment, its route to its audience, in a paragraph that earns the right to interpret by first establishing. Proceed to form, with description disciplined enough that a reader who has not seen the work can follow the claims and a reader who has can test them. Take the narrative's settlement next, naming the contradiction, the device, and the cost. State the viewer position as a hypothesis in the grammar of invitation rather than compulsion. Close with reception, reporting what is known of audiences and marking the boundary between the documented and the surmised. Verdicts distributed through such an essay carry their defeaters with them when the writer states, at each level, what evidence would change the finding: an archive that contradicts the production story, a sequence that refuses the formal description, a body of fan writing that breaks the reception claim.

The commonest structural fault in student work is the essay that performs the steps in the wrong order, beginning with the verdict on the film's politics and ransacking the work for illustrations. The second commonest is the essay that performs only the content reading and appends the word ideology to its conclusions. Both faults are cured by the table, used as a checklist before submission: five rows, five bodies of evidence, five findings, each carrying its own degree of confidence. The VaultBook study notes at [the Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) provide a worksheet form of the five-part analysis that students can keep beside the screen, and candidates preparing for examinations in which film studies questions appear alongside the wider humanities syllabus will find the past-paper practice at [the UPSC PYQ explorer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) useful for testing whether a reading can be produced under time pressure.

## Documentary and the Claim of Transparency

Documentary occupies two of the seven categories because the Cahiers writers judged its characteristic ideological operation to be distinct from fiction's. A fiction announces its manufacture by its credits, its stars, its impossible viewpoints. A documentary of the observational or compilation kind can present itself as a window, and the presentation is the operation: the selection of footage, the order of sequences, the voice that binds them, and the exclusions that shape the whole are withdrawn from view, so that the finished work appears as the past or the present simply recorded. The tradition's criticism of such work is not that it lies. Selection can be honest and still be selection. The criticism is that the form claims an innocence no construction possesses, and that the claim itself, the effacement of manufacture, is the oldest device in the classical repertoire, transferred from fiction to fact.

John Grierson, who coined the word documentary in English usage in a review of 1926 and built the British documentary movement of the 1930s, defined the form as the creative treatment of actuality, a phrase whose candour the later critics might have acknowledged more warmly. Grierson's movement was state and corporate sponsored, devoted to public instruction, and explicit about its persuasive aims; its films for the Empire Marketing Board and the General Post Office made manufacture a point of pride, with directors, composers, and poets on salary. The movement therefore complicates the sixth category from inside the documentary tradition itself: here was a cinema of fact that announced its sponsorship and its intentions on its credits, yet whose liberal imperial framing of its subjects, the herring fleet, the mail train, the housing estate, performed ideological work of the sort the categories were built to catch, in content and in the paternal hierarchy of its voices. The grid survives the complication, because the grid's question is never whether a work is sincere but where its work is done.

### What is wrong with a documentary that claims to show things as they are?

The claim conceals the construction that any film requires: footage was selected from more footage, sequences were ordered, a commentary or structure was imposed, and the financing and commissioning set the boundaries of the enquiry. Concealment matters because it removes those decisions from the audience's judgement, presenting conclusions as observations. The criticism is of the claim, not of documentary as such, and the seventh category exists to honour work that declines to make it.

The reflexive alternative of the seventh category has its own history, running from Vertov's inclusion of his cameraman and editor inside Man with a Movie Camera through the direct cinema debates of the 1960s to the essay films that make the filmmaker's uncertainty part of the record. Direct cinema's founding claim, that the camera could observe without intervening, was itself a transparency claim in new dress, and the French cinéma vérité, named in homage to Vertov's Kino-Pravda, corrected it by putting the filmmaker inside the frame and the interview's artifice on display. The categories map that quarrel exactly: the sixth holds the claim, the seventh the correction, and the student who can narrate the quarrel through the grid understands why two of the seven places were reserved for fact.

## The Essay Film and the Thinking Image

A form that the founding generation did not foresee in its mature shape deserves notice because it realises several of the tradition's hopes at once. The essay film, practised from the 1940s onward by figures such as Chris Marker, whose Letter from Siberia of 1957 includes the celebrated device of one sequence commented in three opposed political voices, treats the film as an act of thought conducted in images and words whose friction is the meaning. Marker's device is a complete film theory in miniature: identical footage, three commentaries, three films. It demonstrates the montage proposition at the level of the soundtrack, refutes the transparency claim by exhibition, and instructs the audience in the constructedness of every documentary it will ever see, all inside a work of charm and brevity. Teachers use the sequence constantly, and its lesson generalises: wherever a student is tempted to treat images as evidence that speaks for itself, the three voices are the corrective.

The essay film also answers, in practice, the Brechtian demand for a cinema that thinks. Its characteristic devices, the first person commentary that doubts itself, the digression that becomes the argument, the archive image interrogated rather than illustrated, refuse absorption without refusing pleasure, and its audiences, gathered through festivals and television arts slots, accepted the terms in numbers the austere wing of the Brechtian programme rarely reached. A Marxist analysis notes the conditions as readily as the virtues: the form flourished where public broadcasters and cultural funds financed work outside the commercial test, a production fact that belongs in any account of its aesthetics.

### What do the three commentaries in Letter from Siberia demonstrate?

One sequence of Siberian footage is presented three times under opposed commentaries, celebratory, hostile, and descriptive, and becomes three different films. The demonstration isolates the variable that documentary's transparency claim hides: the sense of images is governed by the discourse that frames them. Because the footage is held constant, the experiment is as clean as film studies possesses, and it can be cited wherever a student needs the framing argument in a single example.

## Authorship and Its Critics

No account of the field is complete without the quarrel over authorship, since the production step's insistence on manufacture collides with the auteur theory that dominated postwar criticism. The auteur critics, writing in the same Cahiers du Cinéma that later produced the ideology classification, had argued that the director is the work's author and that a policy of auteurs could rescue artistic seriousness from the studio system's anonymity. The Marxist turn of 1969 was thus an argument inside one journal's history: the generation of Comolli and Narboni inherited a house style of close attention to mise en scène and redirected it from the celebration of authors to the analysis of ideology, keeping the attention and discarding the cult. Their readings of Ford are unthinkable without the auteur criticism that taught them to read a studio director's work as a coherent object; the debt is part of the record and a useful reminder that traditions advance by metabolising their predecessors.

The mature position of the field treats authorship as a variable rather than a doctrine. Some productions are director led in ways the evidence supports: contracts granting final cut, a consistent body of work across studios and genres, collaborators testifying to a single controlling intelligence. Others are producer led, star led, or genuinely collective, and the evidence of credits, archives, and trade reporting decides the case film by film. The analyst who assumes the director's authorship in advance has skipped the production step, and the analyst who denies it in advance has skipped it too, from the other side. What the tradition insists on is only that the question be asked with the documents open.

## The Screen Education Movement and the Classroom

The tradition had an institutional wing that pure theory histories omit. In Britain during the 1970s, the arguments of Screen were carried into education by the journal's associated education work and by teachers who treated film and television literacy as a civic skill: students were to be taught how images are made, how institutions organise them, and how their own positions as viewers are constructed, so that the passive audience of the apparatus argument could be replaced, classroom by classroom, with an analytical one. The irony is productive and worth stating. A theory that described spectatorship as machinery's effect generated a pedagogy premised on the spectator's educability. The pedagogy is the tradition's practical verdict on its own determinism, delivered years before the theoretical correction was fully written: whatever the apparatus does, it plainly does not prevent a sixteen year old with a good teacher from learning to see the joins.

 classroom practice also generated the evidence problem in its sharpest form. Teachers who screened a classical work and led a reading against its grain watched students occupy, negotiate, and refuse positions in real time, and the experience made the strong apparatus claim untenable for anyone who had done it. The movement's descendants, in media studies curricula internationally, kept the five questions of the procedure in a simplified form long after the psychoanalytic vocabulary faded: who made this and by what means, whose interest it serves, whom it addresses, and what responses it met. A student meeting those questions in a school syllabus is meeting this article's tradition in its most successful disguise.

### What does the classroom evidence do to the apparatus thesis?

Teaching is a standing experiment in whether constructed positions compel occupation, and its results are unambiguous: they do not. Students guided to notice construction routinely read against the grain of works designed for absorption, while remaining capable of enjoying them. The observation does not refute the claim that positions are constructed.

## Feminist Revisions and the Look

The essay of 1975 opened a field as well as a controversy, and the feminist film theory that developed through the following decade revised both the apparatus model and its founding essay from within the same journals. Writers including Mary Ann Doane and E. Ann Kaplan pressed the question the early model could not answer: if the spectator position is masculine by construction, what is the female spectator's relation to the classical film, and what would a cinema addressed to her look like? The answers multiplied positions inside a model that had begun with one. The female spectator was described as oscillating between identifications, as adopting a kind of transvestite mobility among the positions a film offers, or as addressed by specific genres, the woman's picture and the maternal melodrama, whose organisation of looks and knowledge differs systematically from the action film's. Each revision weakened the single position thesis and strengthened the underlying method, which was to derive spectatorship from the organisation of the work rather than from the intentions of its makers.

The melodrama research deserves particular notice in a Marxist account, because it reconnected the feminist argument to the narrative step. The family melodrama, as read by critics of the 1970s and 1980s, stages the contradictions of property, marriage, and female labour inside the home, and its excesses of music, colour, and coincidence mark the points where its settlements fail to convince even itself. Such readings placed the melodrama beside the western and the gangster picture in the genre analysis offered earlier: a standing settlement under strain, legible in its style. The traffic between traditions here is the healthiest in the field's history, Marxist categories of contradiction and settlement doing work inside feminist readings, psychoanalytic accounts of the look doing work inside Marxist ones, and both answerable to the films.

### How did feminist revisions change the model of the spectator?

The revisions pluralised the spectator. Where the early model offered a single masculine position constructed by the apparatus, the feminist work of the following decade described multiple, mobile, and genre specific positions, with the female spectator's negotiations given their own analysis. The change preserved the founding insight, that positions are built by works, while abandoning the founding simplification, that one position is built for everyone by the machine as such.

## The Tradition Beyond Its Journals

By the time the apparatus thesis was being given up, its vocabulary had escaped into the discipline's common speech. Terms such as positioning, the gaze, suture, and the classic realist text are used by scholars of every allegiance, usually with the theoretical charge drained away. The political economy strand, meanwhile, consolidated as a subfield of communications research with its own journals and methods, studying ownership concentration, labour, and policy with tools closer to economics and sociology than to the essay tradition. The montage strand had long since become film education's foundation. The classification survives in syllabuses. Taking the inventory honestly: the tradition's concepts conquered the discipline's vocabulary, its strongest claim was refuted, its procedures were absorbed into ordinary good practice, and its name is invoked less often than its tools are used. That pattern, in which a school's instruments outlive its banner, is familiar from the other cultural articles in this series, and students should learn to recognise it as a normal fate rather than a defeat.

A final strand should be recorded for completeness. Marxist film criticism continued as a practice of reviewing and essay writing outside the academy, in journals of the left and in the film pages of political publications, where the questions of production and ideology are asked in a compressed, topical form. The academic tradition and the journalistic one have rarely honoured each other, the first finding the second impressionistic, the second finding the first unreadable, but they share the founding conviction that a film is a made thing in a world of interests, and a student who reads both will understand the conviction's range better than a student who reads either alone.

## Historical Method: Dating the Traditions

Examinations reward a secure chronology, and the field's chronology has a shape worth memorising as an argument rather than as a list. The 1920s give the founding unity of theory and practice inside a state industry: Kuleshov's workshop, Eisenstein's films and the essay of 1929, Vertov's manifestos and the film of 1929, Pudovkin's manual. The 1930s give the diaspora and the theory's first political economy: Brecht's writings on theatre and his lawsuit over the film of his opera, Benjamin's essay of 1935, the consolidation of the studio system and its censorship code in America, the Grierson movement in Britain. The postwar decades give the auteur interlude and the art cinema, against which the next wave defined itself. The years around 1970 give the theoretical concentration: Althusser's essay and Baudry's in the same year, the Cahiers classification a year earlier, Comolli's series following, Metz's work and the essay of 1975 completing the formation. The late 1970s and 1980s give the correction: Hall's decoding model circulating from 1973, Morley's audience studies, the feminist revisions, the Bordwell and Carroll polemics, and the migration of the argument into cultural studies and communications research. The 1990s give the settlement of accounts, in which the strong apparatus claim is retired and the procedures are absorbed.

Stated as an argument, the chronology teaches the field's rhythm. Each concentration of theory answers the arrangement of its moment's industry: montage answers the young state industry and the silent screen; the apparatus answers the perfected classical auditorium at the moment of its dissolution into television and the art house; reception answers the domestic, interruptible viewing that broadcasting normalised. A theory of cinema is always, among other things, a theory of the last arrangement of cinema its authors knew, which is the deepest reason the defeater discipline matters: the next arrangement will test every claim again, and the claims stated with their evidence and their limits marked are the ones that will survive the test in usable form.

### Why does each theory fit the industrial arrangement of its moment?

Theorists generalise from the cinema they inhabit. Montage theory arose where editing was the scarce skill and the state the employer; apparatus theory arose where the classical auditorium had perfected its ritual; reception theory arose when viewing had moved into the home and become interruptible. Recognising the fit does not refute the theories. It explains their emphases and their blind spots, and it warns the analyst against universalising any arrangement, including the one outside the window.

## What Examinations Ask

The query ladder for this field is stable enough to be worth addressing directly, since the article exists to be used under assessment conditions. The head question, what Marxist film theory is, is answered by the definition given at the start and by the five-part table. The mid-tail questions concern the named devices: apparatus theory explained, which the snippet and the apparatus sections answer; Soviet montage theory, answered by the founding sections; the seven categories, answered by the classification section and its worked programme; the procedure, answered by the worked example. The long-tail questions are the discriminating ones, and they have been answered where they arise: whether the male gaze is a Marxist concept, whether apparatus theory is still accepted, what intellectual montage is, what a Marxist analysis examines, what distanciation does in film, how the political economy of film works, and where a beginner should start. A candidate who can move between those levels, from the definition to the discrimination, demonstrates the command the field's examiners are trained to reward, and a candidate who can add the defeater for the field's central verdict, the evidence that would revive the apparatus thesis, demonstrates the habit this series exists to teach.

One further examination skill deserves explicit statement, because it is the one the tradition itself models. When a question invites a verdict on a school of thought, the strong answer separates the school's claims by their fates: which were established, which survive as graded hypotheses, which were abandoned and by whom. The weak answer either celebrates the school whole or dismisses it whole, and both gestures mark the candidate as a consumer of summaries. The apparatus claim and its fate is the type case: a candidate who writes that the field's most original proposition was surrendered by the field itself, on evidence the field's own researchers produced, and who can say what the surrender left standing, has written the paragraph that distinguishes the top band.

### How should a candidate handle a question inviting a verdict on the whole field?

Separate the claims by their fates and give the evidence for each separation. State which propositions were established, which survive in graded form, and which were abandoned, naming who abandoned them and on what grounds. A verdict that discriminates among a tradition's parts is worth more than praise or dismissal of the whole, because it shows the candidate commanding the material rather than repeating a survey's tone.

## The Field's Usable Residue

What a student carries away, after the history and the controversies, is a small set of durable instruments. The five-part analysis, which disciplines any reading of any film. The seven categories, which locate a work's ideological labour. The distinction between content and form, which prevents the commonest error in political criticism of art. The grading of claims about spectators, which the reception correction installed. And the habit of the defeater, which this series applies across its cultural articles: every verdict in this article has been stated beside the evidence that would overturn it, from the fate of the apparatus thesis to the placement of a single film in the fractured category. The instruments are modest compared with the founding ambition, which was nothing less than a science of the cinema's place in the reproduction of a social order. Modesty, in this case, is not the tradition's failure. It is the shape a research programme takes after it has survived its own refutation and kept the parts that work.

## The Culture Industry Connection

The production argument of this article descends in part from the culture industry chapter of Dialectic of Enlightenment, composed in exile in the 1940s by Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer. Their chapter on the culture industry argued that industrially produced entertainment is not a degenerate form of art but a distinct social mechanism: standardised products, differentiated by superficial marks, reconcile audiences to the existing order by occupying the hours of leisure with the same rhythms as the hours of work, so that amusement becomes the prolongation of labour by other means. Film was their exemplary medium, the most capital intensive and therefore the most completely administered. The account is treated in its own right in the companion article on the culture industry, and the present article borrows only its production premise.

The later film tradition both used and corrected that inheritance. The apparatus theorists shared the Frankfurt pessimism about the audience's position but relocated the mechanism from the product's standardisation to the viewing situation itself. The reception researchers kept the Frankfurt insistence that leisure is socially organised while refusing its conclusion that organisation settles response, pointing to the evidence of audiences making negotiated and oppositional uses of the most standardised goods. Adorno's own exchange with Benjamin, conducted in letters of the 1930s, already contained the whole later quarrel in embryo: Benjamin's hope for the reproducible work against Adorno's fear of its administration, the distracted testing spectator against the regressed one. A student who reads the correspondence beside the 1970s debate sees the tradition arguing with itself across forty years, which is a more accurate picture of its life than any story of schools succeeding schools.

### What did the film tradition change in the culture industry argument?

The film tradition changed the location of the mechanism and the estimate of the audience. The mechanism moved from the standardised product to the situation of viewing and then to the encounter between text and socially placed audiences. The audience moved from a mass reconciled by amusement to a set of publics whose responses are patterned, negotiated, and occasionally oppositional. The production premise, that leisure goods are manufactured under the same relations as other goods, survived every change intact.

## National Cinemas and the Import Question

The political economy of film acquires a second dimension wherever a national industry faces imported product, which is most places. The dominance of American distribution in world markets after the First World War made film a trade policy question in Europe and beyond: governments imposed quotas on imported pictures, levied duties whose proceeds subsidised domestic production, and founded state institutes and studios. The Marxist analysis of such measures is characteristically double. Protection can shelter a domestic industry in which national audiences see their own landscapes, languages, and concerns, and several of the century's significant cinemas grew behind such shelters. Protection also creates a dependent industry oriented to the state's favour, prone to the same settlement making as any subsidised culture, and the Soviet case shows the extreme of the dependence. The analyst's questions remain the production step's: who finances, on what conditions, with what freedoms priced in.

The import question also disciplines the category work, because a film's relation to a dominant ideology depends on which society's dominance is in view. A Hollywood picture of the first category in its home market may function differently in a market where its assumptions about property, family, and success are foreign doctrine, and audiences in such markets have historically produced the negotiated and oppositional decodings that reception research later catalogued: enjoying the spectacle, discounting the ethic, remaking the story in local retelling. The global career of the classical cinema is therefore one of the strongest natural experiments against the apparatus thesis. The same apparatus, the same films, and wildly divergent decodings, sorted by the social experience of the audiences, is the pattern the thesis could not accommodate and the pattern a mature Marxist analysis predicts.

### Why do imported films weaken the apparatus thesis?

Imported films hold the apparatus and the text constant while varying the audience's social world, and the decodings vary with the audience. Spectators formed by different relations to property, family, and authority negotiate or refuse positions that home audiences occupied without friction. A theory that derives effect from equipment predicts uniformity across that variation. The observed variation therefore counts against the theory in exactly the way a controlled comparison should, which is why the global evidence mattered to the correction.

## Censorship, Markets, and the Margins

Between the state censor and the market's discipline lies a territory the tradition has mapped with care: the self censorship of producers who internalise the boundaries, the ratings and classifications that replace prohibition with sorting, the informal blacklists that govern employment, and the festival and art house circuits that create a protected margin where the boundaries are wider. The margin matters analytically because it is where a society's permitted dissent becomes visible as a quantity. Work that can be shown in a festival but not distributed, financed abroad but not at home, praised by critics and unseen by publics, marks the exact contour of the permissible, and the contour is evidence about the order that drew it. The Brechtian filmmakers worked that margin deliberately, as did the Soviet directors of the thaw in their season, and the political economy of the margin, who funds it, who attends it, what its existence legitimises, is a research topic the tradition bequeathed to the sociology of culture.

A related margin is the repertory and revival circuit, where old films acquire new decodings. A classical picture screened for a revival audience is watched with a historical consciousness its first audience could not have: its settlements are visible as settlements, its ideology legible as a period style. Revival is thus a natural Brechtian device that history supplies free of charge, and teachers exploit it constantly, since distance does the interrupting that the filmmakers of 1968 had to manufacture. The observation completes a circle in the argument. Distanciation, proposed as an artificial technique against absorption, turns out to be something time does to every work, and the analyst's craft lies partly in accelerating it: restoring to a film the visibility of its manufacture, its moment, and its assumptions, so that a viewer can judge what a first audience could only inhabit.

## The Star Image Revisited: A Short Case in Method

The method's integration can be shown compactly on the star image, since a star crosses every row of the table. Production: the image is manufactured by an organisation, through casting, contract, publicity departments, and the assignment of roles that repeat a successful formula while varying its surface. Form: the image is carried by technique, by lighting conventions that isolate the star's face from the scene's general illumination, by the close up's privilege, by entrance structures that delay and then deliver the star to the audience's expectation. Narrative: the star's persona constrains the settlements available, since a persona built on incorruptibility cannot be permitted a sordid ending, and scripts were rewritten to protect the asset. Position: the star is the principal object of the audience's identification and desire, the figure through whom the constructed seat is furnished. Reception: audiences complete the image in fan practice, collecting, imitating, and arguing over it, and sometimes refuse it wholesale when a role violates the persona's terms. Five steps, five bodies of evidence, and a single phenomenon explained as a social construction rather than a mystery of charisma: that is the procedure earning its keep, and Richard Dyer's studies of stars remain the model performance of it.

### Why is charisma an unsatisfying explanation of a star?

Charisma names the effect to be explained and presents the name as the explanation. The evidence shows an image assembled by identifiable labour: roles selected, faces lit by convention, publicity written, scandals managed, personae adjusted after failures. Whatever residue of personal quality remains after the manufacture is accounted for is real but small, and it cannot explain the pattern of which performers an era manufactures into stars and which it leaves as competent actors in supporting parts.

## Realism as a Contested Word

Every tradition in this article claims realism for itself, and the contest over the word is a compact history of the field. The classical cinema claimed realism as transparency: the world shown as it is, an effect of the continuity style's concealment. Bazin claimed realism for the long take and deep focus, on the ground that they respect the ambiguity of the real and the spectator's freedom. The montage school claimed a higher realism, of relations rather than surfaces: the truth of a process shown by construction rather than by transcription. The Brechtians claimed realism for a theatre and cinema that show the causal network behind appearances, arguing that a photograph of a factory tells nothing about the relations inside it, a point Brecht himself made about the limits of photographic evidence. The Cahiers critics claimed realism as the stake of their categories: the first category's films are those whose realism is the dominant ideology's self image, and the fractured works are those where a more recalcitrant reality breaks the surface. The apparatus theorists claimed that the impression of reality was itself the ideological product to be explained.

A student should treat the word, in any critical text, as a flag rather than a fact: ask which of these senses is in play, what evidence would support the claim in that sense, and what the claim excludes. The exercise generalises beyond film. The same contest appears over the realist novel in this series' literary articles, and the parallel procedure for prose, given in the companion article on reading a novel, trains the same suspicion of the word. Realism is not a property works have. It is a claim works and their defenders make, with a history, and the claim is evidence about the claimant's theory of the world.

### What should a reader do when a critic calls a film realistic?

Ask which realism is meant: transparency of style, fidelity of surface, truth of relations, or exposure of causes. Each sense implies a different test, and a work can pass one while failing the others. A film of impeccable surfaces may falsify every relation it depicts, and a film of glaring artifice may state the relations exactly. The question converts a compliment into an analysis, which is what the tradition exists to perform.

## Television, Video, and the Moving Image After the Auditorium

The traditions were formed on the theatrical cinema, and their application to later arrangements of moving images is a test of the grading discipline this article has practised. Broadcasting dispersed viewing into the home, made it interruptible and accompanied, and placed the set among furniture and conversation. The apparatus argument, indexed to the darkened hall, lost its object, and its defenders' attempts to rebuild it for the domestic screen persuaded few. The other instruments travelled better. The production step applies unchanged to broadcasters and to the firms that succeeded them, with commissioning structures replacing studio units and schedules replacing slates. The hierarchy of discourses was in fact first generalised on television drama. Hall's decoding model was built for broadcast material. Genre settlements are if anything more legible in series built to return weekly to the same contradiction. The lesson repeats the article's refrain: instruments framed at the level of functions survive the replacement of devices, and claims framed at the level of one arrangement of devices expire with it. Media theory in the broader sense, which takes broadcasting and the successor arrangements as its primary objects, is handled in the companion article on Marxist media theory, to which students should proceed after this one.

## The Question of Pleasure

A thread runs through the traditions that students often sense before they can state: the field's uneasy relation to pleasure. The culture industry argument treated pleasure as the mechanism of reconciliation. The Brechtian programme treated absorbed pleasure as the obstacle to judgement, while Brecht himself insisted that theatre must give pleasure or fail. The apparatus argument treated the spectator's pleasure in mastery as the ideological effect itself. The essay of 1975 proposed destroying pleasure as a radical weapon and was answered, over the following years, by feminist and gay critics who asked whose pleasures were being volunteered for destruction and what a criticism that cannot account for joy will miss. Reception research finally gave pleasure its evidence: audiences describing what they loved, rewatched, memorised, and remade, in terms that no theory of manipulation could paraphrase away.

The mature position the field arrived at is neither celebration nor suspicion but analysis. Pleasure is a datum with causes: in the settlement's efficiency, in the star image's management of contradiction, in the mastery a legible space affords, in the recognition of one's own world on a screen that usually shows other people's. Each cause can be examined by the procedure's steps, and the examination does not spoil the pleasure it explains, any more than the analysis of a joke's mechanism prevents the next joke from working. Critics who fear that analysis murders delight are answered by the classroom evidence: students who learn to see the joins report, with some consistency, that their delight changes its object, attaching to the craft and to the works that survive scrutiny, rather than diminishing. A tradition that began by distrusting the audience's feelings ends by taking them as its most instructive evidence, and that reversal, like the apparatus claim's fate, is a sign of a research programme that could learn.

### Does analysing a film's construction destroy the pleasure of watching it?

The evidence of teaching and of criticism's own history says no. Analysis changes the pleasure's distribution: naive absorption becomes harder to sustain, while the pleasures of craft, of fracture, of performances and constructions that withstand scrutiny, grow correspondingly available. Critics are, after all, people who watch more than almost anyone, and a method that extinguished pleasure would have extinguished its own practitioners first.

Althusser's essay of 1970 is available in his collected writings. Hall's decoding model is collected in the cultural studies readers, and Morley's audience research in his published studies. Bordwell and Carroll's polemics are gathered in their volume of 1996 on the state of film theory. Where this article has characterised a dispute, as with the famine of certainties surrounding the Kuleshov experiments, the characterisation reflects the state of the scholarship: the footage is lost, the descriptions conflict in detail, and the honest course is to say so, as has been done. Quotations have been kept short and confined to phrases whose wording is standard in the literature, such as Vertov's description of his method as catching life unawares, since a survey article's duty is to the arguments rather than to the anthology of phrases.

The verification discipline matters to the field's self understanding. A tradition that insists claims be graded by evidence should expect its own history to be told with the same grading, and the places where this article has hedged, the lost experiments, the disputed readings of Ford, the contested legacy of the later Eisenstein, are marked as hedges rather than smoothed into the narrative. Students writing on the field should imitate the marking. An examiner can forgive a gap acknowledged; a fabrication discovered costs the whole essay its credit, in film studies as in the examination halls for which the companion tools prepare candidates.

## Conclusion Without a Slogan

The article opened with a student, a film, and an assignment, and it returns to that student with the instruments assembled. Marxist film theory, in its usable residue, is a way of asking five questions in order and of grading the answers honestly. Its history supplies the questions with their depth: manufacture from the political economists and the Soviet case, technique from the montage directors, settlement from the genre analysts, position from the apparatus debate with its strong claim surrendered, and reception from the researchers who surrendered it. Its most famous proposition, that the machine itself is ideological, survives as the field's defining cautionary tale, a reminder that a critical tradition earns its authority not by the boldness of its claims but by its willingness to retire them in public when the evidence turns. The student who performs the five examinations on a film, states the findings at their proper levels of certainty, and names what would change them has not merely learned the tradition. The student has joined the part of it that is still alive.

## The Close-Up and the Face

A single device can carry a whole seminar, and the close up is the device teachers reach for when the formal step needs a demonstration. The classical cinema grants the close up chiefly to faces, and chiefly at moments of feeling, so that the face becomes the place where the film's truth is read. The Hungarian theorist Béla Balázs, writing in the 1920s and again after the war, celebrated the close up as the discovery of a new visible world, the physiognomy of things and the micro drama of features that the stage could never show. The Marxist reading adds the questions Balázs did not ask. Whose faces receive the close up, and whose remain in the group shot? In the inheritance melodrama of the worked example, the clerk's reaction shots outnumber the workforce's faces by design, and the distribution is an argument about whose interiority the film values. In the colonial adventure of the classification exercise, the explorers are individuated in close up while the population they restore to order is photographed in masses, a distribution that states the film's anthropology more plainly than its dialogue dares.

The device also illustrates the grading of claims. That close ups direct sympathy is a finding any screening can test. That they construct a bourgeois subject of interiority is a hypothesis requiring the whole apparatus framework and a comparative history of the device across cinemas that used it differently. Soviet practice, which often withheld the psychological close up in favour of the typage of faces chosen as social specimens, and the later European practice that held faces past the point of expression into discomfort, both show the device's meanings varying with its system. The analyst's craft is to keep the levels separate in the writing: this the device does here, this it tends to do in this system, this it was once claimed to do everywhere, with the third claim marked as retired.

### What does the distribution of close-ups reveal in a classical film?

The distribution reveals the film's economy of interiority: which characters are granted an inner life the audience may inspect, which are presented as types, functions, or masses, and how that grant aligns with the story's distribution of sympathy and power. Counting is not the whole of analysis, but a count of faces, checked against the narrative's hierarchy, regularly exposes an argument the dialogue never makes.

## Editing Rhythms and the Management of Time

The montage tradition's most portable legacy is the analysis of cutting rhythm. Eisenstein distinguished metric, rhythmic, tonal, and overtonal montage, categories of the relations among shots by length, by content's movement, by emotional tone, and by the combination of all three, and the distinctions remain serviceable even for analysts who reject the dialectical philosophy that organised them. A sequence cut metrically, at lengths fixed by a scheme indifferent to content, produces a mechanical insistence. A sequence whose cutting rate answers to the action's tempo produces the classical acceleration toward climax. The analyst describes the pattern first, in counts and timings where the claim needs them, and interprets second, asking what the pattern does to the spectator's body before asking what it means: acceleration as arousal, deceleration as gravity or exhaustion, the held shot as either contemplation or the refusal of release.

Music film and the trailer have since made metric cutting a vernacular, and the analyst of classical works should remember that the rhythms which feel natural in a studio picture were conventions installed by practice and codified by editors' handbooks. The cutting room was a workplace with its own division of labour, in which the editor, frequently a woman in the studio era and almost never credited with the authority the work exercised, assembled the film's final form from the director's material under the producer's deadlines. The production step and the formal step meet in that room. A history of editing that names only directors misdescribes the manufacture as thoroughly as a history of stars that speaks only of charisma.

### Why should an analyst count shots before interpreting a sequence?

Counting disciplines description. A claim that a sequence accelerates, lingers, or fragments is testable against the record of shot lengths and contents, and two analysts who disagree about a sequence's effect can return to the count to locate their disagreement in the evidence. Interpretation built on uncounted impressions tends to find in the sequence whatever the analyst's thesis required, which is the failure mode the objection to the whole field warned against.

## The Western and the Settlement of Violence

The genre analysis offered earlier can be deepened on the western, the classical cinema's most examined case. The western's standing contradiction is between the violence that founds the community and the law that must disown violence to govern it. Its classical settlement appoints a figure who stands between the two, the gunfighter whose skill the town needs and whose presence the town cannot survive, and resolves the contradiction by the figure's departure or death after the founding act, so that the community inherits the result of violence without retaining the violent. The settlement's elegance explains the genre's longevity, and its cost is itemised by the narrative step: the prior inhabitants of the land, whose dispossession is the founding act's other face, are placed outside the contradiction altogether, appearing as obstacle or as landscape rather than as parties with a claim the story must answer.

The genre's later history follows the pattern of decaying settlements. As the cost became harder to keep outside the frame, the classical settlement lost its innocence: the gunfighter's departure began to read as the town's ingratitude or hypocrisy, the dispossessed acquired faces and claims, and the genre entered the ironic and elegiac phases that critics periodise. A Marxist account does not treat that decay as a simple progress of enlightenment. It asks what changed in the society whose audiences had once been satisfied: the national experience of later wars, the civil rights movements' pressure on the founding story, the industry's own need to sell the familiar with a difference. Genre history, conducted this way, becomes a sensitive instrument for registering ideological change, because a settlement that stops satisfying is evidence that the contradiction it managed has shifted its social weight.

## The Melodrama and the Home

The melodrama repays the same treatment, since it is the genre in which the contradictions of property and feeling are staged at their most concentrated. The family melodrama of the studio era locates its conflicts inside the home: between the generations over the disposal of property and the choice of spouses, between desire and respectability, between the mother's sacrifice and the children's freedom. Its settlements run through recognition and renunciation. The letter arrives, the parent's hidden sacrifice is revealed, the unsuitable match is given up, and the family closes ranks around a table from which someone is missing. The genre's celebrated excess, its music swelling past the scene's apparent needs, its coincidences and its storms, is read by the tradition as the return of what the settlement represses: the feeling that the resolution cannot justify, discharged as style. Excess is therefore evidence, the place where the film's own account of itself breaks down, and the critic who learns to read excess has acquired the melodrama equivalent of the fractured category.

The reading of melodrama also corrected the field's early gender blindness from inside its own categories. The genre was dismissed for decades as a women's form, beneath the notice of criticism oriented to westerns and gangster pictures, and its recovery by feminist scholars using the tradition's tools, contradiction, settlement, excess, demonstrated that the tools were sounder than the taste of their first users. A method is vindicated when it illuminates what its inventors overlooked, and the melodrama's canonisation is among the strongest such vindications the field can cite.

### What does excess signify in a melodrama?

Excess, of music, colour, coincidence, or gesture beyond the scene's narrative requirement, signifies the pressure of what the settlement cannot say. Where a film's resolution is fully believed by the film itself, style can afford economy. Where the resolution papers over a contradiction the film has made the audience feel, the surplus feeling escapes as style. Reading excess is therefore a diagnostic skill: locate the swelling, and the contradiction it compensates for is usually adjacent.

## Criticism as a Social Practice

The tradition's self understanding includes an account of criticism itself as a practice with a political economy, and a survey that omitted it would be incomplete by the tradition's own standard. Critics write for institutions: journals with editors and doctrines, newspapers with advertisers and deadlines, universities with examinations and promotions. The Cahiers of 1969 was a journal with a line, edited collectively through a political turn whose internal history explains the classification's polemical edge. Screen was an academic journal whose fortunes followed the expansion of film education. The auteur criticism that preceded the ideology turn was itself a weapon in a generational struggle inside French film culture. None of this invalidates the arguments produced inside those institutions, any more than a film's manufacture invalidates the film. It subjects criticism to its own first step: ask who financed the discourse, under what structure, for which audience, before weighing what it says. Students who learn to ask the question of their sources, including this article, which is written for a study site with its own audience and its own examinations in view, have absorbed the tradition's most transferable habit.

### Should a reader distrust criticism written inside institutions with a line?

Distrust is the wrong instrument; locate the line and read with it in view. A journal's doctrine tells the reader which questions the criticism will press and which it will not raise, and an argument's silences are as informative as its claims once the line is known. The genetic question, where an argument comes from, never settles the argument's truth. It settles the reader's expectations, which is a different and legitimate use.

## The Counter-Cinema Idea

Peter Wollen's essay of 1972 on Godard and counter cinema gave the Brechtian programme its most systematic codification, setting the classical cinema's properties in a table of oppositions: narrative transitivity against intransitivity, identification against estrangement, transparency against foregrounding, single diegesis against multiple, closed text against open, pleasure against unpleasure, fiction against reality. The table is a teaching instrument of great clarity, and its fate is instructive. As a description of two poles it remains exact. As a programme it encountered the difficulty that the counter cinema's properties, assembled in one work, describe a cinema almost no audience attended, and Wollen himself, a filmmaker as well as a theorist, spent a career exploring the territory between the poles rather than at either. The tradition's mature judgement treats the oppositions as a map of choices available in every sequence rather than as a choice between two cinemas: a film may foreground its construction in one movement and solicit identification in the next, and the analysis follows the alternation.

That judgement generalises into the field's final position on form. Devices have tendencies, histories, and costs, and works are mixtures. The analyst who grades a device by what it does inside the work at hand, rather than by the camp it came from, is practising the method as its own history taught it to be practised. The binary tables remain useful as maps of the territory's extremes, provided the student remembers that films, like the societies that produce them, are lived in the middle.

## The Question of Real Audiences in the Archive

Reception evidence is the procedure's most fragile step, because audiences leave fewer records than producers. The archive of reception is assembled from survivals: reviews in newspapers and journals, which record the responses of professional viewers with institutions behind them; censorship files, which record what authorities feared audiences would do; fan magazines and letters, which record organised enthusiasm in the industry's own channels; exhibitors' reports to trade papers, which record what played and what emptied seats; memoirs, which record what it was possible to remember having felt. Each source has a known distortion, and the step's craft is triangulation, setting the sources against one another until a pattern survives the setting. A film praised by reviewers, feared by censors, and reported weak by exhibitors in industrial towns has a reception that no single source describes, and the divergences are themselves the finding, since they map the social distribution of the film's meanings in its first moment.

Later audiences add strata rather than replacing the first. The revival, the television broadcast, the classroom screening, and the clip circulated among enthusiasts each remake the work's public, and a full reception history is a stratigraphy. The Marxist interest in the strata is specific: decodings change as the social formation changes, and a work whose settlement satisfied its first public may embarrass its third, not because the work altered but because the contradiction it manages has changed its weight, as the western's history showed. Reception history is thus the procedure's point of contact with the largest claim of the wider theory, that the meaning of cultural goods is settled in the encounter between works and the historically situated people who receive them.

### What is the strongest evidence that a film's meaning changed over time?

The strongest evidence is a documented reversal in the terms of discussion among comparable sources: reviews, censorship deliberations, or exhibitor reports from two periods addressing the same scenes in incompatible vocabularies, one period's commonplace becoming another's scandal or absurdity. Such reversals, anchored in dated documents, free the reception historian from relying on impressions of a period's spirit, and they are commoner in the archive than students expect before they look.

## Ideology Revisited: The Concept Beneath the Article

Every section of this article has used the concept of ideology while deferring its definition to the companion treatment, and a brief restatement of the senses in play will prevent misunderstanding. In the montage sections, ideology names the conclusions a construction leads a spectator to draw about the social world. In the political economy sections, it names the representations that reconcile audiences to an order whose manufacture the same sections describe. In the apparatus sections, it names an effect of positioning, the subject's imaginary relation to its conditions, in the sense the source essay of 1970 gave the term. In the categories, it names the dominant representations of a social formation, against which a film's relation is plotted. The senses are kin but not identical, and the tradition's critics have sometimes exploited the slippage, convicting an argument in one sense by evidence drawn from another. The analyst's defence is the habit this article has practised throughout: state which sense is in use, at the point of use, and let the verdicts be graded accordingly. The full conceptual history, from the term's coinage through its Marxist transformations, belongs to the article on the [marxist theory of ideology guide](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/), which the disambiguation plan for this series assigns to the topic.

A word on the dominant ideology thesis itself is in order, since the categories presuppose it. Critics within sociology and the wider Marxist tradition have questioned whether any social formation possesses a single dominant ideology that saturates its culture, pointing to the plurality of representations in circulation and to the limited evidence that subordinate classes internalise the representations of their rulers. The categories survive the questioning better than the thesis that named them, because the grid can be operated with a weaker premise: that in any formation some representations are endowed with institutional authority, by ownership, by censorship, by education, by sheer volume of circulation, and that a film's relation to those authorised representations can be plotted. Operated on the weaker premise, the classification loses its metaphysical air and gains in applicability, another instance of the pattern in which the tradition's instruments improve as its claims are graded down to their evidence.

## The Actor's Body and the Labour of Performance

Performance is the point where the labour analysis meets the screen most intimately, and the tradition's account of acting corrects two sentimentalities at once. Against the idea of the performance as the actor's self expression, the production step recalls the conditions: roles cast by type, performances assembled in the cutting room from takes the actor did not order, voices sometimes replaced, bodies doubled for action and for song. Against the opposite idea, of the actor as a mere puppet of the apparatus, the record of craft insists on the performer's labour as skilled work with real margins of decision, visible in the differences between two actors in the same role across remakes and revivals. The star studies discussed earlier occupy the middle ground, treating the performance as the meeting point of a manufactured image and a working body, and the meeting is where audiences attach.

Brecht's acting theory remains the tradition's most developed account of performance as labour made visible. The epic actor, in his prescription, demonstrates the character's behaviour as a quotable gesture, showing the audience a person doing what the character does while retaining the standing of a witness who might have done otherwise. The social gest, his term for the gesture that reveals a relation, a master dismissing a servant, a clerk pocketing a bribe, is the unit the Brechtian film actor is trained to deliver cleanly. Even in cinemas far from Brecht's programme, the concept serves analysis: every performance is a tissue of gests, and the analyst can ask which relations the gests make legible and which they soften into temperament. A dismissal played as comedy of embarrassment and the same dismissal played as an exercise of power are two analyses of a workplace wearing the dress of one scene.

### What is a social gest, and why does it matter to film analysis?

A social gest is a gesture or piece of behaviour that makes a social relation visible: deference, command, bribery, solidarity, contempt. Brecht made it the unit of epic acting because a gest can be quoted, repeated, and judged, unlike a feeling, which can only be shared. For analysis, the gest is where performance and ideology meet, since a film's account of how people stand toward one another is delivered in gests before it is stated in any line.

## Props, Sets, and the Material World on Screen

The material world of a film, its rooms, tools, clothes, and streets, is evidence of a kind students underuse. Sets are built by departments working to budgets, dressed from warehouses of accumulated objects, and lit to conventions that assign legibility and glamour by class of space: the office's hard brightness, the cottage's glow, the tenement's clutter photographed as either vitality or squalor according to the film's thesis. Costume records the production's research and its assumptions in cloth. The analyst who attends to the material world gains a check on the dialogue's account of the story's society, because the world on screen is built by many hands working from the production's collective picture of that society, and the picture leaks. A film whose script preaches equality while its sets assign spaciousness, light, and order strictly by rank has stated its operative anthropology in its carpentry.

The Soviet directors understood the material world's eloquence early, since their films had to argue to audiences for whom a tractor, a cream separator, or a communal kitchen was not a prop but a promise. Dovzhenko's Earth makes the point in its purest form: the arrival of a tractor in a village is photographed as an event in the history of the species, and the film's lyricism is inseparable from its inventory of fruit, grain, animals, and weather. The material world there is the argument, and the analysis that confines itself to the plot's collectivisation dispute has missed the film's real address to its audience, which was conducted in images of abundance and continuity.

## Endings Across the Traditions

The narrative step can be sharpened by comparing how the traditions treat the ending. The classical settlement closes the contradiction and restores the order, and its analysis has been given. The montage film ends in a synthesis the spectator has been made to construct, the crowd and the ship united, the old world's representatives expelled from the frame of the future. The Brechtian work refuses the settlement's emotional wage, ending on the unresolved situation and the addressed audience, sometimes literally, with a direct appeal that transfers the contradiction from the stage to the street. The fractured classical work ends with its settlement achieved and its cost visible in the margin, the figure at the edge of the final composition whom the resolution could not accommodate. The art cinema that inherited these possibilities made the open ending a convention of its own, which later hardened into a mannerism as predictable as the closure it replaced, a decay the tradition's genre analysis predicts for every device that becomes a signature.

For the working analyst the comparison yields a checklist of questions rather than a set of labels. What does the ending settle, by what device, at whose expense, with what residue of feeling, and addressed to a spectator imagined as judge, as witness, as accomplice, or as mourner? The answers place the work in its tradition more reliably than any declaration of allegiance by its makers, since works regularly belong to traditions their makers disowned.

## Two Cinemas of a Single Year

The year 1968 concentrates the field's alternatives into a single moment, and a comparison across it teaches more than a survey of either side. In that year the Dziga Vertov Group was formed in Paris to make films politically, the Cannes festival was interrupted by filmmakers in solidarity with striking workers and students, and the classical industries of Hollywood and Europe continued to produce, alongside the disruptions, the polished entertainments their systems were built for. The same cameras, stocks, and laboratories served both cinemas. The difference lay in the organisation of production, the construction of the works, and the address to audiences, which is the strongest single demonstration the record offers against the apparatus thesis in its strong form: identical equipment, divergent cinemas, the variation explained entirely by the social and formal variables the procedure examines.

The comparison also shows the costs on each side with unusual clarity. The counter cinema of 1968 reached small publics, depended on the subsidy of television commissions and the tolerance of festivals, and left many of its works more cited than seen. The commercial cinema of the same year reached its publics at scale and left, among its polished products, works that the fractured category would later claim. A Marxist analysis declines to award the palm on the evidence of intentions. It records reach, construction, and response, and it notes that the subsequent half century absorbed the counter cinema's devices into advertising and music film while preserving its questions in the classroom, an outcome neither side predicted and both sides' theories must digest.

## The Problem of Intention

Students regularly ask whether the analysis requires the filmmakers to have intended the effects described, and the tradition's answer is one of its most useful bequests. Intention is evidence where it can be documented, in statements, drafts, and correspondence, and it belongs to the production step as one fact among others about how the work came to be. The analysis of form, settlement, and position does not wait on intention, because those are properties of the work as released and encountered, and a work regularly does what its makers did not plan: the fracture in the classical film is the standing example, an effect no one intended and everyone can verify. Conversely, an intended effect that the work fails to produce is a biographical fact, not a critical one. The rule protects analysis from two opposite errors, the biographical reduction that asks only what the author meant, and the paranoid reading that treats every effect as a conspiracy. Effects are public; intentions are archival; the procedure keeps the two in their places.

### Does a Marxist reading need the director to have intended the ideology it finds?

No. The reading analyses the work as manufactured and released: its form, its settlement, its constructed position, and its recorded reception are public properties, examinable without access to any mind. Intentions, where documents preserve them, enrich the production step and sometimes explain a feature's origin. They neither license nor veto a finding about the work, which stands or falls on the evidence of the work and its history.

## Film Theory and the Other Arts

The article's arguments have analogues in the series' treatments of literature and music, and a brief comparison fixes the film specific residue. The novel's analysis, as the companion procedure shows, begins from production and income because the novel's material is a represented society. The film's analysis begins from production and apparatus because the film's material includes a machinery of showing. Music's case, treated elsewhere in the cultural cluster, turns on the performance situation and the recording industry. What is specific to film is the conjunction: an industrial product, a photographic claim on the real, a collective and ritual reception, and a formal system built from the cut. Each tradition in this article fastens on one term of the conjunction: political economy on the industry, the documentary categories on the photographic claim, the apparatus on the ritual, montage on the cut. The field's wholeness lies in holding the four together, and its failures have come from absolutising one term against the rest, which is a final way of stating the moral of the apparatus episode.

## A Note on Method for the Working Student

Practical counsel, earned from the marking of many essays, may be gathered in prose. Watch the film twice before writing, once for the experience and once with the notebook, because the first viewing is the only unrepeatable evidence of what the work does to an unprepared spectator, and its record should be kept before analysis revises it. Keep the five rows of the table physically present during the second viewing and let each row accumulate its own notes, resisting the migration of every observation into the row about meaning. Date every claim about reception and name its source class, review, archive, memoir, or inference, in the notes, so that the essay's levels of certainty are settled before the prose begins. When a sequence resists the reading, write the resistance down in full rather than selecting a friendlier sequence, since the resistance is frequently the fracture on which the best paragraph of the essay will be built. And state, in the essay's final movement, the single piece of evidence that would most damage the reading if it appeared. An examiner who finds that sentence knows the candidate has understood the tradition's deepest lesson, which is not a doctrine about cinema but a discipline of claim making that the cinema happened to teach.

The counsel extends to the choice of films for practice. Begin with works whose manufacture is well documented and whose forms are legible: a classical studio picture with a rich archive, a montage film with its director's essays beside it, a documentary whose commissioning is on the record. Difficulty in the object is a virtue only after the procedure is habitual. The student who begins with the most elusive art film will produce the impressionistic essay the procedure exists to prevent, whereas beginning with the documented and the legible yields findings that can be checked, and the confidence that comes from having been right, or instructively wrong, about evidence other people can inspect.

## The Field in One Paragraph, and Its Limits

If the whole article had to be surrendered to a single paragraph, it would run as follows. Marxist film theory holds that a film is a manufactured object before it is a statement, that its techniques do work describable apart from its story's opinions, that its endings settle contradictions at identifiable costs, that its viewing situation constructs a place for a spectator which actual spectators occupy, negotiate, or refuse, and that the record of their responses is the analysis's final court. Its history moves from the unity of theory and practice in the Soviet 1920s, through the Brechtian programme of interruption, to the apparatus theory, and to that theory's surrender at its own tradition's hands, leaving instruments that the wider discipline absorbed. Its limits are the limits of its evidence: production archives are partial, formal description is contestable, reception records are survivals, and every claim in an analysis should carry the mark of the evidence class it rests on. A field that began with a claim about the whole of cinema ends, in its maturity, as a discipline of graded claims about particular works, and the maturity is the achievement.

### What is the single most common fault in student analyses of film?

The single most common fault is beginning from the verdict, deciding in advance what the film's politics are and then illustrating the decision with selected details. The procedure exists to reverse that order: manufacture, technique, settlement, position, and only then, against the audience record, a verdict. Essays that follow the order can still be wrong, but they are wrong in ways that evidence can correct, which is the difference between an analysis and an opinion with footnotes.

## The Newsreel and the Weekly Ritual

The newsreel, exhibited as part of the classical programme, is a small form with an outsized place in the tradition's evidence, because it was the point where the cinema's claim on the real met its audience weekly and where states and companies competed openly for the framing of events. The Soviet Kino-Pravda series was a newsreel before it was a theory, and Vertov's manifestos generalise a newsreel practice: the organisation of actuality fragments into a argued sequence. The commercial newsreels of the Western studios performed the same construction under the sign of mere reporting, their commentaries assigning causes and sympathies while their editing presented the assignment as the event's own shape. The wartime newsreel, in every belligerent country, is the documentary categories' clearest school case, since the same battles appear across enemy reels with identical footage grammars and opposed meanings, an experiment arranged by history on the scale the classroom could not afford.

The newsreel's decline with television news removed the ritual but not the form, whose descendants in broadcast and later in the clipped and captioned fragments of digital circulation reproduce its structure exactly: actuality fragments, an authoritative framing voice or text, and a claim of transparency that the framing belies. The analyst trained on the categories will have no difficulty with the descendants, which is the evergreen point. Forms persist through the replacement of their carriers, and a classification pitched at the level of the relation between construction and claim survives the newsreel, the bulletin, and whatever succeeds them.

## Colour, Widescreen, and the Economics of Spectacle

Two technical transformations of the postwar decades show the production step explaining form at industrial scale. Colour, available from the 1930s, became general in the 1950s not when its chemistry improved but when the industry needed a spectacle the domestic television set could not match; the same pressure produced the wide screens and stereophonic sound of the same years, marketed as revolutions in realism and explicable, in the trade record, as competitive differentiation. Comolli's method reads such episodes without strain: each advance was sold as a conquest of the real, each was adopted on a schedule set by competition, and each installed new conventions, of colour design, of composition across a wider field, that subsequent analysis treats as the period's style. The analyst who knows the schedule can separate what the technology made possible from what the market made necessary, and can resist the industry's own historiography, in which every commercial decision arrives dressed as an aesthetic destiny.

The episode also supplies a clean test of the grading habit. That widescreen was adopted under competitive pressure is documented in the trade record. That its wider field changed the organisation of the spectator's attention is a formal claim, arguable from the works. That it changed the subject position of cinema as such is the apparatus style of claim, and the subsequent history of the format, absorbed within a decade into the ordinary grammar and then into the television frame itself, is the evidence against the strong version. Three levels, three evidence classes, one episode: the exercise is recommended to students as a drill in keeping claims at their proper heights.

### How can an analyst tell a technical possibility from a commercial necessity?

Read the trade record for the schedule of adoption. A possibility may exist for decades before an industry adopts it, and the moment of adoption, with the competitive or regulatory pressure documented at that moment, identifies the necessity that activated the possibility. Styles that follow adoption curves set by competition belong to the production step's evidence before they belong to the history of taste, however sincerely the participants experienced them as artistic choices.

## The Festival as an Institution

The film festival, from its prewar origins as a national showcase through its postwar expansion into a circuit, is the institution where the margin described earlier acquires an address. Festivals sort the world's production into hierarchies of prestige, create the market in which independent and national cinemas find their buyers, and confer the critical capital that converts into distribution. A Marxist analysis of the festival system asks its standing questions: who finances the showcase, whose labour it displays and whose it conceals, which cinemas the selection renders visible and which it leaves unshown, and how the prize economy shapes the works made in anticipation of it. The festival film, as a recognisable kind, formally ambitious, addressed to juries and critics as much as to publics, nationally inflected and internationally legible, is a genre produced by an institution, and its analysis belongs in the production step alongside the studio picture's.

The festival also functions in reception history as the place where decodings are first professionalised. The reading that a festival's critics establish, in reviews written overnight and syndicated widely, frames the work for its subsequent publics, and the stratigraphy of a film's meanings often begins with a festival stratum whose terms later audiences inherit without knowing their origin. The analyst reconstructing a reception should therefore start where the record starts, at the premiere and its coverage, rather than with the settled reputation the coverage eventually produced.

## Montage After the Founders: The Compilation and the Advertisement

The compilation film, assembled entirely from existing footage, is montage theory's purest descendant, since it dispenses with the shot as anyone's creation and works only with relations. Esfir Shub, who had re-edited imported pictures in the Moscow cutting rooms, made the form's founding works in the late 1920s, constructing histories of the revolution and the fallen dynasty from the newsreel archives of the regime she opposed and served in turn. Her practice demonstrated that the archive itself is an argument waiting for an editor: the same tsarist newsreel, recontextualised, convicts its makers. Compilation became a permanent resource of documentary and of the essay film, and its ethics, the seizure of images from their contexts, remains a live question the tradition bequeathed to every maker who works with found material.

Advertising absorbed the montage inheritance from the other direction. The thirty second commercial is intellectual montage industrialised: collision of images engineered to produce a concept, the product as the concept's beneficiary, in a spectator who must conclude the association as though it were a perception. Trailers perform the same operation for films themselves. The tradition's founders intended the method as an instrument of enlightenment, and its most widespread contemporary use is the manufacture of desire, a reversal that a Marxist analysis is professionally obliged to find unsurprising: a technique, like any productive force, serves the relations that own it. The observation returns the article to its first principle with the circle closed. Form has tendencies, not allegiances, and the analysis of any device must end in the analysis of the organisation that deploys it.

### What does the advertisement's use of montage prove about the method?

The advertisement proves that the method is a technique of producing conclusions in spectators, indifferent to the conclusions' content. A device built to teach dialectics sells soap with equal efficiency, because the mechanism, collision producing a concept the viewer experiences as a discovery, works for any concept. The proof cuts in two directions: it vindicates the founders' account of how montage works, and it refutes any claim that the method carries its politics inside itself.

## The Long Take and the Ethics of Attention

Bazin's defence of deep focus and the long take has been mentioned as a convergence from outside the tradition; its full weight belongs here, because the debate between montage and the long take is the formal step's richest historical example of two materialisms in dispute. Bazin held that montage, by imposing a single sense on events, does violence to their ambiguity and to the spectator's freedom, whereas the long take in depth preserves the continuum of the real and obliges the spectator to choose where to look, exercising a freedom that resembles the freedom of attention in life. The Marxist responses divided. The montage school heard in the praise of ambiguity a refusal of analysis, a preference for contemplation over conclusion. Others, including later Marxist critics of a realist persuasion, heard a genuine ethics of spectatorship and a check on the director's authoritarianism, the cut as the point where the filmmaker's power over the audience is most absolute. The dispute was never settled by decree, and its terms still organise practical argument whenever an editor decides whether to cut or to hold. For the student, the value lies in seeing two accounts of the spectator's good, as the site of manufactured conclusions and as a freedom to be respected, argued at the level of the shot, with masterpieces mustered on both sides.

## The City Symphony and the Documentary Poem

Between the newsreel and the feature stands the city symphony, the documentary poem of the 1920s city, of which Man with a Movie Camera stands as the supreme case and Berlin: Symphony of a Great City of 1927, by Walter Ruttmann, the most famous companion. The form organises a day in a metropolis by rhythm and analogy rather than by story: transport rhymes with machinery, crowds with currents, labour with leisure, and the city itself becomes the collective protagonist that the Soviet theory required. Ruttmann's film, made outside the Soviet system for a commercial German company, shows the form travelling with its politics thinned into exhilaration, a comparison the tradition uses to teach the difference between a device and a doctrine. Vertov's city is an argument about production and interconnection, its rhymes drawn between the mine, the mill, the bakery, and the table. Ruttmann's is a celebration of metropolitan energy whose rhymes connect anything that moves. The editing grammars are kin; the analyses the films perform are not, and the classification of the pair, one toward the seventh category's self awareness and one toward the first category's glossy transmission of a modern self image, is a standard exercise.

The city symphony also matters to the reception strand, because its audiences were the citizens it depicted, watching their own routines estranged into pattern. Contemporary accounts record the pleasure of recognition, the crowd scenes searched for familiar streets and faces, a decoding the makers counted on and the theory of the period barely described. Here, decades before reception research, is an audience occupying a film in a mode, self recognition, that neither the montage theory of manufactured conclusions nor the later theory of the positioned subject has a drawer for. The archive keeps supplying such embarrassments, and the tradition's health consists in filing them where they belong, as evidence that its categories of response are incomplete.

## Criticism of the Everyday: The Reviewing Tradition

The journalistic wing of the tradition deserves a fuller word, since for most readers the Marxist analysis of film arrives as reviewing rather than as theory. The critics of the left press, and the film writers formed by the documentary movement, developed a reviewing practice whose marks are recognisable across decades: the review that begins with the conditions of production, that treats the star as an industrial fact, that asks what a popular success is doing for its audience rather than whether it deserves one, and that takes genre pictures seriously as the culture's dreams on an industrial schedule. Siegfried Kracauer, whose writings move between journalism, sociology, and the theory he published in exile, is the practice's most considerable figure: his essays of the 1920s on the mass ornament and on the little shop girls at the movies read popular entertainment as the surface on which a society's structure becomes visible to itself, and his later history of German film applied the method to a national catastrophe. Kracauer's example instructs because his claims are pitched exactly where his evidence lives, in the observed detail of programmes, queues, and picture palaces, and his famous larger theses remain contestable in proportion as they outrun that evidence. The grading habit again: the tradition's journalists at their best model it as reliably as its theorists at theirs.

## The Problem of the Popular

No question divided the tradition more persistently than the standing of popular success. The culture industry argument treated popularity as the system's certificate, the proof that a product had been correctly standardised for its market. The reception researchers, Hall above all, warned against the contempt folded into that treatment: the popular arts are the terrain on which subordinate classes conduct much of their cultural life, and a criticism that dismisses what millions love has dismissed the evidence of how those millions understand their world. The mature position, associated with the cultural studies that grew from Hall's centre at Birmingham, treats popularity as a fact to be explained in both directions: by the industry's power to manufacture and promote, and by the genuine articulations between a work's materials and its audience's experience, without which manufacture alone cannot explain why one standardised product succeeds and its twin fails. The five-part procedure encodes the position: production explains the offer, reception audits the uptake, and neither is permitted to swallow the other.

The point has a direct application to examinations, where candidates are tempted to treat a film's commercial fate as either a verdict on its ideology or an irrelevance. It is neither. A success is evidence that a settlement satisfied, at a moment, for a public, and the analysis that can say which settlement, for which public, at which moment, has converted a box office commonplace into a finding, without ever needing the figures that this article's protocol excludes.

## The Remake and the Sequel as Evidence

The remake is the industry's own controlled experiment, and the tradition uses it as such. A story remade across decades, with its settlements adjusted to each period's permissions, displays the ideology of the settlements with unusual clarity, because the constant story throws the variable resolutions into relief: the adulteress punished in one version and vindicated in another, the worker's claim granted in one settlement and converted into a romance in the next. The sequel performs a related service for the analysis of manufacture, since its existence is the risk structure made visible, and its variations on the original record the producer's reading of what the audience rewarded. Analysts who work comparatively across versions are practising the reception and narrative steps with the archive's assistance, and teachers prize remakes for exactly that reason: the comparison does half the analysis before the student writes a word.

## What the Tradition Owes to Its Opponents

A survey that credits only the faithful misrepresents how the field advanced. The auteur critics taught the ideology critics to read mise en scène. Bazin taught them that the spectator's freedom is a value a materialism must price. The cognitivist and analytic critics of the 1980s and 1990s, Bordwell and Carroll chief among them, taught them to state claims in forms evidence could touch, and the lesson was absorbed even by scholars who resented the teachers. The feminist critics taught them that the spectator has a sex and that genres have genders in the culture's accounting. The historians of the industry taught them that archives outlast manifestos. A tradition is not diminished by its debts. It is diminished only by refusing to record them, and the record kept here is part of the article's own compliance with the discipline it recommends: claims graded, sources named, defeats and borrowings entered in the same ledger as victories.

## The Seminar Exercise: One Film, Five Findings

Teachers who use this article's procedure commonly set a single exercise that tests all five rows at once, and describing it gives students a rehearsal in miniature. The class takes one classical studio production whose production history is documented. Before the screening, the production record is distributed: the studio, the unit, the casting constraints, the censorship correspondence where it survives. The screening is then watched with the table in hand. In the seminar, each row is reported by a different group, and the rule of the exercise is that no group may borrow another row's evidence: the form group may not cite the plot, the reception group may not cite their own responses as though they were the first audience's. The exercise's climax is the confrontation between the fourth and fifth rows, where the constructed position, specified from the film, meets the documented responses, and the class watches its own fourth row hypothesis survive, bend, or break. Students remember the exercise because it is the one occasion in a syllabus where a critical claim is allowed to lose in public, and the memory instructs more durably than the lecture it replaces.

The exercise also scales. A single scene can carry it in an hour, a short film in a week, a season of television in a term's project. What does not scale is the temptation it is designed to break, the rush to the verdict, which grows with the size of the object and must be resisted at exactly the same points whatever the object's length: documents before impressions, description before interpretation, address before effect, and the audience's record before the critic's certainty.

## Sound Stages, Backlots, and the Geography of Production

The geography of manufacture leaves its own evidence in films. The studio backlot, with its permanent streets dressed as many cities, standardised the world's appearance to a few acres of architecture, and the classical cinema's geography, its Paris and its frontier town recognisably neighbours, is a production fact before it is a style. Location shooting, when the industry adopted it for prestige and economy in changing proportions across the decades, altered the evidentiary texture of films and their claims on the real, and the choice between lot and location in a given production is recoverable from the record and legible in the result. The analyst attentive to geography can often date and place a production's circumstances from its spaces alone, and can read in its composite cities the industry's picture of the world it sold to itself. The point generalises the article's refrain into the visual field: what looks like the world is a workplace's output, and the workplace can be reconstructed.

## Neorealism and the Cinema of the Street

Italian neorealism, the movement that runs from the middle 1940s through the early 1950s, is the test case where the traditions of this article meet a cinema none of them designed. Roberto Rossellini's Rome, Open City of 1945 and Vittorio De Sica's Shoeshine of 1946 and Bicycle Thieves of 1948 were made in a defeated country, with damaged studios, scarce stock, non professional performers in leading roles, and stories drawn from the occupation, the black market, and the unemployment of the reconstruction. The production step explains the style's famous marks before any aesthetics is invoked: location shooting because the studios were bombed or requisitioned, unknown faces because the star system had collapsed with the regime that sustained it, contemporary subjects because the recent past was the only capital the industry possessed. A reading that treats neorealism as a pure choice of conscience has skipped the table's opening row.

The formal step then finds the choices that scarcity did not dictate. Cesare Zavattini, the movement's theorist and De Sica's screenwriter, argued for a cinema that would follow an ordinary person's day without inventing crises, on the ground that the facts of ordinary life under those conditions were dramatic enough and that the analytic attention to them was itself a politics. The long observation of a man searching a city for a stolen bicycle, the refusal to supply the recovery the audience desires, the ending that returns the hero to the crowd with nothing settled: these are settlements declined, and the narrative step registers the refusal as the movement's signature. The contradiction, between a worker and the property his labour requires, is opened and left open, because in the Italy of 1948 it was open.

Bazin championed the movement as the cinema of reality's ambiguity, and his championship created the standing classroom contrast with the Soviet school: montage manufactures the conclusion, neorealism withholds it, and the spectator of the neorealist film is trusted with an attention the montage film organises. The Marxist assessment is more divided and more interesting. The movement's subjects were the working class and the poor; its method dignified their ordinary hours; its international success re-founded a national industry and showed filmmakers on several continents that a cinema could be built from a society's own materials at low cost. Its limits were the obverse of its method: the analysis of causes rarely rises above the depiction of conditions, the institutions responsible for the conditions stay off screen, and pity can replace the account of relations that the montage tradition, for all its didacticism, never omitted. The categories place the best of the movement between the second and the fifth, political in address and fractured in effect, and the argument about the placement is, once again, the education the grid exists to give.

### What did scarcity explain in neorealism, and what did it not explain?

Scarcity explained the movement's materials: the locations, the non professional faces, the contemporary stories, the small crews. Scarcity did not explain the movement's patience, its refusal of manufactured climax, or its decision to leave its contradictions unsettled, since other cinemas in equally ruined countries rebuilt the classical machinery as fast as they could. The distinction between what conditions impose and what artists choose inside conditions is the production step's most important discrimination, and neorealism is its clearest school case.

## Socialist Realism as Doctrine

Against the founding generation's experiment stands the doctrine that replaced it. Socialist realism, proclaimed as the official method of Soviet art at the writers congress of 1934 and enforced across the arts by the cultural administration associated with Andrei Zhdanov, required the depiction of reality in its revolutionary development, which in practice meant the present shown in the light of the future it was said to be approaching: heroes positive and legible, conflicts resolved toward the collective, the party's leading role visible in the settlement. In cinema the doctrine converted the narrative step's imaginary resolution from a tendency to be analysed into a specification to be met. Films were planned, vetted, shelved, and remade against it, and the industry's output contracted to a small number of heavily supervised productions in the doctrine's most rigid years.

The tradition's judgement on the doctrine is severe and should be stated plainly, since the article's neutrality rules require the record and not a balance of courtesies. A method that prescribes the settlement in advance abolishes the analysis of settlements, and a cinema forbidden to register contradiction cannot perform the operation this article has described as the field's core. The montage generation's fate under the doctrine is part of the record: Eisenstein's Bezhin Meadow was halted and its materials destroyed, his later work proceeded under supervision and public self criticism, and the theoretical writings of the 1920s were treated as errors to be recanted. Whatever is claimed for the doctrine's intentions, its effect on the first Marxist film culture was to end it, and the ending is the strongest evidence in the whole history for the article's recurring claim that the source of finance and licence is never neutral. The state that had commissioned the freest film theory of the century withdrew the commission, and the theory survived only in exile, in classrooms abroad, and in the archive.

## Third Cinema: A Manifesto of Liberation

The same year as the Cahiers classification produced the tradition's most militant document. Fernando Solanas and Octavio Getino, Argentine filmmakers of the Grupo Cine Liberación, published the manifesto Toward a Third Cinema in 1969, after completing The Hour of the Furnaces, a clandestine documentary of 1968 screened in factories and union halls under a dictatorship's prohibition. The manifesto's taxonomy divides world cinema into three. First cinema is the commercial spectacle of the metropolitan industries, organised to make its audiences passive consumers. Second cinema is the auteur's cinema, an advance in expression that leaves the system of production and address intact. Third cinema is a cinema of liberation, made outside and against the system, whose screenings are acts of organisation: the film as a detonator of discussion, the projector stopped for debate, the audience converted from consumers into participants.

The manifesto's Marxism is explicit and its categories are cruder than the Cahiers grid, which it resembles in structure while reversing in purpose: where Comolli and Narboni classified in order to read, Solanas and Getino classified in order to recruit. Its analytical weakness is the mirror of its strength. The taxonomy cannot accommodate the fractured classical film, the first cinema work whose internal tensions do political work, and its account of the audience, passive in the first cinema and activated in the third, repeats at the level of politics the uniformity the reception researchers were about to refute at the level of the auditorium. Its historical importance is nonetheless secure. It carried the tradition's questions, of production, address, and the social function of the screening itself, into the cinemas of Latin America, Africa, and Asia, where they were taken up by filmmakers working under censorship and scarcity that the Parisian theorists described but did not endure, and it remains the founding text of the decolonial film theory that succeeded it.

### What distinguishes third cinema from the second, in the manifesto's terms?

Second cinema, the cinema of the auteur, wins freedom of expression inside the existing system of production and exhibition, and the manifesto honours the win while judging it insufficient. Third cinema contests the system itself: its production is collective and often clandestine, its exhibition is an organised event rather than a commercial transaction, and its success is measured by the discussion and organisation a screening generates rather than by its receipts or its reviews.

The comparison between the two documents of 1969 is a fitting seminar exercise, because they embody the tradition's two tempers. The Paris text trusts reading: give the critic the grid and the classical cinema will yield its secrets. The Buenos Aires text trusts the event: change the conditions of production and projection and the cinema will change its nature. The subsequent history gave each a partial vindication. The grid proved the more durable instrument, and the manifesto's central intuition, that the screening is a social act whose organisation is part of the work, passed into the common sense of film culture through festivals, collectives, and the classroom itself, where the stopped projector and the ensuing argument are the manifesto's unwitting monument.

## The Blacklist and the Price of Politics in Hollywood

The political economy of the American industry includes an episode that the production step should never omit, because it displays the industry's political discipline in its rawest form. In 1947 the House Committee on Un-American Activities investigated the film industry, and ten witnesses, writers and a director, who refused to testify about their political affiliations were cited for contempt, imprisoned, and blacklisted. The studio executives, meeting at the Waldorf-Astoria hotel, announced that the ten would not be employed and that no one would be hired whose politics were suspect, and for more than a decade an informal machinery of lists, clearances, and loyalty attestations governed employment in the industry. Writers worked under false names and through fronts; careers ended; a literature of allegory and displacement flourished in the genres, the western and the science fiction picture above all, where the period's persecutions could be staged at a safe historical or planetary distance.

The episode is evidence for several of the article's claims at once. It shows the industry acting as a political institution in its own defence, without any need for direct state censorship, since the blacklist was the employers' own instrument adopted under political pressure. It shows the labour market as the point where ideology is enforced most concretely, not on the screen but on the payroll. And its genre displacements gave later critics a corpus in which the fractured category's method, reading a work for what it cannot say directly, has an unusually firm documentary basis, since the constraints that produced the displacements are on the record. The blacklist's slow collapse, through court decisions, through the public crediting of blacklisted writers by producers who found the machinery untenable, and through the general discrediting of the committee, belongs to the reception history of the industry's own product: audiences and courts withdrew the consent on which the machinery rested.

## Cahiers du Cinéma and the Turn of a Journal

The institutional history of one journal concentrates the field's development so completely that students should know its outline. Cahiers du Cinéma was founded in Paris in 1951 by critics formed under André Bazin's influence, and its first fame rests on the politique des auteurs, the championing of directors such as Hitchcock, Hawks, and Ford as authors whose work, made inside the studio system, bore a coherent personal vision. Through the 1960s the journal's attention widened to the new cinemas and to structural questions. After the events of 1968 its editorship turned sharply: the journal declared for a criticism grounded in historical materialism, produced the classification of 1969 and the collective reading of Young Mr Lincoln, engaged with the Maoist currents of the French left, and passed through a period in which film criticism and political doctrine were fused as tightly as the journal could fuse them. Within a few years the fusion loosened, the editors dispersed into filmmaking, teaching, and other journals, and the Cahiers that continued was a different publication wearing a famous name.

The trajectory matters because it is the apparatus debate's social history in miniature. The same journal that taught a generation to read Ford for his mise en scène taught the next to read Ford for his ideology, using the close attention the first lesson had trained. The turn was not a repudiation of the earlier criticism but its radicalisation under political pressure, and the collective Lincoln reading, the turn's monument, is unintelligible without the auteurist reverence for Ford that it simultaneously honours and indicts. Institutions, like films, are fractured texts, and the tradition's own house journal supplies one of the best examples a teacher could ask for.

### Why did the auteur journal become the ideology journal?

The transformation had intellectual and political causes that reinforced each other. Intellectually, the close reading of mise en scène had reached the limits of celebration and began asking what the celebrated constructions were for. Politically, the events of 1968 radicalised a young editorial generation that inherited the journal's prestige and redirected its method. The continuity is the point: the later Cahiers did not discard the earlier one's skill, it conscripted that skill into a new service, which is why its readings remain formidable where a purely doctrinal criticism would have dated.

## The Coming of Sound as Political Economy

The transition to sound between 1927 and the early 1930s is the industry's best documented case of a technical change whose schedule, costs, and consequences were set by finance rather than by art. Sound required patents, and the patents were held by electrical combines whose licensing terms divided the world's markets between them. Studios converted at speeds dictated by their access to capital, theatres that could not afford wiring closed or were absorbed, and the smaller producers of several national industries were extinguished by the conversion cost alone. The international market, which silent film had crossed with a change of intertitles, fractured along language lines, creating the dubbing and subtitling regimes and the brief experiment of multiple language versions shot on the same sets. Each of these facts is in the trade and legal record, and together they compose the standing refutation of the idea that film form evolves by an inner logic of expression. Form follows the patent, the loan, and the wired theatre, and the analyst who starts from the record will never be surprised by the style that results.

The transition also redistributed labour on a scale the credits make visible. Theatre musicians, employed in their thousands to accompany silent programmes, were displaced by the recorded track, a fact their unions contested and lost. Writers of dialogue rose in price and prestige while the visual storyteller's stock fell, until the industry rebalanced. Stars whose voices mismatched their images lost their capital value, whatever the legends exaggerate about individual cases. The coming of sound, taught properly, is a complete political economy in one episode: technology as property, conversion as concentration, labour displaced and repriced, markets redivided, and a new formal system, the dialogue centred continuity style, precipitated out of the settlement.

## Animation and the Manufacture of Movement

Animation tests the tradition's categories in a medium where the photographic claim is absent, and the test is clarifying. The animated film manufactures its movement frame by frame, so the concealment of manufacture that the classical live action cinema achieves by photography must be achieved by other means: by the smoothness of the drawn motion, by the personality animation that assigns each figure a consistent psychology, by the story's absorption. The great studio animation of the classical period was also the most completely industrialised filmmaking of its era, its labour divided into departments of story, layout, animation, inking, and painting, with the inking and painting performed overwhelmingly by women whose names the credits subordinated, a division of labour the production step recovers from the studio's own records. The animator's strike of 1941 at the Disney studio, over credit, pay, and the organisation of that labour, is the industry's labour history written across its most apparently innocent product, and the settlement of the strike reshaped the studio's subsequent output in ways the archive documents.

Formally, animation gave the montage principle a second life, since the animator, like the editor, constructs movement from stills and knows the construction is the art. The Soviet and European avant-gardes used drawn and object animation for essayistic and satirical purposes the live action budget forbade, and the later tradition of the animated documentary presses the transparency question from the opposite side: a film that announces its drawnness while claiming factual reference divides the categories' sixth and seventh places between its form and its address. The exercise of placing such works is recommended precisely because it is difficult, and difficulty at the grid's joints is where the grid teaches most.

## The Biographical Picture and the Great Man Settlement

The biographical picture, a staple genre of the prestige studio output, performs a settlement of a special kind that the narrative step should name. Its standing contradiction is between the historical process, in which outcomes are collective and conditioned, and the genre's requirement that a life explain an outcome. The settlement appoints the great individual as the process's author: the invention, the reform, the victory issue from the hero's character, and the institutions, movements, and accidents that the record shows are redistributed as the hero's obstacles and helpers. The genre thus manufactures, in narrative form, the ideology of the autonomous subject that the apparatus argument located in the viewing situation, a convergence the student should notice, since it shows the content and form arguments arriving at the same figure from opposite directions. The fractured biopics, in which the hero's cost to others or the record's resistance to the life story's shape breaks the surface, are among the most reliable classroom exhibits of the fifth category, and the genre's decline and ironic revival follow the familiar pattern of a settlement decaying under the weight of the history it must suppress.

## Preservation, Archives, and the Survival of Evidence

Every claim in this article depends on survivals, and the conditions of survival are themselves a chapter of political economy. Film stock decays, burns, and was for decades recycled for its silver or discarded when its commercial life ended, so the archive of cinema is a fraction of its production, skewed toward the prestigious, the state sponsored, and the lucky. The Soviet classics survive in state archives because the state that commissioned them kept them. The ordinary programme pictures of the commercial industries survive patchily, and the cinemas of smaller nations and of colonised peoples survive worst of all, their prints dispersed, their archives unfunded, their histories correspondingly harder to write. The analyst's evidence is therefore pre selected by the very structures the analysis studies, a circularity the honest researcher states at the outset: the tradition's picture of film history is drawn from what its institutions chose, or happened, to keep.

The archival turn in film studies, which has occupied much of the discipline's energy in recent decades of scholarship, is the methodological consequence. Scholars reconstruct lost cinemas from paper records, censorship files, programmes, and trade reporting, and treat the surviving print as one witness among several rather than as the work's transparent presence. Restoration itself is an interpretive act with an economy: which works are restored, with whose money, to which version, are decisions that shape the canon the classroom inherits. The five-part procedure absorbs the lesson without changing its shape. The production step acquires an archival dimension, the reception step a scepticism about the record's completeness, and the whole analysis a standing footnote: the evidence was kept by someone, for reasons, and the reasons are part of the evidence.

## Suture: The Stitching of the Spectator

Between the classification and the apparatus essay stands a concept that the examination syllabuses treat as part of the same formation and that students should be able to define: suture. The term entered film theory through Jean-Pierre Oudart's writings in Cahiers in 1969 and was developed for English readers by Daniel Dayan and Stephen Heath. Borrowed from psychoanalysis, where it names the subject's entry into language, suture in film describes the operation by which the spectator is stitched into the film's discourse at the point where the film threatens to expose its own absence. The classic instance is the shot and reverse shot structure. A shot shows a character looking; the spectator, aware of the frame as a frame, senses the absent field from which the look comes and the machinery that produced it. The reverse shot supplies that absent field as another character's view, closing the gap, and the spectator's unease is relieved by being assigned a place inside the fiction, as the imaginary occupant of the position from which the exchange is seen.

The concept refines the apparatus argument by giving it a local mechanism. Where Baudry described the whole situation, suture describes a cut by cut process, repeatable and checkable against particular sequences, and Heath's analyses of narrative space showed the stitching at work across whole films. The concept also inherits the formation's weakness in a soluble form. If every reverse shot sutures, the claim risks the old uniformity, and the critics of Grand Theory said so. The graded version survives as a description of a default operation that works can refuse: the Brechtian cinema's mismatched looks, held frames, and addresses to camera are, in this vocabulary, refusals of suture, and the vocabulary earns its keep by making the refusal describable in the same terms as the compliance. Students should hold the concept at that grade: a named mechanism, demonstrated in canonical sequences, available for refusal, and not a law of the spectator's soul.

### What does the reverse shot do, on the suture account?

The first shot of a pair opens a lack: the spectator sees a look directed at something unseen and becomes aware of the frame and its outside. The reverse shot closes the lack by supplying the seen thing from a position the spectator is invited to occupy. The exchange converts an awareness of construction into a place inside the fiction, which is why the structure is the classical cinema's most reliable stitch and why breaking it is the counter cinema's most reliable signal.

## The Producer as Author

The production step's scepticism about the director's sole authorship has a positive counterpart in the figure of the producer, and two classical cases teach the range. Irving Thalberg at Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer in the 1920s and 1930s supervised a slate of productions through story conferences, previews, and retakes, imposing a house standard of polish and sentiment that contemporaries recognised across directors, and his early death made him the industry's own legend of the producer as artist. David O. Selznick, an independent producer working through the majors' distribution, left memoranda in quantities that fill published volumes, specifying casting, cutting, music, and publicity for productions whose directors changed while the specifications held. The archive in such cases is unambiguous about where a film's decisions were made, and the analyst who attributes the result to the director alone has not read it.

The cases generalise into a rule of method. Authorship in the studio system is a finding, not a premise, and it is distributed differently in every production: the producer's unit, the star's contract, the front office's censorship anxieties, the editor's assembly, and the director's set work each leave distinguishable traces. The tradition's quarrel with auteurism was never a denial that directors matter. It was a refusal to let a critical policy settle in advance a question that the documents answer differently each time, and the refusal stands as the production step's charter.

## War, the State, and the Commissioned Film

The commissioned propaganda film is the limiting case the categories were partly built to describe, and the democracies' wartime production supplies its most instructive archive because the commissioning is documented and the makers were candid. The American Office of War Information reviewed scripts and issued guidance; Frank Capra's Why We Fight series, made for the Army between 1942 and 1945, explained the war to inducted soldiers using the enemy's own footage recut against itself, a compilation practice Shub would have recognised, placed in the service of a state pedagogy its makers believed in. The British Crown Film Unit and its predecessors produced works such as a much shown bombing mission documentary prepared for air crews and the celebrated short Listen to Britain of 1942, Humphrey Jennings's portrait of a nation at war assembled from its sounds and routines, which the tradition reads as the documentary poem conscripted and humanised at once. The Soviet industry, mobilised entirely, produced the collective war documentaries assembled from front line cameramen's footage at a cost in cameramen's lives that the credits record.

The analytical point is that commission does not settle the reading. These works divide across the categories by their construction and their fractures, not by their sponsorship, which they share. Some transmit their state's case in the first category's smooth form. Some, Jennings above all, accumulate details that exceed the case they were commissioned to make, and the excess is the same phenomenon the melodrama analysis taught the student to read. The state that commissions a film purchases the labour of artists, and the purchase, as the Soviet case showed at the extreme, never wholly abolishes the gap between the brief and the work. A Marxist analysis that treated sponsorship as a verdict would be repeating, in criticism, the error of the apparatus thesis in theory: the deduction of the work from its conditions, without the mediation of the work itself.

### Why does the commissioned film resist classification by its sponsorship?

Sponsorship fixes the conditions of a work, not its construction. Commissioned films share a paymaster while differing in every property the categories measure: the relation of form to content, the presence of fracture, the treatment of the audience as recipient or as judge. The wartime archive proves the spread, since one state's programme contains smooth transmission and fractured masterpieces side by side. Classification by paymaster would erase exactly the distinctions the grid exists to preserve.

## Dubbing, Subtitling, and the Politics of Language

The international circulation of films runs through language, and the choice between dubbing and subtitling, which audiences experience as a preference, is a political economy with a history. The large markets of continental Europe built dubbing industries in the 1930s, in several cases under regimes that treated the foreign voice on screen as a contamination to be replaced and used the dubbing studio as an instrument of censorship, rewriting dialogue in the act of translating it. Smaller markets subtitled, from economy and from a different settlement with foreignness, and their audiences acquired the habit of reading films and hearing other languages as a normal condition of cinema going. The consequences ramify through reception: a dubbed film naturalises its world into the viewer's tongue, completing the transparency the classical style seeks, while a subtitled film keeps the other's voice audible and the act of mediation visible in the lower frame, a small permanent distanciation built into the exhibition. The tradition does not award the palm automatically, since dubbing also democratised access for audiences the subtitle excludes, but it insists the choice is a fact about a film's address and its state's cultural policy, documented in the record and visible in the experience, and therefore a proper object of the analysis's first and fifth steps.

## The Film Society and the Organised Audience

Reception has institutions as well as archives, and the film society is the one the tradition helped to build. From the 1920s onward, in London, Paris, Moscow's orbit, and the university towns of several continents, film societies screened what the commercial circuit would not: the Soviet classics, the avant-garde, the censored and the foreign, to audiences who stayed to argue. The societies were the montage theory's second distribution network and its seminar room; Eisenstein lectured to them, his films survived in their programmes through the decades when the commercial screen forgot them, and the canon of film art that the postwar journals inherited was substantially the societies' repertoire. The pattern repeated after 1968, when the counter cinema circulated through societies, campuses, and union halls, and the manifesto of third cinema made the organised screening its explicit model.

The institution instructs the theory at two points. It demonstrates that audiences are made as well as found, that a public for difficult work can be assembled by organisation, subscription, and discussion, which the strong apparatus claim, with its uniform subject, could not have predicted. And it demonstrates that reception is itself a practice with its own political economy, of membership fees, borrowed prints, and volunteer labour, so that the fifth step of the procedure, like the first, begins by asking who organised the encounter it studies. The classroom screening with which this article's seminar exercise works is the film society's direct descendant, and the teacher who stops the projector for argument is practising, whether the lineage is known or not, the oldest alternative exhibition the cinema possesses.

## Credits, Titles, and the Film's First Minutes

The analysis of a film conventionally begins at its first scene, and the tradition suggests beginning earlier, at the credits, which are the film's own account of its manufacture displayed to every audience. The order and typography of credits record contractual hierarchies; the presence or absence of particular crafts records the production's structure; the title sequence's design, which in the 1950s became a small art of its own in the hands of designers such as Saul Bass, performs the film's first act of address, instructing the audience in the register, playful, solemn, erotic, or austere, in which the work asks to be received. A credit block is also a legal boundary, marking where the owning company begins and the work's many makers are enumerated as its instruments, and the analyst who reads the block with the production step's questions in mind learns things about the film that its admirers, waiting for the story, never notice. The exercise of transcribing and interrogating a credit sequence is recommended to beginners as the cheapest available training in the method: the evidence is on the screen, the documents to check it against exist, and the habit formed, of reading the manufacture before the meaning, transfers to every later step.

## The Essay on the Production of Prestige

Prestige is a currency inside the industry, and its production obeys the same analysis as any other output. The prestige picture, expensively mounted, literary in source, restrained in style, addressed to reviewers and awards juries as much as to the general audience, exists because a studio's standing with exhibitors, talent, and regulators is an asset that receipts alone do not measure. The awards system, founded by the industry itself in the late 1920s, institutionalised the currency: a prize converts into bookings, into the ability to hire, into a defence against censorship campaigns, since an industry that honours serious work is harder to portray as a mere purveyor of sensation. The analyst reads the prestige stratum of a studio's output as its diplomacy, and reads individual prestige works with a double attention, to the film and to the function the film performed for its maker in the season of its release. None of this debunks the works, several of which are the fractured classics of the categories. It explains their existence, their budgets, and their endings, which had to satisfy two audiences at once, the public in the seats and the profession at the ceremony, a doubleness that marks their settlements as plainly as the censorship code marks others.

## The Child Audience and the Family Picture

Every generalisation about the spectator meets its hardest test in the child audience, and the tradition's treatment of children's cinema is a useful audit of its claims. The family picture was an industrial category before it was an aesthetic one, defined by the censorship settlement and by the exhibitor's need for programmes that no constituency would protest. Its settlements are the classical ones with the costs itemised differently, its address is to a doubled spectator, the child absorbed in the story and the parent auditing it for suitability, and its pleasures are engineered for both. The apparatus argument, applied here, would have to describe a subject position occupied simultaneously by two differently placed viewers, which the reception evidence of any family audience refutes in the most ordinary way, by the sound of the auditorium itself. The categories handle the case without strain, since a family picture can be placed by its construction and its fractures like any other, and the animation industry's output, with its layers of address sorted by age, became the standing demonstration that a single work can build several seats and fill them all differently. The lesson is the one this article has drawn from every quarter: positions are plural, occupation is an empirical question, and the critic who specifies both has nothing to fear from the evidence.

## Reading a Single Sequence: A Protocol

The whole method can be compressed into the reading of one sequence, and the compression is a useful final exercise. Take a sequence of a few minutes from a classical work and submit it to the rows in order, in miniature. For production, establish the sequence's place in the schedule and the script's history: was it written early or imposed late, was it a set piece the production was sold on, did the censorship office correspond about it. For form, describe its construction without interpretation for one full paragraph: the number and length of shots, the disposition of the camera, the management of looks, the soundtrack's hierarchy. For the narrative, state the sequence's work in the settlement: what it prepares, what it costs, what it allows the ending to assume. For position, specify where the sequence puts its viewer, in whose knowledge and at what distance, and name the shots that do the placing. For reception, find one documented response to the sequence, a review that praised or deplored it, a censor who flagged it, an exhibitor who reported its effect on an audience, and let the document have the last word. Five paragraphs, five evidence classes, one sequence: a student who can produce that performance owns the method, and an examiner who reads it knows the ownership is genuine, because no part of it can be faked from a survey.

The protocol also reveals, by its difficulty, where a student's preparation is thin. The production paragraph fails for want of archive habits, the form paragraph for want of descriptive vocabulary, the reception paragraph for want of the knowledge that such documents exist. Each failure is remediable in a way that the failure of an impressionistic essay is not, since the remedy is a practice rather than a sensibility. That is the deepest reason the tradition's teachers defend the procedure against the charge of schematism. A schema that tells a student what to go and find is not a substitute for thought. It is the form thought takes when it intends to be checked.

## The Trailer, the Poster, and the Film Before the Film

A film reaches its audience preceded by its own advertisements, and the tradition counts those paratexts as part of the work's social existence. The poster selects the film's saleable contradiction and freezes it into an image: the lovers and the obstacle, the hero and the skyline of danger. The trailer performs a montage of the film's most charged fragments under a voice that instructs the audience in the genre of its coming pleasure, and its construction, studied closely, is a small essay in the industry's theory of its audience, since every cut assumes a desire to be aroused and a resistance to be overcome. Audiences decode the film they were promised against the film they receive, and the gap between promise and delivery is a documented source of the negotiated readings the fifth step collects: the spectator who feels mis-sold is already reading oppositionally before the feature begins. The production step therefore includes the marketing department among the film's makers, not as a courtesy but because the department's work is inside the audience's experience of the film, framing the encounter as surely as the auditorium's architecture, and considerably more noisily.

The star's publicity belongs to the same account. The fan magazine profile, the arranged romance, the carefully managed scandal and its carefully managed repentance were, in the studio era, departments of manufacture with budgets and staff, and the image they maintained was the capital the production step describes. A scandal that broke the maintenance, or a star who refused it, repriced the capital at once, which is why the studios policed their contract players' private conduct with a thoroughness that belonged to asset management rather than to morals. The analyst who understands publicity as manufacture will read the era's fan culture, its letters, its scrapbooks, its organised clubs, as the reception of a product that had been in production since long before the premiere.

## Rhythm of the Programme: The Supporting Feature and the Short

The classical programme was a constructed evening, and its construction shaped the feature's reception in ways the single film analysis forgets. A newsreel opened the proceedings with the world's authority, a cartoon lowered the house's guard into laughter, a short subject or a second feature set the register, and the main picture inherited an audience already tuned by two hours of graded stimuli. Exhibitors reported the programme's balance to the trade press as a single quantity, a good show or a weak one, and producers of features designed their openings for spectators arriving from that preparation rather than from the street. The double feature's economics also governed length and pacing in the B picture sector, where a tight hour of narrative was a specification imposed by the programme's arithmetic. When television dissolved the programme into the single broadcast work, and the later theatrical revival reduced it to trailers and the feature, the audience's preparation changed its nature, becoming a matter of scheduling, channel identity, and the viewer's own preceding hours. The fifth step's question survives every version: what had this audience just been given, and by whom, before the work under analysis began?

## The Intertitle, the Subtitle of Silence

Silent cinema's written word, the intertitle, is a small archive of the period's assumptions about its audience. Titles were written by specialists, sometimes by literary figures hired for prestige, and their register, witty, sententious, or briskly functional, was a house style as recognisable as a studio's lighting. The title's placement is a formal fact of the first order: it interrupts the image to deliver speech or exposition, breaking the visual continuum in a way the later cinema's dialogue, woven into the soundtrack, learned to conceal. Theorists of the silent film divided over the device, purists treating it as a literary contamination and practitioners treating it as a resource of rhythm and irony, and the montage school used titles as one more element of collision, a verbal shot among visual ones. For export, titles were the point of translation, swapped in the cutting room for each market, which made the silent film the most internationally fluid commodity the industry has produced and its national versions genuinely different texts, a fact the reception historian of the period must hold constantly in mind. The coming of sound destroyed that fluidity and replaced it with the dubbing and subtitling regimes described earlier, and the loss is one measure of what the transition cost the cinema as a world form.

## The Question of the Ending Once More: Test Screenings

The preview system, by which the studios screened nearly finished pictures before recruited audiences and revised them against the response, is the reception step operating inside production, and its archive is among the most candid in the industry. Preview cards recorded laughter, restlessness, and verdicts scene by scene; endings were reshot when the cards condemned them, villains softened, explanations inserted, and the final settlement of many a classical picture is the negotiated product of a test audience's resistance. The system is a standing embarrassment for two theories at once. Against the auteur account, it shows the work's final form decided by a committee reading cards. Against the apparatus account, it shows the industry treating its audience as anything but a guaranteed subject: the producers who supposedly manufactured consent feared their audiences' judgements enough to build an institution for anticipating them, and paid to reshoot when the anticipation failed. The mature tradition draws the moral it draws everywhere. The audience is a force the industry organises its whole existence around courting and fearing, and any theory, of manipulation or of authorship, that forgets the fear will misread the product.

## The Critic's Screening Room: A Last Institution

One institution remains, the one in which this article's own kind of writing is produced: the professional screening and the review. The critic sees the film in conditions no public shares, in a small room, in the morning, among colleagues, with a press kit's account of the production already in hand, and writes against a deadline for an audience that will see the film in conditions opposite in every particular. The tradition's sociology of criticism, sketched earlier, takes the arrangement seriously: the review is a manufactured object with its own production step, and its judgements arrive pre framed by the press kit's emphases, the season's critical conversation, and the outlet's standing line. None of this discredits reviewing, which remains the liveliest form the analysis takes outside the university. It instructs the student who uses reviews as reception evidence to grade them as what they are, the record of a professional caste's encounter with a work under institutional conditions, valuable precisely because the caste's terms set the vocabulary in which a film's public meanings are first negotiated. The fan's letter, the exhibitor's report, and the censor's file correct the review's distortions from their own, and the triangulated record, as the reception section argued, is the closest the fifth step comes to the audience itself.

## The Whole Argument in Its Historical Proportions

A sense of proportion is the last thing a survey owes its reader. The traditions surveyed here occupied, at their height, a few journals, a few film schools, the programmes of a few brave exhibitors, and the syllabuses that multiplied after them. The cinema they analysed was a planetary industry whose product they influenced hardly at all. Their importance is not industrial but intellectual and pedagogical: they changed what could be said about films, and therefore what could be seen in them, for everyone trained since. A student who finds that disproportion deflating has understood something real about the place of criticism in the culture it criticises, a place the tradition itself, with its analysis of margins and its respect for the audience's intractability, is better equipped to describe than any triumphalism. The questions survive the proportions. Who made this, by what means, in whose interest, addressed to whom, and answered by its audiences in what ways: five questions, asked of a musical or a masterpiece with the same patience, are the tradition's whole bequest, and they are enough.

## A Short Glossary in Prose

Survey articles in this series close their conceptual work by gathering the field's terms with their working definitions, and the gathering is done in prose because the terms earn their meaning from the arguments they serve. The apparatus names the disposition of equipment and viewing situation considered as a producer of effects, and in its strong form the discredited claim that the disposition guarantees a subject. The dispositif, where the distinction is kept, names the wider arrangement of hall, ritual, and expectation around the basic machine. A dispositif analysis and an apparatus analysis may therefore reach different verdicts on the same screening, and the student should state which is being attempted. Distanciation names the family of devices that interrupt identification in order to return the spectator to judgement. Interpellation names the hailing by which, in the source theory, an individual is constituted as a subject by recognising himself in an ideology's address. The male gaze names the alignment of looks, in camera, characters, and spectator, by which the classical cinema assigns the female figure to be looked at and the male figure to do the looking, a psychoanalytic and feminist concept whose company this article has been careful to keep distinct. A negotiated reading names the decoding that accepts a work's framework in general while refusing its application to the reader's own case, and stands between the dominant reading, which accepts the framework whole, and the oppositional reading, which replaces it. Suture names the stitching of the spectator into a film's discourse at the points where the construction threatens to show. The social gest names the quotable behaviour in which a performance makes a social relation visible. Each term has been used in this article in the section where its argument lives, and the glossary's purpose is retrieval, not replacement: a definition memorised without the argument is the survey reader's characteristic possession, and the examinations are designed to detect it.

## The Examination Room as a Viewing Situation

It is fitting to end the survey where its readers will use it. The examination room is itself a viewing situation, with its own apparatus of desks, silence, and supervised time, and the candidate who has internalised this article's discipline will treat the paper as the procedure treats a film. The questions are manufactured objects, set by a board, moderated, and standardised, and their production explains their shapes: the head question that tests the definition, the device questions that test command of the instruments, the long tail questions that test discrimination between neighbouring ideas. Reading the paper with the production step's eyes is not cynicism. It is the recognition that assessment, like cinema, is an institution whose outputs become legible once its organisation is understood, and the recognition steadies the candidate who might otherwise read a hard question as a personal affront rather than as a designed discrimination between bands.

The candidate's answers, in turn, are settlements, and they succeed by the narrative step's standard: the contradiction the question opens, between the claim and its fate, between the device and its limits, is settled by a device whose cost the marker can see the candidate counting. An answer that states the apparatus thesis and stops has settled nothing. An answer that states it, grades it, names the evidence that retired it, and specifies the evidence that would revive it has performed the whole article in miniature, and it will be marked accordingly in any room where the tradition's own standards govern the marking. That convergence, between the method of the field and the method of its assessment, is the last thing the survey can teach, and it is not a small thing: a discipline whose examinations reward its own deepest habit, the grading of claims against evidence, has arranged for its survival in the only institution that reliably reproduces it.

## The Habit That Outlasts the Doctrines

A closing word belongs to the habit of mind the whole survey has been exercising, because students retain habits longer than they retain taxonomies. Faced with any film, the graduate of this tradition slows down at the points where other viewers speed up: at the credits, where the manufacture is confessed; at the joins, where the argument is assembled; at the ending, where the bill for the settlement is presented; at the doorway of the auditorium, where the audience's murmur gives the first, unguarded returns on the work's address. Slowing down is not a doctrine, and it survives every doctrine's revision, including the revisions this article has recorded against the tradition's own proudest claim. The montage directors slowed down the cut until it confessed its manufacture of meaning. The Brechtians slowed down the performance until its arrangements could be judged. The reception researchers slowed down the audience's response until its variety became undeniable. Each slowing was a small refusal of the speed at which the industrial product asks to be consumed, and the refusals, accumulated, are the field's real continuity across a century in which its theories rose, hardened, and were honourably retired. A student who leaves with the habit owns the tradition in the only form in which traditions are ever really owned: as a way of attending, tested against evidence, and ready to be revised by the next screening.

## For students and exam candidates

Build your answer on the definition and the five-part table, and let the history supply the examples. The discriminating material is the fate of the apparatus claim: state the claim at full strength, attribute it to Baudry's essay of 1970, then state its abandonment and name the evidence, the diversity of films from identical equipment and the reception record of divergent decodings. Separate the essay of 1975 from Marxist argument by its mechanisms whenever you cite it. Use the seven categories on one named work rather than listing them in the abstract. Candidates preparing for assessment in the wider syllabus should pair this article with the [marxist literary criticism exam guide](/2020/10/15/marxist-literary-criticism-exam-guide/), since the two procedures, for film and for literature, are examined in the same spirit and reward the same discipline of graded claims.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The seven-category classification is the most teachable device in the field, and the seminar exercise described in this article, one film and five findings with each group confined to its own row's evidence, converts the whole tradition into a single session's work. Sequence the reading historically: montage first, where claims are concrete; the classification second, performed on a contrasting programme; the apparatus essay third, with Bordwell and Carroll beside it; reception fourth. Insist that every placement in the grid be argued from shots and documents rather than from reputations. The moment a group's fourth row hypothesis about viewer position meets the fifth row's audience record is the moment the course's real subject, the grading of claims, becomes visible to the class itself.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research fronts are the production archive and the reception record. Production history continues to revise settled readings as studio files, censorship correspondence, and trade sources are worked through, and the political economy strand offers the field's most cumulative programme, with findings that survive their decade because they rest on documents. Reception history is the younger and more open field: exhibitor reports, fan archives, and revival records permit the stratigraphy of a work's meanings to be written with evidence, and the global circulation of the classical cinema provides natural comparisons that the apparatus debate made theoretically urgent. Journalists covering the industry will find the production step's questions, who finances, who distributes, whose labour, who bears the risk, a durable alternative to the publicity cycle's own account of each season's novelties.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxist film theory?

It is the critical tradition that treats a film as a manufactured object before treating it as a statement. Three questions organise the approach: under what relations of financing, studio organisation, and labour was the work produced; what do its techniques of editing, framing, and sound do to an audience; and what place does the viewing situation build for the person in the seat. Political content inside a story is examined last, as one stratum among others. The tradition's history runs from the Soviet montage school through Brecht's interrupting programme to the apparatus theory of the years around 1970 and the giving up of that theory's strong form.

### Q: What is Soviet montage theory?

The doctrine worked out in the Soviet Union during the 1920s by Lev Kuleshov, Sergei Eisenstein, Vsevolod Pudovkin, and their circle, holding that a shot is raw material and that sense is produced at the cut. Kuleshov's workshop exercises indicated that viewers build emotion and meaning out of juxtaposition itself. Pudovkin developed the gentler version, editing as linkage, where pieces accumulate like bricks into a wall of guided feeling. Eisenstein developed the aggressive version, intellectual montage, where images are set in collision so that the spectator must manufacture a concept that no single image holds, a method he tied explicitly to dialectics in his essay of 1929.

### Q: What is apparatus theory in film?

The argument, launched by Jean-Louis Baudry in a Cinéthique essay of 1970, that the ideological work of cinema is done by its machinery and its viewing arrangements before any story starts. The lens inherits the single viewpoint geometry of Renaissance perspective, so the world on screen arrives pre organised around one centred eye. Darkness, immobility, the hidden projector, and the glowing rectangle then seat the spectator as a sovereign subject to whom that world appears simply given. On this account the content of individual films is secondary: the situation itself trains the spectator in the self understanding a dominant order needs.

### Q: What are the seven categories of films?

Comolli and Narboni proposed the scheme in Cahiers du Cinéma in 1969 as a way of plotting any film against the dominant ideology of its society. One class covers works that transmit that ideology smoothly in message and manner alike. A second covers works that fight it on both fronts at once. A third carries dissenting subject matter inside unexamined classical packaging. A fourth keeps an innocent surface while its construction quietly unsettles the codes it appears to obey. A fifth, the celebrated class, looks entirely compliant yet cracks from within, so a reading can prise it open. The last two divide documentary between the kind that pretends to hand over reality untouched and the kind that puts its own making on display.

### Q: Is the male gaze a Marxist concept?

No, and the distinction is a standard examination point. The term comes from an essay Laura Mulvey published in Screen in 1975, Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema, and the machinery of that essay is psychoanalytic rather than Marxist: scopophilia, castration anxiety, and fetishism do the explanatory work, while ownership, labour, and class do none. Mulvey showed how the classical film aligns the camera's look, the male characters' looks, and the spectator's look so that the female figure carries the burden of being seen. The essay shared journals, institutions, and enemies with the Marxist film writing of its decade, which accounts for the persistent misattribution.

### Q: How do you analyse a film's mode of production?

Work from the paper trail outward. Identify the source of the money and the conditions attached to it, the production structure inside which decisions were taken, whether a studio unit, an independent company, or a state organisation, and the distribution route the finished work was built to travel. Read the credit block as a map of divided labour and lodged authority. Use the trade press for the quarrels, the censorship negotiations, and the company expectations that memoirs smooth over. The step is complete when some visible property of the work, a casting decision, a running time, a softened ending, a genre blend, has been traced back to the organisation that made it.

### Q: What is Brechtian distanciation in film?

The transfer to cinema of Brecht's theatrical refusal of absorption. Where the classical film works to make the spectator forget the seat, the screen, and the manufacture, the distanciating film keeps all three in view: performers demonstrate rather than inhabit, titles announce what will happen so that attention moves to how and why, songs and commentary interrupt the action instead of swelling with it, and the machinery of illusion is allowed to show its edges. The spectator is repositioned from guest inside a story to judge of the arrangements the story displays. Godard's work with the Dziga Vertov Group and the austere films of Straub and Huillet are the programme's canonical screen instances.

### Q: Is apparatus theory still accepted?

Only in weakened descendants. The strong thesis, that the equipment of cinema guarantees one ideological subject position for every spectator, was given up, largely by scholars formed inside the tradition itself. Two bodies of evidence did the work. The same cameras, projectors, and halls demonstrably serve films that address their audiences in incompatible ways, which a deduction from equipment cannot explain. And the reception research of Stuart Hall, David Morley, and their successors recorded audiences decoding identical material along dominant, negotiated, and oppositional lines, a variation the thesis has no variable to register. What survives is a set of graded claims about how viewing conditions, conventions of perspective, and institutions shape attention.

### Q: What does a Marxist film analysis examine?

Five objects, in a fixed sequence. First the film as a product: its financing, the structure that housed its making, the labour that made it, and the channels that carried it to audiences. Second its formal organisation: how editing, framing, camera movement, and sound arrange attention and sympathy. Third its story as a settlement: which contradiction the plot opens and what device closes it, at whose expense. Fourth the seat it builds: the knowledge, alignment, and spatial command it offers a viewer, stated as a claim about address. Fifth the audience's record: reviews, censorship files, exhibitor reports, and fan practice, against which the claim about address is tested.

### Q: How does the political economy of film work?

It reasons from the industry's cost structure to its institutions and checks the reasoning against the record. Because a film is costly to originate and cheap to copy, advantage accumulates where distribution and finance sit, and the subfield studies who holds those positions, how ownership concentrates, how risk is spread across slates, stars, genres, and presold properties, and how labour is organised and rewarded. Its exemplary case is the classical studio system, where single companies united making, distributing, and showing, until the Paramount antitrust judgement of 1948 forced the theatre chains out of their hands while leaving the distribution power, as the approach predicts, substantially intact.

### Q: How does the apparatus claim grade the idea that cinema is ideological in its technology?

Grading splits the idea into three claims of descending strength. The weakest, that the technology and the viewing ritual are historical conventions rather than natural givens, is established beyond serious dispute. The middle claim, that the classical arrangement of perspective, darkness, and immobility invites a centred and mastering relation to the screen, stands as a defensible account of an invitation. The strongest claim, that the machinery by itself installs an ideological subject in every viewer regardless of film or audience, is the one the evidence refused. The idea therefore survives as a research programme about conditions and invitations, not as a property of cameras.

### Q: What should a student separate between ideology in a film's content and ideology in its form of viewing?

Two different locations of ideological work. In the content location sit the represented world, the allocation of sympathy among characters, and whatever the work explicitly commends or condemns. In the form location sit the presuppositions of the viewing arrangement itself: a world organised for one centred eye, a manufacture hidden by seamless joins, one voice silently authorised above the others, a closure that presents its settlement as the shape of things. Keeping the locations apart is what allows the interesting verdicts, since a work can be rebellious in the first location and obedient in the second, or the reverse, and the seven categories exist to plot exactly those combinations.

### Q: Why was the apparatus thesis largely abandoned?

Because its predictions failed wherever they could be checked. A theory that reads ideological effect off the equipment must expect comparable effects wherever the equipment is comparable, yet the decades after 1970 supplied contrary evidence from every side: formally opposite cinemas built with identical machines, audiences whose recorded responses diverged along the lines of their social situations, and viewers who enjoyed works while refusing the positions those works had built for them. The thesis also protected itself badly, treating objections as symptoms of the condition it described, which persuaded philosophers such as Noël Carroll that it was not framed to be tested at all. Its own tradition administered the correction, through reception research and through the second thoughts of its early proponents.

### Q: How does the five-part analysis table order a film reading?

By dependency. The production row stands first because no formal choice can be weighed until the conditions that bounded the choices are known. The form row stands second because a settlement cannot be assessed until the means that deliver it have been described. The narrative row follows, since the ending's device and its cost are the objects the first two rows equip the reader to see. The viewer position row converts the accumulated findings into a hypothesis about address. The reception row closes the table because it alone can confirm or break that hypothesis, by confronting it with the documented behaviour of the people who actually watched.

### Q: What separates a formal feature that positions every viewer from one that some viewers resist?

Evidence, and only evidence. A feature earns the stronger description when consistent positioning has been documented across works of widely differing content and across audiences of widely differing formation, which is the test the apparatus thesis set for itself and failed. Short of that showing, a feature is described by the strength of its invitation: how widely the device is shared across a genre, how completely the exhibition ritual supports it, how uniform the recorded responses are. Most real features fall along that scale rather than at its end, and the mature practice of the field is to place them on it explicitly instead of reaching for the language of determination.
