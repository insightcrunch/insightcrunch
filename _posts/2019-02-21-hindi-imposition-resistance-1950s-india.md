The Hindi imposition contest of the 1950s began inside a constitutional sentence. Article 343 of the Constitution of India, which commenced on 26 January 1950, declared that the official language of the Union shall be Hindi in Devanagari script, and then, in its second clause, permitted the English language to continue for official purposes for fifteen years from commencement. Fifteen years is a deadline disguised as a reassurance. Every government file, every railway form, every Union Public Service Commission examination paper in Madras, Calcutta, Bangalore and Bombay was stamped with an expiry date of 26 January 1965, and the politics of the decade that followed was the politics of that date. The claim defended here is the namable claim of this article: the 1950s Hindi-imposition contest shaped the constitutional language settlement, and the agitation that followed the deadline in 1965 was settlement-confirmation, not a fresh contest. The resistance that began in the 1950s was not a provincial refusal to learn a language. It was an organised argument, conducted in Parliament, in commissions, in state legislatures and on the streets of Madras State, about what kind of Union India had founded in 1950, and it extracted from the Union government first a prime ministerial assurance in the Lok Sabha on 7 August 1959 and then a statute, the Official Languages Act of 1963, Act 19 of 1963, that suspended the deadline indefinitely.

## Article 343 and Hindi Imposition: The History of a Fifteen-Year Compromise

Read the text of Part Seventeen of the Constitution as its drafters arranged it, and the compromise is visible in the architecture. Article 343(1) names Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union and prescribes the international form of Indian numerals for official purposes. Article 343(2) keeps English in use for every official purpose for fifteen years, notwithstanding the first clause. Article 343(3) then hands the question to a future Parliament: Parliament may by law provide for the use of English, or of Devanagari numerals, after the fifteen years expire. The founders did not settle the language of the Union in 1949. They scheduled the settlement for 1965 and trusted a Parliament they would not control to finish the work. Article 344 built the machinery for that finishing: a commission at the end of five years and again at the end of ten, charged with recommending the progressive use of Hindi and restrictions on English, and a committee of thirty parliamentarians to examine the commission's report. Articles 345 to 347 left the states their own official languages and regulated communication between the Union and the states. Article 348 kept English in the Supreme Court and the High Courts until Parliament provided otherwise. Article 351 directed the Union to develop Hindi, drawing primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on the other languages of the Eighth Schedule, so that Hindi might serve as a medium for the composite culture the founders invoked.

That arrangement was the product of the Constituent Assembly's debates of September 1949, and those debates were among the most heated the Assembly held. The Munshi-Ayyangar formula, moved by K. M. Munshi and N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, supplied the compromise text after days of argument in which Seth Govind Das pressed for Hindi with Devanagari numerals alone, and members from Madras and Bengal warned that a Hindi-only Union would grade citizens by birthplace. The Assembly adopted the formula on 14 September 1949, a date the Union government later marked as Hindi Diwas, and the closeness of the voting and the bitterness of the exchanges were recorded in the Constituent Assembly Debates for any reader who doubts that the language question was a founding question. The settlement the Assembly reached was deliberately temporal. Hindi was named first and given the future; English was retained and given the present; the Eighth Schedule, listing fourteen languages at commencement, recorded the multilingual fact that the single official-language clause could not wish away. Granville Austin, in The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (1966), treated the language provisions as one of the Assembly's most delicately balanced compromises, and the balance metaphor is exact: a balance is a device that holds only while nobody presses too hard on either side.

Pressing began almost at once, and the pressing had a geography that this article refuses to euphemise. The resistance to Hindi imposition in the 1950s was organised most powerfully in Madras State, in the Tamil-speaking districts of the old Madras Presidency, and it drew on a language politics with roots in the 1930s, on the Dravidian movement's critique of north Indian and Brahmin predominance, and on a hard material fear: that Union employment, Union examinations and Union administration conducted in Hindi would convert a linguistic accident of birth into a civil service career. Madras politicians named that fear precisely, in assembly debates and in Parliament, and Union ministers answered it precisely, which is itself evidence that the contest was national in scope from its first exchanges. A question that occupies the Prime Minister in the Lok Sabha, a presidential commission under Article 344, a committee of Parliament and two statutes is not a regional footnote to a national story. It is one of the arenas in which the national story was decided, and the federal-unitary tension that [the federal-unitary tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) names in the founding settlement ran through every round of it.

## Before the Republic: The Madras Agitation of 1937 to 1940

The 1950s contest had a rehearsal, and the rehearsal matters because its cast returned in changed roles. In 1937 the Congress ministry of the Madras Presidency, headed by C. Rajagopalachari as Premier, made Hindi a compulsory subject in a first group of secondary schools, extending a policy the ministry defended as national integration. E. V. Ramaswami, known as Periyar, who had built the Self-Respect Movement since 1925 and led the Justice Party from 1938, read the order as cultural subordination and organised against it. The agitation of 1937 to 1940 drew women into picketing lines in Madras city, filled the Presidency's jails with volunteers courted for arrest, and recorded the deaths in custody of the protesters Thalamuthu and Natarajan in 1939, names the Dravidian movement kept in honour for decades after. The British Governor, Lord Erskine, withdrew the compulsion in February 1940 after the Congress ministry had resigned on the war question, and Hindi in Madras schools became optional again. Sumathi Ramaswamy, in Passions of the Tongue: Language Devotion in Tamil India, 1891 to 1970 (1997), reconstructed the emotional register of this mobilisation, in which Tamil was figured as a mother and a goddess whose defence was a sacred duty, and her reconstruction explains a fact the constitutional narrative alone cannot: by 1950, opposition to compulsory Hindi in Madras was not a policy preference but an inheritance, carried by organisations, martyrs' names and a devotional vocabulary that any Union government ignored at its peril.

The inheritance passed to a party. On 27 August 1944, at Salem, Periyar had converted the Justice Party into the Dravidar Kazhagam, a social movement that renounced electoral politics; on 17 September 1949, at Royapuram in Madras city, his lieutenant C. N. Annadurai broke with him, in a split whose immediate occasion was Periyar's marriage and succession decisions, and founded the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam as a party that would contest elections. The DMK's founding programme joined social reform to a demand for a separate Dravida Nadu and to an unqualified defence of Tamil against Hindi. M. S. S. Pandian's studies of the DMK's formation and of Dravidian ideology trace how the party translated the older movement's cultural grievance into the idiom of a modern electoral machine: study circles, a party press, film scripts written by Annadurai and by M. Karunanidhi, and a cadre drilled in oratory. Language was the machine's fuel. When the Constitution commenced in January 1950 with Article 343's fifteen-year clock already ticking, Madras State possessed the one organisation in India for whom the deadline of 1965 was a founding reason to exist.

## The Early Republic: Conferences, Demonstrations and the Calendar of Resistance

The resistance of the early 1950s worked to a calendar that the Union government could read as plainly as the DMK could. The 1953 Madras Anti-Hindi Conference assembled the opposition's strands in a single forum, restating the demand that English continue and that any change command the consent of the non-Hindi states. Annadurai's speeches of these years, delivered in the Madras Legislative Council, to which the DMK's allies carried its arguments before the party won assembly seats of its own, framed imposition as a question of democratic consent rather than of linguistic pride: a Union that governed in a language its southern citizens had not chosen, he argued, taxed those citizens twice, once in the learning and again in the competing. Robert King, in Nehru and the Language Politics of India (1997), the specialist study of the Nehru government's language policy, documented how attentively New Delhi monitored this mobilisation, because the monitoring files are the evidence that the centre never believed its own description of the movement as a fringe. The Prime Minister's correspondence and the Home Ministry's reporting treated Madras language politics as a first-order question of Union stability, ranked with the demand for linguistic states that the same years forced onto the national agenda.

Demonstrations in Madras State during 1956 and 1957 kept the pressure warm between the set pieces. The years of the States Reorganisation settlement were also years in which processions, black-flag demonstrations against visiting Union ministers, and conferences of Tamil scholars restated the anti-imposition case to a widening audience. The DMK contested its first general election in 1957 and won fifteen seats in the Madras Legislative Assembly and seats in the Lok Sabha, converting street capacity into parliamentary presence in both arenas where the language question would be decided. Annadurai entered the assembly as leader of a recognised opposition formation, and the quality of the resistance changed with his arrival: the Union government faced, from 1957, a party that could move amendments to official language legislation, question ministers on the record, and carry the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha debates back to Madras as campaign literature. The resistance had acquired a constitutional instrument to match the constitutional text it contested.

## The Kher Commission of 1955 and 1956: The Report Madras Read Closely

Article 344 required a commission at the expiration of five years from commencement, and on 7 June 1955 President Rajendra Prasad appointed the first Official Language Commission under the chairmanship of B. G. Kher, the former Premier and Chief Minister of Bombay, with twenty-one members drawn from the linguistic regions of the Union. The Commission toured the country, took evidence from state governments, universities, writers and administrators, and submitted its report in 1956. The report recommended the progressive replacement of English by Hindi for Union purposes, a switchover it expected to be substantially complete by 1965, with Hindi as the principal official language and English reduced to a subsidiary place. It proposed Hindi as a compulsory subject in schools, Hindi as the medium of Union Public Service Commission examinations in due course, and a programme of terminology, translation and training to make the transition administrable. The chapters of the report that documented the southern states' position, recording the Madras government's evidence and the apprehensions of the non-Hindi regions in detail, are the under-cited primary source of this history: the report preserved at the National Archives of India shows a commission that heard the resistance respectfully and then recommended past it, and readers who open the digitised chapters as scanned office documents can keep the text beside their own notes through [an office file viewer that opens Excel, Word and PowerPoint documents in the browser](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which is how the report's southern evidence is most conveniently re-read against the use later summaries made of it.

### Why did the Kher Commission's report alarm Madras State?

The report alarmed Madras because it treated 1965 as a working deadline rather than a negotiating horizon. Its recommendations pointed Hindi toward the Union's examinations, schools and files on a published timetable, and Madras readers took the timetable literally: a commission appointed under Article 344 had converted the Constitution's fifteen-year permission for English into a fifteen-year countdown against it.

Madras read the report literally because Madras had the most to lose from a literal reading. A Tamil-speaking candidate for Union employment faced, under the report's logic, examinations conducted in a language that was neither his mother tongue nor the medium of his schooling, against competitors for whom Hindi was both. The report's safeguards, which included continued English for a transitional period and regional languages for state purposes, were framed as concessions within a Hindi destination, and the destination was the objection. Two members of the Commission, the Bengali linguist Suniti Kumar Chatterji and P. Subbarayan of Madras, recorded minutes of dissent that questioned the pace and compulsion of the proposed switchover, and the dissents gave the southern reading an authority inside the Commission's own covers: the alarm in Madras was not a refusal to read the report but a close reading of it, dissents included. Robert King's account of 1997 follows the report through the Nehru cabinet and shows ministers weighing the Madras reaction before any public step was taken, which is the documentary trail of a national negotiation conducted through a commission's paperwork.

## The Pant Committee and the Presidential Order of April 1960

The Constitution's machinery had a second stage. Article 344(4) required a committee of Parliament, thirty members elected by the two Houses, to examine the Commission's recommendations and report its opinion to the President. The committee, constituted in 1957 under the chairmanship of Govind Ballabh Pant, the Union Home Minister, reported in February 1959 and softened the Kher timetable in every dimension that mattered to the south. The Pant Committee held that English should continue as the subsidiary official language for as long as non-Hindi regions required it, that the switchover should proceed by consent rather than by calendar, and that no date should be fixed for the final displacement of English. The Presidential Order issued on 27 April 1960, giving effect to the committee's conclusions under Article 344(6), carried the softened line into executive direction: Hindi would advance, English would remain, and the pace would be set by preparation and acceptance rather than by the arrival of 1965. Between the Kher report of 1956 and the Presidential Order of 1960, the Union's own constitutional machinery had travelled from a deadline toward an assurance, and the distance travelled is the measure of the resistance's effect before any statute was drafted. Critics of the Nehru government in the Hindi-speaking states read the same sequence as retreat, and members from those states said so in Parliament, which completes the evidentiary circle: a process criticised in Allahabad and Patna as surrender and in Madras as insufficient guarantee was, by that double criticism, a national settlement in the making.

## Nehru in the Lok Sabha, 7 August 1959: The Assurance

The assurance has a date, a chamber and a text, and precision about all three is the beginning of understanding it. On 7 August 1959, in the Lok Sabha debate on the language question, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru told the House that Hindi would not be imposed on the non-Hindi states against their will, that English would continue as an associate language for as long as the people of the non-Hindi-speaking areas wished it to continue, and that the decision rested with them rather than with the Hindi-speaking majority. He returned to the assurance in the House in September 1959, restating it as a commitment of his government and of his word. The Lok Sabha debates of 7 August 1959 are accordingly a mandatory primary source of this article, because the assurance became a constitutional fact of a peculiar kind: unentrenched, unenacted, resting on a prime minister's statement, and treated by the southern states as the operative settlement from the day it was given. When Madras politicians later demanded that the assurance be written into statute or into the Constitution itself, the demand conceded its authority; they wanted the promise armoured, not denied.

### What exactly did Nehru promise in the Lok Sabha on 7 August 1959?

Nehru promised that Hindi would not be imposed on non-Hindi areas against their will, that English would continue alongside Hindi for as long as non-Hindi speakers wanted it, and that the choice belonged to the non-Hindi states rather than to the Hindi-speaking majority. The promise was a prime ministerial statement in debate, not a statute.

The assurance also had a limit, and the limit explains the statute that followed. A statement in debate binds the speaker's government morally and politically; it does not bind a successor Parliament, and every Madras lawyer who read the 1959 debates knew the distinction. Nehru knew it too. His government's answer to the limit was the Official Languages Bill, introduced to convert the politics of the assurance into the law of the Union before the deadline arrived, and the drafting of that bill, with its choice between the mandatory shall and the permissive may for the continued use of English, became the single most consequential drafting decision of the language settlement. The resistance of the 1950s had won the assurance. The contest of 1963, over one auxiliary verb, would decide what the assurance was worth, and Madras read the bill of 1963 with the assurance of 1959 open on the table beside it.

## Madras Answers in Office: Kamaraj and the State Government's Position

Resistance is usually chronicled from the street and the opposition bench, but in Madras State the argument was also carried by the government of the state. K. Kamaraj, Chief Minister of Madras from 13 April 1954 to 2 October 1963, led a Congress government that depended on New Delhi for its party standing and yet defended the state's linguistic position without embarrassment. Kamaraj, whose own schooling ended early and whose authority in Tamil politics rested on organisation rather than oratory, treated English as the working language of opportunity for Tamil students and resisted every proposal that would have made Hindi a gatekeeper to Union employment during his tenure. The Madras government's evidence to the Kher Commission and its representations to the Union Home Ministry formed part of the documentary record of the southern position, and the distinction matters for the adjudication this article defends: the resistance was not the DMK's property. The state's Congress government, the state's opposition and the state's scholars disagreed about nearly everything else in Madras politics, and converged on the proposition that the Union's language could not be changed over the state's objection. A stance shared by a state's government and its opposition, and pressed upon the centre by both, is a federal fact, and the Nehru government, which needed Kamaraj's Madras as it needed no other southern prop, negotiated with the fact accordingly.

## Rajagopalachari's Reversal: From Compulsory Hindi to the Swatantra Party

No biography in this history measures the resistance's reach like C. Rajagopalachari's. The man who, as Premier of Madras in 1937, had made Hindi compulsory in Presidency schools and faced Periyar's agitation for doing it, emerged in the 1950s as the most eminent southern critic of Hindi imposition, writing and speaking against the switchover with the authority of a former Governor-General of India, the office he held from June 1948 to January 1950. Rajagopalachari's argument had changed its ground: Hindi in 1937 had been, for him, a subject in a provincial school curriculum under a ministry answerable to a provincial electorate; Hindi after 1950 threatened to become the language of the Union's examinations, files and commands, a different order of compulsion imposed on citizens who had never voted for it. In June 1959 he joined N. G. Ranga, Minoo Masani and other critics of the Congress's economic and cultural centralisation in founding the Swatantra Party, and language-political grievance stood among the new party's founding complaints against the Nehru state. The spectacle of the 1937 compulsion's own author leading the 1959 opposition to compulsion is the cleanest single refutation of the claim that anti-imposition politics was a sectarian Tamil possession. Rajagopalachari was a Tamil Brahmin, a Gandhian, a former head of state and a Hindi teacher's patron turned critic, and his trajectory ran through the same constitutional facts that moved the DMK: the deadline in Article 343, the examinations in New Delhi, and the absence of southern consent.
## What the Resistance Feared: Examinations, Employment and the Price of a Language

Strip the oratory from the 1950s contest and a ledger remains. The Union government was the largest single employer of educated Indians, and its doors opened through examinations conducted, in the 1950s, in English. The Union Public Service Commission's civil service examinations, the railway and postal recruitments, the clerical grades of the secretariat: each represented, for a Tamil, Bengali, Malayali or Marathi family, a ladder out of the province and into the salaried security that the new republic most visibly offered. A switchover of the Union's official language to Hindi on a fixed date threatened to revalue every family's educational investment overnight. The student who had spent twelve years reaching an English-medium degree would compete, in Hindi, against the student for whom the examination language was the language of the home. Madras speakers made this argument with actuarial coldness, in assembly debates and in the evidence given to commissions: the issue was not the beauty of Hindi or of Tamil but the distribution of jobs across a federation whose peoples started the linguistic race from unequal lines. Sumathi Ramaswamy's devotional Tamil and the DMK's examination arithmetic were two registers of one politics, the sacred and the actuarial, and the combination explains the movement's durability across classes that agreed on little else: the Tamil scholar defending a goddess and the Tamil graduate defending a mark sheet marched, in the 1950s, in the same procession.

The Union government's replies were heard in the same ledger's terms. Ministers pointed to the retention of English under Article 343(2), to the phased character of every official proposal, to the provision in Article 344 for staged recommendation and parliamentary review, and to the economic absurdity, as they framed it, of a Union that could not speak to itself in one administrative language. The Hindi-speaking states' representatives added an equality argument of their own: that English, a foreign inheritance mastered by a thin educated stratum in every state, was itself an imposition, and a more exclusionary one than Hindi would be, since Hindi at least was an Indian language spoken or understood across the north. That argument, pressed by members such as Seth Govind Das in the founding debates and by Hindi associations through the 1950s, deserves statement at its strongest because the adjudication of this article must defeat the best opposing case, not the weakest. Its weakness is factual rather than moral: an examination in English handicapped all Indian candidates against a colonial inheritance equally, in the sense that no Indian community owned English by birth, while an examination in Hindi conferred a birthright advantage on one region's candidates over every other region's. Equality of handicap and inequality of advantage are different distributions, and the southern argument of the 1950s consisted, at its core, of insisting on the difference until the Union government conceded it in the assurance of 1959 and the statute of 1963.

## A Comparative Tour: Bengal, Assam, Bombay and the Punjab in the Same Decade

Framework Four of this series reads a political question comparatively across states, and the language question of the 1950s rewards the method because Madras was the loudest, not the only, site of negotiation. Bengal's representatives had contested the language clauses in the Constituent Assembly itself, and West Bengal's political class through the 1950s defended Bengali's place in the Eighth Schedule and in the state's administration while watching the Madras argument for its bearing on the Union's examinations, in which Bengali graduates also held a large stake. Assam carried the decade's most violent language politics inside its own borders, as the Assamese-speaking majority pressed for Assamese as the state language against Bengali-speaking Barak Valley and the hill peoples, a contest that produced the Assam Official Language Act of 1960 and bloodshed in the Brahmaputra valley: evidence that language federalism cut in every direction, and that the Union's 1950s language problem was a general problem of a multilingual federation, not a Tamil peculiarity with a Dravidian accent. Bombay State, bilingual from 1956 to 1960, housed Marathi and Gujarati claims that tore the state apart on linguistic lines in the Samyukta Maharashtra and Mahagujarat movements, proving in the west what Andhra had proved in the south, that language was the decade's master key to state formation. The Punjab's contest braided language with religion, Punjabi in Gurmukhi script against Hindi in Devanagari, in census returns and in the Punjabi Suba demand that the States Reorganisation Commission declined in 1955 and that a later decade conceded.

The comparative tour settles a question the regional reading cannot answer. If resistance to Hindi imposition had been a Tamil Nadu particularism, a cultural reflex of one state, the 1950s would show Madras agitating and the rest of the Union acquiescing in a settlement made elsewhere. The record shows instead a federation arguing with itself in several languages at once: Bengal guarding its examination stake, Assam legislating its state language in 1960 amid riots, Bombay dissolving on a linguistic frontier in the same year, and the Punjab deferring its reckoning. Madras differed from these cases in organisation and in target, not in kind. Its target was the Union's official language itself, the one language decision that bound every state, and its organisation, the DMK founded in 1949 and in the Madras assembly from 1957, converted a decade's argument into parliamentary amendments, debated assurances and, in the end, a statute. The national-constitutional reading of this article rests on that comparison: the contest was national because its object was the Constitution's own language settlement, and its southern concentration was the geography of its best organisation, not the boundary of its scope.

## Language as Federalism: Tension Two in the Nehruvian Settlement

The Constitution's language clauses belong to the federal story before they belong to the cultural one. Article 345 lets each state legislature choose the language or languages of the state; Article 346 routes communication between states, and between a state and the Union, through the language authorised for the Union; Article 347 lets the President direct recognition of a language spoken by a substantial section of a state's population. The design presumes a Union that speaks downward in a language its constituent units have accepted, and it locates the acceptance problem exactly where the 1950s found it. The scholarly treatment of [the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) in the founding text shows a Constitution willing to vary the terms of membership to hold the Union together, and the language settlement became, in practice, one more asymmetry: Hindi named for the Union, English retained without term for the states that required it, and the requirement itself left to those states to declare. Read beside [the 1956 linguistic reorganisation that the language politics paralleled](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/), the pattern is a single Nehruvian technique applied twice: concede the substance of a regional demand inside a Union framework, at a pace the centre negotiates, and call the negotiated pace a national settlement. The technique is the connective tissue of [the Nehruvian-era equilibrium](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/), and the language file shows the equilibrium being struck, clause by clause and assurance by assurance, rather than merely proclaimed.

### How did language become a federal question rather than a cultural one?

Language became federal when the Constitution attached it to power: to the Union's official acts under Article 343, to state legislatures' choices under Article 345, to communication between governments under Article 346 and to the courts under Article 348. A cultural preference needs no one's permission; a language of examination, file and judgment distributes jobs and authority across states.

The federal reading also disciplines the word imposition itself, which the house voice of this article uses as the name of a documented political charge rather than as its own verdict on Hindi. Imposition, in the Madras vocabulary of the 1950s, named a sequence without consent: a deadline fixed in 1949 by an Assembly in which the south held a minority, machinery under Article 344 staffed to advance one language, and a switchover whose costs fell on citizens who had never authorised it. The defenders of the switchover, in Parliament and in the Hindi associations, named the same sequence national integration. Both characterisations are attributed here to their holders, as the stance charter of this series requires; the settled facts beneath them, the dates, the votes, the texts and the assurances, are stated plainly, and the settlement that emerged by 1963 bore the marks of both vocabularies: integration retained the name of Hindi in Article 343, and consent retained the use of English without a date for its ending.

## The Eighth Schedule and the Politics of the List

One clause of the founding settlement worked quietly while the Article 343 clock ticked loudly. The Eighth Schedule at commencement listed fourteen languages: Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Malayalam, Marathi, Oriya, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu. Inclusion carried no official status for the Union, yet the list acquired a political life of its own as the republic's roll of recognised tongues, the schedule to which Article 351 pointed when it directed Hindi to draw enrichment from the languages of India. The Constituent Assembly's handling of the list, traced in the debates gathered under [the Eighth Schedule's language settlement](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/), left every major language community with a documented place in the constitutional text, and that documentary place blunted, without removing, the grievance of the single-language clause. Tamil's presence in the Schedule was never in dispute; the dispute was over the hierarchy the rest of Part Seventeen built above the list, in which one scheduled language governed the Union and the others governed their states. Madras's constitutional demand of the 1950s can be stated as a demand about that hierarchy: keep the Schedule's equality of honour, the resistance argued, and drop the deadline that converted one member of the list into the federation's master. The demand was moderate in form and radical in implication, since a Union with no scheduled deadline for English had, in effect, two official languages for as long as any major state required two, which is the arrangement the settlement of the 1960s finally wrote into law.

## The Official Languages Act 1963: A Settlement in a Permissive Mood

Parliament enacted the Official Languages Act in the spring of 1963, and the President's assent followed on 10 May 1963; the statute, Act 19 of 1963, is the second mandatory primary source of this article, and its third section is the most argued-over sentence in the language settlement. Section 3 provided that, notwithstanding the expiration of the fifteen years from the Constitution's commencement, the English language may continue to be used, in addition to Hindi, for all the official purposes of the Union for which it was being used immediately before the appointed day, and for the transaction of business in Parliament. The Act also protected the use of English for communication between the Union and a state that had not adopted Hindi as its official language, and it carried the bilingual form of Union legislation and resolutions that the settlement required in practice. Read as a whole, the Act did what Nehru's assurance had promised in substance: English did not end on 26 January 1965. The deadline in Article 343 expired, and the files of the Union continued in two languages by authority of a statute any future Parliament could amend but no official could ignore.

### What did section 3 of the Official Languages Act 1963 actually say?

Section 3 said that English may continue to be used, in addition to Hindi, for the official purposes of the Union after the fifteen-year period expired on 26 January 1965, and for the transaction of business in Parliament. The verb was permissive, may rather than shall, and that single drafting choice carried the argument of the next two years.

The permissive verb was the flaw Madras had predicted in the assurance itself. During the bill's passage, DMK members and their allies demanded that the continued use of English be commanded by the statute, shall rather than may, and that the assurance of 1959 be entrenched beyond the reach of a simple majority; Annadurai, sitting in the Rajya Sabha from 1962, pressed the case that a permission revocable at pleasure was a deadline postponed, not a deadline abolished. Union ministers replied that may, in the drafting convention of Indian statutes, conferred an authority meant to be exercised, and that the government's intention stood recorded in debate. The exchange is preserved in the parliamentary debates of April and May 1963, and it frames the crisis of 1965 exactly: when Nehru died on 27 May 1964, the settlement lost its guarantor, and the permissive sentence of 1963 had to bear, under a new Prime Minister, the weight the personal assurance had carried. The 1965 agitation is unintelligible without that drafting history, and the drafting history is unintelligible without the 1950s resistance that had forced the bill into existence. Sequence is the argument.

## January and February 1965: The Deadline Arrives in Madras

The deadline arrived as a date on a calendar that Madras had been watching for fifteen years. As 26 January 1965 approached, the DMK observed 25 January as a day of mourning, students in Madras State's colleges took the agitation out of the party's hands, and a clash in Madurai on 25 January between agitating students and Congress workers lit a fire that spread across the state for two months. The record of those weeks includes arson against railway property and Hindi signage, police firing, the burning of two police sub-inspectors by a mob, and self-immolations by young men who gave their deaths to the language cause, among them the DMK worker Chinnaswamy at Tiruchirappalli in 1964, whose act had already entered the movement's martyrology before the deadline year opened. Official estimates put the deaths at about seventy, including two policemen. The Congress government of Madras State under M. Bhaktavatsalam requested central forces; two Union ministers from Madras, C. Subramaniam and O. V. Alagesan, tendered resignations in the crisis; and Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri, in February 1965, gave the assurance that ended the emergency: Nehru's commitment would be honoured, English would continue for as long as the non-Hindi states wanted it, for Union purposes, for communication with the states, and for the Union's examinations. The agitation subsided on the assurance, as the agitation of the assurance's own decade would have predicted.

### Why is the 1965 agitation called a confirmation rather than a new movement?

The agitation of 1965 demanded nothing new. Its slogans, its organisations and its single demand, that English continue without a terminal date, were inherited whole from the 1950s contest, and its success was measured by the restoration of the 1959 assurance under a new Prime Minister rather than by any fresh concession extracted from the Union.

The confirmation reading earns its keep in the details. The students of 1965 did not draft a programme; they enforced a promise. The DMK's leadership spent the crisis negotiating the promise's renewal, and Annadurai's public interventions aimed at the restoration of the status quo of 1959 to 1963, the assurance plus the statute, rather than at any escalation toward the Dravida Nadu demand the party had shelved by 1963. Even the violence followed the older script of 1937 to 1940 and 1953: martyrdom, mourning days, black flags, the targeting of the symbols of the Union's language rather than of the Union itself. A fresh contest would have needed a new object. The object of 1965 was dated 7 August 1959, and the agitators said so, in so many words, on their placards: the demand was that Delhi keep its word.

## The Verdict of 1967: Madras Votes the Settlement In

The Madras Legislative Assembly election of February 1967 converted the confirmation into a government. The DMK, leading an alliance and fighting on the language settlement among its central themes, won 137 seats in a house of 234 and formed the state government; Annadurai took office as Chief Minister on 6 March 1967. K. Kamaraj, the Congress's national president and the former Chief Minister who had defended Madras's linguistic position from inside the Congress for a decade, lost his own Virudhunagar constituency to the DMK's P. Seenivasan, a student leader of the 1965 agitation, a result that reads like the decade's thesis stated as a scoreline: the generation of the assurance was beaten by the generation of the deadline, in the assurance's own home district politics. The Congress has not held office in the state in its own right in the elections that followed, a consequence Indian political commentary routinely traces to 1965 and 1967, and the attribution matters less than the mechanism: the 1967 verdict made the language settlement electorally irreversible in the state that had demanded it, since any Union government contemplating a Hindi-only future thereafter faced a Madras ruled, permanently as it proved, by the party the deadline had created.

## The Amendment of 1967: Bilingualism Without a Date

Parliament completed the settlement's legal form under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi. The Official Languages (Amendment) Act, enacted by Parliament in December 1967 and brought into force on 8 January 1968 as Act 1 of 1968, rewrote the permissive arrangement of 1963 into a durable bilingualism: English would continue in addition to Hindi for the official purposes of the Union until the legislatures of the states that had not adopted Hindi resolved against its continuance and both Houses of Parliament resolved in the same sense. The double-lock of that formula, state resolutions plus a parliamentary resolution, transferred the veto over English's ending to the non-Hindi states themselves, which is the institutional shape the resistance of the 1950s had demanded in the vocabulary available to it: no imposition, rendered as no termination without consent. With the amendment in force, the trajectory that began in Article 343's fifteen-year permission closed into a settlement with no expiry date, and the settlement's open-endedness, so often described as indecision, is better described as its content. The Union had agreed, in law, to stop scheduling the question.

## The Three-Language Formula: The Schoolroom Annex of the Settlement

One annex of the settlement governed schools rather than files. The Education Commission of 1964 to 1966, chaired by D. S. Kothari, recommended a three-language formula for secondary education, and the National Policy on Education of 1968 adopted it: in Hindi-speaking states, Hindi, English and a modern Indian language, preferably a southern language; in non-Hindi states, the regional language, English and Hindi. The formula attempted in the classroom what the Official Languages Acts attempted in the secretariat, a reciprocal burden in which the north would learn a southern tongue as the south learned Hindi, and its asymmetry in practice became a standard count in the southern indictment: Hindi-speaking states widely taught Sanskrit as the third language, discharging the formula's letter, while Tamil Nadu, under Annadurai's government, adopted a two-language policy of Tamil and English in state schools in 1968 and declined the formula altogether. The schoolroom annex confirms the settlement's logic at a second site. Where reciprocity was real, in the Union's bilingual files, the arrangement held; where reciprocity was nominal, in the third-language classroom, the state that had led the resistance simply exited, and the Union, bound by its own consent doctrine after 1967, let the exit stand.
## The Hindi Case at Its Strongest: Integration, Access and the Anti-Colonial Claim

The stance charter of this series requires the losing side of an adjudication to be rendered at full strength, and the Hindi case of the 1950s was neither foolish nor merely majoritarian. Its advocates, who included the Hindi Sahitya Sammelan's organisers, the Congress president Purushottam Das Tandon in 1950 and 1951, members of Parliament such as Seth Govind Das, and educationists who staffed the Hindi directorates of the Union government, argued from three premises that each carried evidence. The first was integration: a Union whose secretariat, army commands and inter-state correspondence ran in a language inherited from the departing empire would remain, in administration, a tenant in a colonial building, and a link language owned by Indians was a condition of completed independence. The second was access: English in 1951 was the possession of a small educated fraction of every province, so that government in English was government by a bilingual elite over monolingual masses in Patna as much as in Madurai, and Hindi, understood in some form across the northern half of the Union and taught in schools beyond it, promised a wider doorway into the state's business than English offered. The third was federal arithmetic: no single regional language could serve as the Union's medium without privileging its home state, and Hindi's plurality in the census made it the least arbitrary Indian candidate. These arguments moved ministries and won votes in the Constituent Assembly in September 1949 and in the parliamentary divisions of the 1950s, and the settlement of 1963 and 1967 should be read as their partial victory as well as their partial defeat: Hindi kept the constitutional primacy of Article 343, the development mandate of Article 351, the school's third-language place in most of the Union, and the future. What the Hindi case lost was the timetable, and it lost the timetable because its opponents converted a distributive objection, the examination ledger, into a constitutional doctrine of consent that the Union's own Prime Minister ratified in 1959.

## Counting Speakers: The Census Beneath the Argument

Both sides of the 1950s contest cited the census, and the census deserved the suspicion each side directed at the other's use of it. The Census of 1951 and the Census of 1961 enumerated mother tongues by the hundred and then grouped them, and the grouping of the north Indian returns under the heading of Hindi, gathering in returns recorded as Hindustani, Urdu, Punjabi in some districts and a family of regional speeches from Rajasthan to Bihar, produced the plurality figures the integration argument quoted. Linguists objected that the grouped heading concealed exactly the diversity the Eighth Schedule recognised, and Suniti Kumar Chatterji, the century's most eminent Indian linguist and a member of the Kher Commission, spent a career insisting that the languages of India be counted as they were spoken and written, with literatures and grammars of their own, rather than as dialects awaiting absorption. The counting dispute matters to the adjudication because it fixes the demographic premise: Hindi was the Union's largest single language by any honest count, and it was the mother tongue of a minority of the Union's citizens by every count. A plurality language carrying a minority's share could plausibly be named first among the Union's languages; the same figures could not carry a deadline by which the majority of citizens would transact with their own government in a tongue not their own. The census, read whole, is the settlement's silent co-author: primacy for Hindi in the text, permanence for English in the practice, and a federation whose arithmetic permitted exactly that combination and no stronger one.

## Language and the Linguistic States: Two Settlements, One Decade

The language settlement cannot be prised apart from the decade's other great linguistic act, the redrawing of the states. The States Reorganisation Commission, appointed in December 1953 under Fazl Ali, reported in 1955, and the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 remade the internal map on lines that broadly followed language, a sequence examined in the constitutional register in [the linguistic reorganisation as constitutional politics](/2019/01/19/linguistic-reorganisation-constitutional-politics/) and in the statutory register in the companion treatment of the 1956 Act. The two settlements pulled in opposite directions, and the Nehru government held both ends. Reorganisation conceded that language was a legitimate principle of political belonging, strong enough to dissolve Hyderabad, to divide the old Bombay and Madras presidencies' successor units and to create Kerala and an enlarged Mysore; a government that had conceded language as the ground of the states could hardly deny language as a ground of objection to the Union's own tongue. Madras's advocates noticed the tension and used it, in the assembly and in Parliament: the Union that trusted Tamil speakers to govern a Tamil state in Tamil could not coherently distrust them to correspond with Delhi in English. The States Reorganisation Commission itself had declined, in 1955, to recommend a Punjabi-speaking state and had treated language as one consideration among several, which showed the centre's preferred grammar for every linguistic claim of the decade, concession tempered by counterweight. In the official-language file the same grammar produced the assurance and the amendment: the substance conceded, the schedule refused, the counterweight named consent.

## The Scholarship Adjudicated: King, Ramaswamy, Pandian and the Two Readings

The named disagreement this article must adjudicate is between two readings of the whole episode. The regional-resistance reading, which dominates the mass-content summaries and survives in textbook shorthand, treats Hindi imposition as a Tamil Nadu story: a Dravidian movement with a devotional language culture resisted a northern imposition, won a famous victory in 1965, and returned to its state politics, leaving the Union's language regime as a by-product of one region's temperament. The national-constitutional reading, defended here, treats the 1950s contest as a negotiation over the Constitution's language settlement in which Madras supplied the best-organised delegation: its outcomes, the assurance of 1959, the Act of 1963 and the amendment of 1967, are Union law and Union doctrine, made in Parliament, binding on every state, and anticipated in the machinery of Articles 343 and 344 themselves. Robert King's Nehru and the Language Politics of India (1997) supplies the adjudication's documentary spine, because King reconstructed the Nehru government's internal handling of the question from the Prime Minister's papers and showed a centre that treated language as a first-order problem of statecraft across the whole period from 1947 to 1964, revising its position in response to pressure from several quarters and codifying each revision. A by-product of one region's temperament does not generate seventeen years of prime ministerial statecraft.

Ramaswamy and Pandian discipline the reading from the other side. Sumathi Ramaswamy's Passions of the Tongue (1997) demonstrates that the Tamil devotion the regional reading treats as temperament was itself a constructed political tradition, built between 1891 and 1970 by named intellectuals, associations and martyrs' cults; a construction of that scale, aimed at the distribution of honour and office in a federation, is constitutional politics conducted in a cultural register, and her evidence enlarges rather than localises the contest. M. S. S. Pandian's studies of the DMK's formation after 1949 and of Dravidian ideology's transformation trace the mechanism by which a cultural movement became an electoral party and then a government, the very conversion that carried the language question from the conference hall of 1953 to the statute book of 1963 and the assembly verdict of 1967. Read together, the three scholars leave the regional reading with its facts and without its frame: every event it cites is real, and every event it cites was produced by, addressed to, or ratified in the institutions of the Union. The verdict of this article is therefore for the national-constitutional reading, on the primary evidence the brief assembles: a constitutional article with a deadline, a commission under that article, a parliamentary committee, a prime ministerial assurance in the Lok Sabha, two statutes and a state election that functioned as the settlement's ratification.

## The Strongest Counter: Geographic Concentration, Answered

The counter to that verdict deserves its full force before it is answered. The resistance's crowds, martyrs, conferences and votes were concentrated, overwhelmingly, in one state. Bengal grumbled and legislated for itself; Assam fought its own language war inward; no second state produced a DMK, a 1953 conference series, or a 1965. If scope is measured by mobilisation, the contest was Madras's, and the national frame can look like a courtesy extended to a regional fact. The answer turns on distinguishing the form of a contest from its object and its outcome. The form was southern because the deadline's costs were southern in their sharpest incidence: the states whose citizens faced the examination ledger in a foreign tongue were the non-Hindi states, and among them Madras alone combined a pre-existing language movement, a disciplined party and a state government willing to argue the case in office. The object, however, was a clause of the Constitution of India and the statutes of its Parliament, and the outcome is recorded in Union law that governs Hindi-speaking states as fully as it governs Tamil Nadu, since the bilingual Union secretariat that the settlement produced is the daily administrative reality of Patna and Lucknow no less than of Chennai. Concentration of mobilisation is how most national settlements in federations begin; the American civil rights settlement was mobilised in one region too, and no serious account calls it a regional event. The test is where the decision was taken and what it binds, and on that test the 1950s language contest was decided in New Delhi and binds the republic.

## The Long Arc: Postscripts of 1968 and 1986 and the Discipline of the Settlement

The settlement was tested twice more in the decades the brief's evidence scope reaches, and both tests returned the same result. In 1968, fresh agitation in Madras State, by then on the eve of its renaming as Tamil Nadu in 1969, greeted proposals read as diluting the bilingual arrangement, and the Union government of Indira Gandhi, fresh from enacting the 1967 amendment, held the line the amendment had drawn. In 1986, the National Policy on Education of that year and the proposal to teach Hindi in the Navodaya Vidyalayas provoked a renewed anti-imposition mobilisation in Tamil Nadu, met by the state government's refusal and by the centre's retreat from compulsion. The pattern across 1965, 1968 and 1986 is the strongest evidence for the confirmation thesis this article defends: each later episode reproduced the structure of the first, a Union initiative touching the language balance, a Tamil mobilisation invoking the assurance tradition, and a Union confirmation of the bilingual settlement. None of the later episodes renegotiated the settlement's terms, because the terms had been fixed where settlements live, in the expectations of both sides about what any government in New Delhi may and may not attempt. The 1950s contest is the cause of that fixity. A contest whose outcomes every later government obeys, including governments led by the grandchildren of its antagonists, is not an episode in a state's biography; it is a constitutional settlement in the only sense that finally matters, a durable distribution of what power may do.

## Article 351 and the Hindi That Was Asked to Grow

One clause of Part Seventeen pointed the other way, and its subsequent quietness completes the picture. Article 351 made it the duty of the Union to promote the spread of Hindi and to develop it, drawing primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on the other scheduled languages, so that Hindi might express the composite culture of India. The Union built the clause a bureaucracy: the Central Hindi Directorate of 1960, the Commission for Scientific and Technical Terminology of 1961, Hindi teaching schemes for government servants and the Kendriya Hindi Sansthan of 1960 at Agra. The development project proceeded for decades as an administrative routine, coining terminology, training clerks, funding translation, and it changed the texture of Union paperwork without changing the settlement, because Article 351 governed the growth of a language and said nothing about the displacement of another. Madras's resistance had never objected to Hindi's growth; Annadurai's stated position across the 1950s accepted Hindi's spread by attraction and objected to its advance by deadline and compulsion. The distinction between promotion and imposition, between Article 351's duty and Article 343's clock, is the distinction the whole contest finally wrote into the republic's practice: Hindi would be developed, taught, enriched and honoured, and English would continue, because the first programme required only money and the second had been promised, in Parliament, on 7 August 1959.

## The Settlement That Stopped Scheduling

The language settlement the 1950s produced is easy to miss because its monument is an absence: the absence of a date. Article 343 had given the republic a schedule, fifteen years from 26 January 1950 to 26 January 1965, and every actor in this history organised around that schedule, the commissions to manage it, the assurance to soften it, the statute to suspend it, the agitation to enforce the suspension and the amendment to lock it. What the resistance of the 1950s won, stated in one sentence, was the removal of language from the republic's timetable. After 8 January 1968, no citizen of Madras State, and no citizen of any state that had not adopted Hindi, could be told the year in which the Union would cease to address him in a language he had been educated in, unless his own state legislature first resolved that the addressing should end. That is a constitutional fact of the first magnitude produced by a decade of conferences, dissents, assurances and votes, and it is why the namable claim of this article stands: the 1950s Hindi-imposition contest shaped the constitutional language settlement, and the agitation that followed in 1965 was the settlement's confirmation, not a fresh contest. The founders had deferred the question to a Parliament they would not control. The Parliament they got answered the question the founders had actually asked, which was whether the Union could hold together while its language was decided, and the answer, delivered between 1955 and 1968 by a commission, a committee, a Prime Minister, two statutes and an electorate in Madras, was that the Union held by declining, in the end, to decide on schedule.
## September 1949 Revisited: What the Assembly Actually Decided About Language

Return to the founding chamber for a closer reading, because the 1950s argument was at bottom an argument about what September 1949 had meant. The Constituent Assembly took up the language clauses on 12 to 14 September 1949, near the end of its labours, and the debate recorded in the Constituent Assembly Debates ran through the accumulated grievances of every linguistic province. Members argued over the name of the language itself: Hindi against Hindustani, the composite spoken tongue that Gandhi had championed and that Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, the Union Education Minister, regarded as the natural bridge of the bazaars and the army. Members argued over script, Devanagari against the Roman and Perso-Arabic claims, and over numerals, where the Assembly's insistence on the international form of Indian numerals in place of the Devanagari figures was carried against Seth Govind Das's amendment after a division remembered in the literature for its narrowness. The Munshi-Ayyangar formula that emerged bore the marks of every one of these fights: Hindi in Devanagari script named as the official language, Hindustani's ghost acknowledged in Article 351's direction that Hindi draw on Hindustani and the other scheduled languages, English retained for fifteen years, and the whole question of the endgame postponed to the machinery of Article 344. T. T. Krishnamachari, speaking for the Madras interest in the Assembly, warned the House in the language debates that the south would accept Hindi as a link language by consent and resist it as a command, a sentence that could stand as the programme of the following decade. The 1950s resistance, on the evidence of the debates themselves, invented nothing. It executed a warning the Assembly had heard and minuted before the Constitution commenced.

## The Print Front: Journals, Pamphlets and the Oratory Circuit

A contest over language is fought in language, and the Madras resistance built a print and oratory apparatus whose scale the national frame must register. Annadurai had edited the journal Dravida Nadu from 1942 and wrote essays, plays and film dialogues that carried the anti-imposition argument into the idiom of Tamil popular culture; M. Karunanidhi founded the journal Murasoli in 1942 as a student and raised it into the DMK's organ; the party's newspapers, pamphlet series and conference pandals formed a distribution system for constitutional argument that reached tea shops and reading rooms across the Tamil districts. Sumathi Ramaswamy's reconstruction of Tamil language devotion supplies the content analysis of this material: the Tamilttay, the mother-goddess figure of Tamil, mourned and defended in verse, the martyrs of 1939 commemorated in annual observance, the classical antiquity of Tamil set against Hindi's claimed youth as a literary standard. The apparatus mattered institutionally because it solved the resistance's coordination problem. When the Kher Commission toured, when the Pant Committee sat, when the Official Languages Bill was debated in 1963, Madras opinion did not have to be manufactured for the occasion; it arrived pre-organised, with its arguments already footnoted in a decade of print. Union ministers understood the apparatus because they negotiated with its outputs, and Robert King's narrative of the Nehru government's handling of the file returns repeatedly to the centre's reading of the Madras press as a barometer it trusted more than its own party's state unit.

## The University Front: Medium of Instruction and the Guardianship of English

The resistance's institutional depth showed in the universities. The University of Madras, founded in 1857, and Annamalai University, founded in 1929, educated the Tamil administrative and professional class in English, and their leadership treated the medium of instruction as a trust. A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar, Vice-Chancellor of the University of Madras from 1942 to 1969, defended English-medium education in the councils of the republic with the argument that a sudden change of medium would strand a generation between languages, educated fully in neither; the Inter-University Board and the education commissions of the 1950s heard versions of the same defence from across the non-Hindi states. The point for the adjudication is the resistance's sociology. The anti-imposition cause was carried simultaneously by a devotional mass movement, a disciplined electoral party, a state government and the vice-chancellors of the state's universities, a coalition spanning the temple of Tamil devotion and the senate house of the university. Coalitions of that breadth are not assembled by temperament. They are assembled by a distributive threat that reaches the graduate, the clerk, the scholar and the priest at the same time, and the only threat in the 1950s that reached all four was the dated clause in Part Seventeen of the Constitution.

## The Everyday State: Signboards, Forms and the Resentment of Small Things

Grand constitutional argument is sustained by small daily evidence, and the 1950s supplied it in the material culture of the Union state. The railways that crossed Madras State carried destination boards and reservation forms whose Hindi lettering expanded through the decade under the Union's progressive-use directions; the post offices of the Tamil districts sold forms and money-order books printed under the same policy; the All India Radio's Madras station broadcast under a network discipline set in Delhi. Each artefact was trivial, and the accumulation was the argument's daily proof, photographed and pamphleted by the resistance as evidence that imposition was not a metaphor but a schedule of administrative acts proceeding without any further vote of anyone in Madras. The Union government's own machinery recorded the friction: the Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities, an office created by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act of 1956 under the new Article 350B and filled from 1957, received and reported grievances from linguistic minorities across the Union, and its annual reports to the President, laid before Parliament, constitute a running official archive of the language question's everyday front. A history of the contest that confined itself to statutes and assurances would miss the stratum in which the settlement was actually tested, the stratum of the form, the board and the broadcast, where the citizen met the Union's language before he met its arguments.

## The South Beyond Madras: Andhra, Kerala, Mysore and the Common Memorandum Politics

Madras organised best, and the difference in organisation should not be mistaken for solitude. The Telugu-speaking state created in 1953 and enlarged as Andhra Pradesh in 1956 carried its own language pride, the Andhra Mahajana Sangam's inheritance and a literary establishment that guarded Telugu's scheduled place, and its governments' representations to the Union's language machinery supported the continuance of English while its politics ran on other fuels. Kerala, formed in 1956 and governed from 1957 by the Communist ministry of E. M. S. Namboodiripad, defended Malayalam's domain in the state and English's place in the Union's examinations from a Marxist analysis that treated language equality as a democratic right, arriving at the Madras conclusion by a different road. Mysore State, enlarged in 1956 under the States Reorganisation Act, administered a Kannada-majority state whose capital, Bangalore, housed Union institutions and a cosmopolitan salariat with its own stake in English. The four southern states never formed a common front with a common secretariat, and the adjudication does not invent one; the point is narrower and stronger. When the Pant Committee and the ministry drafting the 1963 bill sounded the non-Hindi states, the answers from Madras, Hyderabad, Trivandrum and Bangalore converged on the same sentence, that English must continue until the non-Hindi states themselves agreed to end it. Nehru's assurance of 7 August 1959 was addressed to that convergence, and its phrasing, which lodged the decision with the non-Hindi areas collectively, recognised in form what the memorandum politics of the decade had established in fact.

## Nehru's Correspondence: The Prime Minister as the Settlement's Clerk

The documentary trail of the settlement runs through one man's desk to a degree unusual even in the centralised Nehru years. Nehru's fortnightly letters to chief ministers, a series he maintained from 1947, returned to the language question across the 1950s with a consistency the letters' published volumes make checkable: the Prime Minister urged gradualness on the Hindi enthusiasts of his own party, warned chief ministers against provocation on either side, and treated every flare of the Madras question as a matter for his personal intervention. Robert King's 1997 study is built substantially on this correspondence and on the Prime Minister's minutes on the language files, and its evidentiary weight is the reason the national-constitutional reading can be defended rather than merely asserted: the head of the Union government conducted the language question as a continuous negotiation with named counterparts, Annadurai's party, Kamaraj's state government, the Hindi associations and his own Home Ministers from Sardar Patel's tenure at the decade's opening through Govind Ballabh Pant's, who chaired the 1957 committee. The assurance of 1959, the Presidential Order of 1960 and the bill of 1963 each bear the marks of that clerkship, drafted and redrafted against the anticipated Madras reading. Settlements acquire their meaning from the intentions recorded at their making, and the intentions recorded here are national in the plainest sense: the preservation of a Union whose southern third would remain inside its administrative language.

## The Deadline Decade in Parliament: An Annual Argument

Between the assurance and the deadline, Parliament kept the question on its annual round, and the debates of those years are the residue from which the settlement's final shape can be reconstructed. The President's directions under Article 344, the reports of the Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities under Article 350B, the Hindi progress reports of the Union ministries and the demands for grants of the Education and Home ministries each gave the Madras members, and after 1962 Annadurai himself in the Rajya Sabha, a scheduled occasion to ask the same question in the same words: does the assurance of 1959 hold, and will it be law before 1965 arrives. The answers given by Lal Bahadur Shastri as Home Minister and then as Prime Minister, and by the ministers who succeeded to the file, form a chain of reaffirmations that the 1965 agitation would later cite as a broken chain only in its final link, the permissive may of the 1963 Act's drafting against the mandatory sense of the debates. Parliamentary government settles questions by exhaustion as often as by division, and the language question was settled by both: exhausted by a decade and a half of annual argument in which the southern case was never answered on its merits by any minister, and divided, in the end, in favour of the only text both sides could carry home, the amendment of 1967 with its double lock.
## The Election of 1957: The Resistance Takes Its Seats

The general elections of 1957 gave the resistance its parliamentary form, and the returns repay attention because they fix the moment the language argument acquired legislators. The DMK, contesting a general election for the first time since its founding on 17 September 1949, won fifteen seats in the Madras Legislative Assembly and returned members to the Lok Sabha, and Annadurai entered the assembly from Kancheepuram as the recognised leader of the anti-imposition benches. Fifteen seats in a house dominated by the Congress was a modest arithmetic with an immodest implication: the party of the deadline could no longer be described, in Delhi or in Madras, as a street movement with a printing press. Its members could table questions on the Official Language Commission's follow-up, move cut motions on the Home Ministry's demands, and place the Madras case on the parliamentary record in the years when the Pant Committee reported and the assurance was given. The sequence from 1957 to 1959, assembly seats to committee report to prime ministerial assurance, is the resistance's institutional biography in three dates, and it is the single most compact disproof of the temperament theory of the movement. Temperaments do not win seats, staff committees with their evidence, or extract dated assurances from prime ministers; organisations do, and the DMK of Annadurai was an organisation built, from its founding programme onward, for the single constitutional errand of stopping the clock in Article 343.

## The Debates of 1963: Shall, May and the Anglo-Indian Voice

The parliamentary debates on the Official Languages Bill in April and May 1963 deserve a section of their own, because the settlement's later crisis was drafted in them in full view. The government's spokesmen, presenting the bill as the assurance's statutory form, resisted amendment after amendment that would have written English's continuance in mandatory words or fixed it to the consent of the non-Hindi state legislatures, the very formula Parliament would adopt four years later in the amendment of 1967. Frank Anthony, the nominated member who represented the Anglo-Indian community in the Lok Sabha across the period and who had defended English in the Constituent Assembly in 1949, argued that English was not a foreign imposition but the working capital of India's courts, universities and all-India services, and that a permission dependent on ministerial goodwill would fail the first test a hostile majority chose to set it. DMK members and their allies pressed the consent formula from the other side of the House. The government's majority held, the permissive text passed, and the House dispersed having enacted a statute whose central ambiguity every party in the chamber had identified on the record. The debates are therefore the adjudication's best single exhibit for the confirmation reading of 1965: when the crisis came, no participant could claim surprise, because the crisis had been described, dated and voted on in advance, and the agitation's demand in 1965 was simply that the assurance of 1959 be preferred to the drafting of 1963, as the agitators' own literature said in terms.

## The Cabinet Crisis of February 1965: Resignations and a Broadcast

The crisis of 1965 reached into the Union cabinet itself, a fact the regional reading rarely accommodates. As Madras burned through January and February 1965, the two Madras members of the Union Council of Ministers, C. Subramaniam, the Minister for Food and Agriculture, and O. V. Alagesan, a Minister of State, tendered their resignations to Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri, placing on the cabinet table the impossibility of their position between a state in flame over the language question and a government whose statute's wording had lit it. Shastri's handling of the crisis closed the confirmation sequence. In a broadcast to the nation on 11 February 1965 and in statements to Parliament in the same days, he reaffirmed that Nehru's assurance would be implemented without qualification, that English would continue as the associate official language for as long as the non-Hindi states wished, that communications from the centre to the states would be in English, and that candidates from non-Hindi states would not be disadvantaged in the Union's examinations. The resignations were not pressed; the assurance was renewed by the only authority that could renew it, the Prime Minister of the day, speaking to the whole Union rather than to the agitating state. A regional disturbance does not ordinarily extract a prime ministerial broadcast redefining the Union's language regime and the tendered resignations of two Union ministers. A constitutional settlement under stress does exactly that, and the February days of 1965 show the settlement's custodians, in Delhi and Madras alike, behaving as custodians of a settlement rather than as parties to a riot.
## What the Settlement Left Open

A settlement is known by its residue, and the language settlement's residue was deliberate. The 1967 amendment ended the scheduling of English's departure without ending the aspiration recorded in Article 343, which still names Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union, and without touching Article 351's development mandate, under which the Union's Hindi directorates, terminology commissions and teaching schemes continued their work into every later decade. The three-language formula survived as national policy with Tamil Nadu outside it under the two-language policy Annadurai's government adopted in 1968, an asymmetry the Union tolerated under the consent doctrine the settlement had made orthodox. The Eighth Schedule grew by amendment from its original fourteen languages, as Sindhi was added in 1967 and later additions followed, and the politics of the list continued as the politics of recognition rather than of command. Each residue item marks the boundary the 1950s contest drew: recognition could expand, promotion could proceed, honour could be distributed, and none of it could be converted into a deadline against the consenting states. The residue is therefore not unfinished business in the ordinary sense. It is the settlement's own inventory of what had been taken off the table, kept visible so that any future government reaching for the timetable would first have to explain, to Madras and to the record of 1959, why the table had been cleared at such cost in the first place.

## The Counter-Mobilisation in the North: Sabhas, Sammelans and the Hindi Public

The resistance had an organised opposite, and its organisation predated the republic. The Nagari Pracharini Sabha, founded at Benares in 1893, had campaigned for the Devanagari script and the Hindi language in the courts and offices of the United Provinces for half a century before Article 343 was drafted, and the Hindi Sahitya Sammelan, founded in 1910, supplied the movement with its annual parliament of writers, teachers and politicians. Through the 1950s these bodies and their state-level allies pressed the Union government from the other side of the assurance: they counted the years remaining to 1965 as a promise made to them, lobbied the Kher Commission for a firm timetable, and read the Pant Committee's softening and Nehru's 1959 statement as breaches of the founding bargain. Members of Parliament from the Hindi-speaking states carried the sabhas' case into the same debates in which Madras carried its own, and the parliamentary record of the decade is unintelligible unless it is read as the minutes of two organised publics arguing past a government seated between them. The existence of the counter-mobilisation matters to the adjudication in two ways. It confirms the national character of the contest, since a question with organised publics in Benares and Madras, each lobbying the same commissions in the same years, is a national question by definition. And it explains the settlement's shape, because a government facing a dated promise in two directions, toward Hindi's advocates who had been promised 1965 and toward the non-Hindi states who had been promised consent, could satisfy the letter of both only by the device the settlement finally adopted: Hindi's primacy affirmed in the text, the date dissolved in the practice.

## Why English, of All Languages: The Neutrality of a Foreign Tongue

The settlement's most paradoxical feature is the identity of the language it preserved. English survived 1965 not because any Indian constituency loved it as a mother tongue, for almost none did, but because its foreignness had become, by the arithmetic of the federation, a form of neutrality. A link language drawn from any Indian region taxed every other region; a link language drawn from the departed empire taxed all regions in the coin of a shared colonial inheritance, and the distribution of disadvantage was, if not equal, at least symmetrical in kind. Madras's advocates made the point without embarrassment: Annadurai's speeches treated English as a window on the wider world and as the one all-India medium in which a Tamil candidate met a Bengali or a Hindi-speaking candidate on ground neither owned. The universities' defence of the English medium, the courts' insulation under Article 348 and the services' reliance on English examinations all rested on the same foundation, that English was the language in which the Union's business could be transacted without any citizen's birthplace appearing on the file. The Hindi case answered, correctly, that a neutrality purchased by permanent dependence on a foreign language carried its own humiliation and its own elite filter, since English fluency tracked wealth in every state. The settlement did not refute that answer; it postponed the reckoning the answer demanded, and the postponement, renewed by the amendment of 1967 without a terminal date, hardened into the arrangement the republic still inhabits on the evidence of its own files: English retained not as a compromise awaiting its expiry but as the load-bearing second language of the Union, held up by the consent doctrine the resistance of the 1950s had written into law and into expectation.

## Annadurai in the Legislatures: The Argument Conducted on the Record

The resistance's leader spent the decisive years inside the institutions whose records this article cites, and his parliamentary biography is a thread on which the whole sequence can be hung. Annadurai led the DMK's fifteen members in the Madras Legislative Assembly from 1957 to 1962, moved to the Rajya Sabha in 1962, and took office as Chief Minister of Madras State on 6 March 1967, holding the post until his death on 3 February 1969, twenty-three months that carried the 1967 amendment's commencement and the two-language policy. In each chamber his method was the same: the constitutional text read aloud, the assurance quoted against the ministry, the consent principle stated as the south's single non-negotiable term. His assembly speeches of the late 1950s addressed the Kher report clause by clause before many Union ministers had answered for it anywhere; his Rajya Sabha interventions of 1962 and 1963 placed the may and shall argument on the parliamentary record in the form the 1965 agitators would quote. Oratory is usually treated as the decoration of politics, and in Annadurai's case it was the mechanism: a movement whose grievance was linguistic conducted its business in speeches of celebrated craft, in Tamil for the conference pandal and in English for the chamber, demonstrating in performance the bilingual competence the settlement existed to protect. Pandian's studies of the DMK's ideological transformation register the same point from the scholarship's side: the party's genius was translation, of devotional Tamil into electoral organisation, of cultural grievance into constitutional amendment, and the leader's two tongues were the instrument of both translations. The settlement bears his fingerprints although his name appears in no statute, which is the normal fate of the men who write constitutions by opposing them.

## The Stage and the Screen: The Argument in Popular Form

No account of the resistance's reach in the 1950s is complete without the theatre and the cinema hall, the two rooms in which the Tamil public actually sat. Annadurai wrote plays and film scripts whose dialogues carried the movement's themes, the critique of priestcraft, the dignity of Tamil, the case against northern domination, into a popular form that reached audiences no pamphlet could gather, and M. Karunanidhi's dialogue for the film Parasakthi of 1952, the debut of the actor Sivaji Ganesan, became the decade's most quoted specimen of the new Tamil oratorical style, its courtroom speech recited by schoolboys who had never read a party programme. Ramaswamy's study of Tamil language devotion explains the fit between medium and message: a politics that figured Tamil as a mother wronged required forms in which the wrong could be dramatised, and the stage supplied them nightly in every district town of Madras State. The cultural apparatus is sometimes treated as the soft edge of the hard constitutional story, and the sequence of this article suggests the opposite accounting. The assurance of 1959 was extracted from a Prime Minister who could read the Madras returns, the Madras press and the Madras box office as one continuous signal, and the signal's volume, which the cultural apparatus set, was a term in the negotiation no less real for being unquantifiable in a commission's annexures.

## The Witnesses: How the Episode Is Known

The episode's evidence survives in four deposits, and naming them closes the argument's evidentiary circle. The Constituent Assembly Debates and the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha debates hold the words spoken in the chambers, from September 1949 to the divisions of 1963 and the reaffirmations of 1965. The Kher Commission's report, preserved at the National Archives of India, holds the testimony the states chose to give, including the southern chapters later summaries neglected. The statutes hold the outcomes: Act 19 of 1963 and Act 1 of 1968, short texts that repay the closest reading this article has given them. The scholarship holds the interpretations, Granville Austin in 1966, Robert King and Sumathi Ramaswamy in 1997 and M. S. S. Pandian across his studies of the DMK, each anchored to the deposits beneath it. A reader who checks the claim of this article checks it in those four places, and the claim was built to survive the checking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Hindi-imposition controversy?

The Hindi-imposition controversy is the dispute, running from the Constituent Assembly debates of September 1949 through the settlement of the 1960s, over whether Hindi should become the sole official language of the Union on a fixed date and over the objection of the non-Hindi states. Its constitutional text is Article 343, which named Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language while permitting English for fifteen years from 26 January 1950. Its political form was the resistance organised in Madras State by the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam under C. N. Annadurai, supported at points by the Madras state government itself, and its resolution was the sequence of the assurance of 7 August 1959, the Official Languages Act of 1963 and the amendment of 1967, which together kept English in official use without a terminal date.

### Q: What does Article 343 of the Indian Constitution say?

Article 343, in Part Seventeen of the Constitution, provides in its first clause that the official language of the Union shall be Hindi in Devanagari script, with the international form of Indian numerals used for official purposes. Its second clause provides that English shall continue to be used for the official purposes of the Union for fifteen years from the commencement of the Constitution, that is, until 26 January 1965. Its third clause empowers Parliament to provide by law for the continued use of English, or of the Devanagari form of numerals, after that period. The article therefore named Hindi, retained English on a dated permission, and left the question of what followed the permission to a future Parliament, which is why the politics of the 1950s gathered around its deadline.

### Q: Was Hindi made the only official language of India?

No. Hindi never became the only official language of the Union. Article 343 named Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language from 26 January 1950, but its second clause kept English in use for fifteen years, and before that period expired Parliament enacted the Official Languages Act of 1963, whose third section provided that English may continue to be used in addition to Hindi after 26 January 1965. The Official Languages (Amendment) Act of 1967 then placed the continuance of English on a durable footing, providing that it would continue until the legislatures of the states that had not adopted Hindi resolved otherwise and both Houses of Parliament concurred. The result is a bilingual Union in law: Hindi as the official language named by the Constitution, English continuing alongside it without a terminal date.

### Q: What was the Official Languages Act of 1963?

The Official Languages Act of 1963, Act 19 of 1963, which received the President's assent on 10 May 1963, is the statute by which Parliament answered the approaching expiry of the fifteen-year period in Article 343. Its central provision, section 3, stated that notwithstanding the expiration of that period the English language may continue to be used, in addition to Hindi, for all the official purposes of the Union for which it was being used before the appointed day, and for the transaction of business in Parliament. The Act also regulated the language of communication between the Union and states that had not adopted Hindi. Its weakness, identified in debate in 1963 and exposed in 1965, was the permissive word may, which Madras read as a revocable permission rather than the guaranteed continuance Nehru's assurance of 1959 had promised.

### Q: Why did Tamil Nadu protest against Hindi?

The protest in Madras State, the state renamed Tamil Nadu in 1969, rested on three grounds its leaders stated repeatedly. The first was distributive: Union examinations and Union employment conducted in Hindi would advantage candidates born into Hindi and penalise Tamil-speaking candidates competing for the same posts. The second was democratic: the change was to be made on a date fixed in 1949, without the consent of the states whose citizens bore its costs, and the DMK demanded that no imposition proceed against the will of the non-Hindi states. The third was cultural: the Dravidian movement, whose scholarship Sumathi Ramaswamy documented in Passions of the Tongue (1997), held Tamil, a classical language with its own literary tradition, to be the equal of any language of India and entitled to defence against subordination. The grounds reinforced one another, which is why the movement united students, graduates, scholars and parties.

### Q: What was the three-language formula?

The three-language formula was the school-language arrangement recommended by the Education Commission of 1964 to 1966 under D. S. Kothari and adopted in the National Policy on Education of 1968. In Hindi-speaking states, students were to learn Hindi, English and a modern Indian language, preferably one from the south; in non-Hindi-speaking states, students were to learn the regional language, English and Hindi. The formula tried to distribute the burden of multilingualism reciprocally across the Union. Its working was uneven: many Hindi-speaking states offered Sanskrit as the third language, which southern critics read as an evasion of reciprocity, and Tamil Nadu under C. N. Annadurai's government adopted a two-language policy of Tamil and English in 1968 and declined to apply the formula in state schools.

### Q: Did Nehru promise that Hindi would not be imposed?

Yes. In the Lok Sabha on 7 August 1959, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru stated that Hindi would not be imposed on the non-Hindi-speaking areas against their will, that English would continue as an associate language for as long as the people of those areas desired it, and that the decision on the continuance of English rested with the non-Hindi states rather than with the Hindi-speaking majority. He restated the assurance in the House in September 1959. The promise was a prime ministerial statement rather than a statute, and that legal fragility drove the politics that followed: Madras demanded the assurance in statutory or constitutional form, the Official Languages Act of 1963 was presented as its enactment, and the crisis of 1965 arose when the Act's permissive drafting appeared to fall short of the promise's terms.

### Q: How many languages did the Eighth Schedule list when the Constitution commenced?

The Eighth Schedule listed fourteen languages when the Constitution commenced on 26 January 1950: Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Malayalam, Marathi, Oriya, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu. The Schedule's function in the founding settlement was recognition: it named the major literary languages of the Union in the constitutional text, and Article 351 later pointed to the scheduled languages, together with Hindustani, as sources from which Hindi was to draw enrichment. Inclusion in the Schedule did not make a language an official language of the Union, a distinction the 1950s debates kept in view, since the Madras argument accepted the Schedule's equality of honour while rejecting the hierarchy that placed one scheduled language over the Union with a deadline against the rest. The Schedule has since grown by constitutional amendment, beginning with the addition of Sindhi in 1967.

### Q: What does Article 344 require the President to appoint, and at what intervals?

Article 344 requires the President to constitute a commission on official language at the expiration of five years from the commencement of the Constitution, and thereafter at the expiration of ten years, to recommend the progressive use of Hindi for the official purposes of the Union, restrictions on the use of English, and related questions including the language of the courts and of communication between the Union and the states. The article also requires a committee of Parliament, thirty members drawn from both Houses, to examine the commission's report and state its opinion to the President. The machinery was used once in full during the period of this article: President Rajendra Prasad appointed the first commission under B. G. Kher on 7 June 1955, and the parliamentary committee under Govind Ballabh Pant reported in February 1959, leading to the Presidential Order of 27 April 1960.

### Q: What choice does Article 345 give to a state legislature about official language?

Article 345 provides that the legislature of a state may by law adopt any one or more of the languages in use in the state, or Hindi, as the language or languages to be used for all or any of the official purposes of that state. Until a state legislature provides otherwise, English continues for the official purposes for which it was being used at the commencement of the Constitution. The article is the federal counterweight to Article 343: the Union's official language is set by the Constitution and Parliament, while each state's official language is set by its own legislature, which is the arrangement that let Madras State conduct its administration and education in Tamil and English while the Union's language was contested in New Delhi. Article 346 then routes communication between a state and the Union through the language authorised for the Union, subject to agreements between states to use Hindi for their mutual communication.

### Q: Which language does Article 348 prescribe for proceedings in the Supreme Court and the High Courts?

Article 348 provides that all proceedings in the Supreme Court and in every High Court shall be in the English language until Parliament by law provides otherwise, and it extends the same rule to the authoritative texts of bills, acts, ordinances, orders and rules issued under the Constitution. A state Governor may, with the previous consent of the President, authorise the use of Hindi or another official language of the state in the proceedings of its High Court, but the article expressly keeps judgments, decrees and orders in English. The provision mattered to the 1950s contest because it insulated the higher judiciary from the 1965 deadline: whatever happened to the secretariat's language, the courts in which the settlement itself might be litigated were to continue in English unless Parliament chose to legislate, which Parliament did not do.

### Q: What does Article 351 direct the Union to do for the development of Hindi?

Article 351 declares it the duty of the Union to promote the spread of the Hindi language and to develop it so that it may serve as a medium of expression for all the elements of the composite culture of India. It directs that Hindi be enriched by assimilating, without interfering with its genius, the forms, style and expressions used in Hindustani and in the other languages of India specified in the Eighth Schedule, and by drawing, wherever necessary or desirable, for its vocabulary primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on other languages. The Union built institutions on the article, including the Central Hindi Directorate established in 1960 and the Commission for Scientific and Technical Terminology of 1961. The article authorised promotion, not displacement, and the resistance of the 1950s consistently distinguished the two, accepting the first and rejecting the second.

### Q: Who chaired the Joint Parliamentary Committee on Official Language appointed in 1957?

The committee of Parliament constituted in 1957 to examine the report of the Official Language Commission under Article 344(4) was chaired by Govind Ballabh Pant, the Union Home Minister. The committee, thirty members elected by the two Houses of Parliament, reported in February 1959 and moderated the Kher Commission's timetable in the respects that mattered most to the non-Hindi states: it advised that English continue as the subsidiary official language for as long as the non-Hindi regions required it, that the changeover proceed by consent, and that no fixed date be set for the displacement of English. The President's Order of 27 April 1960 gave executive effect to the committee's line, and Nehru's Lok Sabha assurance of 7 August 1959, delivered between the committee's report and the Order, carried the same position into the political record as a prime ministerial commitment.

### Q: What did the DMK demand in Parliament during the 1963 debates on the Official Languages Bill?

During the debates of April and May 1963, members of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and their allies demanded two amendments to the Official Languages Bill. The first was verbal and fundamental: that the bill command, with the word shall, the continued use of English after 26 January 1965, instead of permitting it with the word may, so that continuance would be a legal right rather than a ministerial practice. The second was structural: that English's continuance be tied to the consent of the legislatures of the non-Hindi states, so that no future parliamentary majority could end it alone. The Union government resisted both demands, arguing that may in statutory drafting conferred an authority intended to be exercised, and its majority carried the permissive text. The 1965 agitation vindicated the demands in politics, and the amendment of 1967 enacted the consent formula in substance four years after the House had refused it.

### Q: What happened in Madurai on 25 January 1965?

On 25 January 1965, the day the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam had called to be observed as a day of mourning on the eve of the Article 343 deadline, a clash in Madurai between students agitating against Hindi and members of the Congress party escalated into a full-scale riot. The violence spread from Madurai across Madras State and continued for about two months, marked by arson against railway property and Hindi signage, attacks on police, police firing on crowds, and self-immolations by protesters. Paramilitary forces were deployed at the request of the Congress government of Madras State under Chief Minister M. Bhaktavatsalam. Official estimates put the deaths at about seventy persons, including two policemen who were burned to death by a mob. The scale of the disorder, and its spread far beyond the DMK's organisational control into the student body of the state, forced the Union government's February intervention.

### Q: How did Lal Bahadur Shastri respond to the Madras agitation in February 1965?

Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri answered the agitation by renewing and enlarging Nehru's assurance in his own name. In a broadcast to the nation on 11 February 1965 and in statements to Parliament in the same days, Shastri declared that Nehru's commitment would be honoured in full: English would continue as an associate official language for as long as the non-Hindi states wanted it, communications from the centre to the non-Hindi states would be in English, and candidates from those states would suffer no disadvantage in the Union's examinations and recruitment. The two Madras ministers in his cabinet, C. Subramaniam and O. V. Alagesan, who had tendered their resignations during the crisis, withdrew them as the assurance took hold. The agitation subsided within weeks. Shastri's intervention is the hinge of the settlement-confirmation reading of 1965, because the Prime Minister conceded nothing new; he restored, under his own authority, the promise of 1959.

### Q: What did the Official Languages (Amendment) Act of 1967 change about the position of English?

The Official Languages (Amendment) Act, passed by Parliament in December 1967 under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and brought into force on 8 January 1968 as Act 1 of 1968, converted the permissive continuance of English under the 1963 Act into a durable bilingual settlement. It provided that the use of English, in addition to Hindi, for the official purposes of the Union would continue until the legislatures of all the states that had not adopted Hindi as their official language resolved against its continuance and both Houses of Parliament resolved in the same sense. The double lock was the amendment's substance: the non-Hindi states acquired a collective veto over the ending of English, exercisable through their own legislatures, which was the consent formula the DMK had demanded and lost in the debates of 1963. After 1968 the language settlement carried no terminal date.

### Q: How many seats did the DMK win in the 1967 Madras assembly election?

The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam won 137 seats in the Madras Legislative Assembly election of February 1967, in a house of 234 members, fighting at the head of an alliance and forming the state government with C. N. Annadurai as Chief Minister from 6 March 1967. The result ended Congress rule in the state. Its emblematic constituency was Virudhunagar, where K. Kamaraj, the Congress's national president and the state's dominant politician of the previous decade, was defeated by P. Seenivasan, a young DMK candidate who had been a student leader in the agitation of 1965. The election is treated in this article as the settlement's ratification: the electorate of the state that had led the resistance installed the resistance's party in office, and every subsequent Union government has had to reckon with a Madras, renamed Tamil Nadu in 1969, governed by parties descended from that verdict.

### Q: Why did C. Rajagopalachari oppose Hindi imposition in the 1950s after introducing compulsory Hindi in Madras in 1937?

Rajagopalachari's two positions addressed two different compulsions. As Premier of Madras in 1937 he made Hindi a compulsory subject in a limited group of the Presidency's secondary schools, a provincial curriculum decision taken by a ministry answerable to a provincial electorate, which the government of Lord Erskine reversed in 1940 after Periyar's agitation. By the 1950s, as a former Governor-General of India writing and speaking outside office, he confronted a different proposal: the installation of Hindi as the working language of the Union's files, examinations and commands, over citizens of states that had never consented to be governed in it. Rajagopalachari held that the second compulsion lacked the first's democratic warrant and would corrode the Union it claimed to integrate. Language stood among the grievances that led him, with N. G. Ranga and Minoo Masani, to found the Swatantra Party in June 1959.

### Q: What did Annadurai announce about the Dravida Nadu demand in 1963?

In 1963 C. N. Annadurai announced that the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam was giving up its demand for a separate Dravida Nadu. The announcement followed the Sixteenth Amendment of 1963, by which Parliament, in the aftermath of the war with China of 1962, amended the Constitution to permit restrictions on advocacy of secession and required oaths of allegiance to the sovereignty and integrity of India from candidates and legislators. Annadurai presented the renunciation as the party's answer to the changed constitutional ground and to the national emergency through which the country had passed, and the DMK contested every later election as a party of the Indian Union. The announcement matters to the language history because it cleared the settlement's path: the party that led the resistance to Hindi imposition had formally separated that resistance from any claim against the Union itself, which let the centre treat the language demand as negotiable federal business.
