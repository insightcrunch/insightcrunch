Ask how the RSS BJP relationship works and Indian politics offers two ready answers, each armed with a decade of post-2014 evidence. The first answer holds that the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh commands and the Bharatiya Janata Party obeys: the party is the Sangh's political instrument, its ministers report to Nagpur, and its governments execute an ideological programme drafted elsewhere. The second answer inverts the first: a party led by Narendra Modi, a prime minister whose authority inside his own organisation has no parallel in the post-2014 record, takes its decisions in New Delhi and treats the Sangh as an honoured ancestor whose counsel is received with ceremony and set aside at will. Both answers quote real meetings, real appointments and real speeches. Both fail on the evidence they cite, because the post-2014 RSS BJP relationship operates through neither command nor courtesy. It operates through a coordination architecture: a set of standing mechanisms, regularised after May 2014, by which the Sangh supplies strategic, ideological and personnel inputs and the party returns political, electoral and policy-execution outputs, continuously rather than episodically.

![Coordination meetings and personnel exchanges between the RSS headquarters at Nagpur and the BJP leadership in New Delhi - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-88.webp)

The claim defended here is institutional, and it is falsifiable. If the subordination reading were correct, the documentary record would show instructions issued by Sangh bodies and executed without alteration by party bodies, and it would show party office-holders removed at Nagpur's instance when they deviated. If the autonomy reading were correct, the record would show a party that staffs itself, frames its ideology and sequences its policies without reference to the Sangh's calendar, resolutions or personnel. The record shows neither. It shows inputs flowing through identifiable channels, being priced, negotiated, sometimes amended and sometimes declined, inside a standing architecture that both organisations maintain and neither abolishes. The architecture has three dimensions. Strategic-ideological inputs travel from the Sangh to the party through resolutions of the Akhil Bharatiya Pratinidhi Sabha and the Akhil Bharatiya Karyakari Mandal, through the Sarsanghchalak's annual Vijayadashami address, and through the Sangh's public interventions on contested questions. Personnel moves through a rotation system whose hinge is the pracharak deputation and whose most visible joint is the BJP office of general secretary (organisation), held by a Sangh full-timer in every state and at the centre. Consultation proceeds through regularised encounters, from the three-day coordination conclave held in Delhi in September 2015, which the Prime Minister attended, to the annual Akhil Bharatiya Samanvay Baithak at which the BJP sits as one organisation among more than thirty.

The date 2014 is the hinge because of what the election of that year changed in the dyad's arithmetic. Every earlier BJP government had been a coalition artefact or a state government, configurations in which the Sangh's inputs competed with allies' vetoes and its personnel with allies' claims. The verdict of May 2014 gave the party 282 seats and a single-party majority in the Lok Sabha, and the verdict of May 2019 raised the tally to 303, which meant that for a full decade the relationship could be observed without the mediation of coalition partners, in its direct form: an ideological principal with no vote in Parliament and a parliamentary party with no ideological supplier of comparable depth. The decade is therefore the cleanest natural experiment the dyad has produced, and it is bounded with unusual precision, opening with the coordination conclave at which a prime minister first presented his government to the Parivar in September 2015 and closing, for the purposes of this article, with the conclave at Palakkad at which the Parivar reviewed the experiment's first failure in September 2024. The articles of this pillar have traced the dyad's components, its ideology, its founder-generation, its movement phases; this closing article assembles the components into the working machine and submits the machine to the record.

The renegotiation of Indian politics around this dyad has been tracked in this series through [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), and the present article closes the pillar on the Sangh Parivar by turning from tensions to mechanisms. Its primary sources are the resolutions of the RSS National Executive and of the Akhil Bharatiya Pratinidhi Sabha between 2014 and 2024, the resolutions of the BJP National Executive in the same years, and the documented reports of meetings between Mohan Bhagwat, Sarsanghchalak since 21 March 2009, and the party and government leadership. Its mandatory secondary sources frame the disagreement: Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle, in *The RSS: A View to the Inside* (2018), produced the canonical organisational analysis from inside the Sangh's own records and interviews; Christophe Jaffrelot, in *Modi's India: Hindu Nationalism and the Rise of Ethnic Democracy* (2021), produced the strongest scholarly statement of the interpenetration thesis; and Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in essays for *The Indian Express* across the decade, produced the sharpest account of the institutional centralisation that the autonomy reading mistakes for the whole story. An under-cited primary source runs beneath all three: the office records of the Sarkaryavah and Sah-Sarkaryavah at the Sangh headquarters in Nagpur and at Keshav Kunj in Delhi, which preserve the procedural paper trail of coordination, who was deputed where, which conclave took which decision, and which consultation preceded which announcement. The journalists who reconstruct that trail from briefings at Keshav Kunj protect their notebooks as a condition of access, and the tradecraft described in [accounts of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) belongs to the evidentiary economy of every closed institution in the capital.

The Sangh's own vocabulary for this machinery is samanvay, coordination, a word its functionaries use without embarrassment and its critics hear as camouflage. The word deserves to be taken seriously as a description of form rather than dismissed as a euphemism, because the things it names are enumerable: a calendar of meetings with dates and venues, a roster of offices with occupants and tenures, a published stream of resolutions and addresses, and a traffic of named individuals between Nagpur, Keshav Kunj and the party headquarters on Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Marg. Where the description fails, it fails on a checkable point, a meeting that did not happen, a resolution that went unpriced, a consultation that left no trace. The three-dimension map offered in Table 1 is built to expose exactly those checkable points, and the sections that follow hold each dimension against the documentary exhibits that defenders and critics of the dyad have themselves placed on the record.

## Two Readings of the RSS BJP Relationship

The subordination reading is the older of the two, and its evidence is not imaginary. Its scholarly form is Jaffrelot's. In *Modi's India* (2021), Jaffrelot argued that the post-2014 state underwent a systematic interpenetration by Sangh personnel and priorities: appointments to cultural, educational and research bodies went to figures from the Sangh's ecosystem, the party's candidate lists bore the imprint of Sangh preferences conveyed through its organisational secretaries, and the Sarsanghchalak commented on questions of national policy, from reservation to the economy, with a public confidence no earlier holder of the office had exercised while his party held power. The examples Jaffrelot and other critics cite are documented. Yellapragada Sudershan Rao, a historian associated with the Sangh's historical bodies, was appointed chairman of the Indian Council of Historical Research in 2014. The appointment of Gajendra Chauhan to head the Film and Television Institute of India in 2015 provoked a student strike precisely because it was read, by supporters and opponents alike, as an ideological placement. Journalists covering the BJP's ticket distribution in election after election recorded the presence of Sangh feedback in the winnability assessments placed before the party's Central Election Committee. Bhagwat's annual Vijayadashami addresses at Nagpur, carried live on national television through the decade, pronounced on matters from population policy to the economy in terms no government could treat as private opinion.

The political form of the subordination reading was fixed within sixteen months of the 2014 verdict. When the three-day coordination conclave of September 2015 closed in Delhi, with Union ministers having presented accounts of their ministries before the Sangh's office-bearers, the Polit Bureau of the Communist Party of India (Marxist) stated that the meetings showed the RSS to be "not merely the remote control but in direct control" of the government, and Congress spokesmen described a "super government" sitting in judgement on the elected one. The vocabulary of remote control and super-cabinet has organised opposition rhetoric on the dyad ever since, and it draws strength from every visible point of contact: ministers at Nagpur, pracharaks in party offices, the Sarsanghchalak's interventions in live controversies. On this reading, coordination is a courtesy title for command, and the post-2014 period differs from earlier phases only in the directness of the channel.

The exhibits accumulate across the decade's middle years. The Indian Council for Cultural Relations received as its president in 2018 Vinay Sahasrabuddhe, a BJP vice-president formed in the Sangh's student organisation, and the council's programming acquired a pronounced civilisational register. The National Council of Educational Research and Training's textbook rationalisation exercises from 2017 onward drew sustained criticism from historians who documented deletions touching the Mughal period, the Gujarat violence of 2002 and the circumstances of Mahatma Gandhi's assassination, each deletion defended by the government as load rationalisation and read by the critics as ideological grooming of the school curriculum. Vice-chancellors of central universities appointed through the period repeatedly carried biographies from the Parivar's academic organisations, and governors posted to several states came from the party's senior ranks. No single exhibit proves command. The subordination reading's strength is the pattern the exhibits compose, an ecosystem in which the same cadre background recurs at every level at which the state touches culture, education and ceremony, as if a single personnel department stood behind the appointments.

The autonomy reading assembles an opposite dossier from the same decade. Its central exhibit is the concentration of decision-making inside the government itself: a Prime Minister's Office that clears appointments, sequences legislation and disciplines ministerial initiative, of the kind examined in [the post-2014 PMO architecture](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/). The party organisation under Amit Shah, president from July 2014 to January 2020, was rebuilt as an electoral machine answerable to its own leadership: membership drives run on party targets, candidate surveys commissioned by the party, campaign command centralised in the party president's office. Modi's public interventions repeatedly cut across Sangh-adjacent sentiment when electoral arithmetic required it, most sharply on 6 August 2016, when he told a Delhi town hall that some eighty percent of self-described cow protectors were anti-social elements by night, and on 7 August 2016 at Gajwel in Telangana, where, after the flogging of four Dalit men at Una in Gujarat, he declared that attackers should shoot him rather than his Dalit brethren. Neither statement was cleared with the cow-protection constituency of the Sangh Parivar, and both were delivered at moments of maximum political cost. The autonomy reading reached its canonical statement in May 2024, when BJP president J. P. Nadda told *The Indian Express* that the party of the early years had been less capable and had needed the Sangh's scaffolding, whereas the party had since become saksham, capable, and conducted its own affairs. Political journalism built on such exhibits a figure of Modi as a strongman whose party is a vehicle and whose ideological parent is a spectator.

The autonomy dossier also includes the party's repeated elevation of leaders whose formation owed nothing to the Sangh. The choice of Yogi Adityanath as Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh in March 2017 elevated the mahant of the Gorakhnath Math, heir to a lineage older than the Sangh's presence in the state, whose Hindu Yuva Vahini had operated as a parallel machine in eastern Uttar Pradesh and whose dealings with the Sangh's provincial leadership had produced documented friction. In Assam the party's governments rested first on Sarbananda Sonowal, formed in the All Assam Students' Union, and from 2021 on Himanta Biswa Sarma, imported from the Congress in 2015 and elevated over leaders of longer standing in the party itself. A party staffed at the pleasure of a single external headquarters does not hand its most demanding state governments to men the headquarters never trained. A party joined to its ideological parent by a coordination architecture can, because the architecture governs dealings between organisations and not the biography of every office-holder the party chooses.

Each reading, stated at its strongest, captures a real stratum of the record and misdescribes the stratum it cannot see. The subordination reading cannot explain why a commanding headquarters failed to obtain the policy outcomes its affiliates publicly demanded: the Bharatiya Kisan Sangh's demand for a statutory guarantee of remunerative prices, pressed from September 2020 and through a nationwide protest on 8 September 2021, was not conceded; the Swadeshi Jagran Manch's campaigns against disinvestment proceeded without halting a single strategic sale; and the farm laws whose passage Sangh affiliates had criticised were repealed by the government on 19 November 2021 for reasons of electoral management in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh, not on Nagpur's instruction. The autonomy reading cannot explain why a self-sufficient party keeps its most sensitive organisational office staffed by the other body's full-timers, routes its ideological calendar through the other body's resolutions, and sends its president to sit through a three-day conclave of the other body's affiliates a decade into its supposed emancipation. The disagreement is therefore not resolved by weighing the two dossiers. It is resolved by changing the unit of analysis from hierarchy to mechanism, which is the work of the three sections that follow.

The two readings also rest on different evidentiary positions, and the disagreement is partly a disagreement about access. Andersen and Damle wrote from decades of interviews inside the Sangh, including with its senior leadership, and their account of the organisation's internal life, its factional textures and its self-understanding, carries a granularity no outside archive can match; critics of their work answer that access of that depth is purchased with sympathy, and that the Sangh appears in their pages substantially as it wishes to be understood. Jaffrelot wrote from the opposite position, from the published record and the scholarly literature, and his account gains in detachment what it yields in interiority. Mehta's essays work the seam between them, reading the public record for the institutional logic it betrays. The present article's method follows the seam: the coordination synthesis is built wherever possible from exhibits all three positions accept, the published resolutions, the dated statements, the office tenures, the conclave calendar, and it treats each reading's favoured anecdotes as evidence to be priced rather than verdicts to be repeated.

## Dimension One: Strategic and Ideological Inputs

The first dimension of the architecture is the flow of strategic and ideological frameworks from the Sangh to the party, and its channels are formal enough to be dated. The Akhil Bharatiya Pratinidhi Sabha, the Sangh's apex representative body, meets each March, elects the Sarkaryavah every three years, reviews the organisation's annual report and passes resolutions that state the Sangh's position on the questions of the year. The Akhil Bharatiya Karyakari Mandal, its national executive, meets between sessions and issues statements in the same register. These texts are inputs, not instructions: they bind no legislator, and the BJP's constitution owes them nothing. Their force inside the architecture is different in kind. A Pratinidhi Sabha resolution tells every pracharak deputed to the party, every swayamsevak inside the party's booth committees, and every minister who began in a shakha what the parent organisation counts as the year's priorities, and a party that ignored the signal would pay for the ignoring in cadre morale long before it paid in votes.

The documentary sequence shows the channel carrying weight in both directions of the relationship. At Bengaluru on 20 March 2021, the Pratinidhi Sabha elected Dattatreya Hosabale as Sarkaryavah in succession to Suresh Bhaiyyaji Joshi, who had held the executive office since 2009; the succession was an internal Sangh act with no party participation, and it determined who would sit across the table from the BJP's leadership for the rest of the decade. At Samalkha in Panipat district, from 12 to 14 March 2023, the Sabha passed its resolution on the resurgence of the nation based on Swa, selfhood, calling for educational, economic, social and judicial systems to be rethought in the light of Bharatiya categories during the Amrit Kaal, and adopted the five dimensions of social transformation, social harmony, family values, ecological conservation, swadeshi conduct and civic duty, that Sangh and party programmes alike carried into the 2024 campaign season. At Nagpur in March 2024 the Sabha re-elected Hosabale for a second term, a continuity decision taken weeks before polling that guaranteed the coordination channel a stable counterpart through the election. Read as the brief's mandatory sources direct, the run of National Executive and Pratinidhi Sabha resolutions from 2014 to 2024 is the closest thing the dyad possesses to a published strategic correspondence.

The second channel of the first dimension is the Sarsanghchalak's own voice, exercised through two set pieces. The first is the annual Vijayadashami address at Nagpur, delivered each autumn on the festival of the Sangh's founding.

### What does the Sarsanghchalak's Vijayadashami address do?

The Vijayadashami address is the Sarsanghchalak's annual statement of ideological position, delivered at Nagpur on the festival that marks the Sangh's founding day in 1925. It reviews the year, names the organisation's priorities, comments on contested national questions, and is read by party and government as the authoritative signal of the Sangh's strategic line.

The address functions inside the architecture as a public input that the government must price. On Vijayadashami in October 2015, Bhagwat assessed the sixteen-month-old Modi government as moving in the right direction and generating an atmosphere of expectation, an endorsement delivered weeks after the coordination conclave and read by the party as a certificate. On 11 October 2016, the address praised genuine cow protection while condemning those who took the law into their own hands, a two-sided formulation that gave the government cover for its August crackdown on vigilantes while protecting the cow-protection constituency's standing inside the Parivar. In the 2018 address, Bhagwat pressed for a law to enable the construction of the Ram temple at Ayodhya, converting a judicial wait into a legislative demand the government then had to manage through the election season. Each address is formally a speech to swayamsevaks. Institutionally, it is the one input the Sangh issues that cannot be quietly filed.

The second set piece was built for the post-2014 period. Between 17 and 19 September 2018, Bhagwat delivered a three-day lecture series, Bhavishya ka Bharat, at Vigyan Bhavan in Delhi, before an invited audience of diplomats, academics, journalists and public figures, and answered questions on the final day. The lectures restated the Sangh's positions in a register designed for national broadcast: the Sangh accepted the Constitution and the national flag, it did not seek to exclude Muslims from its conception of the nation, it acknowledged the Congress party's role in the freedom movement, and, on the question this article adjudicates, it neither ran the government nor controlled the organisations inspired by it, offering advice where asked and leaving decisions to the decided. The series was an ideological input of a distinctive kind: a recalibration of the frame within which the party would fight the 2019 election, issued by the Sangh over the heads of its own cadres to the country's opinion-forming classes. A subordinate party does not need its parent to re-explain itself to the diplomatic corps. A coordinating system does exactly that when the terms of the alliance require public restatement.

What travels through these channels is an agenda, and the post-2014 decade shows the agenda being executed on the government's timetable rather than the Sangh's. The abrogation of Article 370 on 5 August 2019, the Citizenship Amendment Act of December 2019, and the consecration of the Ram temple at Ayodhya on 22 January 2024 after the Supreme Court judgment of 9 November 2019 closed a sequence of commitments that Sangh resolutions had carried for decades. The government's contribution to each was the part the Sangh cannot supply: parliamentary arithmetic, drafting, and the choice of moment. Input and output are separable in every case, and the separation is the architecture's first law. The ideological content itself is not a single block: as [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) distinguishes, Savarkarite, Golwalkarite and organisational-pragmatic strands coexist inside the Sangh's inheritance, and the resolutions of 2014 to 2024 blend them in proportions that vary by question, which is one reason the inputs arrive as frameworks to be priced rather than texts to be applied.

The resolution channel also carries the Sangh's own ideological self-correction, which the party must then absorb. The Pratinidhi Sabha at Nagaur in March 2016 passed a resolution on caste that condemned discrimination in temples, cremation grounds and sources of water and called for the practice of untouchability to end in conduct rather than merely in law, a text the Sangh's publicity department circulated as guidance to its own ranks. The resolution mattered beyond the Sangh's walls because the party's electoral coalition in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh rested on the very caste blocs the text addressed, and because it supplied the moral vocabulary in which Bhagwat's December 2015 reservation clarification, examined in the disagreement section of this article, could be delivered without loss of face. A headquarters dictating to a subordinate party has no need to legislate for its own cadres' prejudices in public. An ideological principal in a coordination architecture does, since the framework it issues binds its own movement first and reaches the party only as a priced text.

The Vijayadashami addresses of the decade's second half show the channel operating at the level of doctrine. At Nagpur on 8 October 2019, Bhagwat described lynching as a word imported from foreign religious history with no place in Indian tradition, a formulation that dissociated the Sangh from vigilante violence in the government's second term while placing the dissociation inside a civilisational argument the party could repeat without conceding the opposition's framing. At Nagpur on 5 October 2022, the address turned to demography, speaking of population imbalance between communities and the need for a comprehensive population policy, and to the induction of women into the Sangh's public-facing work; the policy demand entered the party's debating chamber through the front door, where it met the government's stated position, delivered in Parliament that year, that no population-control legislation was under preparation. The channel's outputs, in other words, include demands the government publicly declines, recorded in the same public texts that carry the demands it enacts. Any reading of Dimension One that counts only the enacted half of the traffic is measuring the architecture with half its exhibits removed.

The Citizenship Amendment Act of December 2019 supplies the channel's hardest test, enactment under street pressure. The amendment, fast-tracking citizenship for non-Muslim migrants from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan, had stood in the Parivar's texts for decades before it reached the statute book; its enactment in the teeth of nationwide protests, and of criticism from governments abroad, was the government's choice and the government's risk. The Sangh's contribution ran through the input channel in its public register: its functionaries defended the measure in print and on platforms across the winter of 2019 and 2020 as a humanitarian obligation toward persecuted minorities, and Bhagwat, addressing a gathering at Guwahati in July 2021, stated that the citizenship law and the proposed national register were not directed against any citizen of India and that no Indian Muslim had cause to fear them. The statements did not still the protests, and they were not intended to; their function inside the architecture was to mark the ideological principal's ownership of a contested law at the moment of its maximum unpopularity, so that the party carried the parliamentary fight with its flank covered by the movement's public credit. Subordination would have required no such expenditure, since a command does not advertise itself to reassure the commanded. Coordination predicts exactly this division of labour, the principal spending reputation, the party spending votes, the account settled in the next conclave.

The party's own texts complete the dimension's circuit, because the input channel terminates in documents the BJP signs. The election manifestos of 2014 and 2019 carried the Parivar's standing commitments in the party's own programme: the construction of the Ram temple at Ayodhya within the constitutional framework, the abrogation of Jammu and Kashmir's special status, a uniform civil code referred to consultation, and the protection of the cow in the language of the Directive Principles. The 2019 manifesto, the Sankalp Patra released by the party's leadership in April of that year, renewed each commitment after five years of the party's own experience of office, which disposes of the suggestion that the texts were inherited boilerplate the party had not read. What the manifestos establish, read beside the Sangh's resolutions, is the direction of travel the coordination reading predicts: frameworks originate in the movement's deliberative cycle, enter the party's programme through its own drafting organs, and return to the movement's conclaves as government action to be reviewed. The circuit is visible end to end in published texts, and its visibility is the point at which the subordination reading's central metaphor fails, since a remote control does not publish the instructions, and the controlled device does not debate them for five years before complying.

## Dimension Two: Personnel Rotation and the Deputation System

The second dimension is the one on which the architecture's claim to be an institution, rather than a mood, principally rests: people move between the two organisations through a regulated rotation, and the rotation long predates the period under review. Its foundation is [the RSS cadre architecture](/2019/09/13/kb-hedgewar-founding-rss-1925/) built under K. B. Hedgewar after 1925, in which the pracharak, a full-time worker maintained by the Sangh, unmarried or separated from household life, and posted where the organisation sends him, became the standard carrier of the Sangh's methods into every body it inspired. A pracharak deputed to the BJP does not resign from the Sangh, draw a party salary or acquire a party career. His posting can be recalled. His promotion inside the party is real, his accountability runs in two directions, and the arrangement is not concealed: both organisations describe it openly as a loan of personnel between allied bodies.

The largest fact in the post-2014 record is that the Prime Minister is himself a product of the rotation. Narendra Modi became a full-time worker of the Sangh in 1971 and a pracharak in Gujarat, worked underground during the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, and was assigned by the Sangh to the BJP in 1985, becoming organising secretary of the party's Gujarat unit in 1987 and its national general secretary (organisation) in May 1998. Every step of that ascent was a deputation decision taken inside the mechanism described here. The man who heads the government whose autonomy the second reading celebrates reached the headship through the personnel system of the first reading's supposed headquarters, and his working habits, cadre vocabulary, organisational discipline and suspicion of intermediary power centres were formed inside it. Neither reading digests this fact comfortably, which is why the coordination reading begins from it.

The rotation's hinge is the office of general secretary (organisation) in the BJP, at the centre and in the states. The office controls the party's organisational appointments, its training, its internal communications and much of its candidate-selection groundwork, and by unbroken practice it is held by a pracharak on deputation. Ram Lal held the national office for thirteen years, from 2006, spanning the party's opposition decade and its first Modi term, and on 13 July 2019 he was recalled to the Sangh as its Akhil Bharatiya Sah Sampark Pramukh. On 14 July 2019, Amit Shah elevated B. L. Santhosh, a pracharak from Karnataka who had served as joint general secretary (organisation) with charge of the southern states since 2014, to the national office. Contemporary reporting described the post in terms that require no commentary: the occupant acts as the bridge between the two organisations, and the bridge carries traffic in both directions, cadre assessments and ideological signals travelling one way, organisational requirements and electoral intelligence travelling the other. Below the national office, the joint general secretaries of the period, V. Satish, Saudan Singh and Shiv Prakash among them, and the organising secretaries of the state units, replicate the same joint at every level of the party's federal structure.

The rotation also runs between the Sangh's executive and the party's front rank, in both directions and on the record. Ram Madhav, a pracharak who had served as the Sangh's national spokesperson, was deputed to the BJP in 2014 and made a national general secretary, handling the party's work in Jammu and Kashmir and the Northeast through the government's first term; in March 2021 he returned to the Sangh's Akhil Bharatiya Karyakari Mandal. Chief ministers emerged from the same pool: Manohar Lal Khattar, a pracharak since the late 1970s, became Chief Minister of Haryana on 26 October 2014; Trivendra Singh Rawat, a pracharak who had served as the BJP's organising secretary in Uttarakhand and as a national secretary, became Chief Minister of Uttarakhand in March 2017; Biplab Kumar Deb, who had worked as a pracharak in Tripura, became its Chief Minister in March 2018. The counter-flow matters as much to the analysis. The party also promotes leaders with no pracharak past, and its parliamentary leadership contains many whose formation owes nothing to the shakha. A subordination model predicts uniformity of personnel origin. The record shows a mixed cadre in which one regulated channel, the deputation channel, is reserved for the organisational spine.

The office that administers the rotation from the Sangh's side is the Sarkaryavah's, and its occupants across the period frame the personnel story.

### Who was Suresh Bhaiyyaji Joshi?

Suresh Bhaiyyaji Joshi was the Sarkaryavah, or general secretary, of the RSS from 2009 to 2021, the executive office that runs the Sangh's daily organisation and its deputation system. A pracharak from Maharashtra, he served four three-year terms, handed over to Dattatreya Hosabale at Bengaluru in March 2021, and remained the Sangh's organisational manager across the first two Modi governments.

Personnel rotation on this scale requires money that is itself institutionalised. The pracharak draws no salary from the party he serves; his maintenance, and the upkeep of the Sangh's offices from the Nagpur headquarters to the district karyalaya, comes from the Sangh's own collection system.

### What is gurudakshina in the RSS system?

Gurudakshina is the annual offering by which swayamsevaks fund the Sangh: each volunteer places an unsigned envelope of money before the saffron flag at a yearly function, and the collection maintains the full-time pracharaks and the organisation's offices. The Sangh publishes no donor list, accepts no institutional grants, and cites the practice as proof of financial independence.

The friction inside the rotation is documented as faithfully as the rotation itself, and it is friction of a kind neither rival reading predicts. In Karnataka, relations between Santhosh, then the party's joint general secretary (organisation) for the south, and B. S. Yeddyurappa, the party's tallest state leader, were strained through the 2018 assembly election season, with candidate selection among the contested questions; the dispute was between two men inside one party, one of whom held his post on the Sangh's deputation. Read through the subordination frame, the episode is inexplicable, since the supposed subordinate and the supposed headquarters' man were on opposite sides. Read through the coordination frame, it is the system working as designed: the deputation channel transmits assessments, not obedience, and assessments can be fought over.

The deputation system's depth can be gauged in the states, where the pattern repeats with local variations. In Uttar Pradesh, the party's 2014 breakthrough and its 2017 and 2019 victories were organised substantially by Sunil Bansal, a pracharak who served as the state unit's general secretary (organisation) from 2014 and was credited across the political press with building the booth-level machinery that the party's opponents described, with reluctant admiration, as an army. His elevation in July 2022 to national general secretary, with charge of West Bengal, Odisha and Telangana, carried the deputation pattern to the party's most contested frontiers and confirmed that the bridge's senior ranks are a career ladder inside the system rather than a series of one-off favours. Correspondents who surveyed the party after the 2019 victory reported that a majority of its state organising secretaries were Sangh pracharaks on deputation, a proportion the party has never published and never disputed. The organisational spine of the largest political party in the democratic world is thus staffed, in the main, by men whose salaries it does not pay, whose discipline it does not own and whose recall it cannot refuse.

The party has also copied the deputation principle into its own volunteer economy. The vistarak system, expanded by Amit Shah for the 2019 campaign, recruited full-time workers who left their homes and occupations for a year to serve a constituency, sustained by the party and bound by rules of conduct modelled openly on the pracharak's regime of celibate simplicity; the party's own accounts put the corps in the tens of thousands. A command relationship has no need of imitation: headquarters issues an order and the instrument obeys. What the vistarak corps shows is a party internalising its parent movement's personnel technology because the technology works, and staffing the copies with its own recruits under its own discipline. The dyad's personnel traffic, traced whole, runs in three lanes: pracharaks deputed into the party's spine, party workers trained in the pracharak mould, and, at the margins, heavyweight imports such as Jyotiraditya Scindia, who arrived from the Congress in 2020 and entered the Union Council of Ministers in 2021, proof that the lanes coexist with open recruitment at the top.

The channel's discipline runs in both directions, and its history includes a recall that reads, in retrospect, as the system's stress fracture. Sanjay Joshi, a pracharak deputed to the party and its national general secretary (organisation) in the early 2000s, was forced out of the party's national executive in 2005 amid his feud with Narendra Modi, then Chief Minister of Gujarat, and the Sangh, whose officer he remained, absorbed the humiliation without public protest; his later marginal postings inside the organisation closed the file without closing the lesson. The episode predates the period under adjudication, and it is entered here as the baseline the post-2014 record must be compared against. Between 2014 and 2024 no deputed officer of senior rank was publicly destroyed by an internal feud, no recall was contested in print, and the bridge office's single change of occupant, in July 2019, was executed as a routine succession with the outgoing officer received back into the Sangh's executive. A channel that once transmitted a factional execution has come to transmit successions; the difference is the difference between a deputation practice and a deputation system, and it dates, on the record, from the period this article examines.

## Dimension Three: Consultations as Procedure

The third dimension is the procedural one, and it is where the post-2014 period most plainly innovated. Consultation between the Sangh and the party is old; what the decade built was a calendar. The exhibit that fixed the calendar in public view was the coordination conclave of 2 to 4 September 2015 in Delhi. Some ninety-three functionaries of the Sangh and fifteen allied organisations sat for three days; Union ministers, including the Finance Minister Arun Jaitley, the External Affairs Minister Sushma Swaraj, the Home Minister Rajnath Singh and the Defence Minister Manohar Parrikar, presented accounts of their ministries and heard the affiliates' assessments; BJP president Amit Shah attended; and the Prime Minister joined the proceedings. Manmohan Vaidya, the Sangh's spokesperson, described the conclave as part of a routine of interaction among the allied organisations, a description the opposition derided and the record since has vindicated in the narrow sense that matters here: the meeting recurred, on schedule, in government and would have recurred in opposition. A command summit does not need a spokesperson to call it routine. A procedure does.

The conclave's institutionalised form is the Akhil Bharatiya Samanvay Baithak, the annual all-India coordination meeting at which the Sangh's allied organisations, thirty-two of them at the Palakkad meeting of 2024, exchange reports, align calendars and record differences. The BJP attends as one organisation in the room, its president in the front row rather than at the head of the table, a seating fact that the subordination reading finds indigestible and the autonomy reading finds embarrassing. Below the annual conclave, coordination runs through bilateral channels with their own cadence: meetings between the Sangh's state leadership and the BJP's state units, consultations in Delhi between the Sarkaryavah's office at Keshav Kunj and the party's general secretary (organisation), and the standing practice by which senior Sangh office-bearers of Sah-Sarkaryavah rank are present in the hall at the BJP's National Executive meetings, where the party's resolutions are debated and adopted. The BJP National Executive resolutions of 2014 to 2024, read beside the Pratinidhi Sabha resolutions of the same years, show the documentary trace of this traffic: the party's texts adopt the Sangh's frameworks on questions from national education to demographic anxiety, translated into electoral and legislative language, and the divergences, where they occur, occur on timing and method rather than on the stated end.

At the apex of the system sit the personal consultations between the Sarsanghchalak and the Prime Minister, and between Bhagwat and the party president of the day. Andersen and Damle, writing in 2018 from interviews inside both organisations, described consultation between the Sangh's leadership and the government as regular and expected, a channel through which the Sangh conveyed assessments and the government explained constraints, and the press record of the decade is dotted with its visible instances. Amit Shah called on Bhagwat at Nagpur on 9 November 2015, the morning after the Bihar assembly results were declared, before the party's own post-mortem began; the sequence, party president to Sarsanghchalak before party president to parliamentary board, is the consultation system photographed mid-operation. The consultations are not minuted in public, and their content reaches the record through the statements each side issues afterwards, which is precisely why the Sarkaryavah's office records at Nagpur and Keshav Kunj, the brief's under-cited primary source, matter to the eventual historian of the dyad: they are where the procedure's paper trail, deputations proposed, conclaves convened, consultations logged, is preserved.

One feature of the third dimension deserves isolation, because it discriminates between the three readings better than any other. The consultation system has no published decision rule. No text states whose view prevails when the Sangh's assessment and the government's judgement diverge, and both organisations, asked directly, deny that a hierarchy exists to be consulted: Bhagwat at Delhi in September 2018 said the Sangh could advise and the decision belonged to the organisations and governments concerned. The subordination reading treats the missing rule as a veil. The autonomy reading treats it as proof the channel is decorative. The coordination reading treats it as the finding: the architecture is a standing negotiation between two bodies that share personnel and ideology and hold separate mandates, and a negotiation does not require a tie-breaking rule to be real, continuous and consequential. It requires only that neither side can afford to leave the table, and the decade's evidence, in which the party never skipped the conclave and the Sangh never withdrew the deputation, establishes exactly that.

The consultation channel's everyday end sits in Delhi at Keshav Kunj, the Sangh's capital office in Jhandewalan, where senior functionaries carried the liaison with the party through the decade. Arun Kumar, a Sah-Sarkaryavah through the government's first two terms and the Sangh's publicity chief from the 2021 reshuffle, was identified across the political press as the Sangh's principal interface with the BJP's leadership, the officer through whom messages that required no conclave and no press release could travel in both directions. The arrangement mirrors the deputation system's logic at the level of information: a named, accountable channel, staffed by a known officer, whose existence both sides acknowledge and whose traffic neither side minutes. At the state level, bilateral coordination sittings between the Sangh's provincial executives and the BJP's state units performed the same function between conclaves; in Uttar Pradesh after 2017, such sittings were reported around every major appointment and campaign, and their reported agenda, candidatures, caste arithmetic, the management of the Yogi Adityanath government's relations with the organisation, reads as the agenda of a standing joint committee in all but name.

The decade opened with the consultation channel under its brightest light. In May and June 2014, as the BJP moved from victory to government, Bhagwat stated publicly and repeatedly that the Sangh had no role in the selection of ministers and that the party was fully competent to compose its government, statements issued while the names under discussion for the Council of Ministers circulated through every newsroom in the capital and while speculation about Nagpur's preferences filled the gaps the statements left. The episode is a gift to both rival readings: the subordination school notes that the disclaimer was thought necessary, the autonomy school notes that it was given and broadly observed in the government's composition. The coordination reading takes the episode as the channel's founding protocol of the period, an agreement, performed in public, that consultation would be continuous and deniable at once, which is to say procedural. The annual conclave cycle then carried the protocol forward: the Samanvay Baithak at Pune in September 2023, attended by the representatives of some thirty allied organisations, reviewed the Parivar's work ahead of the general election and the Sangh's approaching centenary, in the same format, with the same absence of published minutes, that Palakkad would repeat after the verdict.

The consultation channel's largest outputs are electoral. In the campaigns of 2014 and 2019 the Sangh's volunteers worked inside the BJP's effort at a scale neither organisation quantified and both acknowledged in principle: the Sangh's stated position, repeated by its publicity chiefs in both cycles, was that swayamsevaks were free as citizens to assist the party whose programme they endorsed, and that the organisation as such contested nothing. The distinction between the organisation and its members is the dyad's constitutional fiction and its operating truth at once; the shakha network that mustered volunteers for booth duty in 2019 was the same network whose office-bearers sat in the year's coordination sittings, and the assessments those campaigns generated, of candidates, of caste alignments, of cadre morale, flowed back through the state bilateral channels into the consultations of the following cycle. Political scientists who studied the 2014 campaign's ground operation documented the overlap constituency by constituency; party managers of rival formations priced it into their own planning as a standing fact of the electoral landscape. For the architecture, the campaigns are the consultation system's proof of concept at national scale: an input of assessments and personnel, an output of votes, and a review, conducted in the conclaves, of the exchange's terms.

A final feature of the consultation channel deserves separate notice, because it distinguishes the post-2014 procedure from every earlier configuration: the channel publicises itself. The Sangh's publicity department, professionalised across the decade under its prachar pramukhs and equipped with spokesmen who brief on the record, treats the conclaves as events to be announced, their attendance listed, their closing briefings delivered to cameras, as Sunil Ambekar's Palakkad briefing of 2 September 2024 was delivered. The party reciprocates in its own register, its presidents' presence at the sittings acknowledged in its releases. The publicity is selective, agendas and attendance published, deliberations and disagreements withheld, and the selectivity is itself the procedure's signature: the dyad has decided that being seen to coordinate costs less than being rumoured to command, a calculation the coalition decades, when the same meetings would have been denied, never permitted either side to make. Historians of the dyad will mine the published surface for what it conceals; analysts of the dyad should first register what the surface's existence proves, that by the middle of the Modi decade coordination had become the relationship's official description, offered by both organisations, in their own names, on the record.

The three dimensions, with the mechanism that carries each, a named documented example, and the moment at which the dimension visibly evolved, are assembled in Table 1, the three-dimension coordination-architecture map, which subsequent comparative work on other party-movement dyads can cite row by row.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Three properties of the map repay attention. The first is symmetry of dependence. Each row runs in both directions: resolutions carry party feedback gathered by deputed pracharaks before they are drafted, and consultations transmit government constraints as reliably as Sangh assessments. The second is redundancy. No row depends on a single occupant: the Sarkaryavah changed in 2021, the bridge post changed hands in 2019, the party presidency changed in 2020, and the conclave calendar survived each change without a missed cycle. The third is publicity. The architecture's major acts, resolutions, addresses, conclaves, appointments, are performed in the open and reported in the press, which distinguishes the post-2014 system from the informal soundings of earlier phases and makes the coordination reading, unlike its rivals, an account of evidence that can be checked against dates. A reading of the dyad that cannot be tabulated against the calendar is a metaphor. The coordination reading can, and Table 1 is the tabulation.

The map is offered with a citation discipline attached. Each cell of Table 1 is sourced to a named, dated exhibit in the sections around it: the mechanisms column to the bodies' own published constitutions, calendars and office lists; the examples column to the documented episodes the brief for this article mandates, from the Delhi conclave of September 2015 to the Palakkad conclave of September 2024; the evolution column to the transitions the record actually contains, the bridge office's change of occupant in July 2019, the Sarkaryavah's change in March 2021, the architecture's adaptation to coalition outcomes in 2024. A reader who disputes the synthesis can attack it cell by cell, substituting a better-documented example or contesting a mechanism's description, without dissolving the whole into competing impressions of intimacy and distance. That is the sense in which the map, rather than either rival reading, is the citable object: it converts an argument about atmospherics into an argument about exhibits, and it leaves its gaps visible, in the unminuted apex consultations and the closed coordination records, where further evidence would have to land.

## Earlier Configurations of the Same Dyad

The architecture's novelty appears only against the configurations it replaced, and the comparison runs through four distinct arrangements. The first, from 1951 to 1977, joined the Sangh to the Bharatiya Jana Sangh through [the pre-1977 RSS-Jana Sangh relationship comparator](/2019/09/24/bharatiya-jana-sangh-phase-1951-1977/): cadre-electoral separation as a matter of doctrine, with the Sangh formally outside electoral politics and the party formally autonomous, connected by loaned pracharaks of the calibre of Deendayal Upadhyay, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Nanaji Deshmukh, and by consultation that was personal and episodic rather than calendared. The arrangement's informality was its weakness. When the Janata Party, into which the Jana Sangh had merged in 1977, demanded in 1979 and 1980 that its ex-Jana Sangh members sever their Sangh membership, the dyad had no standing machinery in which to negotiate the dual-membership question, and the dispute broke the governing party and propelled the ex-Jana Sangh leadership into founding the BJP in April 1980.

The texture of that first configuration explains its brittleness. The Sangh's capital with the Indian state was civic before it was electoral: its volunteers' traffic and supply work in Delhi during the war of 1962 earned an invitation for a Sangh contingent to march in the Republic Day parade of 1963, an honour Jawaharlal Nehru's government extended and the Sangh's memory still cites as recognition earned outside politics. The Jana Sangh, meanwhile, entered coalition governments in the states after the 1967 elections, where its ministers governed beside socialists and Congress dissidents, and the dyad's dealings ran through individuals, Deendayal Upadhyaya above all, rather than through standing machinery. When the Emergency of 1975 banned the Sangh and jailed its leadership, the underground resistance the swayamsevaks sustained, documented in the memoir literature of the period and acknowledged across the political spectrum after 1977, fused the two bodies' cadres in shared danger; the Janata merger of 1977 was the political form of that fusion, and the dual-membership crisis of 1979 was its price. The lesson the dyad carried forward was not that coordination had failed. It was that coordination without a constitutionally separate party and an acknowledged machinery between the two bodies collapsed the moment a third party demanded to see the join.

The second configuration, from 1980 to 1989, is documented in [the 1980 BJP founding's RSS-organisational-support context](/2019/10/05/1980-founding-bjp-explained/): the Sangh supplied organisational depth, cadres and credibility to a party that, under Vajpayee's presidency and the banner of Gandhian socialism, guarded its electoral-strategic autonomy jealously and kept the ideological parent at a deliberate distance. The party's two seats in the 1984 general election were won inside this configuration, and the Sangh's response to the disaster is the period's most instructive datum: it neither withdrew the deputed cadres nor imposed a line, and the party's turn toward the Ayodhya movement after 1989 was argued out between the two bodies rather than dictated by either.

The third configuration, from 1989 to 1996, ran the coordination through a mediator. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad, which owned the Ram Janmabhoomi campaign, set the movement's calendar, from the Rath Yatra of 1990 to the demolition of 6 December 1992, and the Sangh and the BJP each coordinated with the campaign rather than primarily with each other. [The post-1992 institutional-electoral phase context](/2019/10/16/1992-babri-demolition-politics/) marks the turn: after the demolition and the dismissal of the BJP's state governments, the party's incentives pointed toward parliamentary respectability and coalition-building, while the movement organisations' incentives pointed toward the temple, and the mediated configuration gave way to a bilateral one in which the frictions were direct.

The phase's mechanics rewarded the mediator and taxed the party. When the V. P. Singh government fell in November 1990 after the BJP withdrew its support over Advani's arrest during the Rath Yatra, the dyad's public lesson was that the movement's timetable could terminate a national government the party had sustained; when the demolition of 6 December 1992 destroyed the movement's own discipline in an afternoon, the Sangh's leadership faced the inverse lesson, that the electoral organisation would carry the legal and political consequences, the dismissal of four state governments and a fresh ban on the Sangh itself, for an act its kar sevaks had performed. The BJP's repositioning under Advani and then Atal Bihari Vajpayee across the middle 1990s, the moderation of its platform into the coalitionable programme of 1996 and 1998, was argued inside the Parivar as the party's unilateral revision of a shared agenda, and the argument was never formally closed; it was overtaken by office. That unresolved quality is the phase's bequest to the post-2014 architecture, whose procedures can be read as the dyad's determination, learned across 1989 to 1996, never again to let a disagreement over means accumulate without a forum, a calendar and a published text in which it could be priced.

The fourth configuration, from 1996 to 2014, is the coalition phase, and it is the post-2014 architecture's immediate ancestor and clearest contrast. The Sangh's influence on candidate selection and ideological positioning was substantial and acknowledged; the 1998 to 2004 Vajpayee government governed with Sangh deputed organisers throughout the party. Yet the operating system remained personal, resting on the Vajpayee-Advani leadership's equations with the Sarsanghchalak of the day, and it produced friction it could not process. K. S. Sudarshan, Sarsanghchalak from March 2000, publicly criticised the economic direction of the government and the influence of the Prime Minister's Office in terms that made the newspapers of 2000 and 2001; the Swadeshi Jagran Manch campaigned against the government's liberalisation from inside the Parivar; and in April 2005 Sudarshan used a television interview to suggest that both Vajpayee and Advani should make way for younger leaders, an intervention delivered through the media because no conclave existed in which to deliver it privately. The coalition phase thus exhibited influence without procedure: inputs arrived as public pressure, outputs as public defiance, and the dyad's business was transacted in headlines. The post-2014 settlement differs from all four configurations in the same respect. It kept the deputation system of the first, the party autonomy claims of the second, the movement inheritance of the third and the candidate-selection traffic of the fourth, and it wrapped the whole in standing procedure, the calendar of resolutions, conclaves and consultations mapped in Table 1.

The coalition phase's signature episode shows what processing disagreement looked like before the machinery existed. In June 2005, L. K. Advani, visiting Karachi, praised Muhammad Ali Jinnah's speech of 11 August 1947 as a charter of a secular state, intending, by his later account, a statesman's opening toward Pakistan. The Sangh's leadership signalled its displeasure through intermediaries and friendly newspapers; Advani offered his resignation as party president, withdrew it under pressure from colleagues, and finally demitted the office in December 2005, with the argument running in public for six months because no conclave, no consultation calendar and no acknowledged channel existed in which it could be closed. The party's president had been, in effect, removed by a frown transmitted through the press, and the Sangh's role was simultaneously undeniable and undocumented, the precise combination that taught a generation of the dyad's managers the value of procedure. When the post-2014 architecture routed its far larger disagreements, on reservation, on cow protection, on farm prices, through conclaves, clarifications and published addresses, it was building, whether by design or by accumulated scar tissue, the machinery whose absence the summer of 2005 had advertised.

## Disagreement Inside the Architecture

A coordination system is tested not by agreement but by the processing of disagreement, and the decade supplies four documented episodes in which the dyad's positions diverged in public and were then managed without rupture. The first and most instructive is the reservation episode of 2015. In an interview published in the Sangh's organs *Organiser* and *Panchjanya* in September 2015, weeks before the Bihar assembly election, Bhagwat proposed that an apolitical committee examine the reservation system, who benefited from it and for how long it should run. The Rashtriya Janata Dal and the Janata Dal (United) converted the remark into the election's central charge, that a BJP victory meant the end of quotas. The BJP issued a formal statement that it was not in favour of any reconsideration of the constitutional provisions for reservation, a public distance between party and Sarsanghchalak without precedent in the period. The National Democratic Alliance lost Bihar heavily on 8 November 2015. The management of the episode then ran through the architecture's own channels: Shah's call on Bhagwat at Nagpur on 9 November, the party's official post-mortem, in which Arun Jaitley attributed the defeat to the opposition's superior vote transfer and not to the remark, even as the BJP's Bihar MP Hukumdev Narayan Yadav said publicly that the remark had agitated backward-caste voters, and finally Bhagwat's own statement at Nagpur on 17 December 2015 that the Sangh saw no question of scrapping reservations and that they should continue as long as social discrimination prevailed. The disagreement was real, the electoral damage was real, and the repair was executed by clarification inside the system rather than by either side's submission.

The second episode concerns cow protection. Modi's statements of 6 and 7 August 2016, condemning the majority of self-described cow protectors as anti-social and identifying the Prime Minister with the Dalit victims of vigilante violence, struck at a constituency the Vishwa Hindu Parishad claimed as its own, and the VHP's leaders said so in print, a Vishwa Hindu Parishad office-bearer from Uttar Pradesh calling the statement an insult to those who had sacrificed for cow protection. The Sangh's own response was calibrated to hold the architecture together: its general secretary Suresh Bhaiyyaji Joshi condemned the attacks on Dalits, and Bhagwat's Vijayadashami address of 11 October 2016 separated genuine cow protectors, whom it praised, from law-breakers, whom it condemned, giving the government its crackdown and the movement its dignity in a single formulation. The third episode is economic. The Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, the Parivar's trade union, withheld its participation from the all-India general strike of 2 September 2015 after the coordination conclave's exchanges, a dividend of the consultation system; the same union spent much of the following decade opposing the government's strategic disinvestment programme in terms its leadership did not soften for the government's convenience, and the Swadeshi Jagran Manch campaigned openly against the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, from which India withdrew in November 2019, an outcome the Manch claimed and the government described as a sovereign trade judgement arrived at independently.

The fourth episode, the farm laws of 2020 and 2021, shows the architecture's limits as clearly as its strengths. The Bharatiya Kisan Sangh had consulted its village committees, some fifteen thousand of them by its general secretary Badrinarayan Chaudhary's account, and sent its suggestions to the Agriculture Ministry before the bills were drafted; the enacted laws ignored the core of the advice, and the Kisan Sangh said so, complaining that ministry bureaucrats had prevailed over the consultation. From September 2020 the Kisan Sangh demanded publicly that any new settlement guarantee remunerative prices, in the mandis and in the open market alike, and it held a nationwide protest on 8 September 2021 to press the demand, even as it refused to join the Samyukt Kisan Morcha's agitation and condemned the use of force against farmers. The government's repeal of the three laws, announced on 19 November 2021, answered the street agitation and the electoral calendar of Punjab and Uttar Pradesh. The Kisan Sangh's statutory price guarantee remained unconceded. Inside the architecture, an affiliate had been heard, partially answered and partially refused, and it stayed at the table: the episode is the autonomy reading's best evidence and the subordination reading's worst, and for the coordination reading it is the normal operation of a system built to process exactly this.

A fifth disagreement, over sequence rather than substance, ran through the decade's middle. The temple at Ayodhya was the Parivar's oldest shared commitment, and the organisations diverged on method: the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's dharma sansads, culminating in the Allahabad gathering of January and February 2019, demanded legislation and set the government deadlines in public, while the government held to the judicial route and declined to bring the ordinance the movement demanded. Bhagwat's Vijayadashami address of October 2018 had itself urged the government to clear the way for the temple through law, which placed the Sarsanghchalak's public preference on the record against the government's chosen method. The Supreme Court's judgment of 9 November 2019 resolved the title dispute, the trust route followed, and the consecration of 22 January 2024 closed the question on the judiciary's timetable, not the movement's. The episode refines both rival readings: the ideological principal had stated its preferred instrument in its most public text, and the government had declined the instrument while delivering the end, an outcome the subordination reading cannot process and the autonomy reading cannot price, since the end itself had been carried on the agenda for three decades by the principal that failed to choose the means.

A sixth field of divergence, economic regulation, shows disagreement becoming a standing condition rather than an episode. Bhagwat's call at Nagpur on 5 October 2022 for a comprehensive population policy met a government whose health ministry had told Parliament that year that no population-control legislation was contemplated, and neither side revised its position in public through the remainder of the period. The Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh welcomed the Code on Wages of 2019 and recorded objections to clauses of the Industrial Relations Code of 2020, including the higher threshold for the application of standing orders, and the labour codes' full implementation remained pending through the decade, a delay the union's leadership claimed as evidence that consultation worked and labour scholars attributed in part to the cost of overriding the Parivar's own union. In each case the pattern of the earlier episodes repeats with lower drama: a position taken in the open by an affiliate or by the Sarsanghchalak, a government that concedes in part, refuses in part and schedules the remainder, and a relationship that emerges from the exchange with its machinery intact and its divergences on the record.

## The Personality Complication

The strongest objection to the coordination reading is biographical. The architecture's smooth operation between 2014 and 2024 coincided with a singular overlap: a Sarsanghchalak, Bhagwat, in office from 2009 and committed to public engagement, and a Prime Minister who was the deputation system's most successful product, fluent in the Sangh's idiom and trusted by its cadres as one of their own. On the objection, the mechanisms tabulated in Table 1 are stage machinery that two particular men happened to operate well, and the architecture will lapse into subordination or estrangement when successors without their shared formation inherit it. The objection deserves its strength. [The personality-as-institution framework](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) warns against mistaking a durable arrangement for the person who animates it, and Indian political history is littered with institutions that proved to be one leader's habits wearing a constitution's clothes.

The complication has a structural form that the biographical version understates. The two principals of the dyad hold office on incommensurable clocks. The Sarsanghchalak's office carries no term prescribed by the Sangh's constitution: M. S. Golwalkar held it for thirty-three years, and Bhagwat entered his second decade in the chair in 2019, answerable to no electorate and removable by no calendar. The party's leaders face the opposite discipline, general elections at five-year intervals, assembly verdicts several times a year, and internal presidential terms of three years. The architecture's operation bears the marks of that asymmetry throughout. The Sangh can afford public patience, waiting out the farm-law year without escalating its farmers' affiliate's protest into a confrontation, because its principal's horizon runs in decades. The party cannot, and its responsiveness to electoral signals, the repeal of November 2021, the course corrections after June 2024, repeatedly outruns the Sangh's preferred tempo. The succession problem is asymmetric in the same way. The Sangh's bench is ordered and visible, the Sarkaryavah line supplying each Sarsanghchalak of the modern period, while the party's post-Modi leadership is an open question the architecture has no procedure to settle, since the one input it cannot generate from within is a prime minister.

The reply distinguishes activation from availability. The mechanisms predate the pair. The deputation system ran through the century's turn and supplied the party's organisers through its weakest decades. The Pratinidhi Sabha's March cycle and the Vijayadashami address are older than both men's careers. The Samanvay Baithak met before 2014 in smaller form and would meet under any leadership because the affiliates' need to align calendars does not depend on who holds which office. What Modi and Bhagwat supplied was activation at high intensity: a prime minister who attended the conclave in person, a Sarsanghchalak who used prime-time instruments, the lecture series, the televised address, to address audiences beyond the cadre. The test of the reply came in instalments inside the period itself. The bridge post changed hands in July 2019, mid-term, without a tremor in the traffic it carries. The Sarkaryavah changed in March 2021, Joshi to Hosabale, and the consultation cadence continued through a pandemic and a general election without a published hitch. The party presidency changed in January 2020, Shah to Nadda, and the new president, whose formation ran through the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad rather than the pracharak line, operated the same machinery, sat in the same conclaves and, in May 2024, spoke the autonomy language from inside the architecture rather than against it.

A residue of the objection survives the reply, and the coordination reading is obliged to carry it as a stated limit. The apex consultations, the Modi-Bhagwat channel, are personal by design, unminuted and unreplicated; no table can show what a successor pair would make of them. The honest verdict is disaggregated: the architecture's first two dimensions, inputs and personnel, are institutional in the strong sense, staffed, calendared and documented; its third dimension is institutional in form and personal in operation at the top. The claim defended in this article is sized accordingly. The post-2014 relationship operates through coordination architecture. Whether the architecture's apex survives the departure of the two men who built its habits is a question the record of 2014 to 2024 cannot answer, and the events of 2024, examined next, supply the nearest thing to a stress test the period affords.

## The Stress Test of 2024

The general election of 2024 subjected the architecture to its first adverse result. The BJP, which had won 282 seats in 2014 and 303 in 2019, won 240 on 4 June 2024, short of a majority, and the government formed on 9 June rested on the National Democratic Alliance's 293 seats and the support of coalition partners including the Telugu Desam Party and the Janata Dal (United). The campaign had carried visible strain inside the dyad. Nadda's May 2024 statement of party capability was delivered mid-campaign and was read across the political press as a declaration of independence; correspondents in state after state reported Sangh cadres campaigning at reduced intensity in the early phases, and in Uttar Pradesh, where the party's tally fell from 62 seats in 2019 to 33, the shortfall was widely attributed in post-poll analyses, a disputed attribution, to some combination of candidate selection, caste arithmetic and cadre coolness.

### Why did the BJP fall short of a majority in 2024?

Post-verdict analyses by the Election Commission's data and by party and Sangh reviewers converge on no single cause: anti-incumbency in Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh, caste consolidation behind the opposition in the Hindi belt, candidate-selection grievances, and, in the Sangh's own internal reading, uneven cadre mobilisation in the early phases all appear in the record, with their relative weights disputed.

The architecture's response to the verdict is the decade's most valuable evidence, because adversity separates procedure from habit. Six days after the results, on 10 June 2024, Bhagwat addressed the closing of a Sangh training camp at Reshimbagh in Nagpur. The address praised the outgoing government's decade on the economy, science and education, and then spoke the language of correction in public: a true sevak, a servant, works without arrogance; the decorum of the campaign season had been breached by both camps; the opposition was a pratipaksh, an alternative side to be heard, not an enemy; and Manipur, burning since May 2023, had waited a year for peace and had to be given priority. Opposition leaders quoted the speech against the government within hours, Congress figures urging the Prime Minister to heed his own ideological house. The Sangh issued no clarification and withdrew nothing. In the coordination reading, the address was Dimension One operating in coalition conditions: a strategic-ideological input, delivered publicly, impossible to file quietly, and timed to the moment when the party's dependence on allies had raised the price of every signal.

The procedural dimension answered in its own register. The Akhil Bharatiya Samanvay Baithak convened at Palakkad in Kerala from 31 August to 2 September 2024, the conclave's first sitting in that state, with Bhagwat and Hosabale presiding, Nadda attending for the BJP among the representatives of thirty-two allied organisations, and the Lok Sabha results on the agenda beside the Sangh's centenary programme. No communiqué announced a reconciliation, because the architecture does not recognise rupture as its members' public category; the conclave simply met, on the calendar, with the personnel row of Table 1 fully populated and the consultation row fully in session.

### What did the RSS say about a caste census at Palakkad?

At the Palakkad conclave's closing briefing on 2 September 2024, the Sangh's publicity chief Sunil Ambekar said the organisation had no objection to a caste census conducted for the welfare of society, while warning that caste enumeration must not be used as a political instrument in elections, a formulation that left the government's options open and the Sangh's reservations recorded.

The outputs followed through the autumn. In Haryana, where assembly elections were held in October 2024, the BJP won a third consecutive term with 48 seats against the run of published expectations, and election reporting across outlets credited the Sangh's ground machinery, its network of small meetings and door-to-door contact by swayamsevaks, with a mobilisation visibly more complete than the parliamentary campaign's in the state. In Maharashtra in November 2024, the ruling alliance's sweep was likewise accompanied by reporting on the ground campaigns of Sangh-inspired organisations among first-time and women voters. The attributions are the press's, and their precision is arguable; the pattern they describe is not. A party in a subordination relationship does not receive rescue from a headquarters it has supposedly escaped, and a headquarters in command does not wait for an election to remind its instrument of humility. What the autumn of 2024 displayed was the architecture adapting to coalition arithmetic by raising its visibility: the inputs grew more public, the conclaves grew more prominent, and the personnel machine worked the ground harder, each dimension compensating in the open for the loss of a single-party majority.

The consultation row worked through the summer in its quieter register as well. Through July 2024, national newspapers reported a sequence of sittings in Delhi between senior Sangh functionaries and the BJP's leadership at which the campaign's frictions, candidate selection, cadre morale and the Nadda interview among them, were reviewed constituency by constituency; neither organisation published an account, and neither denied the reports. In the same month Bhagwat, travelling in Uttar Pradesh, met Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath at Gorakhpur, an encounter the political press read as a signal that the architecture's state-level channels were open to a chief minister whose relations with the party's central leadership had been the subject of sustained speculation. The readings are the press's; the meetings are matters of report that neither side corrected. What can be stated without inference is the calendar: within eight weeks of the verdict, the dyad's inputs had been delivered in public at Nagpur, its consultations had been reported in the capital and in Lucknow's orbit, and its annual conclave had been convened at Palakkad with the results on the agenda, the full cycle of Table 1 executed under adversity on schedule.

The autumn's second act belonged to the Sangh's own calendar. The organisation entered its centenary year at Vijayadashami on 12 October 2024, and Bhagwat's address at Nagpur ranged across the year's anxieties, condemning the violence against Hindus in Bangladesh after the fall of the Hasina government in August, returning to the theme of societal unity, and framing the centenary as an exercise in national consolidation rather than organisational celebration. In Maharashtra the following month, the ruling Mahayuti alliance won 235 of the assembly's 288 seats, the BJP itself taking 132, a recovery in the state where the parliamentary verdict had been among the party's worst; election reporting again credited the ground campaigns run by Sangh-inspired organisations, including door-to-door contact programmes among women and first-time voters, with a share of the recovery. The credit is an attribution, and the counterfactual is unknowable. The structural point stands independently of it: a movement in possession of its own centenary programme, its own resolution cycle and its own ground machinery chose, in the months after its supposed instrument's worst result in a decade, to spend that machinery on the instrument's restoration, and to be seen spending it.

One boundary of the architecture emerged from the 2024 verdict that no earlier section of this article had occasion to mark, because the decade had concealed it. The government formed on 9 June 2024 depends on allies, the Telugu Desam Party and the Janata Dal (United) principally, with which the Sangh has no deputation system, no shared formation and no coordination calendar; the architecture's writ, which runs the length of the BJP, stops at the BJP's boundary. Coalition management in the third Modi government is therefore conducted by instruments the dyad does not supply, parliamentary arithmetic and ministerial allocation, and the Sangh's public restraint through the government's first budget and its first parliamentary sessions, its silence on portfolio questions and on the allies' regional demands, is consistent with an organisation that knows where its machinery ends. The limit matters for the synthesis: the coordination architecture is a theory of one party and one movement, not of Indian governance entire, and its predictive reach contracts exactly where the BJP's single-party reach contracts, a symmetry the rival readings, which treat the dyad as a theory of the state itself, are less equipped to state.

The personnel dimension supplied the autumn's closing exhibit. The Maharashtra government formed on 5 December 2024 placed the chief ministership with Devendra Fadnavis, a leader whose formation ran through the Sangh's shakhas and its student organisation in Nagpur, the city where the dyad began, and whose return to the office after the parliamentary setback was managed through the party's legislative process with the Sangh's provincial machinery visibly engaged in the campaign that preceded it. No conclave minute records the choice, because none was needed: the channel's product is not instructions for such occasions but a bench, a pipeline of leaders whose formation the movement owns and whose advancement the party decides, from which the party can draw in a crisis without negotiation. The bench is the personnel dimension's deepest answer to the autonomy reading, and its existence is the fact about the dyad that a change of prime minister, whenever it comes, will test least and reveal most.

## Adjudication: Coordination as the Citable Reading

The named disagreement can be settled on the evidence assembled. The subordination reading, in its scholarly form in Jaffrelot's *Modi's India* (2021) and its political form in the opposition's remote-control vocabulary, establishes penetration: Sangh frameworks in the party's texts, Sangh-formed personnel in the party's spine, Sangh commentary on the government's agenda. It fails on the outputs. A commanded party does not leave its commander's farmers' union protesting in five hundred districts, does not repeal flagship legislation on its own electoral calculations, and does not have its president announce its self-sufficiency in a national newspaper without consequence. The autonomy reading, in its scholarly form in Mehta's accounts of institutional centralisation and its journalistic form in the strongman literature, establishes concentration: a Prime Minister's Office of unusual command, a party machine rebuilt around two leaders, a government capable of public defiance of Parivar sentiment on cow protection and of silence toward Parivar preferences on farm prices. It fails on the inputs. A self-sufficient party does not staff its organisational spine from another body's full-timers for forty consecutive years, does not send its president and its ministers to the other body's conclaves, and does not watch its electoral fate turn, in state after state, on the other body's ground machinery.

Pratap Bhanu Mehta's post-2014 essays pose the sharpest version of the challenge the synthesis must absorb, because Mehta does not deny the dyad's intimacy; he reprices it. In his columns through the decade, the period's defining process is the centralisation of the state in a single executive office and the conversion of the BJP into an electoral machine of unprecedented reach, and a movement that supplies such a machine with cadres and doctrine has, on his account, traded its civilisational project for proximity to power whose terms it no longer sets. The coordination reading accepts Mehta's centralisation evidence in full and disputes his inference from it. Centralisation inside the government and subordination between the party and the movement are different processes with different exhibits: the first is documented in the Prime Minister's Office's staffing, the cabinet's working methods and the governor's offices; the second would require the command traces whose absence across a decade of conclaves, clarifications and refusals this article has recorded. A machine can centralise its own operations while remaining one organisation among two, and the Sangh's decade, its resolutions unanswered in part, its affiliates refused in part, its inputs priced annually, is the biography of the second organisation in that arrangement, diminished in some exchanges and decisive in others, which is what coordination between unequal partners looks like when it is working.

The coordination-architecture reading incorporates both dossiers and explains the residue each leaves. It reads the dyad as two organisations with separate constitutions, separate mandates and a shared formation, joined by three standing mechanisms that move ideology, people and consultation between them continuously: the resolution-and-address channel of Dimension One, the deputation-and-recall channel of Dimension Two, and the conclave-and-consultation channel of Dimension Three, tabulated as Table 1. The reading is offered with its falsification conditions attached. It would be damaged by evidence that conclaves had ceased to meet or that the bridge post had been civilianised, filled by a party careerist with no Sangh accountability; it would be destroyed by evidence of a written command chain, Sangh resolutions annotated as instructions in government files, or of a clean organisational separation, the deputation system abolished and the calendars decoupled. None of that evidence exists in the record of 2014 to 2024. What exists is a decade of meetings held, officers rotated, resolutions priced, disagreements processed and one verdict absorbed, the biography of an architecture rather than of a hierarchy or a divorce.

The synthesis carries expectations by which the record beyond the decade can judge it, and they are entered here so that the map's failure, if it comes, will be visible as failure. If the coordination reading is correct, the conclave calendar will continue to meet on schedule through the next change of party president and the next Sarkaryavah election, which falls due at the Pratinidhi Sabha of March 2027 under the office's three-year cycle. The bridge office will continue to be filled by a pracharak whose accountability runs to Nagpur, or its civilianisation will be announced as the deliberate constitutional change it would be. The Sarsanghchalak's Vijayadashami address will continue to contain at least one formulation the government of the day must price in public, since an input channel that delivers only applause has ceased to be an input. And the dyad's next public disagreement, on whatever subject, will be processed in the open, by clarification and conclave, rather than by purge or by schism. A decade's record cannot certify any of this. It can certify that between 2014 and 2024 every one of these expectations was met in every year, including the year the votes ran out.

The map's comparative value can be stated against the nearest structural parallel in democratic politics. South Africa's tripartite alliance joins a liberation movement turned ruling party, a communist party that contests elections only under the movement's banner, and a trade union federation, through standing alliance summits and overlapping leaderships; scholars of that system long ago abandoned the vocabulary of remote control for the vocabulary of alliance management, because the exhibits, summits held, cadres deployed, policies priced and occasionally refused, would not fit the simpler metaphor. The Indian dyad differs in the decisive particular that its movement contests nothing and its party everything, which makes the traffic between them purer: no shared ticket blurs the account of who supplies ideology, who supplies personnel and who answers to the voter. Read against the comparative literature, the post-2014 RSS-BJP relationship is not an anomaly requiring a theory of hidden command. It is the limiting case of a known form, the movement-party dyad, operating under single-party majority conditions and therefore visible, for one decade, without the static that coalition politics usually adds.

Two limits stand on the record and are carried here as findings rather than footnotes. The first is asymmetry of documentation: the party's texts are published, the government's files will open in time, and the Sangh's coordination records, the Sarkaryavah's office papers at Nagpur and Keshav Kunj, remain closed, so every account of the dyad, this one included, is built disproportionately from the visible surfaces of a system whose paper centre is unexamined. The second is the succession question isolated earlier: the architecture's apex channel is personal in operation, and its behaviour under a post-Modi, post-Bhagwat pairing is a prediction, not a finding. The comparative promise of the map lies in the pillars that follow this one, where the Congress party's relations with its own movements and the regional parties' relations with theirs can be tabulated on the same three rows and the Indian pattern, in which party and movement are distinct bodies joined by machinery, can be separated from the Indian habit of describing every such machine as a remote control. For the post-2014 decade, the verdict of the record is the namable claim with which the pillar closes: the post-2014 RSS-BJP relationship operates through coordination architecture, and the subordination and autonomy framings are inadequate to its institutional mechanisms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the relationship between RSS and BJP?

The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh is a cultural and social organisation founded at Nagpur in 1925; the Bharatiya Janata Party is a political party founded in 1980 whose founding leadership came largely from the Sangh's Jana Sangh tradition. The two are separate bodies with separate constitutions and separate finances, joined by a shared ideology, a shared pool of workers and standing mechanisms of coordination: Sangh resolutions supply ideological frameworks, pracharaks on deputation staff the party's organisational posts, and annual conclaves align the two bodies. Scholars describe the party variously as the Sangh's political arm, affiliate or partner. The organisations themselves describe the tie as familyhood between independent bodies, and the post-2014 record of cooperation punctuated by public disagreement supports that description better than either command or estrangement.

### Q: Who is Mohan Bhagwat?

Mohan Bhagwat, born on 11 September 1950 at Chandrapur in Maharashtra, is the sixth Sarsanghchalak, or chief, of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, an office he has held since 21 March 2009, when he succeeded K. S. Sudarshan. Trained as a veterinary graduate, he became a pracharak, served as the Sangh's Sarkaryavah from 2000 to 2009, and rose through the organisation's executive ranks. His tenure has been marked by an unusual degree of public engagement for the office: televised Vijayadashami addresses at Nagpur, the three-day Bhavishya ka Bharat lecture series at Delhi in September 2018, and public interventions on questions from reservation to the situation in Manipur, each of which the BJP and the government of the day have had to price into their calculations.

### Q: Does the RSS control the BJP?

No documentary evidence of a command chain has entered the public record: no Sangh resolution binds a BJP legislator, no government file carries a Sangh instruction, and both organisations deny the existence of control, Bhagwat stating at Delhi in September 2018 that the Sangh advises and the decisions belong to the organisations and governments concerned. The opposition's contrary claim, voiced by the Communist Party of India (Marxist) in September 2015 in the language of direct control, rests on real surface evidence, ministers at conclaves and pracharaks in party offices, read as hierarchy. The record assembled in this article supports a third description, coordination through standing mechanisms, which explains both the visible intimacy and the documented occasions on which the party declined the Sangh affiliates' demands.

### Q: How is the post-2014 RSS-BJP relationship different?

Earlier configurations of the dyad ran on personality and episode: loaned organisers and private equations in the Jana Sangh decades, mediated coordination through the Ayodhya movement between 1989 and 1996, and public friction without processing machinery during the coalition years from 1996 to 2014, when Sarsanghchalak K. S. Sudarshan criticised the Vajpayee government through television interviews. The post-2014 relationship regularised the contact into procedure. A Prime Minister attended a coordination conclave in September 2015. Resolutions, conclaves and consultations acquired a fixed calendar. The Sarsanghchalak's inputs became prime-time public events. And the relationship survived its first adverse election in 2024 by intensifying its visible machinery rather than by either side withdrawing, the adaptation that distinguishes an architecture from an arrangement of convenience.

### Q: What are RSS-BJP coordination committees?

Coordination committees are the standing forums, formal and informal, through which the Sangh, the BJP and the allied organisations of the Sangh Parivar exchange assessments and align their calendars. At the national level the type specimen is the coordination conclave, institutionalised as the annual Akhil Bharatiya Samanvay Baithak, at which the BJP sits as one organisation among more than thirty; the three-day Delhi conclave of September 2015, attended by the Prime Minister and Union ministers, displayed the format to the country. At the state level, bilateral meetings between the Sangh's provincial leadership and the BJP's state unit perform the same function between conclaves. The committees have no constitutional status, issue no binding decisions and publish no minutes; their product is alignment, and their sanction is the shared cadre's morale.

### Q: Did Modi rise through the RSS?

Yes, through the precise mechanism this article describes. Narendra Modi joined the Sangh as a young swayamsevak in Gujarat, became a full-time worker in 1971 and a pracharak, worked underground during the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, and was assigned by the Sangh to the Bharatiya Janata Party in 1985. Inside the party he rose through its organisational rather than its parliamentary wing: organising secretary of the Gujarat unit from 1987, national secretary in 1995, and national general secretary (organisation) in May 1998, the bridge office between the two bodies. He became Chief Minister of Gujarat in October 2001 through the party leadership's decision, led the state for thirteen years, and was named the party's prime ministerial candidate in September 2013. His formation is the deputation system's most consequential product.

### Q: How does the RSS influence government policy?

The Sangh's influence on policy travels through the architecture's three channels rather than through directives. Its resolutions and the Sarsanghchalak's addresses set the ideological agenda, which is how commitments carried for decades, the abrogation of Article 370 in August 2019 and the Citizenship Amendment Act in December 2019 among them, reach the statute book when parliamentary arithmetic permits. Its deputed personnel staff the party machinery that selects candidates and frames campaigns. Its consultations give it a hearing before major decisions. The limits are equally documented: the farm laws were enacted against its farmers' affiliate's advice in 2020, the statutory price guarantee that affiliate demanded was refused, and disinvestment proceeded against its trade union's opposition. Influence of this kind is agenda-setting and personnel supply, and it is real without being command.

### Q: What is the Akhil Bharatiya Pratinidhi Sabha and what does it do?

The Akhil Bharatiya Pratinidhi Sabha is the apex representative body of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, comprising roughly fifteen hundred elected and nominated delegates from across the country. It meets each March, reviews the organisation's annual report, debates and passes resolutions stating the Sangh's position on the questions of the year, and every three years elects the Sarkaryavah, the general secretary who runs the organisation. Its recent sittings mark the coordination calendar's fixed points: Bengaluru in March 2021, which elected Dattatreya Hosabale; Samalkha in Panipat district in March 2023, which passed the resolution on national resurgence based on Swa, selfhood; and Nagpur in March 2024, which re-elected Hosabale for a second term weeks before the general election.

### Q: Who is B. L. Santhosh, and why did his 2019 appointment matter?

B. L. Santhosh is a chemical engineer from Karnataka who became a full-time pracharak of the Sangh in 1993, served as the BJP's organising secretary in Karnataka from 2006, and from 2014 was the party's national joint general secretary (organisation) with charge of the southern states. On 14 July 2019 the party president Amit Shah elevated him to national general secretary (organisation), in succession to Ram Lal, who returned to the Sangh after thirteen years in the post. The appointment mattered because the office is the hinge of the personnel dimension of the RSS-BJP relationship: its occupant supervises the party's organisational appointments, training and candidate-selection groundwork and carries the daily traffic between the party headquarters and the Sangh. His elevation signalled that the deputation system would continue unchanged into the government's second term.

### Q: How are BJP candidates selected, and what part does the RSS play?

Formally, BJP candidates are chosen by the party's Central Election Committee from panels prepared by state election committees, informed by commissioned surveys and by the assessments of the party's organisational machinery. The general secretary (organisation) and the state organising secretaries, pracharaks on deputation in the great majority of cases, channel ground reports into that process, including feedback gathered through the Sangh's shakha networks, which reach settlements no survey firm covers. The channel's weight is contested inside the party itself: candidate selection in Karnataka in 2018 produced documented friction between the deputed organisers and the state leadership of B. S. Yeddyurappa. The formal decision, and the accountability for it, rest with the party's election committee and its parliamentary board.

### Q: What happened at the September 2015 coordination meeting in Delhi?

Between 2 and 4 September 2015, some ninety-three functionaries of the Sangh and fifteen allied organisations met in Delhi for a three-day coordination conclave. Union ministers including Arun Jaitley, Sushma Swaraj, Rajnath Singh and Manohar Parrikar presented accounts of their ministries; BJP president Amit Shah attended throughout; and Prime Minister Narendra Modi joined the proceedings. The agenda ranged across the census figures on religious demography, the One Rank One Pension demand, the Patidar agitation in Gujarat and the government's land legislation, and the Sangh's spokesperson Manmohan Vaidya described the conclave as part of a routine of interaction among allied organisations. The opposition read the same event as proof of a super government, and the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh's absence from the general strike of 2 September 2015 was widely attributed to the conclave's exchanges.

### Q: Where have the RSS and the BJP publicly disagreed since 2014?

The documented disagreements cluster in four areas. On reservation, Bhagwat's September 2015 proposal for an apolitical review committee forced the party into a public distancing statement during the Bihar election and required a clarifying statement from the Sarsanghchalak that December. On cow protection, the Prime Minister's condemnation of vigilantes in August 2016 angered the Vishwa Hindu Parishad while the Sangh's leadership held a two-sided line. On economic policy, the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh opposed strategic disinvestment and the Swadeshi Jagran Manch campaigned against the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership across the decade. On agriculture, the Bharatiya Kisan Sangh demanded a statutory guarantee of remunerative prices from 2020 onward and protested nationally in September 2021 without obtaining it. Each disagreement was processed without rupture, which is the architecture's defining behaviour.

### Q: Who is Dattatreya Hosabale, and what does the Sarkaryavah do?

Dattatreya Hosabale, born on 1 December 1954 in Shivamogga district of Karnataka, joined the Sangh in 1968, became a full-time worker of the Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad in 1978, served as that organisation's national organising secretary, and was a Sah-Sarkaryavah, joint general secretary, of the RSS from 2009. The Pratinidhi Sabha at Bengaluru elected him Sarkaryavah on 20 March 2021, in succession to Suresh Bhaiyyaji Joshi, and re-elected him at Nagpur in March 2024. The Sarkaryavah is the Sangh's elected chief executive: where the Sarsanghchalak guides, the Sarkaryavah runs the organisation day to day, convenes its executive, administers the deputation of pracharaks to allied bodies, and sits across the table from the BJP's leadership in the coordination system.

### Q: Does the RSS fund the BJP?

No evidence of institutional funding has entered the public record, and the Sangh's office-bearers state the opposite: the RSS finances itself through gurudakshina, the annual unsigned offerings of its swayamsevaks, accepts no institutional grants, and makes no grants to the party. The BJP, a registered political party, raises its funds through donations and subscriptions reported to the Election Commission of India in its audited filings. What passes between the two organisations is not money but labour: the unpaid time of swayamsevaks in party campaigns and the salaries forgone by pracharaks deputed to party posts, a transfer scholars of the dyad treat as the real subsidy, substantial precisely because it never appears in either body's accounts.

### Q: What did Bhagwat argue in his September 2018 Delhi lectures?

Across three days at Vigyan Bhavan, from 17 to 19 September 2018, in a lecture series titled Bhavishya ka Bharat, Bhagwat presented the Sangh's positions to an invited audience of diplomats, academics and journalists, closing with a question-and-answer session. He stated that the Sangh accepted the Constitution of India and the national flag, that its conception of a Hindu Rashtra did not exclude Muslims, and that the Congress party had played a major role in the freedom movement. On the Sangh's internal inheritance, he treated parts of M. S. Golwalkar's Bunch of Thoughts as bound to their time. On the question at the centre of this article, he stated that the Sangh neither ran the government nor controlled the organisations inspired by it: it could offer advice, and decisions belonged to those charged with them.

### Q: What did J. P. Nadda say about the BJP and the RSS in May 2024?

In an interview to The Indian Express published in May 2024, in the middle of the general election campaign, the BJP president J. P. Nadda said that the party of its early years had been less capable and had needed the Sangh's support, whereas the party had grown, had become saksham, capable, and conducted its own affairs, with the Sangh pursuing its own social and cultural work in its own sphere. The statement was read across the political press as the autonomy reading's canonical text, and opposition parties cited it for months as evidence of a rift. The Sangh issued no rebuttal. Four months later Nadda attended the Palakkad coordination conclave as the BJP's representative, seated among the thirty-two allied organisations, a sequence the coordination reading treats as the system's ordinary operation.

### Q: What did Bhagwat say at Nagpur after the 2024 election results?

On 10 June 2024, six days after the results and a day after the new government was sworn in, Bhagwat addressed the closing ceremony of a Sangh training camp at Reshimbagh in Nagpur. He credited the outgoing government with a decade of achievement in the economy, science and education, and then set out correctives in public. A true sevak, he said, works without arrogance; the decorum expected in an election campaign had been breached; the opposition was a pratipaksh, an alternative viewpoint entitled to a hearing, not an enemy; and Manipur, in unrest since May 2023, had waited a year for peace and required priority. Opposition leaders quoted the address against the government within hours. The Sangh clarified nothing and withdrew nothing, and the speech stands as the clearest post-2014 instance of a strategic input delivered through the architecture's public channel.

### Q: Is the BJP formally the political wing of the RSS?

Formally, no. The Bharatiya Janata Party is a separately registered political party with its own constitution, adopted at its founding in April 1980, its own membership, finances and electoral accountability, and it contests elections in its own name. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh is a cultural and social organisation that contests no elections and maintains no political fund. Functionally, the BJP is the electoral organisation of the Sangh Parivar's division of labour, founded by leaders formed in the Sangh, staffed in its organisational spine by Sangh deputed pracharaks, and aligned with the Sangh through the coordination mechanisms this article maps. Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle describe it as the Sangh's political affiliate. The shorthand of a political wing is rejected by both bodies, by the Sangh because it claims no political role, by the party because it claims an independent mandate.

### Q: How did the Sangh affiliates react to the farm laws of 2020?

The Bharatiya Kisan Sangh, the Sangh's farmers' affiliate, had consulted its village committees before the bills were drafted and sent its suggestions to the Agriculture Ministry; its general secretary Badrinarayan Chaudhary stated publicly that the enacted laws ignored the core of that advice and blamed ministry officials for the outcome. From September 2020 the Kisan Sangh, joined by the Swadeshi Jagran Manch, demanded that minimum support prices apply in open-market purchases as well as in the mandis, and that a law guarantee remunerative prices. It refused to join the Samyukt Kisan Morcha's agitation, opposed the use of force against farmers, and held its own nationwide protest across more than five hundred districts on 8 September 2021. The laws were repealed that November. The price guarantee was not conceded.

### Q: Can the RSS-BJP coordination architecture survive a change of leaders?

The mechanisms point one way and the apex channel the other. The resolution cycle, the deputation system and the conclave calendar are institutional in form: offices are elected for fixed three-year terms, and the system absorbed a change of Sarkaryavah in 2021, of the bridge office-holder in 2019 and of the BJP presidency in 2020 without a missed cycle. The dyad's longer history also suggests form outliving occupants, since the Sangh-party tie survived the passage from Jana Sangh to BJP in 1980. The reservation concerns the personal consultations at the top, which are unminuted and depend on the occupants' shared formation, as the Modi-Bhagwat overlap supplied in unusual measure. Scholars divide on the weight of that reservation. The architecture's own claim, entered here as the record supports it, is that the machinery is built to be inherited.
