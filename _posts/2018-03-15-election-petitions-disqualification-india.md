The returning officer has declared the result, the certificate has been issued, and the candidate who lost by a narrow margin is convinced that the count was wrong, that money moved through the constituency in the last week of the campaign, or that the winner was never eligible to sit at all. An election petition is the only door through which any of those complaints can be carried, and it is a door with a clock fitted to it. The Representation of the People Act 1951 reserves the challenge of a declared result to a single proceeding, fixes a filing period of forty five days that no court may extend, demands a standard of pleading and proof closer to a criminal trial than to an ordinary civil suit, and then, having done all of that, allows the proceeding to run for years while the member whose title is in question sits, votes and draws the salary of the office. This article follows the remedy from the moment of declaration to the end of the appeal, and then turns to the second half of its subject: what happens to a seat when its holder is convicted of an offence, when a disqualification question is referred for decision, and when the defect lies not in the election but in the member.

![Blog Image](/assets/images/blog/blog-68.webp)

The structure of the subject matters before any detail, because election law distributes its remedies across three separate tracks that are frequently confused with one another. The first track is the petition itself, governed by Part VI of the Representation of the People Act 1951, through which the election of a returned candidate is called in question before the High Court. The second track is disqualification, governed principally by Chapter III of Part II of the same Act and by Articles 102 and 191 of the Constitution, under which a member who incurs a disqualification loses the seat whether or not any petition was ever filed. The third track is disclosure and prosecution: the affidavit regime that obliges every candidate to place criminal antecedents, assets and liabilities on the public record, and the offence created by Section 125A for a candidate who files a false affidavit or conceals what the law requires to be stated. Each track has its own forum, its own clock and its own consequences, and a grievance taken down the wrong track fails however meritorious it may be. The petitioner who wants a result undone must use the first track within forty five days. The citizen who wants a convicted member out must look to the second. The voter who finds a false affidavit has a complaint under the third, and a petition ground folded inside it, but no direct route to unseat anyone by complaint alone.

## The Exclusive Remedy: One Door, and It Closes Early

Article 329(b) of the Constitution provides that no election to either House of Parliament or to a House of the Legislature of a State shall be called in question except by an election petition presented to such authority and in such manner as may be provided for by or under any law made by the appropriate Legislature. The sentence does two things at once. It withdraws the whole subject of election disputes from the ordinary jurisdiction of the courts acting on their own motion, and it hands the design of the remedy to the legislature, which has exercised the power in the Representation of the People Act 1951. The Act's Section 80 repeats the bar in statutory form: no election shall be called in question except by an election petition presented in accordance with the provisions of Part VI. Between the constitutional sentence and the statutory sentence, the space for any other proceeding disappears. A writ petition under Article 226 asking a High Court to set aside a result fails at the threshold, not because the grievance is small but because the Constitution has specified the exclusive means of raising it.

The Supreme Court gave the bar its enduring shape in N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer, Namakkal, decided in 1952, within the first years of the Republic. The Court held that the word election in Article 329 is used in a wide sense, comprehending the entire process from the notification calling the election to the declaration of the result, and that the bar therefore operates across the whole process and not merely after its conclusion. The practical consequence is the intermediate stage rule: while an election is in progress, the courts will not interrupt it. A candidate aggrieved by the rejection of a nomination paper, by the allotment of a symbol, by the conduct of the poll or by any other step in the sequence must hold the grievance until the process ends, and then carry it into a petition, where it becomes one of the grounds on which the finished election may be attacked. The design is deliberate. An election is a collective act performed on a fixed timetable by an entire constituency machinery, and a legal system that permitted interruption at every stage would make the timetable itself impossible. The price of an uninterrupted process is that every error, however obvious, must wait for a single post declaration proceeding in which the whole result stands or falls together. Readers who want the constitutional scheme around the petition procedure should work through [the complete guide to election law in India](/2017/12/01/election-law-india-complete-guide/), which sets the petition inside the wider architecture of Articles 324 to 329.

The exclusivity has a second, quieter consequence that petitioners discover too late. Because the petition is the only remedy, everything the petitioner wants must be claimed inside it, in the form the Act prescribes, within the period the Act allows. There is no follow up proceeding in which a ground omitted from the petition can be raised separately, no second petition by another elector on a fresh ground once the period has run, and no collateral attack on the result in a civil suit, a service dispute or a prosecution. A corrupt practice that could have been pleaded but was not is, for the purpose of unseating the returned candidate, a corrupt practice that did not happen. The Act does permit more than one petition against the same election, and the High Court may then try them together, but each must independently satisfy the filing period and the pleading rules, and the expiry of the forty five days shuts the door on all of them alike.

The scheme covers every election the Act governs, and its reach is wider than the direct elections that dominate discussion. Elections to the Council of States and to the Legislative Councils of States, conducted by electoral colleges rather than by the general electorate, are elections within Part VI, and the petition lies against them in the same High Court, with the elector who may present it read as a person entitled to vote at that election, which in an indirect election means a member of the electoral college concerned. Presidential and Vice Presidential elections stand outside the Act altogether, under their own statute and the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction under Article 71. The unity of the petition scheme for parliamentary and assembly elections, direct and indirect alike, is part of its constitutional design: one remedy, one forum pattern, one limitation period, whatever the size of the electorate whose choice is impugned.

## Standing, Forum and the Forty Five Days

### Where is an election petition filed, and who tries it?

An election petition is filed in the High Court of the State in which the constituency is situated, and it is tried by a single judge of that High Court assigned for the purpose by the Chief Justice. Section 80A of the Act vests the jurisdiction to try petitions in the High Court.

The jurisdiction is exercised ordinarily by a single judge so assigned, and a petition affecting constituencies in more than one State, or a parliamentary constituency spread across a State boundary, follows the seat of the constituency as the Act directs. There is no election tribunal and no appeal to a district forum: the trial court for the largest questions in democratic life is a single judge of a constitutional court, hearing the matter as a civil trial of an elevated and heavily regulated kind.

The petitioner must first be someone the Act allows through the door. Section 81(1) provides that an election petition calling in question any election may be presented to the High Court by any candidate at such election or any elector. The two categories are wider than they first appear. A candidate at the election includes a person who offered himself for election and whose nomination was rejected, a person who withdrew in accordance with the rules, and of course every candidate who went to the poll and lost. The elector is defined by the Explanation to Section 81 as a person who was entitled to vote at the election to which the petition relates, whether he has voted at that election or not, so the right to petition does not depend on having actually cast a vote, on having voted for the losing side, or on any personal loss beyond membership of the constituency's electorate. A person whose name was not on the electoral roll of that constituency at that election, however public spirited, has no standing, and a political party as an organisation is not itself a petitioner unless it acts through a candidate or an elector who petitions in his own name. Petitions are in practice financed, organised and argued by parties, but the signature on the petition must be that of a person within the two statutory categories, and the defect is incurable if it is not.

The period is the sharpest feature of Section 81. The petition must be presented within forty five days from, but not earlier than, the date of election of the returned candidate, and where more than one returned candidate is elected at a general election and the dates of their election are different, the later of those dates is the starting point for petitions against that election. The date of election is itself a defined term: under Section 67A, the date of election of a candidate is the date on which he is declared by the returning officer to be elected. The count therefore runs from the declaration of the result, not from the polling day, not from the counting day where the two differ, and not from the date of the Gazette notification of the result. Forty five days from declaration is, by the standards of Indian litigation, a very short window in which to investigate a constituency wide grievance, gather particulars of specific acts of bribery or undue influence, instruct counsel, settle a pleading of the particularity the Act demands, swear the supporting affidavit and present the whole to the High Court with the statutory deposit. It is shorter still in a large parliamentary constituency where the acts complained of may be spread across hundreds of polling stations. The window is what it is because the legislature weighed the public interest in a settled representation more heavily than the petitioner's interest in time to prepare, and every later feature of the proceeding is coloured by that first choice.

The phrasing of the period carries two edges that practitioners mark before anything else. The words but not earlier than close the front end: a petition presented before the date of election of the returned candidate is premature and incompetent, because until the declaration there is no election completed to call in question, and the intermediate stage bar in Article 329(b) occupies the same ground from the constitutional side. The computation of the forty five days follows the ordinary rule of the General Clauses Act 1897, under which a period reckoned from a stated day excludes that day itself, so the day of declaration is day zero and the forty fifth day after it is the last day of the window. Petitioners who count the declaration day as day one file a day early by their own arithmetic and are safe; petitioners who count the Gazette notification, or the day the returned candidate takes the oath, discover at the threshold hearing that the statute counts from an event they treated as a formality. In a general election to a House, where results across constituencies are declared on the same counting day in the modern practice, the later date rule for multiple returned candidates rarely moves the start; in the by election and in the staggered declarations it replaces, it remains a trap for a petitioner who assumes his constituency's declaration date governs a petition that in law runs from a later one.

What the window excludes is as important as what it allows. The Supreme Court has treated the Representation of the People Act as a complete code on the subject of election disputes, and the consequence drawn in K. Venkateswara Rao v Bekkam Narasimha Reddi and in Hukumdev Narain Yadav v Lalit Narain Mishra is that the delay condoning power in Section 5 of the Limitation Act 1963 does not travel into election petitions. A petition presented on the forty sixth day is not a petition presented late with an explanation; it is a petition the High Court is bound to dismiss under Section 86(1), whatever the explanation. The reasons have been consistent across the decades. The election creates rights in the constituency as a whole and in the returned candidate, third parties arrange their conduct on the faith of the declared result, and a limitation regime that could be reopened on equitable grounds would keep every marginal result in suspense for as long as a plausible excuse could be constructed. The narrow relief that does exist comes from the general law of computation rather than from condonation: where the last day of the period falls on a day on which the court is closed, the act of presentation may be done on the day the court reopens, a protection carried by Section 10 of the General Clauses Act 1897 rather than by any indulgence of the election court. Even that protection is about the calendar of the court and not about the diligence of the petitioner.

The mechanics of presentation are prescribed with the same strictness. Section 81(3) requires that every petition be accompanied by as many copies as there are respondents named in it, and that every such copy be attested by the petitioner under his own signature to be a true copy of the petition. The copies are not a clerical courtesy; they are the documents that will be served, and non compliance with the presentation requirements, like non compliance with the limitation period, falls within the dismissal command of Section 86(1). The petition is presented to the High Court in accordance with its rules, the statutory deposit under Section 117 must accompany the presentation, and the proceeding then moves into the phase in which most petitions are actually decided: the examination of the pleading itself.

## The Record Before the Pleading: Election Papers and the Evidence That Exists

A petitioner has forty five days in which investigation and drafting must happen together, and the quality of the petition he can write depends on what the election process itself has preserved. The Conduct of Elections Rules 1961 answer part of that question. Rule 93 governs the production and inspection of election papers: the packets of ballot papers, whether used, counted, rejected or tendered, are not to be opened and their contents are not to be inspected or produced except under the order of a competent court or authority, while the other papers relating to the election, the result sheets, the accounts of votes recorded and the marked copies of the electoral roll among them, are open to public inspection subject to the conditions the rules prescribe. The architecture is deliberate. The secrecy of the ballot, protected by Section 128 of the Act and by the sealed packets rule, survives the declaration of the result, and a petitioner's curiosity about how identifiable electors voted is not a ground on which the packets open. What opens freely is the documentary skeleton of the count: the result sheet in Form 20 recording the votes polled by each candidate at each polling station, the presiding officers' records, the statements of rejected ballots, and the account of votes that accompanies the electronic voting record. A counting grievance is built from those documents, compared polling station by polling station against the campaign's own polling agent records, and the petition that results either identifies specific stations where the record does not hold together or it has no counting case to plead.

The distinction the rule draws explains the shape of two different kinds of petition. The corrupt practice petition lives or dies on human evidence, on witnesses to payments, meetings, vehicles and appeals, and its investigation is a matter of finding people willing to swear to events. The counting petition lives or dies on paper. Its material facts are arithmetical: votes recorded in the result sheet that exceed the votes the station's account supports, ballot papers rejected on grounds the rules do not permit, valid votes of the petitioner refused in a measure that approaches the margin. Because the documents are public from the close of the count, the counting petition can be, and in the well run cases is, substantially complete within the limitation period, while the corrupt practice petition is an investigation compressed into a pleading. The Act treats both under the same forty five days, and the inequality of the two investigations inside the one period is a recurring practical grievance of petitioners that no judgment has relieved, since the period is legislative text and the courts' discipline about it is the subject of the limitation discussion above.

Candidates and their election agents also generate a second archive of their own, and it cuts both ways. Every candidate must lodge an account of election expenses under Section 78 within thirty days of the declaration of the result, and the account, with the supporting vouchers, is a public document on which rivals and journalists work. The expense account is the seed of the Section 77 ceiling cases and of the Section 10A failure to lodge disqualification, and inconsistencies between the account and what a constituency observed during the campaign are the classic starting point of a petition file. The affidavit in Form 26 is the second self created record, and its mining by opponents begins on the day of its display at the returning officer's office. The petitioner who starts from these two archives, the public count record and the candidate's own filings, drafts a petition anchored in documents; the petitioner who starts from constituency rumour drafts one anchored in witnesses, and the difference in their prospects at the threshold hearing under Section 86 is visible in the reported cases.

There is a discipline of preservation inside the forty five days that experienced election lawyers treat as part of the pleading itself. The constituency record is perishable in fact though durable in law: the count documents remain in the district election office custody, but the human record, the polling agents' notes, the complaint copies made to observers and returning officers during the poll, the contemporaneous press reports and the campaign's own publicity material, survives only if someone gathers it before the organisation that made it disperses. The well run petition office opens on counting day with the pleading's headings already printed, and the investigation is the filling of those headings with dated, placed and named incidents while memories and papers are still within reach; the ill run one begins the same work in the fifth week, and its petitions read that way. Journalists who follow a petition across its years face the same discipline in miniature, keeping source notes and constituency records private and intact for a proceeding that may outlast several assignments, the concern addressed by [this account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/).

## The Pleading Discipline: Material Facts, Particulars and the Affidavit

### What must an election petition contain to survive?

An election petition must state concisely the material facts on which the petitioner relies and must set out full particulars of any corrupt practice alleged, including as full a statement as possible of the names of the parties alleged to have committed it and the date and place of its commission.

The petition must be signed by the petitioner and verified in the manner laid down in the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 for the verification of pleadings, and where a corrupt practice is alleged, it must be accompanied by an affidavit in the prescribed form supporting the allegation and its particulars. A petition that fails these requirements is exposed to dismissal at the threshold.

Section 82 fixes the parties. The petitioner must join as respondents, where the petition claims a further declaration that the petitioner himself or any other candidate has been duly elected, all the contesting candidates other than the petitioner, and in any other case all the returned candidates; and he must also join any other candidate against whom allegations of corrupt practice are made in the petition. The logic of the provision is that an election is a single transaction, and a court asked to redistribute its result must have before it everyone whose position the redistribution could disturb. The price of omission is set by Section 86(1): the High Court shall dismiss an election petition which does not comply with the provisions of Section 81, Section 82 or Section 117. The word is shall. The dismissal for pleading and party defects is mandatory once the non compliance is established, it is not a discretion to be exercised in sympathy with the merits, and the Supreme Court has read the provision in that sense across a long line of cases.

Section 83 then separates the two layers of an allegation, and the separation decides the fate of most petitions. Material facts are the primary facts, the basic facts that constitute the cause of action: who did the act, what the act was, in what capacity, with what connection to the candidate. Particulars are the details that fill out the material facts: the date, the place, the persons present, the words used, the amount paid. A petition that alleges a corrupt practice must contain both layers in full at the moment of presentation. The Supreme Court's treatment of the requirement in Azhar Hussain v Rajiv Gandhi remains the standard cautionary text in the subject: a petition that alleged corrupt practices in general terms, without the material facts from which each charge could be made out, was held to disclose no triable cause of action and was dismissed without a trial. The holding rests on fairness as much as on form. A charge of corrupt practice carries a quasi criminal character and grave consequences for the person charged, and a respondent is entitled to know, from the pleading itself, the precise case he must meet, rather than discover it as evidence accumulates.

The affidavit requirement completes the discipline. The proviso to Section 83(1) requires that where the petitioner alleges any corrupt practice, the petition shall be accompanied by an affidavit in the prescribed form in support of the allegation and of the particulars given; the form is Form 25 under the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961. Defects in the affidavit have generated a substantial subsidiary jurisprudence of their own, distinguishing between an affidavit that is absent or so defective as to be no affidavit in law, which strikes at the petition itself, and defects of verification that may be treated as curable irregularities. The practitioner distinction matters less than the structural point: the legislature attached a sworn document to the gravest allegations precisely so that the petitioner commits himself, on oath and at the outset, to a defined set of facts, and cannot roam beyond them as the trial develops.

What cannot be repaired later is defined by Section 86(5). The High Court may allow the particulars of an alleged corrupt practice to be amended or amplified, so that a date may be corrected, a place specified more precisely, a witness list of details enlarged; but no amendment is allowed which would introduce particulars of a corrupt practice not previously alleged in the petition. The line is between amplifying a charge that exists and adding a charge that does not. Evidence follows the same boundary: a petitioner may not lead evidence on a corrupt practice for which no material facts are pleaded, and a trial court that permits the pleading to be reconstructed through evidence commits the error the Supreme Court has corrected most often in this subject. The petitioner therefore prepares, within forty five days and under a limitation period that admits no excuse, a document that will bind him for the whole trial. Election petitions are lost at the drafting table more often than in the witness box.

## The Grounds on Which an Election Falls

Section 100 of the Act states the grounds on which the High Court shall declare the election of a returned candidate to be void, and the list is exhaustive: a petitioner who cannot bring the case within one of its clauses has no case, however discreditable the campaign may have been. The disqualification grid that the first clause engages is set out in full in [the pillar article on the Representation of the People Act 1951](/2017/12/15/representation-of-people-act-1951/), and the treatment here takes that grid as given and follows what a petitioner must do with it.

The first ground, Section 100(1)(a), is that on the date of his election the returned candidate was not qualified, or was disqualified, to be chosen to fill the seat under the Constitution or under the Act. The date is decisive. A disqualification that attached after the date of election belongs to the disqualification track and to Articles 102 and 191, not to a petition; a disqualification that existed on the date of election, whether a subsisting conviction disqualification under Section 8, a dismissal disqualification under Section 9, a government contract disqualification under Section 9A or a disqualification under the Tenth Schedule's terms incorporated into the Act's scheme, makes the election itself voidable in the petition. The second ground, Section 100(1)(b), is that a corrupt practice has been committed by the returned candidate or his election agent, or by any other person with the consent of the returned candidate or his election agent. Consent is the hinge: the acts of the candidate and the election agent are attributed directly, while the acts of the wider campaign, of workers, supporters and intermediaries, unseat the candidate only where his consent or his election agent's consent is proved. The third ground, Section 100(1)(c), is the improper rejection of a nomination, which stands alone because a wrongly excluded candidate damages the election itself: the constituency was denied a choice the law required to be offered, and no inquiry into how votes would have fallen is needed or permitted.

The fourth ground, Section 100(1)(d), is the materially affected ground, and it reverses the burden pattern of the first three. The petitioner must show that the result of the election, in so far as it concerns the returned candidate, has been materially affected by one of four causes: the improper acceptance of a nomination, a corrupt practice committed in the interests of the returned candidate by an agent other than his election agent, the improper reception, refusal or rejection of a vote or the reception of a vote that is void, or non compliance with the provisions of the Constitution or of the Act or of any rules or orders made under it. Material effect is a demanding standard. It is not enough that an irregularity occurred, or even that it was serious; the petitioner must satisfy the court, on evidence, that the irregularity changed or may properly be found to have changed the result as between the returned candidate and the field. Where the margin is large and the irregularity small, the ground fails. Where votes were wrongly received or wrongly refused in numbers approaching the margin, the count itself becomes the battleground, and the petition turns into an inquiry into ballots.

Section 100(2) supplies the candidate's defence to the agent based charges, and it is drawn narrowly. Where the court is of opinion that a corrupt practice was committed by an agent of the returned candidate other than his election agent, the election is not to be declared void if the court is satisfied that the corrupt practice was committed contrary to the orders and without the consent of the returned candidate or his election agent, that the candidate and the election agent took all reasonable means for preventing corrupt practices at the election, and that in other respects the election was free from corrupt practice on the part of the candidate or any of his agents. The defence is cumulative: all three conditions must be satisfied together, and the second of them means that a candidate who insulates himself from his own campaign by design, declining to issue instructions against the very practices that benefit him, will struggle to show that all reasonable preventive means were taken.

Running beneath every ground is the standard of proof, which the Supreme Court fixed in a line of authority of which Rahim Khan v Khurshid Ahmed is the most cited: a charge of corrupt practice is quasi criminal in character, and it must be proved beyond reasonable doubt, with the evidence weighed as evidence in a criminal charge is weighed, and with suspicion, however strong, kept distinct from proof. The standard does the work the pleading rules begin. Together they express a legislative and judicial judgment that runs through the whole subject: an election result is the recorded will of a constituency, it is not to be disturbed on probabilities assembled from grievance, and the petitioner who asks a court to annul it must arrive with a case built like a prosecution.

## The Trial: A Civil Suit Run as a Constitutional Obligation

Section 87 provides that every election petition shall be tried by the High Court, as nearly as may be, in accordance with the procedure applicable under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 to the trial of suits, subject to the provisions of the Act and of rules made under it, and that the provisions of the Indian Evidence Act 1872 shall be deemed to apply in all respects to the trial. The petition is therefore tried like a suit: issues are framed, documents are proved, witnesses are examined and cross examined, and judgment follows argument. But two provisions of Section 86 wrap the ordinary civil trial in an obligation that ordinary suits do not carry. The trial shall, so far as is practicable consistently with the interests of justice, be continued from day to day until its conclusion, unless the High Court finds adjournment beyond the following day necessary for reasons recorded. And every petition shall be tried as expeditiously as possible, with an endeavour made to conclude the trial within six months from the date on which the petition is presented to the High Court. The contrast between the two sentences is the whole pendency problem in miniature. The day to day direction is qualified by practicability and by recorded reasons. The six month direction is, in terms, an endeavour. Neither carries a consequence for breach: a trial that enters its third year is a trial that has disappointed the statute, not one that has lost jurisdiction, and the returned candidate against whom the allegations stand unproved continues in office throughout.

### When will a court order a recount of votes?

A recount is ordered only where the petitioner has first pleaded the material facts that make the count suspect and has then made out, on evidence, a prima facie case that the reception, refusal or rejection of votes was improper in a measure capable of affecting the result.

The purity of the election and the secrecy of the ballot are the two interests the court balances: an inspection of ballot papers is not a discovery exercise available to a petitioner who hopes that the boxes will reveal a case he could not plead, and the Supreme Court's recount jurisprudence, running from Ram Sewak Yadav v Hussain Kamil Kidwai onwards, insists that the order for scrutiny follows proof and does not precede it.

Inside the trial sits one device that belongs to election law alone. Section 84 allows a petitioner, in addition to calling the election in question, to claim a declaration that he himself or any other candidate has been duly elected, and Section 97 then arms the defence against that claim with recrimination: when a seat is claimed for an unsuccessful candidate, the returned candidate or any other party may give evidence to prove that the election of that candidate would have been void if he had been returned and a petition had been presented against him. The machinery is precise. Notice of the intention to give recriminatory evidence, together with a statement of the grounds and the particulars, must be delivered within fourteen days from the date of commencement of the trial, and security must be furnished as the section prescribes. Recrimination converts the trial into two trials running together: the petitioner's attack on the returned candidate, and the respondents' counter attack on the candidate the petitioner wants installed. A petitioner who claims the seat must therefore audit his own candidate's campaign, and his own candidate's eligibility, with the same severity he applies to the winner's, because the declaration he seeks invites exactly that audit.

The evidentiary conduct of the trial is where the quasi criminal standard meets the volume of a constituency. A corrupt practice charge is rarely a single act; it is a pattern alleged across meetings, vehicles, payments and publications, and each incident must be pleaded with particulars and proved by witnesses whose credit survives cross examination. Witnesses in a constituency are seldom neutral: they are workers, relatives, rivals and dependents of the contending camps, and the trial judge must reconstruct a campaign from partisan testimony months or years after the event, applying a criminal standard to civil recollection. The Act's answer to the difficulty is front loaded into the pleading rules rather than relaxed at the proof stage, and the answer is unforgiving by design: what was not pleaded is not proved, what was pleaded vaguely is dismissed at the threshold, and what survives both filters must still be established beyond reasonable doubt before it can move a result.

## The Vote Records: Ballots, Result Sheets and the Anatomy of a Counting Case

Counting cases deserve a closer treatment of their own because they are the petitions in which the materially affected ground does its characteristic work, and because their proof obeys rules that surprise petitioners formed by ordinary litigation. The first rule is that the count attacked is the statutory count, recorded in the result sheet and founded on the ballot papers or the voting machine record as the case may be, and the court's inquiry is into the legality of what was received, refused or rejected within that count. A ballot paper is not invalid because the petitioner's agents disliked the returning officer's ruling at the table; it is invalid on the grounds the Conduct of Elections Rules prescribe, principally where it bears a mark by which the elector can be identified, where it records votes for more than one candidate or for none in a manner the rules treat as void, where it is spurious, or where the mark is placed so that the candidate for whom it was cast cannot be determined. The grounds are technical, they are applied ballot by ballot, and a scrutiny converts the courtroom into a counting hall in which each disputed paper is argued over as an individual legal question. The second rule, already met in the recount discussion, is the gate before the hall opens: secrecy is a constitutional value in the scheme, inspection of the ballot packets requires the court's order under Rule 93, and the order follows a pleaded and prima facie established case rather than preceding it, so that a petitioner cannot purchase with a court fee the fishing expedition the rules forbid.

Within those rules, counting cases resolve into a small number of recurring patterns. There is the rejection case, where the margin is smaller than the number of rejected ballot papers and the petitioner contends that papers rejected for a defective mark or a stray impression were in law valid votes for him; the scrutiny then examines the rejected packet alone, and the case stands or falls on the mark by mark rulings. There is the reception case, built on votes received that were void: tendered votes counted as genuine, votes of persons impersonated, votes recorded in the result sheet in excess of the account of votes the station issued. There is the arithmetical case, where the result sheet does not reconcile with the station records and the error, conceded or proved, is transposed into the declaration. And there is the postal ballot case, a category whose weight has grown with the expansion of service and absentee voting, where the complaint fastens on the attestation, the declaration or the envelope requirements that condition the validity of a postal vote. Each pattern ends at the same legal destination: the court must be satisfied not only that the count was wrong but that the error, corrected, materially affects the result as Section 100(1)(d) requires, and where the correction leaves the returned candidate ahead by a reduced margin the petition fails on the very ground that carried it into court.

The introduction of voter verifiable paper audit trail units added a further document to this record without changing its governing principles. Where the paper slip count and the electronic count diverge in a constituency, the candidate's remedy follows the Commission's counting directions at the count itself, and any challenge afterwards runs through the petition and its disciplines; the slip record is evidence within the case, not an alternative forum beside it. The verification exercise ordered by the Supreme Court in 2019, raising the physical verification of paper slips to five polling stations in each Assembly segment, belongs to the administrative law of the count rather than to the petition, and its significance for petitions is indirect: it widened the audited sample from which a counting grievance may draw its first particulars. The petitioner who invokes discrepancies must still plead them station by station, prove them to the standard the ground requires, and show their material effect on the result, and the bulk of counting petitions continue to fail at one of those three steps rather than at the level of the technology used to record the vote.

Two smaller records complete the counting picture, and both recur in petitions more often than their size suggests. The first is the tendered vote: where a person presents himself to vote and finds that another has already voted in his name, the rules permit him to record a tendered ballot, which is marked and kept apart from the ordinary count rather than added to it, so that the genuine elector's protest is preserved without disturbing the poll. Tendered ballots become evidence in the petition where personation is alleged on a constituency scale, and a pattern of tendered votes across stations is one of the recognised ways a petitioner builds the prima facie case that opens the sealed records. The second is the rejected vote at the postal ballot stage, where the declaration, the attestation and the envelope requirements operate as conditions of validity and are applied with a strictness that surprises candidates whose supporters lose votes to a missing signature. Neither record decides a petition by itself. Each supplies the pleaded particulars, station by station and envelope by envelope, without which the materially affected ground remains an assertion.

## Costs, Deposits and the Economics of the Petition

The money provisions of Part VI are short, and they do more work than their length suggests. Section 117 requires the petitioner, at the time of presenting the petition, to deposit in the High Court, in accordance with the rules of the Court, a sum of two thousand rupees as security for the costs of the petition, and permits the High Court at any time during the trial to call upon the petitioner to give further security for costs as it directs. The figure is unindexed 1951 money carried through every amendment since, and its smallness is itself a policy choice of a piece with the rest of the access design: the door to the only remedy for a vitiated election is not priced for the wealthy, and a petitioner of modest means is not asked to stake a sum beyond reach as the price of questioning a result. The real cost discipline lies elsewhere, in the further security the court may demand as the scale of the trial becomes clear, and in the costs orders at the end. Section 118 makes parallel provision for security for costs from a respondent in the cases it specifies, so that a respondent who opens a recriminatory front does not litigate it free of the same exposure he invokes against the petitioner. Sections 119 to 122 complete the circuit: payment of costs out of the deposit during and at the end of the trial, the disposal of any balance, and the execution of the High Court's costs order through the machinery the Act prescribes, with the costs of the petition resting in the court's discretion exercised through the order under Section 99.

Practitioners read these provisions together with the conduct rules rather than in isolation. A petition dismissed at the threshold for a pleading failure will ordinarily carry costs against the petitioner, because the respondent has been put to the defence of a result by a case that was never triable; a petition that fails after trial on proof carries the same exposure, heavier with the length of the trial the respondent was made to endure. A petitioner who succeeds recovers his costs in the ordinary course, though never his time, and the deposit returns to him through the Section 120 route subject to the orders made. The deposit is therefore best understood not as the price of the proceeding but as its earnest: a small fixed sum anchoring a costs jurisdiction whose real sanctions are assessed at the end, on the conduct and the outcome of the case. The economics that deter speculative petitions are not in Section 117 at all; they are in the years of counsel fees a constituency scale trial consumes, in the exposure to a costs order measured against that scale, and in the knowledge, which every election agent carries into the first consultation, that the proceeding will likely outlast the political moment that made it worth filing. A reform proposal heard periodically in the subject, that the deposit should be raised steeply to discourage frivolous petitions, mistakes the location of the filter: the Act already filters at the pleading threshold and at the standard of proof, and raising the price of entry would filter by wealth, the one characteristic the merits of an election grievance do not track.

## Judgment: What the High Court Can Do

At the conclusion of the trial, Section 98 requires the High Court to make one of three orders: an order dismissing the election petition, an order declaring the election of all or any of the returned candidates to be void, or an order declaring the election of all or any of the returned candidates to be void and declaring the petitioner or any other candidate to have been duly elected. The three orders map the whole range of outcomes. Dismissal leaves the result exactly as declared. A declaration of voidness annuls the return and starts the machinery of a fresh election. The third order, the most intrusive, substitutes the court's declaration for the constituency's count, and Section 101 confines it to two situations: where the candidate in whose favour it is made in fact received a majority of the valid votes, or where, but for the votes obtained by the returned candidate by corrupt practices, that candidate would have obtained a majority of the valid votes. Outside those two situations the court annuls and stops; it does not guess at how a constituency would have voted under cleaner conditions, and the seat goes back to the electors.

With the Section 98 order, Section 99 requires the High Court to record findings on the corrupt practice questions tried before it: whether any corrupt practice has or has not been proved to have been committed at the election, and the nature of that practice, and the names of all persons who have been proved at the trial to have been guilty of any corrupt practice, together with the order as to costs. The naming jurisdiction is the petition's longest shadow, because a person named under Section 99 faces the disqualification machinery of Section 8A, under which the case of a person found guilty of a corrupt practice by an order under Section 99 is taken up by the President, who decides the question of disqualification and its period, up to six years, on the opinion of the Election Commission. The Act surrounds the naming power with a safeguard that mirrors the gravity of its consequence: no person who is not a party to the petition may be named unless he has been given notice to show cause why he should not be so named, an opportunity to cross examine any witness who has already been examined against him, an opportunity to call evidence in his defence, and an opportunity to be heard. A finding of corrupt practice also carries evidentiary weight beyond the petition: it is the record on which the Section 8A reference proceeds, and it cannot be reopened as though the trial had not occurred.

The order, once made, is transmitted under Section 106 to the appropriate authority and to the Election Commission, and published in the Official Gazette, and under Section 107 an order under Section 98 or Section 99 takes effect as soon as it is pronounced by the High Court, subject to any stay granted in appeal. The immediacy matters. A declaration that an election is void does not wait for the appeal period to expire; the member's title ends when the judgment is pronounced, and his continuance in the seat thereafter depends on obtaining a stay from the Supreme Court. Costs, finally, are dealt with by the order under Section 99 and the machinery of Sections 117 to 122: the petitioner's deposit stands as security, the High Court may call for further security during the trial, and at the conclusion the deposit is applied to costs awarded and the balance handled as those sections direct. Election litigation is expensive in a way that ordinary civil litigation is not, and the costs order is a real part of the judgment, capable of punishing a speculative petitioner and of compensating a respondent who has been put to a trial on allegations that failed.

## After Judgment: The Vacancy, the By Election and Acts Already Done

A declaration that an election is void sets three kinds of consequence in motion. The first is the vacancy itself. The seat of the returned candidate falls vacant on the pronouncement of the order, subject to the stay jurisdiction in appeal, and the vacancy is notified through the machinery by which the House and the Election Commission learn of it, the Gazette publication under Section 106 being the order's formal communication to both. The second is the fresh election. Where no other candidate is declared duly elected, the constituency returns to the electoral process, and Section 151A of the Act obliges the holding of a by election within six months from the date of the vacancy, subject to the exceptions the section recognises, where the remainder of the term of the member whose seat fell vacant is less than one year, or where the Election Commission, in consultation with the Central Government, certifies that it is difficult to hold the by election within the period. The six month by election clock is one of the few time limits in the Act addressed to the state rather than to the citizen, and it is met as a matter of administrative course; the contrast with the six month endeavour addressed to the courts inside Section 86(7) is the sharpest single illustration of this article's structural claim, because the same legal system that fills a vacancy within six months of its arising routinely takes longer than six months to decide whether the vacancy should arise at all.

The third consequence runs backwards, and it is a mercy the law extends to everyone except the unseated member. The acts done by the returned candidate while he sat, the votes he cast in the House, the committee work he performed, the salary and allowances he drew for the period of actual service, are not unwound by the annulment of his election. The proceedings of a legislature would be unworkable on any other footing: a division carried by a margin smaller than the votes of members whose elections were later annulled cannot be re-run, and the validity of what the House did rests on the composition it had when it acted. The member's personal position is less protected. Where a person sits and votes in a House knowing that he is disqualified or that his election has been declared void, Articles 104 and 193 impose a penalty of five hundred rupees for each day of such sitting and voting, recoverable as a debt due to the State; the provision is rarely invoked, but it marks the line the Constitution draws between the protected acts of the institution and the exposed position of the individual who persists after his title has failed. Where a candidate is declared duly elected in the unseated member's place, his membership is reckoned from the declaration for the purposes the Act specifies, and the term he serves is the remainder of the term the constituency elected, not a fresh term of his own.

For the petitioner, the end of the proceeding also settles the money. The costs order under Section 99 is executed through the machinery the Act provides in Sections 119 to 122: the deposit is applied as the sections direct, costs awarded may be recovered, and an order for costs operates with the force the Act gives it, so that the financial judgment of the court on the litigation is enforceable and not merely declaratory. The named persons under Section 99 carry their finding into the Section 8A reference, where the period of disqualification is fixed, and the trial record follows them there. Nothing in the conclusion of a petition is therefore private to its parties; the result sheet of the constituency, the membership of the House, the eligibility of individuals for the next election and the public money of costs all move with a single judgment, which is the final reason the Act protects the judgment's production with a pleading discipline and a standard of proof that ordinary civil outcomes are never asked to bear.

## The Appeal: Thirty Days to the Supreme Court

Section 116A gives an appeal to the Supreme Court from every order of the High Court made under Section 98 or Section 99, and the appeal must be preferred within thirty days from the date of the order. The Supreme Court may entertain an appeal after the period has expired if it is satisfied that the appellant had sufficient cause for not preferring it within the period, a condonation door that exists at the appeal stage and pointedly does not exist at the petition stage. The asymmetry is characteristic of the Act's design. The petitioner's forty five days define the existence of the dispute itself, and on them the certainty of the whole constituency depends; the appellant's thirty days govern a dispute that already exists and has already been decided once, and a measured indulgence there unsettles less. Even the threshold dismissal of a petition reaches the Supreme Court: because Section 86(1) declares that its dismissal order shall be deemed to be an order under Section 98(a), the Supreme Court held in Hari Shanker Jain v Sonia Gandhi that an appeal against such a dismissal is maintainable under Section 116A. A petitioner dismissed on limitation, on parties or on the deposit is therefore not without remedy, but the remedy is appellate correction of the dismissal, not an extension of the period that produced it.

Under Section 116B, the Supreme Court may, on sufficient cause being shown and on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit, stay the operation of the order appealed from, and the stay is the instrument that decides where the seat effectively sits while the appeal runs. Where a High Court has declared an election void and the Supreme Court stays the operation of that order, the returned candidate's position continues under the protection of the stay for as long as it operates; where no stay is granted, the Section 107 effect of the High Court's order governs, the seat stands vacated, and the constituency proceeds towards a fresh election even though the appeal is alive. Appellate practice in election matters reproduces, at one remove, the central fact of the petition itself: time and possession run together, and the party in occupation of the seat holds an advantage that no eventual judgment fully removes, because a successful appeal after a by election cannot restore the unexpired term that was lost, and an unsuccessful one merely confirms a vacancy that has already been filled.

The appeal itself is a full appeal. The Supreme Court, hearing a matter under Section 116A, is not confined to errors of law in the manner of a second appellate court under the ordinary civil regime; the appeal is a rehearing on the record, and the Court re-appreciates the evidence, tests the findings on the corrupt practice charges against the quasi criminal standard, and may confirm, reverse or vary the High Court's order, recording its own findings where the record permits, including findings of the Section 99 kind on persons proved guilty of a corrupt practice. The volume of the record disciplines the hearing in practice: a petition tried over years generates a paper book of depositions and documents that the appellate bench must master, and the Court's election appeals are among the heavier regular matters on its civil side. Reversal rates reward neither complacency nor despair. Findings of corrupt practice have fallen in the Supreme Court for want of proof to the criminal standard where the High Court had been persuaded on a lower appreciation, and petitions dismissed below on the pleading threshold have been restored where the material facts were in truth present. What the appeal cannot supply is the one commodity whose absence defines the whole subject: it cannot give the constituency back the years between the declaration of the result and the final word.

## Withdrawal, Death and the Life of a Petition

An election petition, once presented, is not the petitioner's private property to abandon at will. Sections 109 to 111 control its exit. An application for withdrawal may be made at any time after presentation, but where there is more than one petitioner it requires the consent of all of them, and Section 110 bars the grant of the application where the High Court is of opinion that it has been induced by a bargain or consideration that ought not to be allowed. Election petitions are public proceedings about a public result, and a withdrawal bought by the respondent, whether the price is money, office or the withdrawal of another petition in exchange, is precisely the corruption of the remedy that the provision exists to prevent. Where leave is granted, the petitioner is ordered to pay the respondents' costs as the court directs, the notice of withdrawal is published in the Official Gazette and in such other manner as the High Court specifies, and any person who might himself have been a petitioner may, within fourteen days of that publication, apply to be substituted in place of the withdrawing petitioner and continue the proceedings on compliance with the conditions as to security. Section 111 then requires the High Court to report the withdrawal to the Election Commission. The design refuses to let a meritorious challenge die in a private settlement: the constituency's interest in the integrity of its result survives the petitioner's change of heart, and the fourteen day substitution window is the mechanism by which another elector or candidate can keep the question alive.

Death is treated by matching rules in Sections 112 and 113, and the two are deliberately asymmetrical in their starting points. An election petition abates only on the death of a sole petitioner or of the survivor of several petitioners, because the petition right is personal to the statutory categories and cannot descend to heirs; but abatement is not the end, for the High Court causes the fact to be published, and any person who might himself have been a petitioner may, within fourteen days of the publication, apply to be substituted and continue the proceeding on the prescribed terms. The death of the respondent produces the opposite rule: the petition does not abate. The proceeding concerns the validity of an election, a question that outlives the returned candidate, and Section 113 directs that notice of the event be published in the constituency, whereupon any person who might have been a petitioner may, within fourteen days, apply to be substituted in place of the respondent to oppose the petition. A petition that nobody is willing to defend still runs its course, because the constituency's result, and not the respondent's convenience, is its subject matter.

The term of the House is set alongside, because the comparison between the statutory clock and the constitutional term is the subject of the rest of this article.

## The Pendency Problem: A Remedy That Expires with the House

The challenge clock states the difficulty without argument. The Act allows forty five days to start, directs a trial from day to day, expresses an endeavour to finish within six months, allows thirty days for the appeal, and then falls silent about total duration. The silence is filled by the realities of the forum. The petition is tried by a single judge of a High Court who also carries the ordinary civil and writ roster of that court; the evidence concerns events spread across an entire constituency; witnesses number in the dozens and cross examination runs for days; threshold objections, amendment applications and interlocutory challenges to the pleading consume the early hearings before a single witness is called; and every order short of the final judgment carries its own appellate possibilities within the trial. A petition concluded at the High Court within the six month endeavour is the exception that practitioners remark on. The ordinary career of a contested petition is measured in years, and the appeal adds a second span of comparable length behind it. The returned candidate sits throughout, a full member for every purpose of voting, salary, privilege and office, because the Act contains no provision suspending the membership of a member whose election is under challenge, and none could be written without handing every disappointed candidate a weapon against every narrow result.

Comparison sharpens the point, because the Constitution and the Act know how to impose harder clocks when they choose. The disqualification questions referred under Articles 103 and 192 travel to the Election Commission and are decided there without a trial at all. Complaints of defection under the Tenth Schedule carry their own chronic delays, documented in the treatment of [Tenth Schedule defection practice](/2018/02/01/tenth-schedule-defection-practice/), but even that comparison flatters the petition, since the defection question at least concerns a member whose election is not itself in dispute. The petition alone combines a full evidentiary trial, a criminal standard of proof, a single judge forum already burdened with the general jurisdiction of a High Court, and a subject matter whose entire value expires on a date fixed by the Constitution. Where judgment arrives after dissolution, the law still pronounces: the election is declared void or the petition dismissed, findings on corrupt practice are recorded with their Section 8A consequences, and costs follow. What judgment cannot do is restore the constituency's representation for the term that has run. The electors of that constituency were represented, for the whole term, by the candidate whose title the court was examining, and the examination's conclusion arrives too late to alter the representation they received.

The consequences flow outward from that single fact. A candidate calculating the value of a corrupt practice prices it against the probability of a petition, the probability of proof beyond reasonable doubt, and the probability of judgment before the term ends; as the third probability falls, the effective deterrent weakens even where the first two hold. A petitioner calculating whether to file prices the litigation against the same clock: the deposit and the costs exposure are immediate, the trial is measured in years, and the prize, if judgment comes late in the term, is a by election for the remainder or a bare declaration after the remainder has expired. Parties internalise both calculations, and the pattern of filings reflects it: petitions cluster in seats where the margin was small, the grievance spectacular or the political stakes high enough to justify a proceeding whose practical yield is uncertain and deferred. The structural claim of this article can be put plainly. The deadline that binds the citizen is enforced to the day, and the deadline that binds the state is an endeavour: the asymmetry is written into Sections 81, 86(6) and 86(7) themselves, and it converts a remedy designed to police the term into a remedy that frequently expires with the House.

Two refinements keep the picture honest. The first is that dissolution does not kill the petition. The proceeding concerns the validity of a past election and the guilt or innocence of named persons, and those questions retain legal consequences, in the Section 99 findings, in the Section 8A reference that may follow them, and in costs, after the House that the election composed has ceased to exist; High Courts have on that footing continued and decided petitions after the term ended, and the law reports of every decade carry judgments declaring the character of elections whose Houses had already been dissolved. The petition outlives the term not only in the colloquial sense of arriving late but in the doctrinal sense that nothing in the Act makes the term its outer limit. The second refinement concerns the interval itself. The member under challenge is, during the whole pendency, a member undiminished: he takes his seat, speaks, votes in divisions that may decide governments, holds committee office, and is eligible for ministerial appointment, and no doctrine of suspended legitimacy attaches to his acts. If the petition eventually succeeds, the law protects what was done in the interval, as the treatment of acts after judgment above records, so the member's period in office is retrospectively valid even where his title to it is retrospectively destroyed. Time, in an election petition, is never merely delay; it is a transfer of value from the constituency and the petitioner to the returned candidate, made by the calendar and irreversible by any court.

## Conviction and the Seat: Disqualification Without a Petition

The second track requires no petitioner, no forty five days and no trial, because it operates on the fact of conviction itself. Chapter III of Part II of the Act lays down the disqualifications for membership of Parliament and of the State Legislatures, and the definitions section confines the statutory grounds strictly: a person is disqualified if and only if he is disqualified under the provisions of that Chapter. Section 8 states the conviction rules in three bands. Under Section 8(1), conviction of any of the listed offences, ranging across offences against the State, bribery and undue influence at elections, rape and offences against women, offences under the protection of civil rights and untouchability legislation, customs and foreign exchange offences, narcotics offences, terrorism legislation, corruption offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988, and offences against national honour and religious harmony, disqualifies on conviction alone, whether the sentence is a fine or imprisonment, for six years from conviction or, where imprisonment was imposed, from release and six years beyond it. Section 8(2) covers offences under the laws against hoarding and profiteering, adulteration of food and drugs, and the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961, disqualifying where a sentence of not less than six months is imposed. Section 8(3) is the residuary band: conviction of any other offence with a sentence of not less than two years disqualifies from the date of conviction and for six years after release. The [IPC versus BNS comparison](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/) maps the offence provisions that feed these bands across the old and new penal codes; the disqualification consequences described here attach under the Act whichever code supplies the conviction.

Until 2013, the bands carried a shelter. Section 8(4) provided that the disqualification of a sitting member under the first three subsections would not take effect until three months had elapsed from the conviction, and where an appeal or revision was filed within that period, until the appeal or revision was disposed of. The shelter meant that a convicted legislator held his seat through the appellate process, and the appellate process, in the ordinary course, outlasted much of the term. The Supreme Court ended the shelter in Lily Thomas v Union of India, decided on 10 July 2013 by a bench of A. K. Patnaik and S. J. Mukhopadhaya JJ, holding Section 8(4) ultra vires the Constitution. The reasoning went to the structure of the legislative power itself: Articles 102(1)(e) and 191(1)(e) authorise Parliament to make law on disqualifications, but Articles 101(3)(a) and 190(3)(a) fix the consequence, that the seat of a member who becomes subject to a disqualification becomes vacant, and Parliament has no power to defer the date on which the disqualification takes effect or to prescribe different dates for sitting members and for candidates. A sitting member convicted of a Section 8 offence is therefore disqualified from the date of conviction, and the seat falls vacant from that date, subject only to the appellate court's power over the conviction itself. A legislative attempt to restore the shelter by ordinance later in 2013 was withdrawn, and the judgment's rule has governed since.

The appellate qualification is the distinction on which the modern cases turn. A stay of the execution of the sentence, of the kind routinely granted when a convicted person is released on bail pending appeal, suspends the punishment but leaves the conviction standing, and the disqualification, which flows from the conviction, continues to operate. A stay of the conviction itself, an exceptional order granted where the consequences of the conviction are shown to be irreversible or where the conviction is ex facie unsustainable, suspends the disqualification from the date of the stay order. The three judge bench in K. Prabhakaran v P. Jayarajan stated the position, the Lily Thomas bench endorsed it in terms, and the same distinction structures the earlier civil sphere authority in Rama Narang v Ramesh Narang and Ravikant S. Patil v Sarvabhouma S. Bagali. Its most visible modern application came in August 2023, when the Supreme Court stayed the conviction of the Congress leader Rahul Gandhi in a defamation case arising from a 2019 campaign speech, a conviction carrying the two year sentence that engaged Section 8(3) and had vacated his Lok Sabha seat, and the membership stood restored within days of the stay. The episode compresses the whole track into a single sequence: conviction, immediate vacancy, appellate combat over the conviction rather than over the sentence, restoration following the stay. It also shows who bears the risk of delay on this track. The convicted member is out of the House from conviction, and the appellate court that moves slowly costs him the seat for the duration, the mirror image of the petition track, where delay is borne by the challenger and the constituency.

Two softening powers stand against the rigour of Section 8, and both are deliberately narrow. Section 11 empowers the Election Commission, for reasons to be recorded, to remove any disqualification under Chapter III, other than the Section 8A disqualification that follows a corrupt practice finding, or to reduce its period; the power was used in 2019 to reduce a Section 10A disqualification from three years to thirteen months, a use that drew public controversy precisely because the power is real and exercised on recorded reasons alone. Separately, the disqualification questions that arise about a sitting member otherwise than on conviction, allegations of office of profit, of unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, of undischarged insolvency, of a citizenship defect, or of a statutory ground, travel by the reference route described next, and the petition track has nothing to say to them unless the same defect existed on the date of election.

## The Seat in Practice After a Conviction

The automatic character of the conviction track makes its administrative working worth a separate look, because there is no judgment to announce the result and no petitioner to pursue it. When a sitting member is convicted of an offence within Section 8, the disqualification attaches by operation of law on the date of conviction, and the seat becomes vacant by the command of Article 101(3)(a) or Article 190(3)(a) as the case may be. The House secretariat acts on the record of the conviction: the vacancy is notified, the member's name is removed from the roll of members, and the machinery that notifies casual vacancies to the Election Commission for the purposes of a by election is set in motion, the by election itself following the six month discipline of Section 151A unless an exception applies. No institution votes on whether the member should go, and the secretariat exercises no discretion about the legal consequence; its function is to register a vacancy the law has already created. Where the conviction is afterwards stayed by the appellate court, the same machinery runs in reverse, and the notification restoring the membership follows the stay, as the sequence of August 2023 showed in public view.

Disputes within the track take predictable shapes, and each has its own destination. A dispute about whether the offence of conviction is among the listed offences of Section 8(1), or whether the sentence crosses the floor of Section 8(2) or Section 8(3), is a dispute about the meaning of the conviction record, and where it is raised as a question about a sitting member it travels the reference route, the Commission examining the judgment and the sentence and opining on the statutory fit. A dispute about whether the conviction exists at all, as where identity is mistaken, is a question of fact for the same inquiry. A convicted person who is not a sitting member faces the track at the nomination table instead: the disqualification under Section 8 is a disqualification for being chosen as a member, the returning officer must reject the nomination of a candidate who stands disqualified on the date fixed for scrutiny where the fact is brought home under the nomination rules, and the candidate's remedy for a wrongful rejection is the petition, where the improper rejection of a nomination is a ground that annuls without inquiry into material effect. The two ends of the track meet there: the petition polices exclusions from the election that were wrongly made, and the conviction track polices inclusions that the law forbids.

The member restored by a stay of conviction returns to a seat whose remainder has been running in his absence, and the episode leaves two residues that the law does register. The by election, if one was set in motion and held, cannot be unheld for his benefit; the restoration works where the vacancy was notified but the seat not yet refilled, which is the ordinary case because the appellate intervention in the modern instances has come within days or weeks. And the period of the disqualification itself runs on its own terms from conviction and release as Section 8 prescribes, so that a conviction ultimately affirmed re-imposes the vacancy with the sentence's appellate history already consumed, while a conviction ultimately set aside on appeal extinguishes the disqualification root and branch, the member or candidate standing as though the conviction had not been. The track is therefore unforgiving in sequence but exact in logic: everything turns on the conviction, an event in a criminal court in which the election law takes no part, and the election law's whole apparatus of petitions, periods and pleadings is bypassed by a single page of a sessions judgment.

## The Reference Route: Disqualification Questions Decided Without a Trial

### How can a sitting member be disqualified without an election petition?

A sitting member is disqualified without a petition where the disqualification flows from conviction under Section 8, which operates automatically on the conviction itself, or where a question whether the member has become subject to any of the disqualifications in Article 102 or Article 191 is referred for decision.

The question goes to the President for a member of Parliament and to the Governor for a member of a State Legislature; the President or Governor obtains the opinion of the Election Commission, and the decision is given in accordance with that opinion.

Article 103 provides that if any question arises as to whether a member of either House of Parliament has become subject to any of the disqualifications mentioned in Article 102(1), the question shall be referred for the decision of the President, whose decision shall be final, and that before giving any decision the President shall obtain the opinion of the Election Commission and shall act according to such opinion. Article 192 makes the identical arrangement for State Legislatures, substituting the Governor. The finality language is shared with the opinion mandate, and the Supreme Court read the two together in Brundaban Nayak v Election Commission of India: the opinion of the Commission is not advice in the ordinary administrative sense, it is the substance of the decision, and the constitutional head's order gives it formal effect. The procedure before the Commission is inquisitorial rather than adversarial in the petition sense: the Commission inquires into the allegation, hears the member and the complainant where it thinks fit, and returns an opinion on the fact and the law of the disqualification. There is no pleading discipline of the Section 83 kind, no forty five day limitation keyed to a declaration of result, and no six month endeavour; the question travels because an informant, a rival, a citizen or the House itself has raised it, and it is answered as a reference rather than contested as a suit.

The route has boundaries that the text itself fixes. It covers the disqualifications in clause (1) of Article 102 or Article 191: holding an office of profit under the government other than an office Parliament or the State Legislature has by law declared not to disqualify, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, undischarged insolvency, loss or voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship or acknowledgment of allegiance to a foreign State, and disqualification under any law made by Parliament, the category under which the Act's own grounds, including the conviction grounds when a question about them is raised in reference form, and the office and contract grounds of Sections 9, 9A and 10, are brought in. Defection under the Tenth Schedule is expressly outside the route: paragraph 6 of the Schedule commits the question to the Chairman or the Speaker of the House, whose decision is final subject to the judicial review recognised in Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu, and neither the President nor the Governor nor the Commission enters it. The division of labour is exact: the petition polices the election, the reference polices the member, the Schedule polices the member's loyalty, and the conviction track polices itself by automatic operation. Confusion between the routes is the single most common error in public discussion of a legislator's fall, and it produces the familiar spectacle of a disqualification being demanded from a forum that has no power to give it.

The office of profit ground is the reference route's most frequent traveller, and its working shows the Commission's method at its clearest. The Constitution nowhere defines an office of profit; the working test, settled across the cases, asks whether the office is one under the government, in the sense that the government appoints, can remove, pays and controls the holder, and whether the office is capable of yielding a profit or pecuniary gain, the actual receipt of gain being unnecessary where the office carries the possibility of it. Governments, Union and State, have fenced large classes of offices away from the ground by exemption legislation, of which the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act 1959 is the Union exemplar, listing offices whose holding does not disqualify a member of Parliament; a member who holds an office within the list is safe, and the contest moves to whether the particular office, with its particular appointing authority, remuneration and functions, falls within or outside the schedule. The reference against the actor and member of the Rajya Sabha Jaya Bachchan in 2006 ran the full route: a complaint alleged that her chairpersonship of the Uttar Pradesh Film Development Council was an office of profit, the President referred the question to the Election Commission under Article 103, the Commission inquired and returned an opinion that she had held an office of profit and stood disqualified from the date of her appointment to it, and the President's order followed the opinion, the Supreme Court declining to disturb a conclusion that turned on the capability of the office to yield gain rather than on any gain shown to have been received. The episode is the reference route in normal operation: slow by the standards of a headline, unbothered by any filing window, and conclusive without a trial, because the question was one of status and law rather than of contested constituency fact. It also shows the route's characteristic politics, since references arrive as complaints from rivals and citizens, and the State Legislature's power to enlarge the exemption lists retrospectively has repeatedly been used, and repeatedly litigated, as the answer to a pending allegation.

Section 8A completes the map by showing where the petition track and the reference track meet. A person found guilty of a corrupt practice by the High Court's order under Section 99 does not stand disqualified by the finding alone; Section 8A requires that the case of every such person be submitted to the President, that the President decide, on the opinion of the Election Commission, whether the person shall be disqualified and, if so, for what period, not exceeding six years from the date of the order. The Commission holds an inquiry, the person is heard, and the disqualification that results is a disqualification for membership and for voting, the only one in the Act's scheme from which Section 11's power to remove or reduce is withheld. The convicted member under Section 8, by contrast, is disqualified by the conviction itself before any institution has deliberated about him at all. The three routes therefore differ in the most important respect a remedy can differ: whether anything must be proved, decided or ordered before the seat moves.

## Disclosure, the False Affidavit and the Candidate with a Record

The third track began as litigation rather than legislation. In Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms, decided on 2 May 2002, the Supreme Court directed the Election Commission to require candidates to disclose pending criminal cases, convictions, assets and liabilities, and educational qualifications, grounding the direction in the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a): a vote cast in ignorance of the candidate's record is not the informed choice the freedom of expression protects. Parliament responded with provisions including Section 33A of the Act, which wrote a part of the disclosure into the statute, and Section 33B, which attempted to confine disclosure to what the statute itself required; the Court struck Section 33B down in the People's Union for Civil Liberties case on 13 March 2003, and the fuller affidavit regime survived in the form the Commission administers, the affidavit in Form 26 under the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961. Every candidate files it with the nomination: criminal cases pending and concluded, assets and liabilities of the candidate, the spouse and dependants, educational qualifications. The returning officer displays it; rival candidates mine it; and its contents feed both of the other tracks.

The false affidavit carries two distinct consequences, and the difference between them is the point on which seed complaints most often fail. The first is prosecution. Section 125A, inserted with effect from 24 August 2002, makes it an offence for a candidate, with intent to be elected, to fail to furnish the information Section 33A requires, to give false information which he knows or has reason to believe to be false, or to conceal any information, in the nomination paper or the affidavit; the punishment extends to imprisonment for six months, or fine, or both, and a private complaint in respect of the offence is maintainable. The offence punishes the falsehood; it does not vacate the seat, and a conviction under Section 125A, carrying at most six months, does not by itself reach the two year floor of Section 8(3) or the listed offences of Section 8(1). The second consequence lives inside the petition track. In Krishnamoorthy v Sivakumar, decided in 2015, the Supreme Court held that the concealment of criminal antecedents in the affidavit is capable of amounting to undue influence within Section 123(2), a corrupt practice, because the suppression denies the electors the information on which the disclosure regime entitles them to act, and in Lok Prahari v Election Commission of India, decided in 2018, the Court held that the failure to disclose assets, and the sources of income of the candidate, the spouse and the dependants, bears on the same informed choice and may found a challenge as undue influence. The false affidavit therefore unseats not through the criminal provision aimed at it, but through the corrupt practice provisions aimed at the distortion of the voter's choice, and only within the forty five days, on the pleading discipline, and to the standard of proof that the petition track demands.

The harder question is whether the candidate with a record should be permitted to reach the affidavit stage at all. The pattern the disclosure regime was built to expose is documented in the Supreme Court's own narration in Rambabu Singh Thakur v Sunil Arora: the share of members of the Lok Sabha against whom criminal cases were pending stood at 24 per cent in 2004, 30 per cent in 2009, 34 per cent in 2014 and 43 per cent in 2019. Reform proposals pressed on the Court have included the disqualification of candidates against whom charges have been framed for serious offences, a measure the Election Commission and the Law Commission have both supported in various forms. The Constitution Bench refused it in Public Interest Foundation v Union of India, decided on 25 September 2018. The refusal rested on the constitutional text: Article 102(1)(e) and Article 191(1)(e) commit the grounds of disqualification to law made by Parliament, Section 7(b) defines a disqualified person as one disqualified under Chapter III and not otherwise, and a court that added charge framing to the grounds would be legislating a disqualification the Constitution reserves to Parliament, besides drawing a line of enormous practical consequence, between the framing of a charge and conviction, on material that includes cases that end in acquittal. The bench also declined the indirect device of denying the party symbol to parties that field such candidates, holding that the court could not achieve obliquely what the text forbade directly, and it urged Parliament to enact a law strong enough to keep persons facing heinous and serious charges out of the political arena.

What the 2018 bench did instead was to multiply the publicity of the record. Its directions required the candidate to state the pending criminal cases in bold in the prescribed form and to inform the political party, and required the party to place the information on its website and both the candidate and the party to declare the antecedents in widely circulated newspapers and in electronic media at least three times after the filing of nomination. The contempt proceedings of 2020 exposed the gap between direction and practice, and Rambabu Singh Thakur v Sunil Arora, decided on 13 February 2020, tightened the screws: every political party at the central and the State level must upload on its website the details of candidates with pending criminal cases, the nature of the offences, whether charges have been framed, the court and the case number, together with the reasons for selecting the candidate and the reasons why persons without criminal antecedents could not be selected in his place; the reasons must relate to the qualifications, achievements and merit of the candidate and not merely to winnability; publication must also run in one local vernacular newspaper and one national newspaper and on the party's social media accounts, within forty eight hours of the selection or not less than two weeks before the first date of filing nominations, whichever is earlier; and a compliance report must reach the Election Commission within seventy two hours, on pain of the Commission reporting the default to the Supreme Court as contempt. The regime that results is disclosure with escalating pressure and no bar: the candidate with a record may contest, the record must be proclaimed by the candidate, the party and the press, and the remedy for the voter who objects remains what it has been since 1951, the vote itself, followed, for the defects that the law recognises, by the petition and the reference.

The limits of the disclosure philosophy deserve the same candour as its achievements, because the two decades of the regime supply the evidence for both. Disclosure assumes an electorate that reads, a press that amplifies and a party system that fears the publicity it is ordered to generate. Where the candidate's record is locally known and discounted, where criminality is read by voters as evidence of the capacity to protect a community or to deliver against an indifferent administration, publication informs without deterring, and the rising percentages the Supreme Court recited in 2020 measure precisely that gap between information and consequence. The publicity directions also bind asymmetrically: a candidate fielded by a small or unregistered party, or standing as an independent, generates less coverage than the candidate of a major party, and the contempt machinery reaches a party's website more readily than it reaches the ground level silence of a constituency organisation. None of this returns the argument to charge framing, which the 2018 bench refused on constitutional grounds that disclosure cannot displace; the charge that has been framed by a court after hearing is still not a conviction, cases still end in acquittal, discharge and quashing in substantial numbers, and a disqualification keyed to framing would hand rivals and prosecutors a lever over candidature that the framers of Articles 102 and 191 were unwilling to create by implication. The settled position is therefore a considered one, and not merely a default: Parliament holds the power to add the ground and has not exercised it, the Court holds the publicity lever and has pulled it to its limit, and the candidate with a record stands or falls at the count, exactly where the Constitution Bench of 2018 concluded he must.

## Where Petitions Are Lost: The Recurring Errors

The first error is temporal, and it is unforced. The forty five days are spent in deliberation, in waiting for party authorisation, in negotiating with prospective petitioners, or in assembling a campaign grievance at leisure, and the petition is settled in the final week with the particulars the schedule allows rather than the particulars the facts require. The petitions that survive are prepared in reverse: the pleading is drafted first, the investigation conducted against its headings, and the filing made with days in hand. Court fees, the deposit and the affidavit are treated as closing formalities; in the Act's design they are conditions of existence, and a petition short of copies, short of the deposit or short of a compliant affidavit has failed before its merits are opened. The second error is pleading generality, the assumption that a corrupt practice can be alleged as a pattern and proved as a record. The Act wants incidents: this payment, this meeting, this vehicle, this person, this date, this place. A petitioner with a true constituency wide story must still reduce it to a finite set of pleaded incidents, proved beyond reasonable doubt, and a petitioner who cannot name the incidents does not yet have a petition whatever he may have by way of conviction about the campaign.

The third error is omission of the Section 84 claim or of the parties it drags in. A petitioner who wants the seat, or wants his candidate seated, must claim the declaration in the petition as presented and must join every contesting candidate; the claim cannot be added when the trial turns favourable, and the recrimination notice period runs from the commencement of the trial whether or not the petitioner is ready for the counter attack it invites. The fourth error belongs to the conviction track: instructing a convicted member's defence as though the sentence were the seat. Bail and suspension of sentence leave the disqualification untouched; the application that matters is the stay of the conviction, and it must be moved and argued on the conviction's merits and on the irreversibility of the consequences, not on the hardship of imprisonment alone. The fifth error is forum confusion, the pursuit of a reference answer to a petition question or a writ against a result, consuming the only weeks in which the correct proceeding could have been started. The sixth error is sequential neglect at the end of the case: parties who win at the High Court and treat the appeal period as a formality, or who lose and file the appeal without moving the stay application as the first and urgent business, discover that under Section 107 the judgment operates from its pronouncement and that the seat, the by election notification and the constituency's representation all move on the trial court's order while the appeal is still being prepared. On both tracks and at both ends of the proceeding, the calendar is not the background of the case; it is a substantial part of its merits, and it is the part least capable of repair after the event. This article explains the law of election petitions and disqualification as enacted and interpreted; it is general information about a statutory scheme and not legal advice for any individual case, and a person contemplating a petition, a complaint or a defence should take advice on the facts and the record of the particular election. The remedy is technical in a way that forgives nothing: its clocks are short, its forms are conditions, and its consequences, for the constituency and the member alike, are decided as much by the calendar as by the merits.

## The Counter Reading, and the Reform Agenda

The strictness of the petition regime has a defence, and it deserves to be stated at full strength because the stance of this series is that the better argument is the one that survives its opponent in good order. A declared result is the legal form of a constituency's collective decision, assembled from the individual acts of hundreds of thousands of voters, and a legal order that allowed it to be unsettled on probabilities, on sympathy or on a judge's impression of a campaign would not have fairer elections; it would have fewer settled ones, and every narrow loser would litigate as a matter of course. The quasi criminal standard and the pleading discipline protect more than the returned candidate. They protect the voters whose choice is being annulled from annulment on evidence that would suffice in a commercial dispute but is thin beside the act it displaces. The short limitation period protects the constituency's right to know who represents it, and the refusal of condonation protects the equality of petitioners: the well advised and the ill advised stand under the same clock, and no judge's sense of a meritorious excuse bends it for one and not the other. On this reading, delay in disposal is a failure of judicial administration, not of statutory design, and the cure lies in the roster and the calendar rather than in the Act.

The objection accepts much of that and presses where it hurts. A six month endeavour that is habitually missed by years is not a timetable but a sentiment, and a remedy whose realistic duration equals or exceeds the term it polices fails the first test of a remedy, which is that it should be capable of restoring what was taken before the taking is complete. The counter reading also points to the asymmetry inside the statute itself: the petitioner's lapses are jurisdictional and incurable, while the state's lapse, a trial that runs into its fourth year, attracts no consequence for anyone, not for the court, not for the parties, and not for the member who holds the seat in the interval. Strictness at entry paired with indulgence in disposal is the worst combination available, because it screens out the meritorious late case with total efficiency while leaving the timely meritorious case to decay at leisure. The defenders of the design answer that an enforceable trial timetable in a constitutional court raises separation problems of its own, that petitions fail on proof far more often than they fail on time, and that the constituency loses nothing irrecoverable because findings and disqualification consequences travel beyond the term. The objection replies that a constituency represented for five years by a candidate later found to have been returned by corrupt practice has lost precisely the thing the election existed to give it, and that the finding is a record, not a representation.

The reforms on the table, proposed across the decades by the Election Commission in its reform proposals and examined by the Law Commission in its 255th Report on electoral reforms of 2015, cluster into three families. The first is capacity: dedicated election benches in the High Courts, so that petitions are not queued behind the general civil roster and the assigned judge's acquaintance with the subject accumulates rather than resets with every transfer. The second is time with teeth: converting the six month endeavour into a statutory period whose breach carries a stated consequence, whether the automatic reporting of pending petitions, the loss of the trial court's seisin to a designated bench, or mandatory day to day listing enforced by the Chief Justice's administrative side. The third is triage: a summary procedure for defined categories of petition, those resting on pure questions of law such as a subsisting disqualification on the date of election, or on counting disputes capable of resolution from the statutory record, so that the full trial machinery is reserved for the corrupt practice cases that genuinely need it. None of the three families has been enacted. The pattern of the series is completed here in its sharpest form: the diagnosis of the petition's failure has been agreed, in substance, by the institutions charged with the subject for a generation, and the amendment that would cure it remains, like the judgment in the petition itself, awaited beyond the term.

For candidates, researchers and civil services aspirants who need the petition machinery placed inside the wider examination map of the subject, the companion treatment in [the UPSC polity guide to election laws](/2018/04/15/election-laws-upsc-polity-guide/) arranges the same provisions for study. The verdict of the practical study is narrower than the debate. An election petition in India is a remedy of exacting design and uncertain duration: complete in its coverage of the defects that can vitiate a result, strict to the point of harshness in the conditions of its use, and unenforced in the single respect, time, on which its value most depends. The citizen who uses it must be early, precise and patient in about that order. The constituency that depends on it receives, in the ordinary case, a correct answer to the question who should have represented it, delivered after the representation has ended. Until Parliament writes a consequence into the six month endeavour, the petition will remain what the challenge clock shows it to be: a remedy that expires with the House.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How is a declared election result challenged?

A declared result is challenged only by an election petition presented to the High Court of the State in which the constituency is situated, under Part VI of the Representation of the People Act 1951. Article 329(b) of the Constitution and Section 80 of the Act close every other route: no writ, civil suit or complaint can call the election in question. The petition must be presented by a candidate at the election or an elector of the constituency within forty five days from the date of election of the returned candidate, the date on which the returning officer declared him elected. It must join the prescribed respondents, state the material facts and the particulars of any corrupt practice alleged, carry the supporting affidavit in Form 25 where a corrupt practice is charged, and be accompanied by the security deposit of two thousand rupees under Section 117. The High Court tries it through a single judge assigned by the Chief Justice, applying the Civil Procedure Code and the Evidence Act, and may dismiss it, declare the election void, or declare another candidate duly elected. An appeal lies to the Supreme Court within thirty days.

### Q: What are the grounds for declaring an election void?

The grounds are stated exhaustively in Section 100(1) of the Representation of the People Act 1951, and a petition that fits none of them fails. The High Court shall declare the returned candidate's election void if, on the date of his election, he was not qualified or was disqualified under the Constitution or the Act; if a corrupt practice was committed by the returned candidate or his election agent, or by another person with the consent of either; if any nomination was improperly rejected; or if the result, so far as it concerns the returned candidate, was materially affected by the improper acceptance of a nomination, by a corrupt practice committed in his interests by an agent other than his election agent, by the improper reception, refusal or rejection of votes or the reception of a void vote, or by non compliance with the Constitution, the Act or the rules and orders under it. The first three grounds annul on proof of the fact alone. The fourth requires the petitioner to prove, in addition, that the irregularity materially affected the result, a burden that defeats petitions where the established error is smaller than the margin of victory.

### Q: What is the time limit for filing an election petition?

The limit is forty five days, prescribed by Section 81(1) of the Representation of the People Act 1951. The period runs from, but not earlier than, the date of election of the returned candidate, which under Section 67A is the date on which the returning officer declares him elected; where several returned candidates at a general election were elected on different dates, the later date governs. The period is absolute. The Act is treated as a complete code for election disputes, Section 5 of the Limitation Act 1963 does not apply to it, and no court may condone delay for any reason, however strong the excuse; a petition presented on the forty sixth day must be dismissed under Section 86(1). The only calendar relief comes from the general law of computation: where the last day falls on a day on which the court is closed, presentation on the day the court reopens is protected by Section 10 of the General Clauses Act 1897. Because the deadline admits no excuse, preparation of the pleading, the affidavit and the deposit must be completed inside the same forty five days.

### Q: Can a candidate be disqualified for a false affidavit?

Not directly. The false affidavit is an offence, not a disqualification. Section 125A of the Representation of the People Act 1951 punishes a candidate who, with intent to be elected, fails to furnish the information required by Section 33A, gives false information he knows or has reason to believe is false, or conceals information in the nomination paper or the affidavit, with imprisonment up to six months, or fine, or both. A conviction carrying at most six months does not meet the two year floor of Section 8(3) and the offence is not among those listed in Section 8(1), so the conviction alone does not disqualify. The affidavit's real danger to the candidate lies in the election petition. Under the reasoning of Krishnamoorthy v Sivakumar, concealment of criminal antecedents in the affidavit may amount to undue influence under Section 123(2), a corrupt practice that voids the election; and under Lok Prahari v Election Commission of India, the failure to disclose assets and the sources of income of the candidate, spouse and dependants bears on the voter's informed choice in the same way. The falsehood unseats through the petition, within forty five days and on proof, not through the prosecution.

### Q: What happens to a seat when a legislator is convicted?

The seat becomes vacant from the date of conviction, automatically. Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 disqualifies a person convicted of the offences listed in Section 8(1) on conviction alone, a person convicted under the laws named in Section 8(2) where the sentence is at least six months, and a person convicted of any other offence under Section 8(3) where the sentence is at least two years, in the last two cases from the date of conviction and continuing for six years after release. Since Lily Thomas v Union of India, decided on 10 July 2013, the disqualification takes effect immediately: the three month shelter and the pendency protection that the struck down Section 8(4) gave sitting members no longer exist, and Articles 101(3)(a) and 190(3)(a) make the seat vacant when the disqualification attaches. The member's only protection is appellate: a stay of the sentence leaves the disqualification untouched, while a stay of the conviction itself suspends the disqualification from the date of the stay order, and the membership then stands restored through the same secretariat machinery that notified the vacancy.

### Q: Are candidates with criminal cases barred from contesting?

No. A pending criminal case, even one in which charges have been framed, is not a disqualification under the Representation of the People Act 1951, whose Chapter III grounds begin with conviction. The Constitution Bench in Public Interest Foundation v Union of India, decided on 25 September 2018, refused to add charge framing as a ground: Articles 102(1)(e) and 191(1)(e) reserve disqualification grounds to law made by Parliament, and the Court would not legislate one, nor achieve the same end indirectly by denying the party symbol to parties fielding such candidates. The bench urged Parliament to enact a law to keep persons facing heinous and serious charges out of politics, which Parliament has not done. What the law requires instead is disclosure and publicity: the candidate must declare the pending cases, the party must publish them with reasons for the selection, and the declaration must be repeated in newspapers and electronic media. A candidate is barred only where a ground in the Act is made out, principally a conviction within Section 8, an undischarged insolvency, or a subsisting disqualification whose period has not run.

### Q: What must parties disclose about candidates facing criminal cases?

Following Public Interest Foundation v Union of India of 25 September 2018 and the directions in Rambabu Singh Thakur v Sunil Arora of 13 February 2020, a political party that fields a candidate with pending criminal cases must publish the record prominently and explain the choice. The party must upload on its website the details of each such candidate: the nature of the offences, whether charges have been framed, the court concerned and the case number. It must also state the reasons for selecting that candidate and the reasons why persons without criminal antecedents could not be selected in his place, and those reasons must rest on the candidate's qualifications, achievements and merit, not merely on winnability. The same information must be published in one local vernacular newspaper and one national newspaper and on the party's social media accounts, within forty eight hours of the selection or not less than two weeks before the first date for filing nominations, whichever is earlier. A compliance report must reach the Election Commission within seventy two hours of the selection, and a default reported by the Commission to the Supreme Court is treated as contempt of the Court's directions.

### Q: Why do election petitions outlast the term?

Because the Act enforces every deadline except its own. The petitioner's forty five days are jurisdictional and admit no condonation, but the trial provisions are framed as endeavours: Section 86(6) directs a day to day trial so far as practicable, and Section 86(7) directs an endeavour to conclude the trial within six months, with no consequence prescribed for breach. The trial is a full civil trial before a single judge of a High Court who carries an ordinary roster, on evidence spread across a constituency, with threshold objections, amendment disputes and interlocutory challenges consuming the early years. Witnesses to a campaign are partisan, documents are voluminous, and the quasi criminal standard extends cross examination. Judgment, when it comes, is followed by an appeal to the Supreme Court with its own span, and the returned candidate sits as a full member throughout, because the Act contains no provision suspending membership under challenge. The House itself runs for five years, so a petition that takes three years at trial and two in appeal decides the validity of a representation that has already been fully exercised, and its findings matter chiefly for disqualification, costs and the record.

### Q: Who can file an election petition?

Section 81(1) of the Representation of the People Act 1951 allows an election petition to be presented by any candidate at the election or by any elector. A candidate at the election includes every person who contested, a person whose nomination paper was rejected by the returning officer, and a person who withdrew his candidature in accordance with the rules, so a grievance about exclusion from the contest can be carried by the excluded person himself. The Explanation to Section 81 defines an elector, for this purpose, as a person who was entitled to vote at the election to which the petition relates, whether he has voted at that election or not; standing therefore depends on being on the electoral roll of the constituency for that election, not on having cast a vote, and not on having voted against the returned candidate. A person outside those two categories cannot petition however genuine the grievance: a political party as an organisation, a voter of another constituency, and a citizen moved by a reported corrupt practice must all act, if at all, through a candidate or an elector of that constituency who presents the petition in his own name.

### Q: Can an election petition challenge an unopposed return?

Yes. An unopposed return is still an election, and the returned candidate is declared elected without a poll because the number of contesting candidates equals the number of seats to be filled. The petition lies against that declaration on the grounds Section 100 supplies, and the ground that does the characteristic work is the improper rejection of a nomination under Section 100(1)(c): where the returning officer wrongly rejected the nomination of another candidate, the constituency was denied the contest the law required, and the court annuls without asking how the excluded candidate would have fared, since a wrongly excluded candidacy vitiates the election. A petition may also rest on the ground that the unopposed candidate was, on the date of his election, not qualified or was disqualified under Section 100(1)(a). The petitioner must be a candidate at the election, which includes the candidate whose nomination was rejected, or an elector of the constituency, and the forty five day period runs from the declaration of the unopposed return. What the petitioner cannot do is treat the absence of a poll as a ground; the complaint must still be brought within the Section 100 clauses.

### Q: Can the runner-up be declared elected if the winner's election is set aside?

Yes, but only where the petitioner claimed that declaration and proves one of the two situations Section 101 prescribes. The claim is made under Section 84, in addition to calling the election in question, and it changes the shape of the case: every contesting candidate must be joined as a respondent under Section 82, and the respondents gain the right of recrimination under Section 97, to give evidence that the candidate proposed for the seat would himself not have been duly elected. On the merits, the High Court may declare the petitioner, or another candidate, duly elected only if in fact that candidate received a majority of the valid votes, or if, but for the votes obtained by the returned candidate by corrupt practices, that candidate would have obtained a majority of the valid votes. Outside those cases the court annuls and stops, and the seat is filled by a fresh election. The declaration is therefore a finding about the votes actually cast, not a judgment about who deserved to win, and a petitioner who wants it must plead and prove the count, or the corrupt practice and its vote consequences, with full particularity.

### Q: What happens to the constituency when an election is declared void and no other candidate is declared elected?

The seat falls vacant and the constituency goes to a by election. The High Court's order under Section 98 declaring the election void takes effect as soon as it is pronounced under Section 107, subject to any stay granted by the Supreme Court in appeal, and it is transmitted to the appropriate authority and the Election Commission and published in the Official Gazette under Section 106. The Election Commission then fills the vacancy under Section 151A of the Act, which requires a by election to be held within six months from the date the vacancy arose, except where the remainder of the term is less than one year or the Commission, in consultation with the Central Government, certifies that holding the by election within the period is difficult. The fresh election is a complete election for the remainder of the term, with nominations and a fresh poll, and the candidate whose election was annulled may contest it unless a disqualification attaches to him, as it will where the annulment rested on a corrupt practice finding carried into a Section 8A disqualification or where a conviction ground applies. The constituency is unrepresented in the interval.

### Q: Does a returned candidate remain a member while the election petition is pending?

Yes, fully. The presentation of an election petition does not suspend, qualify or shadow the returned candidate's membership in law. He takes his seat, speaks and votes, draws salary and allowances, holds committee positions and is eligible for ministerial office, and the Act contains no provision for interim suspension pending trial. His position changes only at judgment: an order declaring his election void takes effect on pronouncement under Section 107, and his continuance thereafter depends on a stay of that order from the Supreme Court under Section 116B. The validity of what he did while a member is protected even if the petition succeeds, because the acts of the House are not unwound by the later annulment of one member's title. This undisturbed possession is the central practical fact of election litigation. It means the petitioner bears the whole cost of delay, that the deterrent value of a corrupt practice finding is discounted by the years of office enjoyed before it, and that a petitioner deciding whether to file must value the remedy realistically, as a judgment about status, findings and costs first, and as a route to the seat only where the trial concludes well within the term.

### Q: What is the time limit for appealing to the Supreme Court against a High Court order in an election petition?

Thirty days from the date of the order, under Section 116A of the Representation of the People Act 1951. The appeal lies from every order of the High Court under Section 98 or Section 99, which includes dismissal of the petition, a declaration that an election is void, a declaration installing another candidate, and the findings and costs recorded under Section 99. A dismissal at the threshold under Section 86(1), for limitation, parties or deposit defects, is also appealable, because the section declares it to be deemed an order under Section 98(a), as the Supreme Court confirmed in Hari Shanker Jain v Sonia Gandhi. The thirty day period differs from the petitioner's forty five days in one important respect: the Supreme Court may entertain an appeal after the period expires if satisfied that the appellant had sufficient cause for the delay, a condonation power the Act withholds at the petition stage. Filing the appeal does not itself halt the judgment; the appellant who needs the seat, or needs to stop the by election machinery, must obtain a stay of the order's operation under Section 116B, on the terms the Court imposes.

### Q: Can an election petition be withdrawn after it is filed?

Only with the leave of the High Court, and never as a private bargain. Sections 109 to 111 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 govern the exit. An application to withdraw may be made after presentation, but where there is more than one petitioner it requires the consent of all of them, and Section 110 forbids the grant where the High Court thinks the application was induced by a bargain or consideration that ought not to be allowed, a rule aimed at the respondent who buys his peace. Where leave is granted, the petitioner is ordered to pay the respondents' costs as the court directs, and the notice of the withdrawal is published in the Official Gazette and as the court specifies. Publication opens a substitution window: any person who might himself have been a petitioner may, within fourteen days of the publication, apply to be substituted in the withdrawing petitioner's place and continue the proceedings, on complying with the conditions as to security. The High Court reports the withdrawal to the Election Commission under Section 111. A petition therefore survives its petitioner's second thoughts wherever another candidate or elector is willing to carry it.

### Q: What happens to an election petition if the petitioner or the respondent dies?

The two deaths are treated differently. On the petitioner's side, Section 112 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 provides that a petition abates only on the death of a sole petitioner or of the survivor of several petitioners, since the right to petition is personal and does not pass to heirs. Abatement is not the end of the matter: the High Court causes the fact to be published, and any person who might himself have been a petitioner may, within fourteen days of the publication, apply to be substituted as petitioner and continue the proceedings on the prescribed terms. On the respondent's side, Section 113 proceeds from the opposite rule: the petition does not abate on the death of the respondent, because its subject is the validity of the election. The High Court causes notice of the event to be published in the constituency, and any person who might have been a petitioner may, within fourteen days of the publication, apply to be substituted in place of the respondent to oppose the petition, on such terms as the court thinks fit. A petition that nobody steps forward to defend still runs its course.

### Q: What are the consequences for a person named by the High Court as guilty of a corrupt practice?

A naming under Section 99 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 exposes the person to disqualification through Section 8A. Along with its order under Section 98, the High Court records whether a corrupt practice has been proved and names every person proved to have been guilty of one. The case of each person so found guilty is then submitted to the President, who decides, on the opinion of the Election Commission and after the inquiry the Commission holds, whether the person shall be disqualified and, if so, for what period, which may extend to six years from the date of the order. The disqualification covers membership of Parliament and the State Legislatures and voting at elections, and it is the one disqualification the Election Commission's general power under Section 11, to remove a disqualification or reduce its period, cannot touch. Because the consequence is severe, Section 99 protects a person who was not a party to the petition: he may not be named unless he was given notice to show cause, an opportunity to cross examine witnesses already examined against him, an opportunity to call evidence in his defence, and an opportunity to be heard.

### Q: What security deposit must an election petitioner make, and can it be refunded?

Section 117 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 requires the petitioner, at the time of presenting the petition, to deposit in the High Court, under the rules of the Court, a sum of two thousand rupees as security for the costs of the petition. Failure to make the deposit is non compliance within Section 86(1), so the petition is liable to mandatory dismissal before its merits are reached, and during the trial the High Court may direct the petitioner to give further security for costs as the trial grows. The deposit is security, not a fee. Under Sections 119 and 120 it stands available for the payment of costs awarded against the petitioner, and at the conclusion of the trial the deposit is applied as those sections direct, with any balance remaining after the costs orders are satisfied disposed of according to the court's order, which in the ordinary case means return to the petitioner. A petitioner who succeeds will ordinarily also recover his costs from the respondent through the costs order recorded under Section 99. The modest figure, unchanged for decades, keeps the only remedy for a vitiated election open to petitioners without wealth.

### Q: What is the difference between bribery and undue influence as corrupt practices?

Both are corrupt practices under Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act 1951, but they corrupt the vote by different means. Bribery, under Section 123(1), is the giving or offering of a gratification to a person with the object, directly or indirectly, of inducing an elector to vote or refrain from voting, or of rewarding an elector for having voted or refrained, and the clause reaches the agreement and the receipt sides of the transaction as well; the vice is the purchase of the vote. Undue influence, under Section 123(2), is any interference or attempt to interfere with the free exercise of an electoral right, and it includes threats of injury of any kind, threats of social ostracism or excommunication, and inducing a candidate or elector to believe that he will become an object of divine displeasure or spiritual censure; the vice is coercion of a choice money was not needed to move. The clause expressly protects legitimate persuasion: a declaration of public policy, a promise of public action and the mere exercise of a legal right are not undue influence. Both must be pleaded with full particulars and proved beyond reasonable doubt.

### Q: What standard of proof must an allegation of corrupt practice meet?

The standard is the criminal standard: proof beyond reasonable doubt. The Supreme Court has held consistently, in a line of authority of which Rahim Khan v Khurshid Ahmed is the leading statement, that a charge of corrupt practice is quasi criminal in character, because it carries grave consequences, including unseating and a possible Section 8A disqualification, and it must therefore be established with the rigour of a criminal charge. Suspicion, however strong, is not proof; the evidence must be clear, consistent and credible, and where the testimony of partisan witnesses leaves the incident in genuine doubt, the benefit goes to the returned candidate. The standard works together with the pleading discipline: the petitioner must first plead material facts and full particulars under Section 83, may not lead evidence beyond the pleading, and must then prove the pleaded incidents to the criminal standard by witnesses who survive cross examination. Other grounds in a petition, such as counting errors under the materially affected clause of Section 100(1)(d), are proved on the civil standard applicable to the fact in issue, but the corrupt practice charges that dominate election trials are governed throughout by the stricter measure.
