Tamil Nadu politics presents a puzzle that no other large Indian state reproduces. Since the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam won the Madras assembly election of 1967, every government the state has known has been led by the DMK or by its offshoot, the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, and every assembly contest has been a fight between two fronts that one of these two parties heads. The Indian National Congress, which governed the state for the first twenty years after independence, has not headed a Tamil Nadu government since 1967. The Bharatiya Janata Party, the dominant party of Indian national politics after 2014, won a single Tamil Nadu Lok Sabha seat on its own symbol in 2014, at Kanyakumari, and won none in 2019 or 2024. Party systems elsewhere in India have fragmented, fused, realigned and collapsed within a single decade. This one has held its shape for fifty-seven years.

![The Tamil Nadu DMK AIADMK bipolar system and the Dravidian movement substrate beneath it - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Two standard explanations compete for this record, and each captures something real. The first reads Tamil Nadu through the Dravidian movement: the DMK and the AIADMK appear in this telling as the electoral continuation of a cultural and social revolt that began with the Justice Party in 1916 and Periyar E.V. Ramasamy's Self-Respect Movement in 1925, a revolt against Brahmin predominance in the Madras Presidency's services, colleges and public life, expressed in the Tamil language and armed with a reservation politics older than the Indian republic. M.S.S. Pandian's Brahmin and Non-Brahmin, published in 2007, gives this reading its most rigorous scholarly form, and Sumathi Ramaswamy's Passions of the Tongue, published in 1997, supplies its account of Tamil language devotion as political energy. The second explanation reads Tamil Nadu through personality: the state appears as the stage of four commanding figures, C.N. Annadurai, M. Karunanidhi, M.G. Ramachandran and J. Jayalalithaa, whose writings, films, oratory and organisational grip made and remade the parties in their own images, so that the politics of the state becomes a sequence of biographies.

The argument defended here subordinates both readings to a third. The DMK-AIADMK system is best understood as a bipolar architecture: a durable two-party structure, resting on a four-component Dravidian substrate that both parties share, inside which cultural inheritance supplies the content of competition and personality supplies its operating mechanism, while neither culture nor personality explains the structure's persistence. The evidence for the architectural reading is the system's behaviour under stress. The architecture survived the death of its founder in office in 1969, a split that divided its founding party in 1972, the death of the AIADMK's founder in 1987, the dismissal of state governments by New Delhi in 1976, 1980 and 1991, the near-annihilation of the DMK at the polls in 1991, corruption convictions that removed a sitting chief minister in 2001 and again in 2014, and the deaths, within twenty months of each other, of Jayalalithaa in December 2016 and Karunanidhi in August 2018. Structures that survive their founders, their splits and their disgraces are not held together by charisma or by sentiment. They are held together by design features that reproduce themselves, and it is those features, named and dated, that this article sets out.

The claim can be stated in one sentence. Tamil Nadu's DMK-AIADMK bipolar architecture has operated stably since 1967 because a substrate of anti-Brahmin politics, Tamil-language commitment, resistance to Hindi imposition and social-justice positioning fixes the boundaries of legitimate competition in the state, so that rivals must fight inside the substrate or not at all, and leadership succession, however violent, is processed by two party machines built to absorb it. The claim is falsifiable, and its tests are stated here so that the reading can be judged against the record rather than applauded for its sweep. The bipolar reading would fail if a party outside the two fronts took and held state power without adopting the substrate; if either Dravidian party, in office, abandoned a substrate component, on reservation, on the language formula or on the anti-imposition stance, and prospered by the abandonment; or if leadership succession in either party produced a durable third force rather than a processed transfer or a reabsorbed splinter. None of those events appears in the fifty-seven years under review, and the sections that follow document the near cases, the AIADMK's annihilation-defying returns, the DMK's two-seat nadir of 1991, the breakaway of 2018, precisely because near cases are where a structural claim earns its keep.

The sections that follow build the substrate first, then trace the founding election of 1967, the split of 1972, the alternation machine that ran from 1977 to 2011, the place of personality and welfare inside the architecture, the succession crises of 2016 to 2021, and the failure of the national parties, above all the BJP after 2014, to displace the structure. The 2021 assembly verdict, in which the DMK-led front won 159 of 234 seats and the AIADMK-led front 75, and the 2024 Lok Sabha verdict, in which the DMK-led front won all 39 Tamil Nadu seats, are the tests on which the reading stands or falls, and they are treated as tests rather than as decorations.

## The Four Components of the Dravidian Substrate

The substrate is the layer of political commitments that the DMK and the AIADMK hold in common and that no serious competitor in Tamil Nadu can publicly oppose. It has four components, each with a dated origin, a documentary record and a line of descent into both parties. The four-component substrate map set out in this section names each component, its historical antecedent and the way each Dravidian party operationalised it, and it is the artifact on which the article's central claim rests, because the map shows the same four commitments being carried by rival organisations across a century. A party system whose competitors share a foundation of this depth does not compete over whether the foundation exists. It competes over which party serves the foundation better, and that is a competition the foundation always wins.

Component One is anti-Brahmin politics, and its date of birth is precise. On 20 November 1916, at Victoria Public Hall in Madras, T.M. Nair, P. Theagaraya Chetty and C. Natesa Mudaliar founded the South Indian Liberation Federation, the organisation the public soon called the Justice Party after its English daily newspaper, Justice. The Non-Brahmin Manifesto, issued in December 1916, stated the grievance in statistical form: Brahmins, roughly three percent of the Madras Presidency's population, held a grossly disproportionate share of its gazetted posts, its professional college seats and its places in the services. The Justice Party's argument was not cultural in the first instance. It was an argument about employment, education and the distribution of state patronage, and it won: the Justice ministry that took office under the dyarchy scheme in December 1920 governed the presidency for most of the period to 1937, and it converted the manifesto's arithmetic into administrative practice. When the DMK was founded in 1949 it inherited this component entire, and when MGR founded the AIADMK in 1972 he did not discard it. The AIADMK softened the rhetoric, dropped the Dravidar Kazhagam's atheism and addressed a broader rural electorate, but no AIADMK government has ever defended Brahmin predominance or questioned the non-Brahmin settlement of public employment. The component defines what cannot be said in Tamil Nadu politics, which is the strongest form of political power a substrate can exercise.

Component Two is the Tamil-language commitment, and its roots run deeper than party politics. Sumathi Ramaswamy's Passions of the Tongue (1997) traces the Tamil devotional current from the 1890s, in which the Tamil language itself became an object of veneration, figured as a goddess, Tamilttay, and its speakers as her children bound to sacrifice for her. Maraimalai Adigal's Pure Tamil movement, launched in 1916, sought to purge Tamil of Sanskrit vocabulary, and the linguistic purism of the movement gave the Self-Respect current a literary programme to match its social one. A.R. Venkatachalapathy's essays on Tamil print culture document how journals, pamphlets and conference oratory built a Tamil public sphere in which this devotion could be organised. The DMK converted the devotion into a party identity with unmatched literary force: Annadurai's plays and essays, Karunanidhi's dialogue for the film Parasakthi in 1952, and a cadre culture in which the district conference speech was an art form made the language commitment flesh. The AIADMK, whose founder MGR had acted in films written by both Annadurai and Karunanidhi, carried the same commitment in a different register, and Jayalalithaa, educated at Church Park Convent in Madras and a formidably precise speaker of Tamil, guarded it in office. When the state was renamed from Madras to Tamil Nadu on 14 January 1969, under Annadurai's government, the renaming enacted the component in law: the polity itself would bear the language's name.

The language commitment also acquired the state's ceremonial machinery, and the ceremony was deliberate. In January 1968 Annadurai's government hosted the Second World Tamil Conference in Madras, a week of processions, exhibitions and scholarly sessions that presented Tamil antiquity to the Tamil public as a state pageant; the government installed statues of the language's canonical figures, Thiruvalluvar, Kamban, Avvaiyar and the Kannagi of the Silappathikaram among them, along the Marina beachfront, where they stand as the city's permanent pantheon. A.R. Venkatachalapathy's work on the Tamil public sphere shows why this mattered institutionally: the conference circuit, the journal and the statue were the technologies by which a literary devotion became a mass political identity, repeatable in every district town. The DMK's genius was to give the devotion a party address. The AIADMK, inheriting the same pantheon, has never been able to subtract from it, and no government of either party has dared treat Tamil sentiment as a private taste rather than a public obligation.

Component Three is the stance against Hindi imposition, forged in two agitations a generation apart. In 1937 the Congress ministry of C. Rajagopalachari, Premier of Madras, moved to make Hindi a compulsory subject in the presidency's secondary schools, and the order took effect in 125 schools in 1938. Periyar, who took the presidency of the Justice Party in December 1938 while imprisoned at Bellary, led the resistance with Maraimalai Adigal and a generation of Tamil scholars; two volunteers, Thalamuthu and Natarajan, died in custody in 1939 and entered the movement's martyrology. Rajagopalachari resigned in October 1939, and the Governor, Lord Erskine, withdrew the compulsion in February 1940. The second agitation was larger and bloodier. The Official Languages Act of 1963 left Hindi to become the Union's sole official language on 26 January 1965, and Madras erupted: students boycotted classes, the DMK observed 25 January 1965 as a day of mourning, Chinnasamy, a party volunteer at Tiruchirappalli, burned himself to death in protest in the last week of January, and police firing across the state killed, on the figures most often cited from the period's reporting, around seventy people. Lal Bahadur Shastri's broadcast assurances of 11 February 1965 and the Official Languages (Amendment) Act of 1967, which wrote the continuance of English into statute, closed the crisis, a sequence traced in the reading of [the Hindi-imposition resistance and 1965 agitation](/2019/02/21/hindi-imposition-resistance-1950s-india/). The component's electoral consequence is the hinge of this article: the agitation trained the DMK's organisation, gave it martyrs and made the defence of Tamil a test of political legitimacy that the Congress, identified with New Delhi's language policy, repeatedly failed in the state.

Component Four is social-justice positioning, the administrative heart of the substrate. In September 1921 the Justice ministry issued the first Communal Government Order, distributing appointments in the Madras services among the presidency's communities in stated proportions; the Communal G.O. of 1927, issued under the independent ministry of P. Subbarayan, refined the proportions and extended the principle. Reservation in Tamil Nadu is therefore not a post-1950 constitutional import. It is a century-old local practice that the Constitution's framework met halfway, and when the Supreme Court struck down the Madras communal order in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan in April 1951, the state's political class treated the judgment as an assault on a settled local compact, a grievance Periyar and Annadurai both pressed in the agitation that preceded the First Amendment. The DMK consolidated the inheritance into the state's 69 percent reservation framework, and the Karunanidhi government of 1989 carved a 20 percent Most Backward Classes quota out of the Backward Classes share after the Vanniyar Sangam's agitation of 1987. The AIADMK defended the same framework in office, and it was Jayalalithaa's government that carried the Tamil Nadu reservation statute into the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution in 1994, placing the 69 percent framework beyond ordinary judicial review. On the substrate's fourth component, the two parties have never differed in substance, only in the credit they claim.

## A Party Built Before Power: The DMK from 1949 to 1962

The DMK was founded on 17 September 1949 at Robinson Park in Royapuram, Madras, and the circumstances of its founding explain the discipline with which it was built. Periyar had renamed the Justice Party the Dravidar Kazhagam at Salem in August 1944, on a resolution Annadurai himself moved, converting a political party into a social movement that renounced electoral office. By 1949 the renunciation had become a crisis. Annadurai and the DK's younger organisers wanted a party that could contest elections and hold power; Periyar, aged seventy, married his associate Maniammai in July 1949 and moved to settle the movement's leadership and property on her, which the younger leadership read as the conversion of a public movement into a private household. Annadurai broke, and the breakaway organisation he founded took the name Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Dravidian Progressive Federation. The DMK's founding cadres were DK organisers, its founding doctrine was the substrate in party form, and its founding quarrel taught it a lesson the Congress never learned: an organisation that cannot process a succession dispute loses its ablest people to a rival machine.

The apprenticeship that followed was long, and it was an apprenticeship in organisation rather than in office. It included the party's first internal rupture, and the rupture's handling is an early specimen of the processing capacity the system later made famous. E.V.K. Sampath, Annadurai's nephew and one of the founding circle's most gifted organisers, broke with the leadership in 1961 after a season of disputes over the party's direction and his own standing, and carried a section of the cadre into a Tamil National Party of his own. The DMK neither collapsed nor purchased the rebel's return; it contested the 1962 election without him, improved its tally fourfold and watched the breakaway formation negotiate its own dissolution into the Congress by 1964. A splinter that dies while its parent grows is a data point about where the organisation's centre of gravity lay, in the district committees and the conference circuit rather than in any single lieutenant, however gifted. The pattern of 1961 rehearses, in miniature, the pattern of 1972, with the difference that in 1972 the departing lieutenant was big enough to build a second complete machine, and the system emerged with two custodians instead of one. In [the post-1951 first general election context](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), the DMK stood apart from the contest, putting up no candidates of its own under its rising-sun symbol in 1952 while its speakers campaigned across the presidency's Tamil districts. The States Reorganisation of 1956 then gave the party its arena: the old Madras Presidency, with its Telugu, Malayalam and Kannada districts, was dismembered, and the residuary Madras State was Tamil-speaking in its great majority, a transformation examined in [the post-1956 linguistic-state architecture](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/). A movement whose capital was the Tamil language had acquired a state whose boundaries matched its constituency. The DMK contested its first assembly election in 1957 and won thirteen seats, Annadurai taking Kanchipuram; the Congress under K. Kamaraj, chief minister from April 1954, still won 151 of 205 and governed comfortably. But the DMK had acquired what it needed: a legislative beachhead, a recognised symbol and a decade in which to build district committees, branch secretaries, student and labour wings, and the conference circuit that doubled as the party's parliament.

By 1962 the apprenticeship was nearly complete. The DMK won fifty of the assembly's 206 seats in the election of that year and became the state's principal opposition, although Annadurai himself lost at Kanchipuram and took his seat in the Rajya Sabha instead. Kamaraj's Congress still governed, and Kamaraj was the most formidable state leader the Indian Congress produced, a builder of schools, dams and the school midday meal that his government introduced in the mid-1950s; but two structural facts were moving against his party. The first was generational: the Congress in Madras was a party of the freedom struggle's veterans, and its district organisations, as the Kamaraj Plan of 1963 implicitly conceded when it pulled senior ministers into organisational work, had thinned into patronage chains. The second was programmatic: the Congress held office in New Delhi, and New Delhi's language policy, its food policy during the scarcity of the mid-1960s and its fiscal demands all landed on the state Congress's desk. The DMK, holding no office anywhere, could promise the substrate without qualification. In November 1963 Annadurai announced in Parliament that the DMK had set aside its demand for a separate Dravida Nadu, a decision forced by the Sixteenth Amendment's prohibition of secessionist advocacy after the war with China and taken, on Annadurai's stated reasoning, because the demand for a Tamil homeland could be pursued as autonomy within the Union. The renunciation removed the one position that made the DMK unacceptable to the wider electorate. What remained was a party of the substrate, organised to the village level, waiting for the issue that would carry it to power. The issue arrived in January 1965.

Two further developments of the apprenticeship years deserve record, because both recur throughout the system's later life. The first is municipal: in 1959 the DMK captured the Madras Corporation, the capital's civic government, its first taste of elected executive office, and used the corporation's schools, contracts and ceremonial platform to demonstrate administrative competence eight years before it won the state. The second is the adversary's self-inflicted confusion. Kamaraj, at the height of his authority, resigned the chief ministership on 2 October 1963 under his own Kamaraj Plan, the scheme by which senior Congress ministers across India left office for organisational work, and his successor M. Bhaktavatsalam inherited the government without inheriting the authority. The Madras Congress thereafter spoke in two registers, Kamaraj's organisational prestige and Bhaktavatsalam's embattled ministry, while rice grew scarce and dearer through 1964, 1965 and 1966 and the state's students took to the streets over Hindi. The DMK's journals, Murasoli among them, its touring drama troupes and its district conferences kept a single register throughout: the substrate, in Annadurai's prose, at election pitch.

## 1967: The Election That Founded the DMK-AIADMK System

The assembly election of February 1967 is the founding moment of the bipolar architecture, and the Election Commission's statistical report for Madras records its dimensions exactly: the DMK won 137 of 234 seats on 40.69 percent of the vote, the Congress won 51 seats on 41.10 percent, and the Swatantra Party, the DMK's senior ally in the anti-Congress front, won 20. The figures contain the system's first lesson. The Congress actually polled a slightly larger share of the vote than the DMK and won barely a third as many seats, because the anti-Congress electorate had been organised into a single front that fielded one candidate per constituency, while Congress votes spread evenly and unproductively across the state. Bipolar politics in Tamil Nadu was born as an arithmetic of fronts before it became a habit of mind: two alliances, each anchored by a Dravidian party, dividing the state's seats between them, with every smaller party obliged to choose a front or waste its vote. Every subsequent assembly election in the state has reproduced that arithmetic.

The causes of the verdict were specific, and the scholarship names them consistently. The 1965 anti-Hindi agitation had destroyed the Congress's standing among the student generation and the urban lower-middle class, and the party's state leadership, headed after Kamaraj's departure for national office by M. Bhaktavatsalam, chief minister from October 1963, had answered the agitation with police firing. Food scarcity and rice prices through 1965 and 1966, in a state dependent on imports from Andhra Pradesh and on a public distribution system under visible strain, supplied the material grievance. The DMK supplied the organisation and the theatre: its candidates included the film star M.G. Ramachandran, shot in the neck by the actor M.R. Radha on 12 January 1967 in an assassination attempt whose hospital photographs, the wounded star with his throat bandaged, were pasted across the state's walls as the campaign's most reproduced image; MGR won his St Thomas Mount seat from a hospital bed. Annadurai, the front's leader, had spent two decades building the persona of the elder brother, Anna, whose essays and plays made the party's doctrine a literary inheritance. On 6 March 1967 Annadurai was sworn in as chief minister of Madras State, the first non-Congress government in the state's history.

The composition of the victorious front is itself evidence for the substrate's absorptive power. Annadurai's alliance joined the DMK to C. Rajagopalachari's Swatantra Party, to the Communist Party of India (Marxist), to the Muslim League and to M.P. Sivagnanam's Tamil Arasu Kazhagam. Rajagopalachari was the very premier whose Hindi order of 1938 the movement had fought, the elder statesman of the Congress tradition the DMK existed to displace; in 1967 he blessed the DMK's candidacy as the instrument for removing Congress rule from Madras, and his party took twenty seats in the common cause. The front's campaign ran on rice, language and the charge that the Congress had become a Delhi durbar in the state; Kamaraj himself, the Congress's tallest Tamil leader, lost his Virudhunagar seat to the DMK's young candidate P. Srinivasan, a result that announced the generational transfer more plainly than any aggregate figure. A party system in which the old order's own patriarch crosses to the challenger's front is a system whose centre of legitimacy has already moved.

The founding government's two years fixed the system's institutional habits. Annadurai's ministry legalised self-respect marriages through the Hindu Marriage (Madras Amendment) Act of 1967, writing the Self-Respect Movement's oldest social demand into statute; it introduced the two-language formula in schools, Tamil and English, that every successor government has kept; it renamed the state Tamil Nadu with effect from 14 January 1969; and it began the conversion of the substrate into welfare administration, cheap rice through the public distribution system standing as the government's emblematic promise. When Annadurai died in office on 3 February 1969, after treatment in the United States and at Madras, the succession that followed supplied the architecture's first proof. V.R. Nedunchezhiyan, the party's senior-most minister, held office as acting chief minister and expected the succession; the legislature party instead chose M. Karunanidhi, the party's treasurer and its most tireless organiser, with MGR's public support proving decisive in the count of legislators. Karunanidhi was sworn in on 10 February 1969. The movement's founder was dead within two years of victory, the party's senior claimant had been passed over, and the machine did not split. It counted votes inside its own legislature party and obeyed the result. That internal discipline, demonstrated at the moment of maximum danger, is the single most consequential fact in the system's history, because it established that a Dravidian party's leadership was an office to be filled by procedure and weight of organisation, not a property to be inherited by proximity to the founder. The post-1969 DMK consolidation and the internal debates of 1969 to 1972 are documented in the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly debates and the party records preserved at the Anna Centenary Library, an under-used primary archive for the system's founding years.

## The 1972 Split and the Birth of Bipolar Competition

The split of October 1972 is the event that converted a dominant-party system into a bipolar system, and its mechanics deserve precision, because the conversion was not planned by anyone. The DMK that Karunanidhi led after 1969 was a governing machine of extraordinary reach, and the election of 1971 confirmed it: the DMK won 184 of 234 assembly seats, the largest tally any party has taken in the state's history, in alliance with Indira Gandhi's Congress, while the rival Congress (Organisation) of Kamaraj collapsed to fifteen seats. M.G. Ramachandran, the party's treasurer and its most famous face, had been drifting from the leadership since Annadurai's death, nursing the grievance that the party he had done most to popularise treated him as an asset rather than a principal. In October 1972, at a public meeting, MGR demanded that the party's accounts, and by extension the leadership's stewardship of party funds, be opened to scrutiny, a challenge aimed directly at Karunanidhi's control of the organisation. The DMK's general council suspended him on 10 October 1972. On 17 October 1972 MGR announced a new party, the Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, named for Annadurai, carrying the DMK's founder, its colours' symbolism and its substrate into a rival machine. His fan clubs, the rasigar mandrams built around his film career in every town of the state, converted themselves into party branches within months, giving the new party something no Indian splinter party had ever possessed on its first day: a complete local organisation that it had not needed to build.

### How do the DMK and AIADMK differ if both are Dravidian parties?

They share the substrate and differ in organisation and appeal. The DMK is a cadre party with a literary culture and a leadership drawn from its own organisational ladder. The AIADMK centralised authority in its leader, recruited through fan clubs turned branches, and pitched its appeal beyond the movement's base to rural voters, women and the poor.

The government MGR left behind in 1972 was at the summit of its power, and its record belongs to the architecture's evidence because it shows what a Dravidian party does with a supermajority. Karunanidhi's ministry had appointed the Rajamannar Committee in September 1969, under the retired Chief Justice P.V. Rajamannar, to examine centre-state relations, and the committee's report of 1971 became the doctrinal basis of the state autonomy resolution the Tamil Nadu assembly adopted in April 1974, the high-water mark of Indian federal assertion by a state government. The same ministry lowered the land ceiling to fifteen standard acres by its 1970 amendment of the land reforms statute, took the capital's bus undertakings into public ownership at the start of 1972 and suspended prohibition in August 1971, reopening liquor vends to raise revenue in a decision that scandalised the movement's older moralists. The governing style was programmatic and combative, and it fed the quarrel with MGR in a precise way: a party administered as a federation of district bosses and policy committees offered its film-star treasurer honour without authority, and MGR's accounts challenge of October 1972 was, in substance, a demand to be treated as a principal of the enterprise his face had capitalised.

The new party's first electoral examination came quickly, and it passed. In the Dindigul Lok Sabha by-election of May 1973 the AIADMK's candidate K. Maya Thevar won a seat the DMK had expected its machine to hold, defeating the Congress in a contest the whole state read as a plebiscite between the two custodians of Annadurai's inheritance. The by-election mattered beyond its single seat. It demonstrated that the fan-club organisation had converted into an election-winning machine within seven months of the split, that MGR's rural following was transferable to candidates other than himself, and that the Congress could not exploit the Dravidian family's division. Between 1973 and 1977 the AIADMK built district committees on the fan-club skeleton, absorbed a stream of DMK local notables and presented itself, in MGR's phrase-making, as the true Anna party against a DMK government it charged with corruption and family aggrandisement, charges the Sarkaria Commission's appointment in 1976 kept in public view through the Emergency's end.

The years 1972 to 1977 then sorted the two parties into the roles the architecture assigned them. The DMK government, dismissed by New Delhi on 31 January 1976 after it opposed the Emergency, faced the Sarkaria Commission of inquiry into its administration's corruption, appointed in February 1976, and fought the 1977 elections under a cloud the commission's proceedings kept alive. The AIADMK supported Indira Gandhi's Congress through the Emergency, won the 1977 Lok Sabha contest in the state with seventeen seats in alliance with the Congress, and in the assembly election of June 1977 won 130 of 234 seats; MGR was sworn in as chief minister on 30 June 1977, the first film star to head an Indian state government. The significance of 1977 for the architecture is easily missed. A split in a movement party normally produces a rump and a fragment, one of which dies. In Tamil Nadu the split produced two complete parties, each holding the substrate, each possessing a statewide organisation, each capable of governing. From 1977 the state had not a dominant party with challengers but two governing parties in alternation, and the Congress, which won 27 assembly seats that year and never again approached power in the state, had been displaced from the system's centre altogether. The pattern [the 1977 verdict's geographical unevenness reading](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/) brings out, in which the Janata wave that swept northern India stopped at the Vindhyas, had its Tamil Nadu form: the state's voters changed their government without leaving the substrate, because the substrate had acquired two custodians.

The bipolar conversion also solved a problem that destroys most movement parties, the problem of the excluded voter. A Tamil voter alienated from the DMK in 1972, by its government's corruption or its leader's manner, had nowhere to go that did not require voting for the Congress of New Delhi or for marginal parties of the left. After 1972 the same voter could punish the DMK by voting AIADMK, expressing the protest inside the Dravidian family and against one custodian of the substrate in favour of the other. The system's famous stability is in large part this safety valve institutionalised. Anti-incumbency, the force that periodically demolishes ruling parties in other Indian states and opens the field to new entrants, is absorbed in Tamil Nadu by the rival Dravidian party, which returns the protesting voter to office-seeking respectability at the next election. The architecture does not suppress discontent. It routes discontent into its own alternation, and every rout of one Dravidian party by the other, 1977, 1991, 1996, 2011, confirms the routing.

## The Alternation Machine: Elections from 1977 to 2011

Between 1977 and 2011 Tamil Nadu held eight assembly elections, and the machine's operation can be read directly from the Election Commission's results. The AIADMK won in 1977 with 130 seats, in 1980 with 129, in 1984 with 132 in alliance with the Congress, and in 1991 with 164. The DMK won in 1989 with 150 seats, in 1996 with 173 and in 2006 with 96 as the head of a broad front. The AIADMK won again in 2001 with 132 and in 2011 with 150. No third party headed a government, formed the official opposition for more than one term or survived as an independent pole; the official opposition in each assembly was the other Dravidian party, except in the assemblies of 1991, when the Congress sat as opposition to Jayalalithaa's first government, and 2011, when Vijayakant's DMDK, an ally of the victorious AIADMK, took the opposition benches with 29 seats, the single instance of a non-Dravidian party holding that office, and one it owed entirely to its place inside a Dravidian-led front. The alternation was not mechanical, since the AIADMK held power for the decade 1977 to 1989 across three elections under MGR, and the DMK's victories of 1989 and 1996 were separated by an AIADMK term; but no incumbent party retained power after MGR's death until 2016, and the rhythm of punishment and return gave the system its observable pulse.

Three mechanisms kept the machine closed to outsiders. The first was the front system itself. Each Dravidian party heads a standing alliance of smaller parties, the communists, the Congress (as a junior partner from 2001 in the DMK's front, after earlier decades in the AIADMK's), the Vanniyar-based Pattali Makkal Katchi founded by S. Ramadoss in 1989, the Dalit parties VCK and PT, the Muslim parties and a rotation of caste and regional formations, and the front's seat-sharing bargain is struck before every election, constituency by constituency, so that the anti-incumbent vote is never divided in the seats that matter. The second mechanism was the conversion of the state's welfare apparatus into the currency of competition, treated in a later section: since both parties bid in the same currency of subsidised rice, free electricity to farmers, bus passes, bicycles, laptops and cash assistance, no challenger could outbid the incumbents' delivery record with promises alone. The third mechanism was organisational depth. Both parties maintain branch committees in effectively every village and urban ward of the state, a density built by the DMK from the 1950s and matched by the AIADMK through the fan-club conversion of the 1970s and the membership drives that followed; a new party entering Tamil Nadu politics faces two complete machines, each with its own booth-level workers, in all 234 constituencies at once.

### Why do smaller parties hold the balance in Tamil Nadu elections?

Smaller parties hold the balance because the two fronts divide the state vote closely enough that a bloc of five or ten percent decides many constituencies. A caste party carrying its bloc intact into a front adds seats out of proportion to its size, so the PMK, the VCK, the communists and the Congress bargain for constituency allocations.

The alternation machine also processed the system's gravest shocks without breaking. The dismissal of the AIADMK government in January 1980, weeks after the Congress (I) and DMK had swept the state's Lok Sabha seats in the January 1980 parliamentary election, produced not a Congress restoration but an AIADMK assembly victory five months later, MGR returned with 129 seats by voters who distinguished, with a precision that embarrassed New Delhi's arithmetic, between the government they wanted at the centre and the government they wanted in Madras. MGR's death on 24 December 1987 split the AIADMK between his widow Janaki Ramachandran, sworn in as chief minister on 7 January 1988 and dismissed within the month, and Jayalalithaa, his co-star and political protégée, whose faction won 27 seats in January 1989 against the Janaki faction's two and who thereby inherited the party, its symbol and its electorate in a contest settled by votes rather than by courts. The DMK's victory of 1989, Karunanidhi's return with 150 seats, was followed within two years by the dismissal of his government on 30 January 1991 by the Chandra Shekhar ministry in New Delhi, on the stated ground of the state's failure against the Sri Lankan Tamil militancy operating from its soil. The election that followed, held in the shadow of Rajiv Gandhi's assassination at Sriperumbudur on 21 May 1991 in the middle of the campaign, reduced the DMK to two seats and gave the AIADMK-Congress front the state, Jayalalithaa taking office on 24 June 1991. A party reduced to two seats in a 234-member house is, in most party systems, dead. The DMK treated the verdict as a season, kept its district committees staffed, contested the 1996 election after Jayalalithaa's first government had discredited itself, and won 173 seats. The machine's capacity to absorb annihilation and return is the strongest single evidence that what exists in Tamil Nadu is an architecture and not a mood.

The decades after 1991 add a second body of evidence, from the national arena, where the two parties' behaviour was the reverse of ideological. In 1996 the Congress's decision to ally with the discredited AIADMK split the Congress itself in the state: G.K. Moopanar led his followers out to found the Tamil Maanila Congress, which joined the DMK's front and won thirty-nine assembly seats, another demonstration that Tamil Nadu's political gravity acts on national parties by fission. In 1998 the AIADMK allied with the BJP and its eighteen Lok Sabha seats made Jayalalithaa the pivot of the Vajpayee coalition in New Delhi, and when she withdrew her support the government fell on 17 April 1999 by a single vote, 269 to 270. The DMK, the party of anti-Hindi resistance and state autonomy, then joined the same BJP-led National Democratic Alliance for the 1999 election, sat in Vajpayee's cabinet and defended the arrangement as federal arithmetic, while the AIADMK moved toward the Congress. By 2004 the positions had reversed again, and the DMK-led front, allied with the Congress, won all forty parliamentary seats of Tamil Nadu and Puducherry combined. A cultural reading of the state's politics struggles with this record, since parties of deep doctrine are not expected to exchange a common adversary as coalition partner in successive elections. The architectural reading expects exactly this: the fronts' national alignments are instruments for state purposes, priced in cabinet berths and central forbearance, while the state's internal constitution, two fronts, one substrate, remains untouched by the exchanges. The DMK's minority government of 2006, holding office on Congress support from outside with ninety-six seats of its own, and the AIADMK's return in 2011 with 150 seats as the 2G spectrum scandal consumed the DMK's national standing, close the period on the same note: shocks arrived from Delhi, from the courts and from the national press, and the alternation absorbed each one in turn.

## The Geography of the Two Fronts

The bipolar architecture has a geography, and the map explains several features of the system that statewide totals conceal. Tamil Nadu's regions carry distinct political inheritances, and each front is a coalition of regional strengths assembled afresh at every election. The north, the districts around Chennai and the old North Arcot country, is the movement's birthplace and the DMK's most dependable ground: Annadurai's Kanchipuram, the party's conference towns and the capital's municipal politics, in DMK hands at the corporation level for long stretches since 1959, anchor the front there. The Cauvery delta, Thanjavur, Thiruvarur and Nagapattinam, is Karunanidhi's home country, an agrarian region with an older communist presence from the land struggles of the 1940s, where the DMK's organisation and the left parties' remnants have made the front's floor high in most cycles. The west, the Kongu country of Coimbatore, Erode, Salem, Tiruppur and Namakkal, industrial and dominated socially by the Gounder community, leaned toward the AIADMK through the Palaniswami years and showed its value in defeat: in 2021, when the AIADMK lost the state, a striking share of its surviving sixty-six seats lay in the western districts around its leader's Salem base. The south, Madurai, Theni, Ramanathapuram and the Tirunelveli country, the land of the Thevar community and of MGR's most devoted rural following, sustained the AIADMK through the MGR and Jayalalithaa decades, while the far southern district of Kanyakumari, with its large Christian population and its older Congress tradition, has repeatedly broken the state's pattern, returning the Congress in assembly contests and giving the BJP its single Tamil Nadu Lok Sabha victory of 2014.

The caste parties of the front system are regional parties in this precise sense. The PMK's Vanniyar belt runs across the northern districts of Dharmapuri, Salem, Villupuram and Cuddalore, and its value to a front is the delivery of that belt; the VCK's Dalit organisation is strongest in the north and centre; the Kongu parties speak for the west's dominant community; the smaller Thevar and Nadar formations matter in the south. Seat-sharing in Tamil Nadu is therefore a cartographic exercise before it is an ideological one. A front concedes constituencies to the ally whose community and organisation own that ground, and the front leader's art lies in pricing each region's holder correctly. The architecture's stability has a spatial corollary: because every region's dominant political organisation already belongs to one of the two fronts, a challenger cannot begin, as challengers elsewhere begin, by capturing a province. There is no province to capture. The BJP's strategists, whose Indian method has often proceeded state by state and region by region, confront in Tamil Nadu a map in which every district has two incumbent machines, and the party's best parliamentary results have come precisely where the map has an anomaly, the religious demography of Kanyakumari in 2014 and the urban wards of Chennai and Coimbatore where it finished second in 2024. The exception studies the rule: even the anomaly districts sit inside the fronts' arithmetic, winnable only in years when a front's own bargain collapses.

The capital concentrates the system's geography into a single stage. Chennai holds the secretariat at Fort St George, the assembly, the Marina beachfront where the language martyrs are commemorated and where the crowds of January 1965 and January 2017 assembled two generations apart, and the corporation whose capture in 1959 was the DMK's first executive prize. Metropolitan verdicts in the state are not a separate politics; they are the system's politics at its densest, and the fronts price Chennai's twenty-odd assembly constituencies accordingly. When urban local body elections returned to the state in February 2022 after a decade's suspension, the DMK-led front won every municipal corporation in Tamil Nadu, Chennai included, a result that told the same story at civic scale that the assembly verdict of 2021 had told at state scale: the alternation's incumbent of the season sweeps the institutions, and the rival machine waits, staffed and funded, for its season to return.

## Personality as the Operating Mechanism of the System

The personality-driven reading of Tamil Nadu politics rests on a genuine distinction: the state's four great leaders were not interchangeable, and each left a mark the system still carries. Annadurai invented the party's voice, the elevated, alliterative Tamil oratory that made a political speech a literary event, and governed as the movement's philosopher for two years. Karunanidhi, chief minister in 1969 to 1971, 1971 to 1976, 1989 to 1991, 1996 to 2001 and 2006 to 2011, was the system's great administrator and its most durable organiser, a screenwriter-president who ran the DMK as a federation of district secretaries whose loyalty he audited personally for five decades. MGR, chief minister from 1977 until his death in 1987, converted a film persona, the do-gooder hero who fed the poor and defended women's honour in film after film, into a governing bond with the rural electorate that no party programme has matched in the state. Jayalalithaa, his co-star in twenty-eight films and his successor after the factional contest of 1988 to 1989, governed from 1991 with an imperious centralism that inverted the DMK's federal habits, taking the party's decisions into a single office and surviving, in sequence, electoral annihilation in 1996, a corruption conviction that barred her from office in 2001 and a second conviction in 2014, each time returning to the chief ministership when the courts or the voters restored her. A history of Tamil Nadu written through these four lives would be vivid and largely accurate in its facts. It would still miss the system's secret, which is that each of these singular figures operated machinery built to outlast singularity, and the machinery constrained the figures as much as the figures animated the machinery.

M.S.S. Pandian's The Image Trap (1992), his study of MGR's films, supplies the missing term between personality and structure. Pandian showed that MGR's screen image was not a personal accident but a constructed political instrument: the subaltern hero of films such as Nadodi Mannan (1958), in which MGR played both a king and the commoner who replaces him, enacted the DMK's doctrine of the virtuous poor against the corrupt powerful, and the fan clubs that the image organised became, in 1972, the AIADMK's skeleton. Personality in Tamil Nadu is therefore best understood as a technology of the architecture, the means by which a party built on doctrine reached an electorate of limited literacy in the decades before television saturation. Narendra Subramanian's Ethnicity and Populist Mobilization (1999) makes the complementary argument from the parties' side: the DMK and the AIADMK are populist parties in a precise sense, mobilising a Tamil people defined against entrenched elites, and Subramanian's comparison of the DMK's ordered populism with the AIADMK's more personalist variant explains their different internal regimes without dissolving either into biography. Andrew Wyatt's Party System Change in South India (2010), studying the state's political entrepreneurs, reaches the position this article defends from within the party-system literature: the two-party pattern in Tamil Nadu has persisted across half a century because its leading actors, however entrepreneurial, have found the pattern itself the profitable terrain on which to compete, and have rebuilt it each time their own manoeuvres threatened to break it.

### What part did cinema play in building Dravidian politics?

Cinema was the movement's loudspeaker before it was its ladder to office. Annadurai and Karunanidhi wrote scripts, for Velaikari in 1949 and Parasakthi in 1952, that carried Self-Respect arguments about caste, priests and fate into commercial theatres, and MGR's screen persona converted those arguments into a personal following organised in fan clubs across the state.

The constraint that structure placed on personality shows most clearly at the moments of succession, where the personality reading predicts collapse and the architectural reading predicts processing. In 1969 the DMK passed over its senior-most minister for its best organiser and obeyed the legislature party's count. In 1989 the AIADMK's succession was settled by an election in which the two factions contested as rivals and the loser's two seats against the winner's twenty-seven closed the argument; the merged party then fought the 1991 election as one machine. In both cases the system's parties behaved as institutions with rules of inheritance, and in both cases the alternative custodian of the substrate stood ready to absorb any voter the succession mishandled. Personality decided who led. The architecture decided that leadership contests, however rough, would be fought inside one of two machines, with the loser of an internal contest denied the option of founding a third force with any prospect of power; the one major attempt to do so from within the AIADMK's later succession crisis, the breakaway of T.T.V. Dhinakaran's AMMK in 2018, won a single famous by-election at R.K. Nagar in December 2017 and faded as a state force within one cycle, absorbed back toward the parent party's orbit. The lesson generalises. In Tamil Nadu, charisma is real, consequential and entirely insufficient as an explanation, because charisma there has always had to rent its organisation from one of two landlords.

Beneath the leaders stood a second stratum whose existence the biography-centred account habitually misses: the professional lieutenants who ran the machines between elections. The DMK's district secretaries, the mavatta seyalalars, formed a career cadre whose local command of caste arithmetic, municipal contracts and branch discipline made the party governable by any president who held their loyalty, and Karunanidhi's five decades at the top were, in organisational terms, five decades of auditing that cadre. The AIADMK, for all its court politics, was serviced by organisers of the same professional type: R.M. Veerappan, MGR's principal lieutenant and the party's early administrative manager; Panruti S. Ramachandran, an organiser of its founding years; and a bench of district secretaries recruited from the fan-club network. The fate of lieutenants who mistook their standing for a claim to the succession confirms the stratum's function. Veerappan broke with Jayalalithaa in the mid-1990s and founded his own MGR Kazhagam, which the electorate ignored; the system's voters follow machines and leaders, not managers, and a lieutenant without one of the two machines is a politician without an instrument. The architecture reproduced itself through this stratum in every decade, and it is the stratum's continuity, more than any single leader's gifts, that the alternation's smoothness records.

## The Party Media: Newspapers, Channels and the Manufacture of Presence

A further piece of machinery, invisible in constitutional accounts, deserves a section of its own: both Dravidian parties built media ecosystems that carry their presence into the state's households between elections, and the ecosystems are old. The movement began in print, the Justice daily of 1917, Periyar's Kudiyarasu from 1925 and Annadurai's Dravida Nadu weekly from 1942 establishing the pattern in which a political current in the Tamil country owns its press before it owns an office. Karunanidhi began the paper Murasoli in 1942 as a young movement writer, and it grew into the DMK's enduring daily voice. The cinema decades fused the party and the screen; the television decades institutionalised the fusion in corporate form. Sun TV, launched in April 1993 by Kalanithi Maran, a grandnephew of Karunanidhi, built the largest Tamil satellite network of its era in open sympathy with the DMK, and when the Maran family quarrelled with Karunanidhi's household in 2007, after a succession survey published in the Sun group's Dinakaran newspaper provoked an attack on its Madurai office in May of that year in which three employees died, the party answered by founding its own channel, Kalaignar TV, launched in September 2007. The AIADMK matched the structure on its own pattern: the daily Namadhu MGR, founded in the late 1980s as the party's organ, and Jaya TV, launched in August 1999, carried Jayalalithaa's name and image as the masthead of a parallel information order.

The systemic function of this media ownership is insulation, and its value shows in the crisis years. A party whose voters receive their news through sympathetic channels weathers hostile national coverage at a fraction of the usual cost; the DMK's survival through the 2G spectrum scandal of 2010 and 2011, a Delhi news storm of the first magnitude, owed something to the party's remaining strength in the state's own media ecology even as the scandal destroyed its assembly majority. The same insulation protects the AIADMK in its seasons of disgrace. National parties entering Tamil Nadu face not only two organisations and two welfare apparatuses but two complete environments of publicity, in which the challenger's message is reported, framed and answered by the incumbents' own instruments. Political scientists of the state's media, and the memoir literature of its journalists, treat the party-channel nexus as a settled feature of the landscape rather than a scandal within it, which is itself a measure of the architecture's depth: the system's parties colonised each new communications technology in turn, print, cinema, satellite television and the social platforms, because colonising the means of presence is what the machines exist to do. The observation returns the argument to its foundation. Personality in Tamil Nadu appears so large partly because the parties manufacture presence at industrial scale; the substrate appears so immovable partly because it is rebroadcast daily. Structure, once again, is doing the work that biography and culture are credited with.

## Welfare as the Battlefield Both Parties Share

The fourth component of the substrate, social-justice positioning, acquired through the alternation decades a governing technology that both parties operate and neither can abandon: competitive welfare delivery to the state's poor, financed from the state budget and branded by the ruling party. The genealogy is dated. Kamaraj's Congress government introduced cooked midday meals in the state's primary schools in the mid-1950s, the seed of the programme MGR universalised on 1 July 1982 as the Chief Minister's Nutritious Meal Programme, extended to pre-school children and old-age pensioners and credited by nutrition studies of the period with measurable gains in school enrolment, especially of girls. Karunanidhi's governments answered with their own signature instruments, among them the 1989 decision to waive cooperative farm loans and the free electricity supply to farmers that the DMK had pioneered in the 1970s and that no successor government has dared withdraw. From 2006 the bidding intensified: the DMK's 2006 manifesto promised rice at two rupees a kilogram through the ration shops, colour television sets for households without them and a gas stove with connection for poor women, and delivered the televisions by the million; the AIADMK's 2011 manifesto answered with free laptops for school students, mixer-grinders, fans and milch cattle, and its 2016 manifesto extended the list. The state's election manifestos read, to an outsider, like catalogues. Inside the architecture they are something more disciplined: the mechanism by which each party periodically re-proves its custody of the substrate's fourth component, directing the state's resources to the non-Brahmin majority and the poor in forms a voter can hold in her hands.

The delivery machinery behind the promises is as much a part of the architecture as the promises themselves. The Nutritious Meal Programme of 1982 did not merely feed schoolchildren; at its height it employed well over a hundred thousand women as cooks, helpers and organisers in the state's villages, making the programme a rural employment system whose workforce had a direct stake in the governing party's reputation, and its centres doubled as the state's most ubiquitous public buildings after the panchayat office. The revenue side was engineered with the same care. The Tamil Nadu State Marketing Corporation, TASMAC, created by MGR's government in 1983 to conduct the liquor trade in public hands, grew into one of the state exchequer's largest single revenue sources, and the fiscal sociology of the arrangement is understood by every finance minister the state has had: the welfare budget that wins elections is underwritten substantially by the liquor monopoly the state itself operates. Jayalalithaa's governments added the politics of the brand, most visibly in the Amma canteens launched in Chennai in February 2013, which sold a working meal for a few rupees under the leader's maternal honorific and extended the state's feeding infrastructure from schoolchildren to the urban working poor. A challenger party confronting this system does not face a set of schemes. It faces an interlocked apparatus of employment, revenue and daily feeding that touches the voter's household every single day.

Beneath the branded schemes stands the oldest and largest of the state's delivery systems, the public distribution of food, and its history binds the architecture's two halves together. When the Union government moved India's public distribution system to a targeted model in 1997, dividing households into those below and above an official poverty line and withdrawing the subsidy from the latter, Tamil Nadu refused the logic and kept its system universal: every ration card household in the state, rich or poor, drew its rice, sugar and kerosene from the fair price shop on the same terms. Successive governments of both parties defended universality against the centre's accountants on an explicitly Dravidian argument, that a welfare right held by the whole society cannot be stigmatised as a dole to its poorest, and the competitive bidding of the alternation era then drove the price of the entitlement toward zero: the DMK's 2006 government sold ration rice at two rupees a kilogram and reduced the price to one rupee in 2008, and the AIADMK government of 2011 abolished the price altogether, distributing twenty kilograms of free rice a month to every card-holding family. The fair price shop, some thirty-five thousand of them across the state in the system's mature years, most of them run by cooperative societies whose boards are minor offices of the local political class, is the substrate's fourth component rendered as daily administration. A Tamil Nadu voter encounters the state's social-justice settlement not in a constitution or a manifesto but in a queue that moves, a measure that is honest and a price that the two parties' competition has abolished. Political scientists who study the state's welfare regime treat the ration shop as its emblem precisely because it unites the system's themes in one institution: universality as dignity, delivery as legitimacy and the two parties as rival managers of a settlement neither may repeal.

### How did welfare schemes become the battlefield of Tamil Nadu politics?

Welfare became the battlefield because the substrate settled the argument in advance. With both parties committed to social justice, reservation and Tamil pride, neither could attack the other's ends, so competition migrated to delivery: rice, meals, laptops and cash in the voter's hands. Kamaraj's school meals and MGR's 1982 Nutritious Meal Programme set the template.

The scholarly debate over this technology is real and the article takes its sides seriously. Critics of the state's model, in the public-finance literature and in the reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General on Tamil Nadu's debt, argue that competitive freebie distribution has burdened a state whose finances were once India's most prudently managed, and that goods chosen for visibility crowd out investment in the quality of schools, hospitals and water systems. Defenders, among them economists who have studied the state's social indicators across the alternation decades, answer that Tamil Nadu's outcomes in literacy, infant mortality, institutional childbirth and women's workforce participation stand at or near the top of the Indian state rankings, and that the welfare floor the two parties compete to raise is the material form of the social-justice commitment the substrate demands. The adjudication this article offers is architectural rather than fiscal. Whatever the balance of the fiscal argument, the welfare battlefield performs a systemic function: it gives the two parties a field on which to compete that does not require either to challenge the substrate, and it raises the entry cost for any challenger, who must persuade voters that a party without a delivery record will out-deliver two machines that have been distributing goods, on schedule and at scale, for forty years. The BJP's national welfare repertoire, effective elsewhere in India, enters Tamil Nadu as a late bid in an auction the two incumbents have been running since before the BJP existed.

## The Substrate in Office: Language, Examinations and the Street

The substrate's components are not merely inherited pieties; the record of the system's later decades shows them operating as live constraints on governments of both parties, including governments allied with the very centre the components exist to resist. The clearest case is the language defence of 2019. The draft National Education Policy released by the Kasturirangan committee in May 2019 proposed a three-language formula whose text appeared to make Hindi a compulsory study in the state's schools. The reaction in Tamil Nadu was instantaneous and unanimous across the aisle: the DMK in opposition denounced the draft as imposition revived, and the AIADMK government of Edappadi K. Palaniswami, then the BJP's ally in the National Democratic Alliance, publicly committed the state to its two-language formula and refused the draft's language chapter. Within days the Union government revised the published draft to state that no language would be imposed on any state. An ally government in Chennai had vetoed a signature initiative of its own alliance's centre on a substrate question, and the centre had yielded inside a week. The episode belongs in the evidence for the architectural reading because it isolates the variable: whatever the AIADMK's dependence on New Delhi in that season, and it was considerable, the dependence stopped at the substrate's boundary.

The examination question pressed the same boundary in a grimmer register. The National Eligibility cum Entrance Test for medical admissions, applied to Tamil Nadu from 2017, transferred admission to the state's medical colleges from the state-board marks, on which rural and Tamil-medium students competed on level terms, to a national test aligned with a different syllabus and a coaching economy the state's poorer families could not buy. The death of S. Anitha, a Dalit student from Ariyalur who had scored 1,176 of 1,200 in her state board examinations, had challenged the test's application in the Supreme Court and died by suicide on 1 September 2017 after the exemption she sought was refused, made her the face of a resistance that both parties owned. The state assembly passed bills seeking Tamil Nadu's exemption from the test under an AIADMK government in February 2017 and again under the DMK government of M.K. Stalin in September 2021; the Union's assent, without which the bills cannot operate, was withheld in both instances. The medical-admissions question is the fourth component of the substrate, social justice in education, translated into a federal dispute, and its politics replicate the language politics of 1965 in the idiom of competitive examinations, with the state's two parties competing to defend the same settlement against the same centre.

The street supplied the third demonstration. The Supreme Court's judgment of May 2014 had banned jallikattu, the bull-taming sport of the southern districts, as cruelty to animals, and the prohibition stood until January 2017, when a protest that began with students on Chennai's Marina beach grew within days into the largest youth mobilisation the state had seen in a generation, leaderless in form and insistent that the sport was a Tamil inheritance no court in Delhi could abolish. The state and Union governments moved together under the pressure: an amendment to the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act for Tamil Nadu received presidential assent on 31 January 2017 and the sport resumed under regulation. Neither party led the protest and both parties harvested it, competing to claim the restoration as their custodianship's achievement. The jallikattu winter showed the substrate's language-and-identity component functioning in a political generation that had no memory of 1965, transmitted through the parties, the media ecology and the festival calendar rather than through any living organisation of the old movement. A substrate that reproduces itself in citizens too young to have been taught it is not a legacy. It is a constitution in the material sense, the thing the polity is made of, and the two parties are its constituted authorities.

## The Succession Tests of 2016 to 2021

The architecture's hardest examination came in the five years after May 2016, when both parties lost the leaders who had embodied them for a generation, and the examination was the more severe because the first event broke the system's oldest rhythm. Jayalalithaa's AIADMK won the assembly election of May 2016 with 134 seats in the initial count, rising to 136 when the two deferred constituencies voted, the first time an incumbent party had retained power in Tamil Nadu since MGR's victory of 1984. The alternation machine had produced, for once, a re-election, and it had done so under a leader whose authority inside her party was absolute and whose succession she had never arranged. Jayalalithaa was admitted to Apollo Hospitals in Chennai on 22 September 2016 and died on 5 December 2016, leaving a party constitutionally incapable, by design, of imagining a successor. O. Panneerselvam, her twice-used caretaker of 2001 and 2014, was sworn in as chief minister within hours. In February 2017 the party's general secretary V.K. Sasikala, Jayalalithaa's household companion, moved to take the chief ministership herself; the Supreme Court's judgment of 14 February 2017 restoring her conviction in the disproportionate assets case removed her to a Bengaluru prison, and her chosen nominee Edappadi K. Palaniswami, a highways minister from the state's western Kongu region, was sworn in on 16 February 2017 and proved his majority on the assembly floor two days later. In August 2017 the Panneerselvam and Palaniswami factions merged, expelled Sasikala's nephew Dhinakaran from the party's offices and settled into the dual arrangement, Palaniswami as chief minister and Panneerselvam as his deputy, that governed the state until 2021.

By the standards of the personality reading, the AIADMK should then have disintegrated: a party built as one woman's court, decapitated twice in ten weeks, ought to have dissolved into the state's political soil. The architectural reading predicts something colder and, in the event, truer. The AIADMK's organisation, however centralised its command, possessed a full district and branch structure whose offices carried local power worth keeping, a two-leaves symbol whose recognition among rural voters approached universality, and above all a defined enemy across the aisle whose existence gave every AIADMK officeholder a reason to remain inside the tent. The party held its government for a full term, fought the 2019 Lok Sabha election in alliance with the BJP and the PMK and lost thirty-eight of the state's thirty-nine seats, a catastrophe by any standard, and still did not split into irrelevance: in the 2021 assembly election, four and a half years after Jayalalithaa's death, the AIADMK won 66 seats and its front 75, and Palaniswami took his place as leader of the opposition with the machine intact. The succession was ugly, litigated and incomplete, and it was processed.

The DMK's parallel succession shows the same architecture operated by a party with stronger internal procedure. Karunanidhi had spent a decade preparing the transfer the AIADMK refused to contemplate: his younger son M.K. Stalin, mayor of Chennai from 1996, a minister from 2006 and deputy chief minister from 29 May 2009, was made the party's working president on 4 January 2017, an office created to hold the succession in plain sight while the patriarch lived. Karunanidhi died on 7 August 2018, and on 28 August 2018 the DMK's general council elected Stalin president unopposed, the party's district secretaries, the real electorate of the DMK, having been cultivated by the incoming leader for twenty years. The contrast between the two successions is the contrast between the parties' internal constitutions, the DMK's ladder against the AIADMK's court, and it matters to the adjudication: a system in which the better-organised party processes succession smoothly and the worse-organised party processes it roughly, yet both process it, is a system whose stability does not depend on either party's internal virtue. The 2021 verdict completed the test. The DMK-led front won 159 of 234 assembly seats, the DMK itself 133, and Stalin was sworn in as chief minister on 7 May 2021, the fourth leader of his party to hold the office and the first whose claim rested entirely on organisational seniority rather than on movement founding or literary fame. The substrate had changed custodians' generations in both parties within five years, and the architecture's vital signs, two fronts, one contest, no third force, were undisturbed.

The years after 2021 ran the same test once more, inside the AIADMK, and the result deserves record before the national question is addressed. Palaniswami's claim to the party was contested by Panneerselvam through the courts and the Election Commission until the party's general council of 11 July 2022 abolished the dual leadership, expelled Panneerselvam and installed Palaniswami as interim general secretary; the Madras High Court declined to undo the council's work and the Supreme Court's judgment of February 2023 upheld the resolutions, after which a party election confirmed Palaniswami as general secretary in March 2023. The AIADMK then fought the 2024 parliamentary election under his leadership, having left the BJP-led alliance in September 2023 after a quarrel over the BJP state president's remarks on Annadurai, and it headed its own front with smaller allies. On the DMK's side, Stalin's government consolidated its custody of the substrate with the instruments of the welfare battlefield, among them the Kalaignar Magalir Urimai Thittam of September 2023, paying a thousand rupees a month to women heads of families, and governed under the self-description of a Dravidian Model of administration. The two parties that entered the 2024 election were thus both led, for the first time in the system's history, by men who were neither founders nor founders' kin nor film stars: two professional politicians produced by the machines themselves. The architecture had reproduced not only its parties but its type of leader.

## The National Challenger: Votes Without Seats

Against this record stands the complication the bipolar reading must answer honestly: the BJP's expansion in Tamil Nadu is real, measured in votes, and the party's state unit argues, with some evidence, that a structure can be outvoted before it is out-organised. The BJP's Tamil Nadu vote share in Lok Sabha elections rose from 3.66 percent in 2019, when it contested as the AIADMK's junior partner and lost all five seats it fought, to 11.24 percent in 2024, when it headed its own front, fought twenty-three seats and finished second in several urban constituencies, including all three seats in Chennai city. Eleven percent is not nothing; it exceeds the statewide shares that launched the BJP toward power in several northern states in earlier decades, and the party's 2024 campaign, led by its state president K. Annamalai, a former police officer whose aggressive style broke with the state unit's older deference, was the most visible the party has mounted in Tamil Nadu. Defenders of a coming-realignment thesis can also point to the Naam Tamilar Katchi, a Tamil nationalist party outside both fronts, which took 8.24 percent of the 2024 Lok Sabha vote on its own, proof that a measurable electorate exists beyond the two fronts' combined reach.

### Why could the BJP not convert votes into seats in Tamil Nadu in 2024?

The BJP's 11.24 percent was spread too evenly to beat consolidated fronts under first-past-the-post. The DMK-led alliance polled 46.97 percent behind one candidate per seat; the BJP finished second in a few urban seats and thinned elsewhere. Conversion requires a front of one's own or a regional stronghold, and the BJP had built neither.

The BJP's two earlier attempts define the alternatives it has exhausted. In 2014 the party headed its own National Democratic Alliance front in the state, joining the DMDK, the PMK, the MDMK and smaller parties, and the front won two of thirty-nine seats, the BJP's Pon Radhakrishnan at Kanyakumari and the PMK's Anbumani Ramadoss at Dharmapuri, while the AIADMK, contesting alone, won thirty-seven; the BJP's own share of the state's vote that year, 5.56 percent, stood for a decade as its ceiling. In 2019 the party chose the opposite course and contested five seats as the junior partner of the AIADMK's front, losing all five in the DMK front's sweep of thirty-eight. Between the two attempts lies the architecture's lesson in its purest form: heading a front of the state's smaller parties yields two seats, joining a Dravidian front yields none and subordination, and neither course transfers the substrate's custody. The 2024 attempt, a BJP-headed front with a larger vote and the same seat tally of zero, is the third data point in a series that no longer looks experimental.

The distinction the complication requires is between vote-share shift, which has occurred, and architecture displacement, which has not. The 2024 verdict returned the DMK-led front to all thirty-nine of the state's Lok Sabha seats; the AIADMK, fighting without the BJP for the first time in a parliamentary election since 2014, polled about a fifth of the vote and won nothing; the BJP won nothing. Under first-past-the-post, a challenger does not displace a bipolar system by rising to eleven percent against fronts polling forty-seven and twenty-three percent respectively; it displaces the system by breaking one front's alliance arithmetic, capturing a regional stronghold or inheriting a Dravidian party's collapse, and none of those three events has happened. The state's own history supplies the comparison that disciplines the realignment thesis: the Congress polled 41.10 percent in Madras in 1967, a share any challenger would envy, and was out of power in the state for good within one election cycle, because votes without a front, a substrate and a machine convert to nothing in this system. The BJP's strategists understand the arithmetic, which is why the party's Tamil Nadu project since 2014 has alternated between alliance with a Dravidian party, accepting junior status inside the architecture, and independent assertion against it; the first strategy concedes the architecture, the second has so far decorated it. Whether the post-2024 party can build the district-level organisation and the alliance of caste parties that conversion requires is a question the evidence available to this article cannot settle, and the article does not pretend otherwise. What the evidence settles is narrower and stronger: as of the 2024 verdict, fifty-seven years after its founding election, the architecture had absorbed the strongest national challenger of the modern era at the cost of eleven percent of a parliamentary vote and zero seats.

## Three Readings of One System: An Adjudication

The three readings can be weighed against the record assembled. The Dravidian-cultural reading, in the form Pandian and Ramaswamy give it, explains the content of Tamil Nadu politics better than any rival: why the parties speak the language they speak, why reservation and language defence are the state's sacred subjects, why a wounded film star's photograph could serve as a campaign's central text. Its weakness is that content is not structure. The same substrate, in the same decades, coexisted with a Congress-dominant system before 1967, a single-party Dravidian dominance from 1967 to 1977 and the bipolar alternation thereafter; a constant cannot explain a variable, and the substrate is the system's constant. The personality reading explains the system's episodes better than any rival: why 1977 differed from 1971, why the AIADMK's internal regime centralised as it did, why the successions of 1969 and 2018 were smooth and those of 1987 and 2016 were storms. Its weakness is symmetrical: episodes are not persistence. Four leaders of entirely different gifts, temperaments and generations have headed the system's parties, two of them died in office at the head of their parties, and the alternation outlasted every one; a variable that changes completely while the outcome holds constant is not the outcome's cause. The bipolar-system reading incorporates the other two as its content layer and its operating layer respectively, and it alone explains the three facts that define the case: that competition in Tamil Nadu is confined within a shared substrate which both custodians defend, that anti-incumbency is routed into alternation rather than system change, and that succession, split and disgrace are processed by two machines whose completeness is the system's real foundation.

Yogendra Yadav's periodisation of the Indian party system sharpens the same point from the national side. Yadav's influential scheme reads independent India's electoral history as a succession of party systems, a Congress system giving way after 1989 to a fragmented multi-party order and that order giving way after 2014 to a BJP-centred system. Tamil Nadu fits none of the transitions on schedule. Its Congress system ended in 1967, two decades before the national one; its post-1967 order was not the fragmentation the rest of India entered after 1989, since the state's two parties were already old, complete and ideologically bounded; and the BJP-centred national system after 2014 changed everything in the state's national alignments and nothing in its internal constitution. A state whose party-system clocks run twenty years fast, then decline to change with the national hour, is not a laggard in someone else's sequence. It is a separate system, and the burden of explanation lies on any account that treats it as a regional variant of the national story.

The comparative frame confirms the distinctness. Read against [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/) for contrasting state settlements, Tamil Nadu is the outlier on the dimension of party-system continuity: Uttar Pradesh has cycled through Congress dominance, Mandal-era fragmentation and BJP consolidation within the same half-century; Bihar's parties have formed, split and re-formed around shifting caste coalitions; Karnataka has sustained a three-cornered contest among the Congress, the BJP and the Janata Dal (Secular); West Bengal exchanged thirty-four years of Left Front rule for a Trinamool order built by a single leader's breakaway. Tamil Nadu alone, among the large states, has kept the same two parties, the same two fronts and the same ideological boundaries through ten changes of government. The 2004 parliamentary verdict, whose [three-state sectoral concentration](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/) showed how unevenly national waves break across state systems, displayed the same insulation in miniature: the DMK-led front in Tamil Nadu won all forty seats of the state's parliamentary contingent in that election, Puducherry included in the front's sweep, in a year when the national result turned on entirely different regional logics. State systems in India are semi-sovereign political worlds, and Tamil Nadu's world is the one whose constitution, written in 1967 and amended by the split of 1972, has never been revised.

The adjudication must also carry the bipolar reading's own limit, stated as plainly as its claim, because the substrate's unity has always concealed a fracture the architecture manages rather than heals. The non-Brahmin identity that unites the substrate joins intermediate castes and Dalits in a single political category, while the social order beneath it ranks those same communities in a hierarchy enforced, in the state's harder districts, by violence. Hugo Gorringe's Untouchable Citizens (2005), his study of Dalit movements in the Madurai region, documents the assertion that rose from within the non-Brahmin fold: the Dalit parties, above all Thol. Thirumavalavan's Viduthalai Chiruthaigal Katchi, built an independent Dalit politics that both Dravidian parties must court and neither can absorb. The burning of Dalit colonies in Dharmapuri district in November 2012, after an inter-caste marriage and the death of the Vanniyar bride's father, and the agitation the PMK then led against inter-caste marriages, showed the substrate's first component operating against its own junior partners. The bipolar architecture survives this fracture the way it survives everything else, by alliance: the VCK sits inside the DMK's front, the PMK has sat in both, and caste assertion is priced into seat-sharing rather than permitted to found a third pole. A complete account of the system must therefore say two things at once, and this article says them: the architecture is genuinely stable, and its stability is purchased partly by containing, within its two fronts, a Dalit question the substrate cannot answer on its own terms.

A word on evidence is owed, because the architectural reading rests on records whose survival is itself an institutional fact. The Election Commission's statistical reports give the seat and vote figures used throughout; the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly's published debates give the founding government's legislative record; the 1937 to 1940 and 1965 agitation files rest in the Tamil Nadu State Archives at Chennai. Arguments built on paper trails depend on the habits of those who keep them, a dependence as familiar to the historian of parties as it is to the working reporter, whose craft includes guarding the sources behind a story, a discipline described in the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). The reading defended here invites its own falsification from the same records: if the substrate map's four components can be shown to have been abandoned by either party in office, or if a third force takes and holds power in the state without adopting the substrate, the architectural claim falls, and the cultural and personality readings divide the field again. Scholarship advances by such invitations. Pandian's genealogy, Ramaswamy's history of language devotion, Subramanian's account of populist mobilisation, Wyatt's study of the state's political entrepreneurs and Barnett's earlier anatomy of the DMK's cultural nationalism, The Politics of Cultural Nationalism in South India (1976), are not refuted by the bipolar reading; they are assigned their proper floors in the building, foundation inscriptions and machinery, while the building itself is named.

## Why the Architecture Persists

The persistence has, in the end, four load-bearing causes, and they can be stated plainly. First, the substrate forecloses the ideological market: a challenger must adopt anti-Brahmin social justice, Tamil-language commitment and the anti-imposition stance to be heard at all, and a challenger who adopts them has conceded the premises of the parties it challenges. Second, the front arithmetic punishes independence: in a house of 234 single-member constituencies, a party outside the two fronts converts even a tenth of the vote into a negligible seat tally, as the BJP's 2024 result and the Naam Tamilar Katchi's repeated seatless returns demonstrate, so ambitious politicians and caste parties rationally seek places inside a front rather than martyrdom outside one. Third, the welfare battlefield raises the price of entry beyond any newcomer's treasury of credibility: forty years of delivered goods stand behind each incumbent machine's promises. Fourth, and beneath the rest, both parties are complete organisations, staffed at the booth level in every constituency, capable of losing an election without losing their structure and of losing a leader without losing their electorate. Systems persist when the costs of leaving them exceed the costs of staying for every actor who matters, voters, allies, cadres and leaders alike. Tamil Nadu's bipolar architecture has held that inequality in place since 1967, through the deaths of founders, the disgrace of governments and the arrival of the strongest national party of the age, and the record of fifty-seven years entitles the conclusion the cultural and personality readings resist: the state's politics is not the shadow of a movement or the sum of four biographies. It is a building, and the building stands.

One examination remains on the horizon that none of the four causes has yet faced, and candour requires naming it. The delimitation of parliamentary constituencies, frozen on the 1971 census by constitutional amendment and due to reopen after 2026, threatens the southern states with a reduction of their share in the Lok Sabha precisely because they succeeded at the population policies the Union preached; Tamil Nadu's parties across the aisle have signalled common cause on the question, as the substrate predicts they must, since the defence of the state's weight against New Delhi is the oldest reflex both parties inherit. How the federal argument of the next decade interacts with the state-level architecture is a question the record assembled here cannot answer, and the bipolar reading does not pretend to answer it. The reading's claim is about the fifty-seven years the records cover: within them, every force that has entered Tamil Nadu politics has been compelled to speak the substrate's language, join one of its two fronts or accept marginality. That compulsion, reproduced across three generations of voters and four changes of political era, is the fact of the system, and it is why Tamil Nadu's DMK-AIADMK order, alone among India's great state systems, has never needed to be refounded.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the DMK-AIADMK system?

It is the two-party structure that has governed Tamil Nadu since 1967, in which the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and its 1972 offshoot, the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, head rival alliance fronts and alternate in office. Both parties share the Dravidian movement's founding commitments: social justice through reservation, defence of the Tamil language, resistance to Hindi imposition and hostility to Brahmin predominance in public employment. No other party has headed a state government in the period, the Congress and the BJP have been reduced to junior allies or outsiders, and anti-incumbent votes move between the two Dravidian parties rather than to any third force, which is why scholars describe the arrangement as a system rather than a sequence of elections.

### Q: Who founded the DMK?

C.N. Annadurai founded the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam on 17 September 1949 at Robinson Park in Royapuram, Madras. Annadurai had been Periyar E.V. Ramasamy's principal lieutenant in the Dravidar Kazhagam, the social movement Periyar created from the Justice Party in 1944, and he broke with his mentor when Periyar married Maniammai in July 1949 and settled the movement's leadership on her, and when the DK's refusal to contest elections blocked the younger leadership's path to power. Annadurai took the DK's district organisers, its journals and its doctrine into the new party, gave it the rising sun as its symbol and led it for twenty years, becoming the state's first non-Congress chief minister in March 1967.

### Q: When did DMK first win Tamil Nadu?

The DMK first won the state in the assembly election of February 1967, when the state was still called Madras. Heading a broad anti-Congress front, the party won 137 of 234 seats on 40.69 percent of the vote, against 51 seats for the Congress, and Annadurai was sworn in as chief minister on 6 March 1967. The victory ended twenty years of Congress government in the state and no Congress ministry has held office there since. The state took the name Tamil Nadu on 14 January 1969, under the DMK government the 1967 verdict had installed.

### Q: What is the Dravidian movement?

The Dravidian movement is the century-long political and social current of the Tamil country that organised non-Brahmin resentment of Brahmin predominance into parties, governments and law. It began with the Justice Party of 1916 and the Non-Brahmin Manifesto, acquired a mass and radical form in Periyar E.V. Ramasamy's Self-Respect Movement from 1925, with its attacks on caste hierarchy, priestly authority and the subordination of women, and passed through the Dravidar Kazhagam of 1944 into the electoral parties the DMK and the AIADMK. Its permanent deposits in Tamil Nadu's public life are the reservation system, the two-language formula in schools, the legal recognition of self-respect marriages and a political vocabulary in which social justice is the master term.

### Q: Why is Congress weak in Tamil Nadu?

The Congress lost Tamil Nadu in 1967 and never rebuilt the conditions of its earlier dominance. Its state leadership, formidable under K. Kamaraj until 1963, was identified thereafter with New Delhi's decisions, above all the Hindi language policy behind the agitations of 1965, and with the dismissals of state governments in 1976, 1980 and 1991, which taught voters that a Congress vote empowered the centre against the state. Its organisation thinned as its ablest state politicians departed or died, and the two Dravidian parties occupied the entire space of regional assertion and welfare delivery. Since 2001 the Congress has survived in the state as a junior partner inside Dravidian-led fronts, winning assembly seats allocated by the DMK rather than by its own machinery.

### Q: Did Jayalalithaa's death destabilise AIADMK?

Jayalalithaa's death on 5 December 2016 destabilised the AIADMK's leadership without dissolving the party. She left no designated successor, and the struggle among O. Panneerselvam, V.K. Sasikala and Edappadi K. Palaniswami produced a chief ministerial change, a Supreme Court conviction that imprisoned Sasikala in February 2017, a factional merger that August and a breakaway party under T.T.V. Dhinakaran. Yet the AIADMK held office for its full term to 2021, retained its two-leaves symbol and district organisation, won 66 assembly seats in 2021 and installed Palaniswami as leader of the opposition. The episode separated the party's command, which was thrown into crisis, from its machinery, which carried the organisation through the crisis intact.

### Q: Who is M.K. Stalin?

M.K. Stalin is the president of the DMK and, from 7 May 2021, the chief minister of Tamil Nadu. Born on 1 March 1953, the younger son of M. Karunanidhi, he entered politics through the party's youth wing, was elected the first directly chosen mayor of Chennai in 1996, became a state minister in 2006 and deputy chief minister in 2009. Karunanidhi appointed him the DMK's working president on 4 January 2017, and the party's general council elected him president on 28 August 2018, three weeks after his father's death. His authority inside the party rests on three decades of organisational work and on the district secretaries whose careers he supervised, rather than on the literary standing that marked the party's earlier leaders.

### Q: Who was Periyar E.V. Ramasamy?

Erode Venkatappa Ramasamy, called Periyar, the great man, was the founder of the Self-Respect Movement and the ideological source of Dravidian politics. Born in 1879 at Erode, he worked in the Congress, led the Vaikom satyagraha of 1924 for temple-entry rights and left the party in 1925 over its refusal of communal representation, founding the Self-Respect Movement and its journal Kudiyarasu that year. He took the presidency of the Justice Party in 1938, led the resistance to compulsory Hindi in Madras schools and renamed his organisation the Dravidar Kazhagam in 1944. His rationalism, atheism and assaults on caste and patriarchy supplied the doctrine Annadurai carried into electoral politics in 1949. He died on 24 December 1973.

### Q: What was the Justice Party?

The Justice Party was the political organisation of the non-Brahmin movement in the Madras Presidency, founded on 20 November 1916 at Victoria Public Hall in Madras by T.M. Nair, P. Theagaraya Chetty and C. Natesa Mudaliar as the South Indian Liberation Federation. It took its public name from its English newspaper, Justice. Its Non-Brahmin Manifesto of December 1916 protested Brahmin over-representation in the presidency's services and colleges, and the party governed Madras for most of the dyarchy period from 1920 to 1937, issuing the first Communal Government Order on reservation in 1921. Periyar assumed its presidency in 1938 and converted it into the Dravidar Kazhagam in 1944, from which the DMK descended.

### Q: What was the Self-Respect Movement?

The Self-Respect Movement was the social movement Periyar E.V. Ramasamy founded in 1925 after leaving the Congress, organised through his journal Kudiyarasu and a network of conferences across the Tamil districts. Its programme joined the abolition of caste hierarchy to rationalism and atheism, attacked priestly mediation in religion, demanded women's rights in marriage, property and education, and promoted self-respect marriages conducted without Brahmin priests, a form the DMK government legalised in 1967. The movement turned the non-Brahmin grievance of the Justice Party into a comprehensive social doctrine and trained the generation, Annadurai at its head, that carried the doctrine into party politics after 1949.

### Q: What was the Communal Government Order?

The Communal Government Order was the Madras Presidency's pioneering reservation instrument, first issued by the Justice Party ministry in September 1921. It distributed appointments in the provincial services among the presidency's communities, Brahmins included, in stated proportions, converting the Non-Brahmin Manifesto's complaint about the distribution of government employment into administrative rule. A further order of 1927, under Premier P. Subbarayan's ministry, refined the shares and extended the principle. The Supreme Court struck down the Madras communal order in the Champakam Dorairajan case of April 1951, one of the provocations behind the Constitution's First Amendment, and the order's lineage runs forward to Tamil Nadu's present 69 percent reservation framework.

### Q: What is the Dravidar Kazhagam?

The Dravidar Kazhagam is the social movement from which the DMK split in 1949. Periyar E.V. Ramasamy created it at Salem in August 1944 by converting the Justice Party, on a resolution moved by his lieutenant C.N. Annadurai, into an organisation that renounced electoral office in favour of social transformation and the demand for a separate Dravida Nadu. The DK kept the movement's doctrines of atheism, caste abolition and Tamil assertion outside party politics, and it survived the DMK's breakaway as a pressure movement under Periyar until his death in 1973 and under his successors thereafter, standing as the ideological parent to which both Dravidian parties periodically acknowledge descent.

### Q: Who was C.N. Annadurai?

Conjeevaram Natarajan Annadurai, called Anna, elder brother, was the founder of the DMK and the first non-Congress chief minister of the state. Born at Kanchipuram in 1909, he rose as Periyar's lieutenant and the Dravidar Kazhagam's finest orator, broke with Periyar in 1949 and built the DMK into a statewide party through his essays, plays and film scripts and through a cadre culture that prized Tamil literary skill. He led the party to its 1967 victory, headed the government that renamed the state Tamil Nadu and legalised self-respect marriages, renounced the separate Dravida Nadu demand in 1963 and died in office on 3 February 1969, his funeral drawing crowds contemporary reports counted in the millions.

### Q: Who was M. Karunanidhi?

Muthuvel Karunanidhi was the DMK's dominant figure for half a century and chief minister of Tamil Nadu five times, in 1969 to 1971, 1971 to 1976, 1989 to 1991, 1996 to 2001 and 2006 to 2011. Born at Thirukkuvalai in 1924, he joined the Dravidian movement as a schoolboy, wrote the dialogue of the landmark film Parasakthi in 1952 and rose through the DMK as its treasurer and chief organiser, succeeding Annadurai in 1969 with MGR's support in the legislature party. His governments extended the reservation framework, created the Most Backward Classes quota in 1989 and built the welfare repertoire of competitive distribution. He died on 7 August 2018, having led his party for forty-nine years.

### Q: Who was M.G. Ramachandran?

Maruthur Gopalan Ramachandran, called MGR, was the founder of the AIADMK and chief minister of Tamil Nadu from 1977 until his death on 24 December 1987. Born in 1917 at Kandy in Ceylon, he became Tamil cinema's defining popular hero, playing benefactor-kings and virtuous commoners in films whose scripts the DMK's writers supplied, and he joined the DMK in 1953, becoming its treasurer and its most recognisable campaigner. Expelled in October 1972 after challenging the leadership over party funds, he founded the Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam on the strength of his fan clubs, won power in 1977 and universalised the school noon-meal programme in 1982. His screen persona, converted into organisation, made him the only film star to found a durable Indian party.

### Q: Why did MGR break with the DMK in 1972?

MGR's break with the DMK joined a personal grievance to an organisational one. Since Annadurai's death in 1969 he had been marginalised inside a party whose machine Karunanidhi controlled, treated, in his own account, as a crowd-drawing asset rather than a decision-maker, and denied the organisational standing his popularity warranted. In October 1972 he made the quarrel public by demanding, at a party forum and then in public statements, an accounting of the DMK's funds, which the leadership read as an accusation of corruption against the treasurer's office Karunanidhi's camp held. The general council suspended him on 10 October 1972, and on 17 October he announced the Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, carrying his fan clubs into the new party as its branch network.

### Q: Who was J. Jayalalithaa?

Jayaram Jayalalithaa was the AIADMK's leader from 1989 and chief minister of Tamil Nadu in 1991 to 1996, 2001 to 2006 with an interruption of months, 2011 to 2014 and 2015 to 2016. Born in 1948 and educated in Bengaluru and Chennai, she acted opposite MGR in twenty-eight films, joined his party in 1982 and became its propaganda secretary and a Rajya Sabha member. After MGR's death in 1987 she defeated his widow Janaki's faction at the polls in 1989 and inherited the united party, which she ran with extreme centralisation. Corruption convictions removed her from office in 2001 and in 2014 and acquittals restored her both times. She died in office on 5 December 2016, leaving no designated successor.

### Q: What is the two-front alliance system in Tamil Nadu?

The two-front system is the practice by which every Tamil Nadu election is contested between two standing coalitions, each headed by a Dravidian party. The DMK and the AIADMK allocate constituencies among their respective allies, the Congress, the communist parties, the PMK, the VCK, the DMDK and smaller caste and regional parties, in bargains settled before nominations open, so that each constituency presents the voter with one candidate of each front. The system dates from the DMK's anti-Congress front of 1967, whose seat arithmetic destroyed a Congress that had outpolled it statewide. Its consequence is that smaller parties matter as bloc-holders inside a front and almost never as independent contestants, and that a party outside both fronts faces a wasted-vote barrier in nearly every seat.

### Q: Why is Tamil Nadu's reservation quota 69 percent?

Tamil Nadu's quota stands at 69 percent because the state's reservation practice, begun by the Communal Government Order of 1921, expanded by stages under Dravidian governments and was defended politically at each stage. The framework settled at 69 percent, divided among Backward Classes, Most Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, after the Karunanidhi government created the 20 percent Most Backward Classes share in 1989 following the Vanniyar agitation. When the Supreme Court's Indra Sawhney judgment of 1992 imposed a 50 percent ceiling, the state legislature enacted its own statute preserving 69 percent, and Jayalalithaa's government secured its placement in the Constitution's Ninth Schedule in 1994, insulating the framework from the ceiling's ordinary application.

### Q: Why did the AIADMK retain power in 2016?

The AIADMK's victory in May 2016, with 134 seats in the initial count and 136 after the two deferred constituencies voted, broke a pattern that had held since 1984, because several forces aligned for the incumbent. Jayalalithaa campaigned on delivered welfare, including the free laptops, fans and mixer-grinders of her 2011 promises and a subsidised canteen network under her own brand, while the DMK fought under a ninety-two-year-old Karunanidhi's formal leadership with Stalin not yet the party's face. The opposition vote also divided: a third front, the People's Welfare Front, and Vijayakant's DMDK contested separately and cut into anti-incumbent support without winning seats. The result made Jayalalithaa the first incumbent chief minister returned by the state's voters in thirty-two years.
