Hegemony may be the most borrowed word in social theory and the least examined. It appears in newspaper columns about media power, in textbooks about empires, in seminar rooms about schooling, and in everyday speech as a grand synonym for being on top. Almost everywhere it travels, it arrives carrying the same small definition: domination achieved through ideas, rule by persuasion rather than by force, the quiet manufacture of agreement by those who control the cultural machinery. That definition is not false in the way a wrong date is false. It is false in the more damaging way, by omission. It leaves out the working parts that make Antonio Gramsci's concept do distinctive analytical work, and without those parts hegemony cannot be told apart from ideology, from propaganda, or from false consciousness, which is why students, journalists, and not a few scholars use the four terms as if they were interchangeable labels for a single complaint.

This guide rebuilds the concept from its working parts, in the order in which the parts depend on one another. It begins with the word's career before Gramsci, since hegemony was inherited from Russian social democratic debates and transformed rather than coined, and the inheritance explains the concept's shape. It then sets out the double formula at the centre of the Prison Notebooks, in which leadership over allies is paired with coercion over enemies and the state is grasped as political society and civil society together. From there it isolates the guide's centre, the condition that separates hegemony from every neighbour: a leading group must make real concessions, sacrifices of its own immediate interests, or it is exercising domination and merely calling it leadership. That condition, named here as the concessions condition, becomes the grading instrument of the article's findable artifact, the hegemony test, a four-question procedure for sorting genuine cases from loose usage. The concept's supporting machinery, common sense, contradictory consciousness, the historic bloc, passive revolution, and trasformismo, is assembled along the way, and the strongest objection to the whole construction, that the concept has grown so elastic it can explain anything and therefore explains nothing, is stated at full strength and answered before the guide closes.

## The misreading that has to be corrected first

The dominant misreading treats hegemony as a technique of deception. On this account, a ruling group holds power partly by persuading the ruled to accept ideas that serve the rulers: the newspapers flatter the arrangement, the schools teach reverence for it, the entertainments distract from it, and the subordinate population, its consciousness colonised, consents because it cannot see its own interests clearly. Hegemony, so defined, is brainwashing without a laboratory, propaganda that has soaked so deeply into ordinary life that it no longer looks like propaganda at all. The picture has a certain plausibility and a long pedigree in popularised accounts, and it survives because it flatters the person who holds it, who implicitly stands outside the deception, seeing through what neighbours cannot. Gramsci's notebooks give this picture very little support, and correcting it is the first duty of any serious treatment of the concept.

### Is hegemony a form of brainwashing?

No. Brainwashing implies passive victims and implanted beliefs, while Gramsci's consent is active, organised, and bound to interests. Subordinate groups accept leadership partly because the leading group has accommodated some of their real demands. Where nothing is conceded and nothing is genuinely accepted, his vocabulary supplies a different word: domination.

The correction begins with the texture of consent in the notebooks. Consent, for Gramsci, is not a mood of contentment and it is not the absence of complaint. It is an organised relationship, built through institutions, renewed through negotiation, and sustained in part by material arrangements that subordinate groups experience as gains: a wage settlement that holds, a franchise extended, a school system their children may actually enter, a legal protection that functions when invoked. A theory in which consent rested on deception alone would need the deception to survive daily experience, yet daily experience is exactly where Gramsci locates the subordinate's second, practical awareness of how the world works, the awareness he called contradictory consciousness. Workers who repeat the pieties of their rulers in the abstract also know, in the concrete, the price of bread, the temper of the foreman, and the distance between the promise and the pay packet. A concept of hegemony that required them to be dupes would collapse against its author's own account of their minds.

The second element of the correction concerns agency. In the manipulation picture, culture flows one way, downhill from the powerful to the powerless, and the only interesting question is how the flow is engineered. In the notebooks, subordinate groups are makers as well as receivers: they sustain their own institutions, elaborate their own intellectuals, preserve fragments of older worldviews, and periodically build counter-formations that contest the leading group's account of the world. Consent is therefore better pictured as a settlement under continual renegotiation than as a spell. Settlements can be one-sided, grudging, and unstable, and they can be broken. Spells, in the fairy-tale logic of the misreading, can only be lifted by the awakened observer, which is one reason the misreading is so comfortable: it assigns the observer the hero's part.

Third, the manipulation definition cannot perform the concept's basic sorting work. If hegemony means rule through ideas, then every durable order in history qualifies, since every durable order propagates ideas about itself, and the word adds nothing to ideology. Gramsci's own distinctions are sharper. Propaganda is a practice, not a structure: it is what a group does when it addresses opinion directly, and it can serve any arrangement including naked domination. Ideology, in his usage, is a conception of the world embodied in institutions and practices, something wider and more lived than a message. False consciousness, a phrase he rarely used and the tradition argues about, names a mismatch between position and awareness. Hegemony names something none of those names: a relation of leadership in which a group has made itself, through concessions and organisation, into the representative of interests wider than its own. The distinctions are not pedantry. They determine what evidence would count, what a researcher should look for, and what a political actor should do, which is precisely why the flattened definition, unable to distinguish, is unable to guide.

A final symptom exposes the misreading from the other side. If hegemony were manipulation, its opposite would be clear sight, and political change would consist of unveiling. Gramsci's strategic writings assume almost the reverse: that subordinate groups already harbour the critical fragments he called good sense, and that the obstacle is not blindness but organisation, the absence of institutions, intellectuals, and alliances capable of converting fragmentary criticism into an alternative leadership. The applied vocabulary that grows from the concept, including the war of position, makes sense only on the corrected reading, and the companion treatment of [gramsci and modern politics](/2015/04/01/gramsci-and-modern-politics/) follows the corrected reading into the uses and abuses of the term in political argument. What follows in this guide keeps faith with the same correction throughout: hegemony is a relation to be investigated, with conditions that can fail to be met, not an atmosphere to be deplored.

## A word inherited, not coined: the Russian career of hegemony

Gramsci did not invent the term, and the biography of the word before it reached him is not a curiosity for footnotes. It explains why the concept, in his hands, concerns leadership among classes and the terms on which alliances hold, rather than the management of opinion. The Greek root, hegemon, named the leader of a confederation, the city or commander that others followed, and the word carried a military and political sense of precedence for two thousand years before socialists took it up. Its Marxist career begins in Russia in the final decades of the nineteenth century, inside an argument about who would lead the coming revolution against the autocracy, and that argument fixed the word's grammar: hegemony was always the hegemony of some class over others, exercised in advance of power, and judged by whether the followers' own aims advanced under the leadership offered.

The first systematic user was Georgi Plekhanov, the founder of Russian Marxism, who argued from the eighteen eighties that the working class must win the leading role, the hegemony, in the struggle against tsarism, rather than leaving that struggle to the liberal bourgeoisie. The claim was strategic before it was philosophical. Russia's liberals, Plekhanov held, were too timid and too compromised to carry a democratic revolution through; the workers' movement, though a minority in an overwhelmingly peasant empire, could become the revolution's hegemon by championing demands that peasants, artisans, and the democratic intelligentsia could recognise as their own. Pavel Axelrod, his collaborator and later his opponent, developed the idea with the greatest care, spelling out that proletarian hegemony in a bourgeois revolution meant the working class earning the trust of the peasantry and the petty bourgeoisie by defending their interests against the autocracy, not merely announcing itself their vanguard. The peasantry is the load-bearing fact in this whole lineage. Any class aspiring to lead Russia had to answer the agrarian question, and hegemony named the quality of that answer when it was given in deeds.

Vladimir Lenin took the term into the centre of his strategic writing, above all in the pamphlet Two Tactics of Social-Democracy in the Democratic Revolution of nineteen five, where the proletariat is urged to become the leader of the whole people in the democratic overturn, carrying the peasantry with it against the landlords and the throne. The Leninist usage is instructive precisely because it predates any theory of cultural power. Hegemony there means political leadership of an alliance, won by a class whose programme answers the allies' needs, and it is contrasted, implicitly and explicitly, with tailing behind the liberals or with attempting to rule over allies instead of leading them. Readers who know the later Gramsci can already see the skeleton: leadership distinguished from domination, allies distinguished from enemies, and the leader's authority resting on the allies' recognition that their interests are being advanced. What the Russian usage lacks, and what Gramsci will supply, is the institutional and cultural depth, the account of where consent lives between revolutions, in the schools, churches, newspapers, and associations of a settled order.

### Where did the word circulate before the Prison Notebooks?

It circulated in Russian social democratic strategy, in the writings of Plekhanov and Axelrod and in Lenin's argument that the working class should lead the peasantry through a democratic revolution, and from there it passed into the vocabulary of the Communist International.

The Communist International carried the term into the common speech of the world movement during the nineteen twenties. In the debates over the united front, the formula of the hegemony of the proletariat named the goal of a workers' party that would win the majority of the working class and lead the peasantry and the urban middle strata, rather than confronting the state as an isolated sect. Grigory Zinoviev and other Comintern spokesmen used the word in congress resolutions and theses, and it travelled, in translation, through the parties of Europe. The significance of this transmission route is easy to miss. By the time Gramsci elaborated the concept in prison, hegemony was already a piece of shared strategic vocabulary with a fairly definite meaning in his milieu: the leadership of a class over its allies in a struggle for power. His originality lay not in adopting the word but in generalising it, asking what leadership consists of in a society where power is already held and must be reproduced daily, and in societies where the subordinate classes' consent is organised through dense institutions rather than gathered in moments of insurrection.

Italy gave the word a further local resonance. Gramsci wrote as egemonia, in a country where questions of leadership, of the Risorgimento's incomplete national integration, of the Southern question and the alliance between Northern workers and Southern peasants, were the substance of his political formation. The Lyons Theses of nineteen twenty six, drafted with Palmiro Togliatti for the Italian party's congress, already deploy the vocabulary of proletarian hegemony over the peasantry of the South. The prison writings then deepen a term their author had used for a decade. This genealogy carries a methodological lesson that the rest of the guide observes. When Gramsci's usage puzzles, the Russian grammar is often the key: ask who is leading, who is being led, over what questions, and at what cost to the leader, and the passage usually clears. When interpreters detach the word from that grammar entirely, treating hegemony as a free-floating property of cultures or texts, the puzzles multiply, because the word has been cut from the relations that gave it sense.

One more inheritance deserves marking before the notebooks take over, because it guards against a second misreading, subtler than the brainwashing picture. In the Russian debates, hegemony was something a subordinate class aspired to win on its way to power; in Gramsci, the same word also describes how a dominant class keeps power once won. Scholarship associated with Perry Anderson's long essay on Gramsci's antinomies showed that the concept stretches across both usages, and across shifting balances of consent and coercion, and Anderson pressed the stretches as inconsistencies. The better conclusion, developed in the sections that follow, is that the stretch is the theory: hegemony names a relation that must be constructed before power, exercised through power, and defended after power, and the conditions, above all the concessions condition, are what remain constant while the balance of its elements shifts. A reader who holds that thread can follow the concept through every transformation this guide describes, including its refinement in [antonio gramsci intellectual biography](/2015/01/01/antonio-gramsci-intellectual-biography/), where the term's development is traced through the life and the pre-prison journalism that prepared it.

## The double formula: leadership over allies, coercion over enemies

The Prison Notebooks, written between nineteen twenty nine and nineteen thirty five under censorship and in failing health, do not present hegemony as a finished doctrine with numbered theses. The concept is worked out across scattered notes, returned to from different angles, and refined against the history Gramsci was reading, above all the history of Italy and France. Reconstruction is therefore part of any honest guide's work, and the reconstruction begins from the passage family in which the concept's structure is clearest: the distinction, drawn in the notes on Machiavelli and on the Risorgimento, between two modes of supremacy. A social group, Gramsci writes in paraphrase, is dominant over antagonistic groups it seeks to liquidate or subdue, possibly by armed force, and it is leading in relation to the groups allied or kindred to it. Supremacy thus has two faces with different instruments. Toward enemies, a ruling group dominates, and the instruments are those of the state in the narrow sense: police, courts, prisons, army, the apparatus of coercion. Toward allies and toward the broad population whose support the order needs, the same group leads, and the instruments are those of civil society: the school, the church, the press, the association, the party, the professions, the whole web of institutions in which a conception of the world is elaborated and a way of life made normal.

The famous condensation of this double structure describes the state, in its integral sense, as political society plus civil society, and glosses the combination as hegemony protected by the armour of coercion, a seven-word formulation this guide quotes once and relies on throughout. The image deserves slow reading. Armour is not the body. Coercion, in the image, protects something softer and more vital that does the everyday work of rule, and if the armour is all an observer can see, the observer has missed the organism. At the same time the armour is real armour: Gramsci never dissolves the state into culture, and his integral state includes the coercive apparatus as an essential element, held in reserve and applied against those who refuse the settlement. The formula thus rebukes two opposite simplifications at a stroke. Against the liberal picture of the state as a neutral arbiter standing above society, it insists that the state's coercive and consensual functions form one structure in the service of a determinate social order. Against the crude picture of the state as nothing but a committee of oppression, it insists that durable rule is mostly transacted elsewhere, in the institutions where consent is organised, and that a class which relied on the armour alone would be confessing the failure of its hegemony.

### How does the state combine leadership and coercion?

In Gramsci's integral formula, the state joins political society, the apparatus of government and coercion, to civil society, the institutions where consent is organised. A ruling group dominates its enemies through the first and leads its allies and the wider population through the second, holding coercion in reserve behind consent.

The distinction between direction and domination, leadership and rule by force, does analytical work that cruder vocabularies cannot. Consider what it allows a historian to say about a nineteenth century liberal order. Such an order disfranchised most of its population, broke strikes with troops, and transported or imprisoned its agitators; the armour showed often enough. Yet its historians also record the extension of elementary schooling, the legalisation of trade unions in stages, the successive widening of the franchise, and the conversion of former opponents into parliamentary statesmen. The double formula reads those facts as one process: a bourgeoisie leading a bloc of allies, conceding reforms that cost it something, absorbing the leaders of movements it could not crush, and reserving coercion for the boundary cases, the insurrection, the general strike that threatened property itself. Neither half of the record cancels the other. The coercion does not prove the consent was fake, and the consent does not launder the coercion. Hegemony is the name for the combined operation, graded as stronger or weaker according to how much of the order's reproduction runs through the consensual circuit and how much must be dragged back to the armour.

The same distinction disciplines judgements about the concept's critics. Benedetto Croce, the liberal philosopher whose histories Gramsci studied most closely in prison, had separated the ethico-political history of liberty from the history of force, treating the first as the real story of nations. Gramsci absorbed Croce's insistence that ideas and moral leadership move history, while refusing the separation: in the notebooks, ethico-political leadership is exercised by classes with material bases, and its history is unintelligible when cut from the history of production and coercion. Norberto Bobbio, writing a generation later, argued that Gramsci had inverted the Marxist architecture by giving civil society, the realm of consent, primacy over the economic structure. The charge identifies a genuine emphasis while overstating the inversion. Gramsci's civil society is not free-floating spirit; its institutions rest on material organisation, its intellectuals are produced by classes, and its settlements are underwritten by concessions that cost the leading group real resources. The base is not abolished in the notebooks. It is joined, through the concept of hegemony, to the superstructure it sustains and is sustained by, which is why the historic bloc, treated later in this guide, names their unity rather than the victory of either term.

A word on method belongs here, because the double formula also determines how evidence for hegemony is gathered. If rule ran through deception, the evidence would be messages: editorials, curricula, broadcasts. If rule runs through the consensual circuit described here, the evidence is institutional and material as much as textual: which demands of subordinate groups are met and which refused, which organisations are legal, funded, and heard, which careers are open to the subordinate's children, which of their leaders sit inside the ruling bloc's committees and which sit in its prisons. The hegemony test, when this guide reaches it, is built from exactly that evidence. First, however, the two halves of the formula need separate development, beginning with the half that faces forward in time: leadership as something a group must achieve before it can rule, and keep achieving for as long as it rules.

## Leadership before power: direction as an achievement

The temporal structure of hegemony is its most neglected feature. In the notebooks' most quoted formulation of the matter, a social group can and must exercise leadership already before winning governmental power, and this exercise of leadership in advance is given as one of the principal conditions for the conquest of power itself, after which the group becomes dominant but continues to be leading as well. The sentence rearranges the usual order of political imagination. Leadership is not a prize collected at the door of the state, nor a charisma radiated by the victorious. It is an achievement built beforehand, in the period when the aspiring group controls no ministry and commands no police, and it consists of demonstrating, to classes and strata beyond its own ranks, that it can represent their interests, solve their problems, and articulate their grievances better than the incumbent order can. A group that has done that work arrives at power with its hegemony half built. A group that has not, however dramatic its seizure of the state, inherits the armour without the body, and must either construct consent under emergency conditions or rule increasingly by the instruments it had denounced.

Gramsci's analysis of the Risorgimento, the Italian unification he anatomised in the nineteenth notebook, is the concept's great negative case. The Moderate Party, the Piedmontese liberals around Cavour, achieved a form of hegemony over the Italian national movement, attracting even their republican rivals, the Action Party of Mazzini and Garibaldi, into a subordinate role, largely because the Moderates possessed a state, an army, a diplomacy, and a plausible programme, while the Action Party never built the alliance with the peasantry that alone could have given its democratic programme a social base. The result was a unification accomplished from above, by diplomacy and limited war, without the popular mobilisation that in France the Jacobins had achieved, and therefore a national state whose hegemony over its own South and its own countryside remained thin for generations. Gramsci's counterfactual yardstick is always France in seventeen eighty nine: there, on his reading, a rising class did the hegemonic work, forging the nation people, binding town and country, and universalising its aims in a language the peasantry could make its own. The comparison fixes the meaning of leadership before power with historical concreteness. It is the difference between a transformation that recruits its participants and one that merely processes them.

### What does leadership mean when it comes before power?

It means that a group earns authority over allies while still outside the state, by representing their interests, answering their grievances, and building the institutions and alliances through which a future order becomes imaginable. On Gramsci's account, this prior direction is a principal condition for conquering power at all.

Leadership of this kind has a specific structure, and it is worth disassembling, because each element generates a testable expectation. The first element is programme: the aspiring leader must formulate the allies' demands in a form that can actually be pursued, translating grievance into policy, and the translation always involves selection, since no alliance can promise everything to everyone. The second element is organisation: parties, unions, newspapers, cultural associations, and the intellectuals who staff them, the durable machinery through which a programme is argued, taught, and revised in contact with its intended beneficiaries. The third element is sacrifice, and it is the hinge between this section and the next. The aspiring leader must give things up, must compromise its own maximal programme and its own corporate advantage wherever the alliance requires it, and must be seen to do so, since allies extend trust in proportion to the costs the leader visibly bears for the common project. A leadership that demands all sacrifice from its followers and offers none of its own is already, in embryo, a domination searching for instruments.

The structure also explains why Gramsci treated intellectuals as a political category of the first rank. Every social group, he argued, creates together with itself one or more strata of intellectuals that give it homogeneity and awareness of its function: the entrepreneur's technicians and lawyers and journalists in one case, the workers' party organisers, union educators, and cooperative administrators in another. Intellectuals are the officers of hegemony. They elaborate a class's scattered experience into a conception of the world, carry that conception into the institutions of civil society, and conduct the daily negotiations through which consent is maintained. The traditional intellectuals, the clergy, the professoriate, the literary world, imagine themselves autonomous and above the fray, and that imagined autonomy is itself a hegemonic asset, since their judgements carry the authority of disinterest. A rising group must therefore either win over strata of traditional intellectuals or breed its own organic ones faster, and most successful hegemonies do both at once. When readers ask why a concept of class leadership spends so many pages on schoolteachers and newspaper editors, the answer is that the officers are where the leadership is exercised between elections and between crises.

There is a corollary that keeps the concept honest, and this guide flags it now because the objection section will need it. If leadership must be achieved before power and maintained after it, then hegemony is never a possession. It is a balance, continually struck and continually liable to be upset, as alliances shift, as promises fall due, as the leader's own composition changes. Gramsci's language for the upset is the crisis of hegemony, or organic crisis, in which the ruling class fails the tests it had previously passed: its representatives lose the trust of their traditional allies, its programme ceases to organise the nation's intellectual life, and masses pass suddenly from passivity to activity without finding a new leadership ready to receive them. Such crises, he observed, are the terrain on which Caesarist solutions and mere dominations grow. The temporal structure thus converts hegemony from a label into a narrative discipline: for any order, the analyst must be able to tell the story of its leadership's construction, maintenance, and possible decay, with dates and institutions attached. Vague invocations of cultural power survive none of that discipline, which is the point of imposing it.

## The concessions condition: leadership that costs something

Every concept in this cluster has one condition that does the distinguishing work, the requirement present in the concept and absent from all its neighbours. For hegemony, that condition concerns price. In the thirteenth notebook, examining what the exercise of leadership actually involves, Gramsci holds that the fact of hegemony presupposes that the interests and tendencies of the groups over which leadership is exercised are taken into account, that a certain equilibrium of compromise is formed, and that the leading group makes sacrifices of an economic and corporate kind. The passage continues with the limit that gives the condition its edge: such sacrifices and such a compromise cannot touch the essential, cannot extend to what the leading group experiences as decisive for its position in production. The architecture of the idea is now fully visible. Hegemony is leadership purchased by concessions that are real but bounded: real, because they transfer advantages, protections, or opportunities to subordinate groups at genuine cost to the leader; bounded, because the leader defends the core of its dominance and concedes from its margins, its surpluses, and its secondary preferences.

This guide names that architecture the concessions condition, and the naming is deliberate, because the condition converts an evocative word into a checkable claim. Where a ruling arrangement is said to be hegemonic, the condition instructs the analyst to ask what the leading group has given up, to whom, at what cost, and whether the recipients register the transfer as theirs. Where nothing has been given up, where the subordinate's compliance is extracted by fear, purchased individually as patronage, or merely assumed from their silence, the correct classification, in Gramsci's own vocabulary, is domination, possibly efficient, possibly durable, but not hegemony. The distinction is not a moral compliment to be awarded to nicer regimes. Concessions can be cynical in motive, grudging in delivery, and stabilising in effect precisely because they work. The condition is a diagnostic, and like all diagnostics it cuts against allies as readily as against opponents: movements and parties that claim to lead while conceding nothing to their supposed constituencies fail the test in exactly the same way as the states they oppose.

### Does hegemony require real concessions?

Yes. Gramsci holds that a leading group must make sacrifices of an economic and corporate kind, accommodating some interests of subordinate groups. Hegemony is therefore costly and negotiated rather than purely persuasive, which distinguishes it from propaganda and from domination by force or deception.

What counts as a real concession can be specified further, and the specification protects the condition from two evasions. The first evasion is the symbolic concession, the gesture that costs nothing: a holiday declared, a statue renamed, a minister photographed in a factory canteen. Symbolic acts are not worthless in politics, but they do not satisfy the condition unless they are attached to transfers the recipients can use, since a symbol whose material backing never arrives is quickly reclassified by its audience as decoration, and the reclassification itself corrodes consent. The second evasion is the universal benefit, the public good delivered to everyone at once, which the leading group would have supplied anyway out of its own interest in healthy workers, literate clerks, and passable roads. Public goods belong to the account of good administration. A concession, in the condition's strict sense, is indexed to the subordinate groups as parties to a settlement: it answers their organised demands, it can be pointed to by their representatives as something won, and its withdrawal would be experienced as a breach. Factory legislation limiting the working day in nineteenth century Britain satisfies the description, since it was fought for by organised labour, resisted by many employers, and inscribed in law against the immediate preference of the leading industrial class, which nevertheless, through its more far-sighted representatives, accepted the cost as the price of a stable order. Social insurance in Bismarck's Germany is the instructive boundary case: benefits were real and costly, but they were designed as a gift from above, paired with antisocialist laws and intended to bypass and weaken the workers' own organisations rather than to settle with them, and most analysts of the Gramscian tradition accordingly classify the arrangement as an attempt at domination with concessions attached, a hybrid the test later in this guide is built to grade rather than to force into a single word.

The condition also has a dynamic clause, and it is the clause with the sharpest consequences. Because consent is partly purchased by concessions, an order that begins withdrawing them, under fiscal pressure, ideological conviction, or the simple arithmetic of a leading group that no longer fears its subordinates, is not tightening its hegemony. It is spending it. Gramsci's framework predicts that such an order must then lean harder on the other instruments: on coercion first at the margins, on the absorption of troublesome leaders, on the manufacture of emergencies, and its classification drifts from hegemony toward domination as the withdrawals accumulate. The drift is observable in the record of institutions before it appears in anyone's speeches, which is what makes the condition useful to historians and journalists rather than only to theorists. A settlement's obituary is written in its budgets, its repealed protections, and its closed negotiating tables, and a researcher trained by this guide reads those documents before reaching for the editorials.

It remains to mark what the condition does not claim, since its precision is its defence. It does not claim that concessions are the whole of hegemony: a group could distribute benefits broadly and still lack the institutional and intellectual leadership that makes its worldview the horizon of an epoch, in which case it is a rich patron, not a hegemon. It does not claim that conceded ground is ever conceded permanently: every item in a settlement can be renegotiated, and the history of labour law in every industrial country is a history of advances and clawbacks. And it does not claim that the subordinate side of the bargain is passive in accepting it: organised groups extract concessions by the credible threat of disruption as often as they receive them by the leader's foresight, and the extraction is itself a form of the contestation that the next sections treat as hegemony's normal weather. The condition claims one thing only, and claims it without exception within the concept's proper use: leadership that concedes nothing material is not hegemony, whatever its cultural reach, and any analysis that calls it hegemony has emptied the concept of the very feature that made it worth importing into social science.

## Consent: active, organised, and never finally won

If concessions are hegemony's price, consent is its currency, and the notebooks' treatment of consent is the second place where the popular definition most badly misleads. In popular usage, consent slides toward acquiescence: the ruled do not rebel, therefore they consent, and their consent explains the order's stability. Gramsci's consent is a stronger and more demanding state than the absence of rebellion. It involves the active participation of subordinate groups in the order's institutions and, at its fullest, their adoption of the leading group's conception of the world as the frame within which their own demands are formulated. A population that obeys because the prison is near and the informer is nearer has not consented in this sense, however quiet its streets. A population whose unions negotiate inside the order's procedures, whose parties contest its elections on shared constitutional ground, whose children are taught its history as their own, and whose grievances are phrased as claims upon the order rather than as rejections of it, has consented, even when its members grumble daily and strike annually. The grumbling is compatible with consent. The quiet of the frightened is not evidence of it.

This is why the organisation of consent occupies so much of the notebooks. Consent is not found in nature; it is produced, by identifiable labour, in identifiable places. The leading group's intellectuals elaborate its worldview into forms suited to every level of the culture, the systematic philosophy at the top, the newspaper editorial and the school textbook in the middle, the proverb and the serial story at the base, and the institutions of civil society carry those forms into the routines where they harden into what Gramsci called common sense. Consent is also maintained, against entropy and against rivals, by the settlement machinery described in the concessions discussion: the negotiations, recognitions, and incremental reforms through which subordinate organisations are kept inside the order's procedures and given reasons to police their own militants. And consent is tested, periodically, in the moments Gramsci analysed as crises, when wars, depressions, or scandals break the identification between the led and their leaders, and the masses pass from political passivity to activity in search of a new direction. An order's hegemony is the name for its capacity to survive those tests with its leadership intact, renewed, or successfully transferred to an allied successor.

### Why must consent be active rather than passive?

Because passive compliance can be produced by fear, habit, or exhaustion, none of which distinguishes hegemony from domination. Active consent means subordinate groups participate in the order's institutions and phrase their demands within its frame, and it must be continually rewon because each generation and each crisis renegotiates the settlement.

The activity requirement does another piece of work: it explains hegemony's characteristic fragility, which purely cultural accounts consistently miss. If consent were a deposit of belief installed in childhood, it would decay slowly and evenly, and ideological rule would be the most stable kind. In Gramsci's world, consent is more like a coalition government, perpetually one bad season from a confidence vote. The leading group ages; its promises compound into expectations it cannot meet; its allies' own development produces strata, the skilled worker's son become engineer, the peasant's daughter become teacher, whose interests the old settlement never priced. Meanwhile the subordinate classes are not standing still inside their consent. Their experience of work and neighbourhood generates good sense, the practical criticism of the order's claims, and their organisations keep the memory of every broken undertaking. Hegemony must therefore be rewon in the strong sense: re-argued, re-financed, and re-staffed, by each generation of the leading group, against competitors who promise the subordinates a better settlement. The moment a ruling group treats consent as its property rather than its achievement, the notebooks suggest, it begins the transition toward ruling by the armour, whatever its propagandists still believe about its cultural supremacy.

Historiography supplies the confirming pattern in both directions. Orders that historians retrospectively describe as hegemonic, Victorian Britain in its mid-century decades is the standard case argued over, turn out on inspection to have been noisy with contest: strikes, riots, mass petitions, sedition trials, and a popular press of real ferocity. The consent visible in such orders was never unanimity; it was the containment of ferocious contest inside institutions the contestants mostly accepted as the arena. Conversely, orders that achieved perfect quiet, the police states of the twentieth century in their most controlled years, are precisely the ones Gramsci's vocabulary refuses the title, classifying them as dominations whose stability was borrowed from the apparatus and collapsed with a speed that astonished observers who had mistaken silence for belief. The lesson generalises into a rule of method for anyone applying the concept: treat contest as compatible with hegemony and silence as no evidence for it, and the concept's judgements begin to track the record instead of the observer's impressions. The complementary error, treating every contest as proof that hegemony has failed, is equally common and equally lazy, since Gramsci's whole construction assumes a subordinate population with its own organisations, its own intellectuals, and its own standing capacity to withdraw what it granted.

## Civil society: the terrain where consent is organised

Hegemony needs a place to happen, and Gramsci's name for the place is civil society: the ensemble of organisms commonly called private, the churches, schools, universities, publishing houses, newspapers, professional associations, trade unions, clubs, and cultural institutions that stand between the individual and the coercive state. The concept of civil society has its own full treatment elsewhere in this series, in [gramsci on civil society](/2015/03/15/gramsci-on-civil-society/), and this guide takes from that treatment only what the logic of hegemony requires. Three points are required. The terrain is institutional, not atmospheric: consent is organised by bodies with addresses, budgets, staffs, and gatekeepers, which is what makes hegemony researchable rather than impressionistic. The terrain is contested, not owned: the same institutional field that carries the leading group's worldview also shelters the subordinate groups' unions, chapels, mutual aid societies, and oppositional papers, so that civil society is better imagined as a trench system occupied by both sides than as a loudspeaker in one side's hands. And the terrain is deep, in the developed societies Gramsci called the West, in a way it was not in the Russia of nineteen seventeen: behind the state's outer ditch of coercion stood, in his military metaphor, a sturdy structure of fortresses and earthworks, so that a frontal assault on the state, the war of manoeuvre, shattered against positions the assault never reached.

That depth explains the strategic corollary for which the notebooks are most famous, the war of position, the long contest for the institutions of civil society that must precede and accompany any durable challenge to an entrenched order, and the companion article on [war of position and manoeuvre](/2015/02/15/war-of-position-and-manoeuvre/) develops the strategy in full. For the purposes of this guide, the strategic point matters less than the analytical one it reveals. If consent lives in institutions, then hegemony can be mapped: the analyst can enumerate the institutions through which a given order's worldview is taught, celebrated, and enforced by prestige, can identify the intellectuals who staff them, can trace the resources that sustain them, and can observe the subordinate population building or capturing counter-institutions of its own. Hegemony, on this showing, is closer to an infrastructure than to a spell, and its study belongs as much to the sociology of organisations as to the criticism of texts.

### Which institutions organise consent in civil society?

Schools and universities, churches, newspapers and publishing, professional bodies, trade unions, parties, and the whole range of voluntary associations. Through them a leading group's conception of the world is taught, celebrated, and made normal, and through rival institutions inside the same field, subordinate groups organise consent of their own.

The institutional reading also corrects a distortion introduced by the phrase cultural hegemony, the compound through which the concept reached most of its later audience. The compound suggests that hegemony is a property of culture, of representations and meanings, and it invites the analyst to read novels and advertisements while ignoring school boards and licensing laws. Gramsci's civil society certainly includes the makers of representations, but it includes them as organisations embedded in law and property: the newspaper is a business with owners and advertisers, the school a public or confessional bureaucracy with hiring rules, the church a hierarchy with seminaries and discipline. Culture, in the notebooks, is never disembodied. It is what institutions do to form people, and its power is the power of formation repeated daily at scale: the timetable, the examination, the sermon cycle, the editorial line sustained across decades. That is why the transfer of power, when it comes, so rarely suffices by itself to transfer hegemony. The ministry changes hands in a weekend. The professoriate, the judiciary's habits, the officer corps' ethos, the press's ownership, and the common sense of the population change across a generation, if they change at all, and the interval between the two changes is the space in which revolutions are consolidated or lost.

A structural feature of the terrain deserves separate emphasis, because it will matter to the test: civil society is where the boundary between leaders and led is policed and crossed. Subordinate groups enter the institutions of the order as pupils, parishioners, readers, members, and litigants, and a fraction of their ablest members is continually promoted out of them into the order's service as teachers, priests, journalists, officials, and parliamentarians. Gramsci analysed this circulation under the heading of trasformismo, and it is treated with the other maintenance mechanisms later in this guide. The converse movement matters as much: the institutions can be colonised from below, as unions colonised the industrial tribunal, as working class congregations bent chapels toward socialism in more than one country, as colonised peoples turned mission schools into the training grounds of nationalist leaderships. Civil society is therefore not the leading group's instrument in any simple sense. It is the field on which hegemony is exercised and the field on which its rivals prepare, and any account that lists the institutions without noting the two-way traffic has described the battlefield as if only one army were on it.

## Common sense and contradictory consciousness: the terrain within

Hegemony's outer terrain is civil society's institutions; its inner terrain is what Gramsci called common sense, and his use of the term reverses its everyday meaning. In ordinary speech, common sense is sound practical judgement, the wisdom of people who see through nonsense. In the notebooks, common sense, senso comune, is the folklore of philosophy: the incoherent sediment of conceptions deposited in a population by successive epochs, religions, and ruling groups, held uncritically, contradictory within itself, and conservative in effect because it presents the arrangements of the present as the nature of things. It contains Stone Age survivals and fragments of advanced science, proverbs of resignation and proverbs of cunning, the catechism and the union song, all jumbled without system, and its incoherence is precisely the point. A population's common sense is not a worldview it has chosen. It is the residue of every worldview that has ever been hegemonic over it, and to study it is to read the archaeology of past leaderships in the minds of the living.

Within that sediment Gramsci located a healthier element, which he called good sense: the practical awareness generated by people's own activity, their knowledge in the hands that the official account of the world is not the whole of it. The worker who repeats that the boss deserves his wealth because he takes the risks also knows, from the inside of the working week, who actually bears the risks of the dangerous machine and the short month, and the two knowledges coexist. That coexistence is contradictory consciousness, the condition Gramsci assigned to the subordinate as their normal state: one conception of the world, inherited verbally and affirmed on ceremonial occasions, superimposed on another conception, implicit in practical activity and in the solidarity of the group at work. The contradiction is not a defect of intelligence. It is the accurate registration, inside one mind, of a social position that is itself contradictory, and it is the raw material from which both hegemony and its opposite are built. Hegemony governs through the inherited conception, keeping the practical one private, fragmentary, and ashamed of itself. Counter-hegemony begins when the practical conception finds intellectuals, institutions, and a language in which it can become public and systematic.

### What separates good sense from the sediment around it?

Good sense is the critical nucleus generated by practical activity itself: what people learn from work, cooperation, and the gap between official promises and lived results. The sediment around it is inherited conception, absorbed uncritically from religion, folklore, and past ruling groups, and the two coexist in the same mind.

The full conceptual neighbourhood of consciousness, including the contrast with the classical doctrine of class consciousness and its Lukácsian development, is treated in [class consciousness explained](/2011/09/15/class-consciousness-explained/), and the present guide observes the boundary the brief assigns: contradictory consciousness enters here as a working part of hegemony, not as a topic in its own right. Even within that boundary, three implications deserve development, because they do so much to correct the manipulation misreading at its root. The first is that Gramsci's subordinate is never an empty vessel. The raw material of criticism exists before any agitator arrives, which is why the notebooks scorn the enlightener's posture, the intellectual who imagines he brings light to darkness. The organiser's task, in the famous pedagogical inversion Gramsci drew from the philosophy of praxis, is reciprocal: every teacher is a pupil and every pupil a teacher, and an intellectual formation that cannot learn from the practical consciousness of its base will elaborate a worldview nobody can live inside. The second implication concerns religion, which Gramsci treated as the most complete form of common sense for the popular classes of his Italy: not a fraud perpetrated on the simple, but the historically given shape in which their conception of the world, their morality, and their consolation were organised. A counter-hegemony that merely mocked it would forfeit the population it hoped to lead, a practical conclusion the notebooks press with unusual insistence against the anticlerical reflexes of the movement's own tradition.

The third implication is methodological, and it is the one researchers most often skip. If the subordinate's consciousness is contradictory, then evidence about belief cannot be gathered by asking people what they think and transcribing the answer, since the answer will be drawn from the inherited conception, the one that has words ready. The practical conception must be inferred from practice: from what people do when the wage is short, from the jokes that circulate where the foreman cannot hear, from the saints actually prayed to and the clauses of the official creed quietly dropped. Historians of popular culture who learned this lesson, whether or not they cite the notebooks, transformed the study of past mentalities by reading behaviour against profession. It is also why Gramsci, planning a history of Italian intellectuals, proposed to study common sense through its deposits in language, proverb, and custom rather than through treatises. Hegemony, once consciousness is understood as contradictory, stops being a hypothesis about what the powerful did to minds and becomes a research programme about which of the subordinate's two conceptions the institutions succeed in organising, and for how long, and at what price in concessions paid to keep the practical conception from finding its voice.

## The historic bloc and the national popular: hegemony at full scale

When the elements assembled so far, leadership, concessions, organised consent, intellectuals, and the mobilisation of common sense, are fused with a determinate social base over a whole epoch, Gramsci's name for the resulting formation is the historic bloc. The term is routinely mistranslated in use as a synonym for a political alliance, a coalition of parties or classes, and the mistranslation loses the concept's substance. An alliance is a bargain among partners who remain what they were. A historic bloc is a unity in which the partners are partly remade: the structure of production, the institutions of civil society, and the ruling conception of the world fit together and reinforce one another, so that the social base generates the intellectuals it needs, the intellectuals articulate the base's interests as a universal programme, and the programme, embodied in law and custom, protects and extends the base. Gramsci's formula insists on the materiality of the joint: the bloc is the concrete unity of the economic structure with its superstructures, not a treaty between them, and its solidity is measured by how much strain the joints can bear before the elements begin to move separately, which is his definition of an organic crisis approached from the other side.

The Risorgimento supplies the anatomy lesson again, now at full scale. The Moderates' Italy was a bloc of a peculiarly incomplete kind: a narrow agrarian and commercial base, a brilliant but thin stratum of liberal intellectuals, a monarchy and an army as its cement, and, beneath, a peasantry attached to the bloc by little more than conscription, taxation, and the parish priest. The Action Party's failure to bind the peasantry to the national cause, above all on the land question, meant that the bloc's foundations in the countryside were never laid, and the consequences run through the whole subsequent history Gramsci was diagnosing: the Southern question as a permanent wound, the reliance on trasformismo to govern, and the vulnerability of the liberal order, after the Great War, to a movement that promised the excluded a place in the nation on brutal terms. Against this stands, once more, the Jacobin counter-model, in which a revolutionary leadership bound town and country into something like a national popular bloc by conceding the peasantry its central demand, the land, at the moment of maximum danger, thereby manufacturing the mass base that defended the revolution. The concession appears again, in the role the concessions condition assigns it: not decoration on leadership, but the welding material of the bloc itself.

### Why does the national popular matter to the argument?

Because hegemony is complete only when a class's programme is lived by the wider population as its own national life, its language, literature, and aspirations included. A leadership that remains a foreign body in the nation's culture, however correct its doctrines, has allies on paper and no bloc in fact.

The national popular is the cultural dimension of the same requirement. Gramsci observed, in the notes on literature, that Italy lacked a national popular literature: its celebrated authors wrote for a cosmopolitan caste, its popular reading was supplied by translated French serial novels, and the Italian people, as readers, were absent from Italian letters. The observation generalises into a test of hegemonic depth. A class is hegemonic over a nation when its intellectuals speak the nation's language in every sense, when its tastes and judgements form the common reader's expectations, when the line between its particular programme and the nation's common life becomes difficult to draw. The test cuts against parochial vanguards of every kind. A workers' movement speaking only the idiom of the workshop, a liberalism speaking only the idiom of the salon, a nationalism speaking only the idiom of the barracks: each may dominate a sphere while failing the nation, and Gramsci's category explains the failure as a hegemonic deficit rather than a messaging error. It also explains why his prison research ranged so widely, into serial novels, theatre, journalism, and the history of the Italian language question. He was inventorying the joints of a bloc that had never fully formed, looking for the points where a different formation might have been welded.

Two clarifications prevent the bloc concept from hardening into a slogan. The first is that blocs are historical, not eternal: they are assembled under definite conditions, they exclude as they include, and they carry the terms of their own dissolution inside their founding settlements, since every concession priced into the bloc becomes an expectation the bloc must keep meeting as its base develops. The second is that the bloc's unity is never total even at its strongest. Gramsci's own analysis of the Risorgimento insists on the internal gradients, the North leading, the South led in a subordinate and exploited mode, the alliance of Northern industry with Southern latifundia settled over the peasantry's head. Hegemony inside a bloc is therefore stratified: the same formation can be hegemonic in one region or sector and merely dominant in another, and a careful application of the concept specifies the domain of every claim it makes. That discipline of domains, modest as it sounds, is what separates the concept's serious users from its incantatory ones, and the hegemony test below builds the discipline into its questions.

## Passive revolution and trasformismo: change without a hegemonic subject

Two further concepts complete the working set, and both describe ways in which an order can change, or appear to change, while hegemony in the full sense is absent, evaded, or in decay. The first is passive revolution. Gramsci borrowed the phrase from Vincenzo Cuoco's writing on the Neapolitan revolution of seventeen ninety nine and expanded it into a general instrument: a transformation of the social and political order carried out from above, by the state and its existing elites, in which the demands pressing from below are partly satisfied in molecular, дозиrovanny form, granted in doses, while the population remains passive, excluded from participation and initiative. The Risorgimento was his paradigm, a national revolution accomplished without the Jacobin moment, without the agrarian reform and popular mobilisation that would have made the people its subject. But the instrument proved portable. Gramsci applied it, tentatively and with self-questioning, to the molecular changes of the liberal epoch after eighteen seventy, to the corporative experiments of the regime under which he was imprisoned, and he wondered whether it could interpret transformations in which a new order is assembled by decree, importation, and administrative reform while the classes whose lives are rearranged watch from the stalls.

Passive revolution matters to this guide because it marks a limit case the hegemony test must be able to detect. In a passive revolution, concessions undeniably occur: railways are built, codes are modernised, some careers are opened, some grievances are administratively answered. Yet the settlement lacks hegemony's other marks. The concessions are not negotiated with organised subordinate groups but dispensed to atomised recipients; the new order builds few institutions through which the population actively consents, preferring prefects and plebiscites; and the intellectual leadership of the transformation remains monopolised by the old elites, whose worldview the changes extend rather than replace. The formation that results is characteristically brittle in a specific way: it cannot call on reserves of loyalty in a crisis, because it never banked any, and it must purchase or coerce support afresh at every emergency. Distinguishing passive revolution from hegemony is therefore not taxonomic fussiness. It predicts different futures for the two formations, and the distinction is among the concept-cluster's most valuable exports to comparative history, where the modernisations of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries divide, on inspection, into those that recruited their populations and those that merely processed them, with the brittleness following the line of division.

The second mechanism, trasformismo, works at the level of personnel. Gramsci took the term from the Italian parliamentary practice named for the nineteen seventies and eighties, when Agostino Depretis and his successors governed by absorbing individual deputies of the opposition into the majority, dissolving organised alternatives into a single centrist amalgam of notables. Generalised, trasformismo names the molecular absorption of the subordinate groups' leaders, their intellectuals, organisers, and spokesmen, into the ruling bloc, where their talents are rewarded and their constituencies are left headless. It is hegemony's immune response to rival leadership: cheaper than repression, quieter, and often welcomed by the absorbed, whose personal ascent is real. Its long-run cost is paid by both sides. The subordinate group, decapitated repeatedly, learns that its own institutions are ladders out of itself, and invests its trust accordingly; the ruling bloc, staffed increasingly by converts, loses contact with the constituencies it has absorbed and governs a population it no longer understands, mistaking the silence of the headless for the consent of the led.

### How does trasformismo weaken an opposition?

By absorbing its leaders one by one into the ruling bloc, where their careers are made and their organisations are left without intellectuals, spokesmen, or continuity. The opposition is not defeated in argument or crushed by force; it is decapitated by promotion, repeatedly, until its capacity to formulate an alternative withers.

Both mechanisms illuminate a feature of hegemony that single-aspect accounts flatten: it is an ecology, not a state. Consent is produced by institutions, underwritten by concessions, staffed by intellectuals whose loyalties are continually bid for, and contested by subordinate groups whose own formations grow inside the same field. An order can hold the field while degenerating along every dimension at once, conceding less, absorbing more, substituting administrative change for negotiated settlement, and it will still look stable, because the forms of consent remain standing after their substance has thinned. Gramsci wrote, in a memorable diagnostic image, of the interregnum in which the old is dying and the new cannot be born, and of the morbid symptoms that appear in the gap. The mechanisms in this section are the morbid symptoms' physiology. They are what an analyst inventories when the surface is calm and the question is whether the calm is hegemony's or merely its shell.

## The hegemony test: four questions that do the sorting

Everything so far converges on a procedure. The working parts are now on the table: leadership distinguished from domination, concessions as the price of leadership, consent as active and organised, civil society as the terrain, common sense as the matter worked on, and the bloc as the formation at full scale. The hegemony test converts those parts into four questions that can be put to any arrangement claimed to be hegemonic, and its purpose is classificatory honesty. It asks whether consent is present, whether concessions are made, whether the arrangement is contested, and whether it operates through civil society institutions. An arrangement that passes all four is hegemony in the concept's strict sense. An arrangement failing one or more is something else, and the test's value lies in naming the something else precisely, since each failure points at a different neighbour concept and a different diagnosis.

| Question of the test | Hegemony | Ideology | Propaganda | False consciousness | Simple coercion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Is consent present? | Yes: active participation by subordinate groups in the order's institutions | Not necessarily: a doctrine can be held by a group alone | No: an audience is addressed, not enrolled | No: awareness is mismatched to position, whatever the order does | No: compliance rests on fear of sanction |
| Are concessions made? | Yes: the leading group sacrifices economic and corporate interests for its allies | No requirement: a worldview costs its holders nothing by itself | No: messages are cheap and are priced accordingly | No requirement: the mismatch can persist under any settlement | No: extraction replaces exchange |
| Is the arrangement contested? | Yes: rival leaderships organise inside the same field, and consent must be rewon | Indirectly: doctrines compete, but not for leadership of a bloc | Partly: rival messages compete for attention | Unclear: the category presumes the contest is already settled in minds | Resistance is treated as crime, not as a bid for leadership |
| Does it operate through civil society institutions? | Yes: schools, churches, press, associations, and unions carry the settlement | Yes, partly: doctrines live in institutions, but need no settlement to sustain | Partly: media are used as channels, not as settling institutions | No: the mismatch is located in individual consciousness | No: the apparatus of the state is the whole machinery |

The table's columns deserve a paragraph each in prose, because a test is only as good as the reasoning it compresses. Consent, the first question, separates hegemony from every arrangement in which compliance is sullen, terrified, or purely habitual, and it does so by demanding positive evidence: subordinate organisations participating, demands being phrased as claims on the order, leaders of the led sitting inside the order's councils. Concessions, the second question, separate hegemony from ideology and propaganda at a stroke, since a worldview and a message campaign can both be conducted at no material cost to their sponsors, while leadership on Gramsci's account is invoiced. Contestation, the third question, is the one lazy applications most often fail their subjects on: hegemony exists only where a rival leadership is possible, where consent could in principle be transferred, and a situation so closed that no alternative can be formulated is not hegemony triumphant but hegemony absent, its place taken by administration or terror. The fourth question, the institutional one, separates hegemony from false consciousness as a diagnosis: false consciousness, if the term is used at all, describes a state of individual awareness, whereas hegemony describes a social machinery, and a researcher who answers a question about machinery with a claim about awareness has changed the subject. The fuller three-way comparison of hegemony with ideology and with discourse, which the test deliberately compresses into single rows, is conducted at length in [ideology hegemony discourse compared](/2012/04/01/ideology-hegemony-discourse-compared/), and readers whose question is that comparison should follow the test with that treatment.

### When a case fails the test, what has been found?

A different and more precise classification. Failure on consent indicates domination, on concessions a merely ideological or propagandist rule, on contestation a closed administration rather than a leadership, and on the institutional question a diagnosis about minds where a claim about a social order was needed.

Worked applications teach the test faster than definitions do, and three historical cases, chosen because they grade differently, carry the lesson. The first is the liberal Italian order of the later nineteenth century, Gramsci's own specimen. Consent: present but stratified, real in the propertied North's institutions, thin to vanishing in the South, where the state appeared as tax collector and recruiting sergeant. Concessions: scarce and grudging, the franchise widened in steps under pressure, labour organisation harassed for decades, the land question refused outright. Contestation: vigorous in form, socialists and Catholics organising millions, yet managed by trasformismo's absorption of leaders faster than movements could replace them. Institutions: a genuine civil society in the centre North, a near vacuum in the Mezzogiorno. The verdict the test returns is a graded one, hegemony of a partial and regionally uneven bloc, shading into domination at its southern margin, and the grading matches, and usefully sharpens, the notebooks' own judgement of that order as a hegemony manquée, a leadership that never completed itself nationally.

The second case is the British mid-Victorian settlement as its historians argue over it. Consent: strong evidence, in the absorption of Chartism's remnants into liberal politics, in unions seeking and gaining legal standing, in a popular press and chapel culture that phrased working class aspiration largely within the order's own vocabulary of improvement and respectability. Concessions: identifiable and costly, factory acts enforced against employers, the franchise extended in eighteen sixty seven to urban working men by a Conservative ministry calculating the price of leadership, public health and education built at ratepayers' expense. Contestation: open and continuous, from the Reform League to the rise of independent labour representation, with the order's institutions surviving each enlargement. Institutions: as dense a civil society as the century offered anywhere. The test returns hegemony, and returns it for the same period in which coercion remained available and was used, against Irish nationalism with a severity that fails the test outright, which is exactly the domain discipline the bloc section required: the same state, hegemonic in one relation, dominant in another, and an analysis that cannot say both has not finished its work.

The third case is the negative control, drawn from the colonial historiography that borrowed the concept most rigorously. Ranajit Guha and the Subaltern Studies historians examined British rule in India and concluded that it constituted dominance without hegemony: an order whose coercive superiority was never matched by a capacity to organise the active consent of the colonised, whose concessions were negligible beside its extractions, whose institutions of civil society reached a thin urban layer while the countryside was governed through landlords and police. Guha's verdict is valuable to this guide beyond its Indian application, because it demonstrates the test's teeth. A fashionable concept that ratifies every powerful order would have pronounced the Raj hegemonic on the strength of its schools, its law courts, and its English educated intermediaries. The four questions refuse the ratification, and the refusal is the kind of service the concept exists to perform. Where the test fails an arrangement that power's own publicity calls consensual, the failure is a finding, not an embarrassment, and the finding directs research toward the coercion, the absences, and the rival formations gathering underneath the quiet.

## Counter-hegemony: withdrawing consent and building an alternative

A concept of rule that could not describe its own supersession would be a poor instrument, and hegemony contains its opposite inside itself. Counter-hegemony names the construction, by subordinate groups, of an alternative leadership: their own intellectuals elaborated from within, their own institutions thickened from mutual aid society to party school, their own conception of the world raised from the fragments of good sense into a systematic and teachable form, and their own alliances negotiated with neighbouring strata on the basis of concessions they themselves are prepared to make. The symmetry is exact and deliberate. Whatever the test demands of a ruling bloc, it demands of the challenger too, and a challenger that promises its allies nothing, organises no institutions, and articulates no programme beyond the denunciation of the incumbent is not a counter-hegemony in formation. It is a protest, and protests, in Gramsci's analysis, are the raw material of change rather than its vehicle, since the interregnum's morbid symptoms grow precisely where protest accumulates faster than alternative leadership.

The Turin factory councils of nineteen nineteen and nineteen twenty are the biographical root of the whole construction, the experience from which the prison theory grew. In the council movement, Gramsci and the Ordine Nuovo group saw workers creating, inside the factory, an institution that prefigured a different order: delegates elected by all workers regardless of union membership, recallable, organised by production unit, and capable in embryo of running the plant. Whatever the movement's fate, it taught its theorist the form counter-hegemony takes when it is serious. It does not begin by storming the ministry. It begins by building, inside the existing society, the institutions in which a different common sense is practised daily, where the subordinate experiences himself as a producer and citizen rather than as a hand, and where the intellectuals of a new bloc are trained by the activity itself. The prison writings generalise the lesson into the war of position, the mole's work of institutional construction and contestation that the companion article develops, and the generalisation retained the Turin criterion: an alternative counts as hegemonic in embryo when it forms people, not only when it assembles crowds.

Withdrawal of consent, the dramatic half of the process, is usually the less important half. Consent is rarely withdrawn in a single gesture of refusal; it erodes, as the settlement's terms are missed, as the leading group's representatives are caught serving interests they had pledged to subordinate, as a war or a depression reveals that the bloc's promises were denominated in a currency it cannot print. Gramsci's analysis of organic crisis describes the phenomenology of that erosion: the ruling class has lost its consensus, is no longer leading but only dominant, exercising coercive force alone, while the great masses have become detached from their traditional ideologies and no longer believe what they used to believe. The detachment is the necessary condition of transformation and also its most dangerous moment, since a vacuum of leadership does not stay empty. It is filled by whoever possesses organisation, audacity, and a programme at the moment of detachment: by the counter-hegemony if one was patiently built, by Caesarist arbiters and adventurers if none was. The notebooks' warnings on this point are among their most sombre pages, written by a man watching the vacuum of the Italian postwar years fill with exactly the force his own movement had failed to outbuild.

For the analyst, counter-hegemony supplies the concept's forward test, the one that keeps its judgements honest in the direction of hope as the four questions keep them honest in the direction of description. Claims that a new hegemony is arising, in a movement, a region, or a profession, should be met with the same inventory demanded of incumbents: which institutions has the challenger built or captured, which intellectuals has it formed, which allies has it bound by concessions it can actually pay, and which elements of the population's contradictory consciousness has it succeeded in organising into a rival common sense. Where the inventory is thin, the vocabulary should be thin to match: a mood, a moment, a media success. Where the inventory is dense, the analyst is looking at the early architecture of a different order, whatever the incumbent's publicity says, and Gramsci's whole career, from the Turin councils to the prison cell where he catalogued his enemy's fortresses, is the demonstration that he intended the inventory to be drawable in advance of the event, by observers willing to count institutions instead of headlines.

## From corporate interest to hegemony: the three moments and catharsis

A class does not begin as a potential hegemon. It begins, in Gramsci's most systematic piece of political analysis, at a level of consciousness he called economic and corporate: the solidarity of the individual tradesman with his fellows, of the factory owner with other owners, the sense that the group's immediate interests must be defended against competitors and the tax collector. The first moment is parochial by definition, and most groups, most of the time, live inside it. The second moment widens the circle: consciousness reaches the solidarity of interests among all members of the social group, but still on the economic plane, the demand for legal and political equality with the dominant groups, for a share in legislation and administration, for reform of the existing framework rather than its replacement. Trade union consciousness at its most developed, and the liberalism of a rising commercial class in its reforming phase, are Gramsci's illustrations of the second moment's ceiling. What defines it is that its demands can, in principle, be granted by the existing order without the order ceasing to be itself, and the history of concessions is largely the history of second moment demands being bought off at exactly that price.

The third moment is the strictly hegemonic one, and its criterion is universalisation. The group becomes conscious that its own corporate interests, in their present and future development, transcend the corporate circle and can and must become the interests of other subordinate groups. Its programme is no longer a schedule of its own claims but a conception of the whole society's future, in which peasants, artisans, intellectuals, and the marginal strata can each find their own advancement represented. Gramsci called the passage to this moment catharsis, borrowing the term and bending it: the passage from the purely economic, or egoistic and passional, to the ethico-political, from structure to the sphere of complex superstructures, from necessity recognised and endured to freedom practised. The word is chosen to mark that the transition is not a mere widening of demands. It is a change in the quality of a class's self-understanding, the point at which a class stops asking what it can get and starts deciding what the society should become, and recruits the intellectuals capable of elaborating that decision into law, pedagogy, and common sense.

Catharsis gives the concessions condition its deeper rationale. A group still enclosed in the first or second moment experiences every concession as a loss, a tribute extracted from its winnings, and pays it resentfully and temporarily. A group that has made the passage to the third moment experiences concessions differently, as investments in the universality of its own project, the price of converting its particular ascent into a general settlement in which its leadership appears as the nation's interest. This is why Gramsci's historical judgements so often turn on the quality of a rising class's intellectuals. The passage is an intellectual and moral achievement before it is a political fact; it requires the elaboration of a conception of the world in which subordinate groups can recognise themselves, and classes whose intellectuals fail the elaboration remain, however powerful economically, at the level of domination tempered by bargaining. The distinction supplies one of the test's sharpest interview questions, transferable to any case: does the leading group speak of its settlement as its victory generously shared, or as the form the whole society's progress necessarily takes? The first idiom betrays the corporate moment enduring inside power. The second, where it is matched by concessions actually paid, is hegemony's native tongue.

The schema also arms the reader against a sentimental error about alliances. Because the third moment is defined by the subordination of corporate interest to a universal programme, an alliance in which the leading group merely aggregates the corporate demands of its partners, promising each its price, has not achieved the hegemonic moment at all; it has achieved a cartel, and cartels dissolve at the first change in prices. Gramsci's contrasts return obsessively to this point: the Moderates aggregating notables against the Jacobins universalising a nation, the trade union official bargaining a wage against the party elaborating a civilisation. Hegemony, on this account, is rare for a principled reason. It demands of a class the most difficult operation in politics, the conversion of its own interest into a general interest that remains honest when the bill arrives, and history's more common products, domination with publicity, bargaining with ceremony, are what fill the space where the operation fails.

## The economic nucleus: why hegemony must also be economic

A persistent suspicion about the whole construction should be answered on the evidence of the notebooks themselves: that hegemony, by dwelling on consent, culture, and intellectuals, floats free of the economy and becomes a theory in which ideas rule the world. Gramsci foreclosed the reading in a sentence whose structure this guide paraphrases with care. Hegemony, he wrote, is ethico-political, but it cannot fail also to be economic, cannot fail to have its foundation in the decisive function the leading group exercises in the decisive nucleus of economic activity. The sentence assigns the economy the role of foundation without reducing leadership to a reflex of it. A group qualifies for hegemony by its place in production, by commanding the activities on which the society's material life depends, and its intellectual and moral leadership is built on that qualification, the way a building's upper storeys presuppose, without being deducible from, its foundations. Groups with no decisive economic function, however brilliant their intellectuals, can disturb, adorn, or prophesy; they cannot durably lead, because the settlement they offer cannot be financed and the future they programme cannot be built.

The essay on Americanism and Fordism, written in the notebooks' later phase, shows the foundation and the leadership being constructed together, and it is the closest Gramsci came to watching a new hegemony being born. Henry Ford's factories, on his analysis, were not merely a production method. High wages, the celebrated five dollar day, were the economic instrument of a new civilisation: they purchased a workforce stable enough for the assembly line's discipline, they created consumers for mass production, and they were accompanied, in Ford's own company towns and in the America Gramsci studied from his cell, by an intense moral regulation of the worker's private life, the policing of drink, sexuality, and family budget, which Gramsci read without illusion as the formation of a new type of worker and of human being, fitted in body and in habit to the new production. The case is precious to this guide because it runs the hegemony test inside a single firm and finds the components legible at that scale. Consent: sought and partly won, through wages men queued to earn. Concessions: real, costly, and indexed to the workforce's demands for a life beyond subsistence, yet bounded at the essential, since the line's pace and the union's exclusion were not negotiable. Contestation: present and eventually victorious against the founder, in the union organising Ford resisted for two decades. Institutions: company, suburb, school, and prohibition's moral machinery carrying the settlement into daily life. Fordism, in short, is hegemony's economic nucleus caught in the act of secreting a civilisation, and Gramsci's fascination with it was the fascination of a theorist watching his categories operate on a continent that had never heard his name.

The nucleus doctrine also disciplines the concept's application to classes in decline. A class losing its decisive economic function, the landed aristocracy of the industrial epoch is the type case, may retain for generations the prestige, the institutions, and the intellectuals of its former leadership, and may continue to supply the forms in which a society imagines itself. Gramsci's analysis of such survivals, in the notes on the Italian South's agrarian bloc and on the parasitism of strata living on rent and state employment, treats them as hegemony's husk: the consent they command is inherited rather than renewed, their concessions are paid from the product of other classes' labour, and their leadership lasts exactly as long as the rising class that will replace them takes to complete its own catharsis. The husk can be remarkably durable, and the notebooks are a sustained warning against mistaking its durability for vitality. When the test is applied, the first question after the four is therefore always available as a cross-check: on what decisive economic activity does this leadership rest, and is that activity waxing or waning in the society's life? The answer does not settle the classification, but it tells the analyst whether the settlement being graded is a going concern or an estate in probate.

## The Southern question: the alliance as a practical problem

Abstract conditions become credible only when they are watched at work in a single concrete problem, and Gramsci left one perfectly documented case of alliance construction in his unfinished essay of nineteen twenty six on the Southern question, written weeks before his arrest and treated ever since as the notebooks' seed. The problem was structural. Italy's industrial North and agrarian South were bound in one state and divided by it: Northern industry was protected by tariffs the Southern consumer paid for, Southern agriculture exported wine, oil, and people, and the Southern peasantry, the majority of the nation's poor, was governed through a bloc of landlords, officials, and intellectual intermediaries that delivered its votes and its silence to whatever ministry held Rome. The socialist tradition Gramsci inherited had mostly regarded the South as a ballast of backwardness, and its rural poor as a reserve of reaction to be waited out. His essay reversed the valuation. The peasantry of the South and the islands, he argued, was the Italian revolution's indispensable ally, and the Turin working class, which he had helped to organise, could become hegemonic nationally only by making the Southern peasant's cause its own.

The mechanics he prescribed read as a checklist of this guide's conditions in their practical form. Concessions first: the alliance required the Northern workers' movement to sacrifice positions convenient to itself, above all the protectionist settlement from which Northern industry and its workers drew advantage, and to champion demands, the division of the great estates, an end to the fiscal squeezing of the South, that answered the peasantry's grievances rather than the proletariat's corporate programme. Intellectuals second: Gramsci analysed the Southern intellectual stratum, the rural bourgeoisie's sons become priests, teachers, lawyers, and functionaries, as the cement of the agrarian bloc, mediating between the peasant mass and the landlords and the state, and he argued that the alliance would be won or lost in the contest for that stratum's younger generation, some of whom, he noted in the dissident figures he admired, were already breaking toward the peasantry's side. Consent third: the peasant was not to be lectured into the alliance but enlisted through organisations and struggles in which his own experience taught the connection between his landlord and Turin, between the tariff and his bread. And the whole construction was aimed against the standing temptation of the Northern movement, to treat the South as an object of pity or contempt, attitudes Gramsci named as the precise symptoms of a class still enclosed in its corporate moment, incapable of the catharsis that national leadership requires.

The case earns its place in a concept guide for two reasons beyond its drama. It shows that the concessions condition was not a late prison refinement imposed on an earlier, simpler politics: the condition is present in nineteen twenty six, in the most practical document its author ever wrote, as the arithmetic of an alliance that could not be had for free. And it shows the concept operating in its forward, counter-hegemonic direction with the same rigour the test applies to incumbents. Gramsci does not ask whether the Turin workers are right, which he takes as settled between himself and his readers. He asks what their rightness will cost, in programme surrendered and habits unlearned, before it becomes leadership, and the question's hardness is the measure of the theory. Movements that find the question impertinent, that expect allies to arrive by the justice of the cause, re-enact in every generation the failure the essay was written to prevent, and discover in the event that the agrarian bloc, or its local equivalent, has kept the countryside and the state that goes with it.

## The school: hegemony's slowest institution

Of all the institutions of civil society, the one Gramsci analysed with the most personal intensity was the school, and his notes on education condense the concept into a single policy argument that any reader can test. The liberal Italy of his youth had reformed its schooling under Giovanni Gentile, sorting children early: a classical secondary school, centred on Latin and philosophy, for the stratum destined to rule, and vocational tracks for the rest, presented as respect for different aptitudes. Gramsci's prison response defended the older common school, the disinterested, formative education in which all children, including the children of peasants and workers, were put through the same demanding humanistic curriculum, grammar, Latin, history, and the discipline of study as a form of labour, before any specialisation. His reasoning was hegemonic in the technical sense. The vocational track, however modern its rhetoric, ratifies the existing division between rulers and ruled at the level of formation: it trains the subordinate for a function while reserving to others the general culture from which leadership is recruited. The common school, by contrast, manufactures the possibility of organic intellectuals from the subordinate classes themselves, since a peasant's son who has mastered the instruments of abstraction and expression can become his class's spokesman instead of its clerk.

Two features of the argument generalise. The first is its treatment of effort: Gramsci insisted, against the progressive pedagogies of his day, that learning is work, that the child of the poor is owed not a diluted curriculum in the name of relevance but the same rigorous initiation the ruler's child receives, because the instruments of hegemony are not transferred by sympathy. The second is its identification of the teacher as an intellectual whose formation decides a generation's common sense more durably than any newspaper. A society's school settlement is therefore among the most legible documents an analyst of hegemony can open: who is taught what, in what language, sorted at what age, by teachers formed in which institutions, answering to which ministry and which church. Curriculum wars, which recur in every democracy and bewilder commentators who treat them as distractions from real politics, are on this account real politics in its slowest and most consequential form, the contest over which conception of the world the next generation will absorb as the nature of things. The guide's readers who work in education will find the classroom application developed symmetrically in [teaching ideology in the classroom](/2012/06/15/teaching-ideology-in-the-classroom/), where the same institutional patience is turned to method.

## Evidence: how a researcher would know

A concept earns its keep in the archive, and the working parts assembled in this guide converge on a practical question that students and researchers put with justified impatience: granted the definitions, what would hegemony actually look like in the evidence, and how would its absence be documented? The question deserves a concrete answer, source by source, because the manipulation misreading survives longest among people who have never been told what else to look for. Six families of evidence recur in the serious literature, and each answers to one of the test's questions.

The budget is the first document, and the concessions condition makes it so. A settlement's concessions are expenditure: social insurance contributions levied on employers, factory inspectorates salaried, council houses built, school places multiplied, agricultural prices supported, tax burdens shifted between classes and regions. The analyst reads the fiscal record of an order the way a physician reads a chart, watching the direction of transfers across decades and, above all, the reversals, since the withdrawal of concessions is earlier and more legible in budgets than in speeches. Second come the negotiation archives: the minutes in which organised subordinates and the leading group's representatives actually bargain, the union congresses, the party conferences, the episcopal letters, the memoranda in which a ministry prices a reform against the disorder it would prevent. Bargaining records are hegemony's smoking gun, because they show consent being manufactured as a transaction, with both sides conscious of the exchange. Third, the career evidence: the biographies of the subordinate groups' ablest children, traced in cohorts, reveal whether the institutions are circulating talent upward fast enough to staff trasformismo or slowly enough to let a counter-elite accumulate, and school and university statistics, read by class and region, extend the same measurement to a generation at a time.

The fourth family is curricular and liturgical: what the schools teach as national history, what the churches preach as social doctrine, what the popular press treats as beyond argument, gathered not as specimens of falsehood but as the map of a settlement's common sense, including its silences, since a common sense is defined as much by the questions it never poses as by the answers it rehearses. Fifth, the demands of the subordinate in their own words: strike notices, petitions, union resolutions, and election addresses, read for their frame, since the single most diagnostic fact about a population's consciousness is whether its grievances are phrased as claims within the order's promises, the signature of consent, or as indictments of the order's right to exist, the signature of its withdrawal. Sixth and finally, the crisis record: how the order behaved when its settlement was struck by war, depression, or scandal, whether it could call on loyalty it had banked, improvise concessions under pressure, and transfer leadership among its own fractions without the armour becoming the government. Orders that historians call hegemonic are, in the archive, orders rich in the first five families of evidence and composed in the sixth. Orders that fail the test leave thin files and thick police records, and the contrast is visible to any researcher who knows to weigh the files against each other rather than merely counting the editorials the order produced about itself.

This evidential discipline has a final consequence worth stating, because it separates the concept from the commentary built upon it. Hegemony claims are, in principle, revisable by research. New fiscal series can demote a supposed settlement to a bookkeeping illusion; newly opened negotiation archives can promote an order dismissed as merely repressive into a graded case of partial hegemony; demand frames can be coded and compared across countries with a rigour the impressionistic usage never attempts. The concept therefore belongs to the historian's workshop and not only to the theorist's vocabulary, and the students for whom this series reserves its fullest treatment of the thinker will find the workshop discipline continued in [gramsci exam answers](/2015/05/15/gramsci-exam-answers/), where the evidence families are converted into answer technique.

## The word abroad: hegemony among states

No map of the concept is complete without the export trade, because the most numerous uses of the word in print concern the relations of states, where hegemony names the preponderance of one power over a system of others, a regional hegemon, a hegemonic stability expected to supply order as a public good. International relations theory built this usage partly from the Greek root directly and partly through Gramsci, and the two strands must be distinguished if confusion is to be avoided. The preponderance usage is older, simpler, and innocent of consent: a state is hegemonic in a region when its material capabilities dwarf its neighbours', and nothing in the usage asks whether the neighbours accept its leadership, receive concessions from it, or organise their own common sense around its example. Judged by this guide's test, preponderance so defined is domination's cousin, not hegemony's instance, and much confusion in public argument comes from sliding between the two senses inside a single paragraph, borrowing Gramsci's prestige for a claim about tonnage.

The Gramscian strand in international theory, associated above all with Robert Cox and the scholars who followed him, is a serious extension and deserves its due. Cox transposed the apparatus to world order: a hegemonic order, in his construction, joins material capabilities to ideas and institutions at the international level, rests on the consent of subordinate states organised through international institutions, and is reproduced by the internationalisation of the leading state's civil society forms, its universities, its professional standards, its models of the firm and the central bank. The concessions condition survives the transposition in recognisable form, as the access to markets, the liquidity in crises, and the institutional voice that a leading power must supply if subordinate states are to experience its order as a settlement rather than a racket. Stephen Gill's work on disciplinary configurations of global capital extended the analysis to the constitution-like locking in of settlements. Readers meeting hegemony in the literature of world politics should therefore ask the sorting question this guide has made habitual: is the author measuring preponderance, or is the author claiming leadership, with consent organised and concessions paid? The first is a respectable claim needing capability data. The second is Gramsci's claim, needing the whole inventory, and the inventory, at world scale, is where such arguments are won and lost.

## Relations of force: the analyst's checklist

Hegemony is not asserted; it is located, and Gramsci supplied the location instrument in the notes on the analysis of situations, the most methodologically explicit pages of the thirteenth notebook. Any concrete situation, he argued, presents relations of force at three levels, and a political judgement that skips a level will mistake the possible for the actual. The first level is the relation of social forces, bound to production: the distribution of classes and strata as the economy has made them, measurable almost objectively, in numbers, wealth, and position, and not alterable by anyone's will in the short term. The second level is the relation of political forces, the degree of homogeneity, self-awareness, and organisation the various groups have reached, and it is here that the three moments of consciousness, corporate, solidaristic, and hegemonic, do their work, since the same population, objectively counted, can stand at any of the three levels and its effective force varies accordingly. The third level is the relation of military forces, broadly understood to include the politico-military, the capacity of the opposed camps for organised coercion and the technical and moral state of the instruments that wield it. Hegemony, as a research object, lives mostly at the second level, but it is constrained from below by the first and shadowed from above by the third, and the notebooks' analyses move among the levels with a discipline that their more lyrical readers often drop.

The checklist explains a judgement this guide has relied on repeatedly, that hegemony claims must specify their domain. A formation can stand at the hegemonic moment politically within one region while resting, at the first level, on a base too narrow to sustain it nationally, as with the Italian Moderates; it can possess the third level overwhelmingly while failing the second entirely, as with colonial orders whose subjects never organised consent; it can advance at the second level for a generation and then be thrown back by a shift at the first, when the decisive nucleus of economic activity migrates and the classes formed around it dissolve. Gramsci distinguished, in the same pages, between organic movements, relatively permanent and structural, and conjunctural ones, occasional, immediate, almost accidental, and he warned that political error consists in mistaking one for the other: treating a conjunctural wave of discontent as an organic crisis of the order, or an organic transformation as a passing squall to be waited out. The distinctions are unglamorous, and that is their virtue. They convert the question is this hegemony into a schedule of sub-questions with evidence attached to each, which is where the concept stops being a sensibility and starts being a method.

## The Modern Prince: the party as collective intellectual

Every construction in this guide implies an agent, and Gramsci named the agent in the figure he borrowed from Machiavelli and refounded: the Modern Prince, the political party understood not as an electoral machine or a sect of the convinced but as the collective intellectual in which a social group's will toward hegemony is formed, tested, and organised. The Prince of the notebooks is a protagonist of a new type, not an individual condottiere but an organism, a complex element of society in which a collective will, already recognised and asserted in action up to a point, begins to hold together. Its functions enumerate the working parts of hegemony from the agent's side. It elaborates the group's catharsis, carrying its members from corporate grievance to a conception of the whole. It forms and promotes intellectuals, running the schools, newspapers, and publishing houses through which organic intellectuals are made rather than merely found. It negotiates the alliances and pays the concessions by which a class becomes a bloc. And it conducts the war of position inside civil society, contesting the incumbent's institutions trench by trench while building the alternative formations in which a different common sense is rehearsed.

The Jacobins serve, once again, as the historical proof of concept, and Gramsci's admiration for them is among the notebooks' most consistent threads. The Jacobins, on his reading, were a party in his sense before the type existed: intransigent in programme, rooted in the popular classes of Paris yet capable of binding the peasantry to the revolution by conceding the land settlement, pitiless toward the revolution's enemies and tireless in creating the institutions, clubs, committees, festivals, and a new calendar, in which a new citizen was formed. Their ruthlessness interests him less than their completeness: they understood, as the Italian Moderates never did, that a revolution must manufacture its own hegemony while it fights, or the fighting wins a state and loses a nation. Against them he sets the parties of his own time that had shrunk into parliamentary fractions or bureaucratic apparatuses, organisations that could administer a consent they had inherited but not create one they lacked. The standard is severe, and it applies to the concept's friends first. A party that cannot run a school, sustain a newspaper its intended readers actually read, form intellectuals from the class it claims to represent, and state the concessions it is prepared to pay its allies, has not failed at propaganda. It has failed at the prior task, the construction of the collective intellectual, and no subsequent cleverness in messaging will substitute for the missing organism.

## The state as educator: law, conformism, and the manufacture of consent

One notebook theme draws the consensual and coercive circuits into a single institution and deserves separate treatment, because it resolves an apparent paradox in the double formula. The law, in the liberal picture, belongs wholly to political society: it commands, forbids, and punishes. Gramsci's notes on law and on the state as educator assign it a second, premiative and formative function. A modern state does not only repress the activities it condemns; it rewards, honours, finances, and publicises the activities it wishes to multiply, raising the civilised level of the population in a determinate direction, and its law is the register in which a hegemonic project announces what kind of human being it is trying to produce. Schooling obligations, public health campaigns, the regulation of drink and of working hours, the conferring of decorations and the funding of monuments: the instruments are pedagogical before they are penal, and their target is what Gramsci called conformism, the alignment of the population's habits with the requirements of the order's production and its morality. Conformism, in this usage, carries no sneer. Every social order requires some of it, and the political question is never whether habits will be formed but by whom, toward what type of person, and with what share of the population's own activity in the forming.

The theme completes the account of consent's organisation by catching its most intimate level. Institutions teach doctrines; the educator state forms dispositions, the punctuality of the factory hand, the thrift of the saver, the hygiene of the mother, the patriotism of the conscript, through arrangements so ordinary that their formative work escapes notice, which is exactly what makes the work effective. Gramsci's Fordism notes belong to this register as much as to the economic one: the new industrialism's interest in its workers' marriages, drinking, and domestic budgets was the educator state operating inside a private firm, manufacturing the human material a new production regime required. The analyst of hegemony accordingly reads sumptuary laws, school-leaving ages, temperance campaigns, and public health drives with the same attention given to budgets and curricula, asking of each the guide's standing questions: whose type of person is being formed, at whose expense, with whose active participation, and against which rival formation of the same population by church, union, or movement schools of their own.

## Language: the quietest evidence of leadership

Gramsci was trained as a linguist, a student of Matteo Bartoli at Turin, and the notebooks return to language with a persistence that puzzled editors until the pattern was seen: he treated language as hegemony's most delicate instrument and its most accessible evidence. Every language, he argued, contains a conception of the world, and the unification of a national language, the victory of one dialect and one standard over its rivals, is never a merely technical event. It is the cultural form of a class's leadership, the process by which a leading stratum's speech becomes the nation's norm while other speeches are demoted to dialect, jargon, or error. His distinction between normative grammar, the codified standard taught in schools, and spontaneous grammar, the rules speakers actually follow and remake, is the double formula in miniature: a norm imposed from above that never meets the spontaneous life of speech remains a dead letter, while the living standard is the one continually negotiated between the academy and the street. Manzoni's project of washing Italian in the Arno, the proposal that the nation adopt Florentine usage, served Gramsci as the type case of a linguistic hegemony attempted by decree and failing for want of the social bloc to carry it.

For the working analyst, the linguistic notes license a distinctive kind of evidence. The words an order naturalises, its official names for taxes and wars and welfare, the terms that pass without quotation marks into the speech of its opponents, mark the frontiers of its common sense more precisely than its editorials do, because a vocabulary accepted is a classification of the world accepted with it. Conversely, the subordinate's counter-vocabularies, the renaming of the boss, the tax, and the war in workshop and kitchen speech, are the fossils of good sense at work, and their migration, when it happens, into newspapers and statutes is among the surest traces that a counter-hegemony has begun to bite. A concept of cultural leadership that cannot be checked anywhere is a mood; in language, Gramsci the linguist insists, it can be checked in dictionaries, in schoolbooks, and in the drift of ordinary words, and the checking is available to any patient reader.

## The subaltern: whose consent is at stake

The last working part names the people across the table. Subaltern, in the notebooks' usage, gathers the groups subject to another group's hegemony: the peasants, the industrial workers in their subordinate aspect, the Southern labourers, the women of the popular classes, the colonial peoples as other writers would extend the term, all those whose history, in Gramsci's bleak formulation in the notes on subaltern history, is intertwined with that of civil society and therefore fragmentary and episodic, recorded in the archives of their rulers' initiatives more often than in documents of their own. His methodological notes for the history of subaltern groups read as a research ethic: trace their formations even when dispersed, their compromises with the dominant formations, the birth of their own parties and their reclamations of autonomy, and expect the record to be discontinuous because subalternity itself interrupts the keeping of records. The ethic matters to this guide because it fixes the standpoint from which the hegemony test is administered. The test is not the ruling group's self-description audited. It is an instrument built to be used from below, by asking of any order the questions its subordinates would ask if the archive were theirs: what has been conceded to us, in what institutions are we enrolled as participants rather than as material, by what machinery are our leaders bought, and where, in the interstices of its civil society, are our own formations growing.

That standpoint also disciplines the guide's own tone at its close. Hegemony, in the literature this article has corrected, is too often written as the biography of the victors' cleverness, with the led appearing as the cleverness's medium. The notebooks, written by a prisoner whose movement had been crushed and whose class was being administered by its enemies, never make that mistake: their subordinates are thinkers in fragments, organisers in embryo, and the eventual authors of any settlement that will replace the one under analysis. The concept's final working part is therefore a direction of attention, away from the palace and toward the parish, the workshop, the school queue, and the kitchen table, where consent is actually transacted and where its withdrawal, when it ripens, will first become visible to those who have learned where to look.

## Organic crisis and Caesarism: when the settlement breaks

Hegemony's decay has its own anatomy in the notebooks, and the key distinction is between a conjunctural difficulty and an organic crisis. Governments fall, ministries shuffle, newspapers declare emergencies weekly; none of that touches hegemony. An organic crisis begins when the failure is in the leading class itself: when it has failed in some major political undertaking for which it demanded or extracted the population's sacrifice, a war lost or an economy broken; or when the great masses pass from passivity to activity and press claims the old leadership cannot formulate, let alone meet. In such a crisis, Gramsci's most quoted diagnostic runs, the ruling class loses its consensus, ceases to be leading and becomes merely dominant, while the traditional parties, the very forms through which consent was organised, disintegrate, lose their constituencies, and are revealed as names without bodies. The crisis is therefore of authority and of representation at once: the led no longer recognise themselves in the leaders' language, and the leaders no longer command a language in which the led can be addressed.

Into that vacuum steps the figure Gramsci analysed under the name of Caesarism, the arbitration of a catastrophic equilibrium by a great personality entrusted with the solution when the contending forces balance in a way that threatens mutual destruction. Caesarism is not a synonym for dictatorship in his usage, and its gradations are the point: it can be progressive, when the arbiter's intervention helps the historically advancing force to prevail, as he judged Caesar's and, with qualifications, the first Napoleon's to have been, or reactionary, when it freezes the equilibrium in favour of the declining force, his examples running to Napoleon the Third and Bismarck. What defines the type is structural rather than biographical: Caesarism is the political form of a hegemonic interregnum, the confession, in institutional dress, that no class in the society is able to lead, concede, and organise consent at the level the situation demands. It can also exist, Gramsci adds with characteristic care, without a Caesar, in a parliamentary system whose coalition arithmetic performs the same freezing function by other means. The analyst who has absorbed the earlier sections will read an age of Caesarist solutions as the test's negative print: consent unorganised, concessions suspended, the armour negotiating directly with the crowd, and the morbid symptoms of the interregnum multiplying in proportion to the delay before a new leadership completes its catharsis.

## Intellectuals in depth: officers, ranks, and proportions

The leadership sections introduced intellectuals as hegemony's officers; the notebooks' twelfth book gives them a sociology detailed enough to deserve its own treatment here, because no working part of the concept is more often reduced to a metaphor. Gramsci's starting definition is functional and deliberately unglamorous: intellectuals are the stratum that exercises the subaltern functions of social hegemony and political government, the organisers of a class's awareness of itself, its technicians of consent and, where needed, of coercion, since the officer corps, the judiciary, and the senior bureaucracy fall inside the definition alongside the professors and the poets. Every emergent class creates such a stratum organically, as it creates its entrepreneurs and its organisers, and the stratum's organic intellectuals know themselves part of the class whose life they elaborate. Against them stand the traditional intellectuals, the clergy, the humanists, the military caste, who present themselves as autonomous and continuous across history, an estate above the struggle, and whose claim to stand outside hegemony is itself, Gramsci insists, one of hegemony's most effective products: the prestige of disinterest is a form of power, and it is inherited from the formations that formed the caste.

The analysis turns quantitative in a way readers rarely expect. Gramsci attends to proportions: to the ratio of intellectuals to the population in different countries and regions, to the Southern oversupply of petty intellectuals produced by a society rich in small property and poor in industry, to the democratic or bureaucratic character of the stratum's recruitment, whether it renews itself from below or reproduces as a closed caste. The proportions are data for the hegemony test at one remove. A bloc whose intellectuals are recruited across its whole territory and refreshed from its subordinate classes possesses an organ of self knowledge and a ladder of loyalty at once; a bloc whose intellectuals congeal into a caste begins to misread its own society, to mistake its seminar for the country, and to discover the error in a crisis. The Italian Risorgimento's intellectuals, cosmopolitan since the Renaissance, formed in courts and academies, speaking to Europe over the heads of an Italian people they did not form, are Gramsci's type case of the caste failure, and his prison project of a history of Italian intellectuals was conceived as the case file for the prosecution: an entire national leadership class, brilliant and sterile, whose divorce from the national popular explained, for him, why Italy's transformations kept arriving as passive revolutions administered from above.

## The church: the rival apparatus Gramsci studied most respectfully

In the Italy Gramsci sought to understand, the most accomplished hegemonic apparatus in the field was not the liberal state's. It was the Catholic Church, and his notes on religion repay the attention he gave them, because the church taught him what institutional patience looks like when measured in centuries. The church possessed everything his theory lists: a doctrine elaborated at every level of sophistication, from scholastic philosophy to the peasant's catechism; a stratified corps of intellectuals, religious orders functioning as its parties and its general staffs; schools, seminaries, confraternities, and a press; a machinery of concessions and careers reaching into every village; and above all an organisational genius for holding the learned and the simple within a single faith without allowing the two to split into separate religions of the elite and the crowd, the danger he called the church's standing problem and its recurrent heresy crises. His respect was analytic, not devotional. He measured his own movement against the church's standard and found the movement's anticlericalism a confession of weakness: a counter-hegemony that contents itself with mocking the rival's doctrine, while the rival retains the schools, the feast days, the rites of passage, and the only systematic consolation available to the suffering, has conceded the territory before the contest begins.

The lesson generalises beyond Italy and beyond religion. Every established order contains institutions older than itself that it did not create and cannot simply command, churches, universities, legal professions, learned societies, whose hegemonic machinery predates the ruling bloc and survives its crises, lending it stability in quiet times and harbouring an alternative authority in bad ones. A ruling group that treats such bodies as departments will misjudge its own depth of consent; a challenger that treats them as scenery will misjudge the labour required. Gramsci's church notes also supply a criterion for the depth of any hegemonic formation: the capacity to maintain unity between its highest doctrine and its most popular practice, between the philosopher's version and the grandmother's, without the two becoming strangers. Formations that lose that unity, whose official worldview is recited by its intellectuals and ignored by its base, are exhibiting, in the register of belief, the same divorce between leaders and led that announces organic crisis in the register of parties.

## Clientelism and the counterfeit of consent

The test exists to catch counterfeits, and the most instructive counterfeit is the one that mimics consent's forms while bypassing its substance: the clientelism Gramsci knew from the Italian South, in which the subordinate's support is obtained not through a settlement with his organised groups but through a web of personal favours administered by intermediaries, the job found, the pension expedited, the fine dissolved, the emigrant's papers eased. Clientelism produces gratitude, loyalty of a kind, and electoral reliability, and its practitioners describe it as deep popular consent. The four questions expose it. Its benefits are concessions in appearance, but they are paid to individuals as grace, revocable and unadvertised, never to groups as of right, and they are deliberately structured to prevent the recipients from organising, since an organised clientele could negotiate and a negotiator is precisely what the system cannot afford. Its institutions are real but parasitic, growing inside the state's offices rather than in civil society's open field. And its contestation is managed by the rotation of patrons rather than by rival programmes. The classification the test returns, domination in the dress of intimacy, explains clientelism's celebrated stability and its equally celebrated brittleness: it banks no reserves beyond the patron's capacity to deliver, so an economic contraction or an anti corruption campaign can dissolve in a season loyalties that looked geological, a pattern the political history of more than one region has since illustrated at length.

The counterfeit matters beyond its home terrain because versions of it recur wherever analysts reach for hegemony too quickly: the company town whose welfare is real but whose every benefit terminates with obedience, the machine politics of the great cities in their classic period, the paternalist estate, all exhibit the same structure of benefits without settlement, gratitude without organisation, quiet without consent. Gramsci's Southern writings are unsparing about the intellectuals who administer such systems, the stratum of mediators living on the transaction between the peasant and the state, and his analysis adds a diagnostic refinement the test's users should carry: ask not only whether benefits flow, but whether the recipients may organise to defend them. A concession that may be defended collectively is a settlement term and evidence of hegemony in formation. A benefit that evaporates when its recipients combine is a leash, and the difference between the settlement and the leash is the difference the whole concept exists to name.

## The regulated society: hegemony's horizon and the consenting state

One implication of the double formula is so large that readers often pass it by, and a complete guide should draw it out before turning to the concept's critics and careers. If the state is political society plus civil society, coercion plus consent, then the proportions can change historically, and Gramsci, following a thread that runs from Marx through the classical tradition, entertained the limit case in which the proportions invert: a regulated society in which the coercive element withers as the consensual element absorbs its functions, political society being progressively reabsorbed into civil society as the population's self organisation becomes capable of carrying what the apparatus once imposed. The horizon matters here for two analytical reasons. It reveals that hegemony is not, in its author's hands, a technique for making domination comfortable and permanent; it is the name of the process by which the need for domination is supposed to shrink, since every genuine enlargement of organised consent is a transfer of function from the armour to the body. And it supplies the final discriminating question an analyst can put to a self described hegemonic order: is its consent apparatus making the coercive apparatus more necessary or less, year by year? An order whose hegemony is deepening can afford to retire instruments of force; an order whose hegemony is hollowing must multiply them while redoubling its cultural professions, and the divergence between the professions and the instruments is measurable long before it is admitted.

The horizon also corrects the last of the standing misreadings targeted by this cluster, the assumption that consent in Gramsci is always manufactured by the powerful and suffered by the weak. In the regulated society's direction of travel, consent is something populations increasingly produce for themselves, through their own institutions, as the functions of leadership are generalised; and even in existing orders the notebooks' consent is, as the earlier sections established, bargained, extracted, and periodically withdrawn, never simply injected. Democratic deepening, in the vocabulary this guide has built, can be defined without sentimentality as the process in which the subaltern's share in organising consent grows at the expense of the armour, a definition that gives democrats of several traditions a common instrument while committing them to none of one another's programmes. It is the appropriate note on which to leave the construction itself and take up, first, the cautions that govern the texts it was built from, and then the strongest case against the whole enterprise.

## Reading the notebooks: cautions that govern every quotation

The Prison Notebooks are not a book their author published, and the scholarly cautions attaching to them belong in any guide that quotes them, since they condition the weight every formulation can bear. Gramsci wrote under censorship, in notebooks the prison authorities supplied and inspected, in a hand sometimes hurried by illness, and he developed a prudential vocabulary, most famously the philosophy of praxis for Marxism, whose camouflage function coexists with its genuine conceptual content in proportions interpreters still dispute. He wrote without a library adequate to his references, quoting from memory, planning a systematic restudy he never lived to perform, and he left the notes in states ranging from first drafts to the revised versions he recopied into later notebooks, so that a formulation's status, draft or considered restatement, is part of its meaning. The critical edition prepared by Valentino Gerratana in nineteen seventy five, restoring the notes' chronological order and their repetitions, transformed serious study by making that status visible, and the standard English selection, edited and translated by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith, made a curated Gramsci available to the Anglophone world at the price of the arrangement's invisibility. Readers of this guide, and of the series biography at [antonio gramsci intellectual biography](/2015/01/01/antonio-gramsci-intellectual-biography/) where the life and the pre-prison writings carry the main narrative, should hold two rules. Formulations are cited here at the level of confidence the text supports, which is why this article paraphrases the sacrifices passage and quotes directly only where the wording is stable across editions. And no single notebook sentence, however quotable, outranks the construction the notebooks build across thousands of pages, the construction this guide has attempted to reassemble part by part.

The cautions have a polemical edge as well, because hegemony's career in quotation is a case study in the abuse the cautions prevent. Extracted sentences circulate in secondary literatures shorn of their occasions: the optimism of the will detached from the pessimism of the intellect that disciplines it, the armour of coercion quoted by writers who have never asked what body the armour protects, common sense invoked as a compliment by writers who would be dismayed by its notebook meaning. A concept rebuilt from its working parts, as here, is harder to kidnap than a concept carried by slogans, since every part can be checked against the others and a kidnapped part shows the tear. That, finally, is the justification for the guide's method and its length. Hegemony has been used loosely for half a century because loose use is cheap. The notebooks, read whole, make it expensive again, and expensive in the right currency: evidence about institutions, concessions, intellectuals, and the slow transactions of consent, which no amount of definitional elegance can replace.

## East and West: the geography of civil society's depth

The notebooks' most consequential single distinction, after the double formula itself, is geographical. In the East, Gramsci wrote, the state was everything and civil society was primordial and gelatinous; in the West, between the state and the citizen stood a robust structure of civil society, and the state was only an outer ditch, behind which lay a sturdy system of fortresses and earthworks. The contrast was drawn from the comparison that organised his political life: in Russia in nineteen seventeen, a determined assault on the state could succeed because behind the autocracy there was almost nothing, no dense web of parties, unions, churches, and associations capable of absorbing the shock and reconstituting order; in the advanced West, the same assault would break against positions that the fall of a ministry would leave entirely intact, since the ruling class's hegemony was lodged in the fortresses, not in the ditch. The distinction is not a compliment to the West's civilisation. It is a measurement of where consent is stored, and its strategic consequence, the priority of the war of position in developed societies, follows from the measurement rather than from any preference for gradualness as a temperament.

Handled carefully, the distinction also protects the concept against a mechanical export that its author would have resisted. The depth of civil society is a variable, not a continental essence: it differs by country, by region within a country, Gramsci's own Italy split between a North with fortresses and a South where the state faced the peasant across a near vacuum, and by epoch, since wars and dictatorships can flatten a civil society that took a century to grow, and postwar generations can regrow it in altered forms. Analysts who convert East and West into fixed civilisational labels repeat, in geography, the error the test forbids in classification. The correct use is diagnostic and local: for the society in question, enumerate the fortresses, estimate their garrisons, and only then judge what kind of contest the terrain permits. A hegemony claim about a society whose civil society the claimant cannot inventory is a claim made blind, and Gramsci, who spent eleven years inventorying his own country's fortresses from a cell because the inventory was the strategy, is the worst possible authority to cite for blindness.

## Consent under maximum strain: the Great War as laboratory

Gramsci's generation learned its politics in the trenches, and the notebooks treat the Great War as the laboratory in which the liberal hegemonies of the nineteenth century were stress tested to destruction. The war demanded of each belligerent order exactly what hegemony theory would predict: the mobilisation of consent on an unprecedented scale, the organisation of whole populations through new ministries, unions recognised as partners of the state, rationing accepted as fair or resented as fraud, and sacrifices, of sons before all, that dwarfed every concession the orders had ever paid. The settlements that survived the war, in Gramsci's retrospective analysis, were those whose hegemonic reserves were deepest, whose institutions could convert sacrifice into meaning and grievance into negotiation faster than the trenches could manufacture despair. The settlements that failed, the Romanov, Hohenzollern, and Habsburg orders above all, failed in the sequence the theory describes: the leading groups lost the confidence of their own armies and peasantries, the traditional parties and dynasties stood revealed as names without bodies, and the vacuum filled according to whichever force had built an alternative formation in the war's interstices. Italy, the case he knew in his nerves, survived the war as a state and lost its liberal hegemony in the victory's aftermath, when the promises made to peasants and soldiers in the trenches were presented for payment to a bloc too narrow to pay them, and the unpaid bill circulated until a movement arose that promised to settle it in the currency of national humiliation avenged.

The war also fixed the theory's central metaphor in experience. The war of position is trench warfare transposed, and Gramsci, who watched a continent learn that frontal assault against prepared positions annihilates the assailant, drew the transposition consciously: in politics as in the new warfare, the prepared positions are institutional, the siege is long, the besieger must be supplied by a whole society organised for the effort, and partial local successes mean little against an unbroken system of earthworks. The metaphor's military origin is sometimes treated as a curiosity of style. It is better treated as evidence of the theory's intended realism. Hegemony was not elaborated as a refinement of cultural criticism. It was elaborated as the strategic science of a defeated movement trying to understand why its assault had failed in the West, and every working part this guide has assembled, the institutions, the concessions, the intellectuals, the national popular, bears the mark of that origin: each names something a besieging force must build, buy, or win before the fortresses can be taken, and each names something whose absence explains a specific historical defeat its author had lived through.

## Small scales: the firm, the profession, the city

Nothing in the concept restricts it to nations, and some of its most precise applications are small. Gramsci himself supplied the hint in his scattered attention to formations like the Rotary Club and the freemasons, associations he treated as instruments by which a bourgeoisie organises its own unity and its relations with the state outside the forms of party and parliament, civil society weaving itself at the scale of the luncheon and the lodge. A profession can be read the same way. Modern medicine's authority over the definition of illness, the training and licensing of practitioners, and the deference of lay judgement was not seized; it was constructed across a century, through concessions wrung from and granted to the state, through institutions of formation that manufacture the profession's intellectuals, through a common sense in which the white coat's authority appears as nature rather than as settlement. The test applies cleanly at that scale, and it returns the same kind of graded, domain specified verdicts: a profession hegemonic over its jurisdiction, dominant where it merely licenses, and contested where rival formations, midwives in one century, alternative practitioners in another, organise a counter-consent among the patients.

The municipal scale teaches a complementary lesson about trasformismo's economics. The classic urban machine of the industrial city, the ward organisation that found jobs, coal, and intercession for its immigrant constituents, is often filed under corruption, and the filing is not wrong, but the hegemony framework reads the same facts as a settlement of a specific and limited kind: concessions real but personalised, institutions dense but owned, consent genuine within the ward and absent beyond it, since the machine's formation never attempted the catharsis that would have made its programme a city's common sense rather than a clientele's gratitude. The machine's celebrated durability and its sudden collapses, when reform movements finally organised the same constituents on universalist terms, replays at city scale the pattern this guide traced in the Italian South, and the replay is evidence that the concept's moving parts are genuinely structural rather than metaphors borrowed from one national history. Scale discipline follows: a claim of hegemony must carry its scale with it, over a firm, a profession, a city, a nation, a world order, and claims that drift between scales mid argument, proving consent in a faculty club and concluding about a civilisation, fail by the same standard as claims that drift between domains.

## Errors in application: a field guide

The guide closes its construction with a compact inventory of the errors its users most reliably commit, stated as corrections, since the cluster's ruling is that correction by precision is this concept's first duty. The first error is binarism, the treatment of hegemony as present or absent, when every working part of the concept is graded and the honest verdicts are almost always partial, stratified, and dated. The second is permanence, the treatment of a hegemony once established as a fact of nature, when the theory defines it as a balance requiring daily renewal, so that every sound application carries an implicit question about the renewal's cost and its arrears. The third is the propagandist reduction, in which the analyst studies only the order's messages, its media and its slogans, and reports a hegemony whose concessions, institutions, and negotiations have never been examined; the correction is the evidence discipline of the earlier sections, budgets before broadcasts. The fourth is the reverse snobbery of supposing that because consent is organised it is therefore counterfeit, an error that leaves the analyst unable to distinguish a population's real gains from its illusions, and politically unable to explain why orders with genuine settlements outlast orders without them. Gramsci's subordinates are not fooled by their settlements; they are parties to them, on terms whose inequality is the proper object of criticism, and a criticism that begins by denying the settlement's reality will never locate its joints.

The fifth error concerns the analyst's own position, and it is the one the notebooks' author, who wrote his theory from inside a defeat, would have ranked first. Hegemony talk tempts its user into the posture of the awakened, the one mind exempt from the common sense it diagnoses in everyone else. The theory contains the antidote in its doctrine of contradictory consciousness, which assigns the divided mind to all classes, intellectuals emphatically included, and in its pedagogical rule that the teacher is also a pupil. A researcher applying the concept is inside some formation's common sense while applying it, and the finding that one's opponents' consent is organised, while one's own convictions are simply true, is not a finding the framework licenses. It licenses the harder exercise: to run the four questions over one's own camp's leadership with the same inventory, the same budgets, the same suspicion of silence mistaken for agreement. Applications that survive that exercise have earned the concept. Applications that cannot attempt it are the elasticity objection's best evidence, and this guide hands them to the objectors without a defence.

## Why peasants are led: the agrarian lesson generalised

Gramsci's theory was built in a country where the majority worked the land, and its treatment of the peasantry generalises into one of the concept's most transferable findings: some classes are structurally positioned to be hegemonic and others to be hegemonised, and the difference lies less in virtue or numbers than in the organisation of their working lives. Marx had given the type case its immortal image in the smallholding peasants of France, a mass formed by simple addition of homologous magnitudes, a sack of potatoes, whose dispersion across millions of family plots prevented them from enforcing their class interest in their own name and delivered their representation to others, landlord, priest, or the adventurer who promised to personify them. Gramsci's agrarian writings accept the sociology and refuse the contempt that often travels with it. The peasantry's subalternity is a condition of its production, not of its intelligence: dispersed labour, seasonal rhythms, the village horizon, and dependence on town markets and town credit ensure that peasants generate rich local common sense and fierce local solidarities while lacking the daily intercourse from which a class manufactures its own intellectuals and its national programme.

From that diagnosis the practical corollaries flow, and they governed the Southern strategy already examined. A peasantry can be led well or badly, by its landlords' bloc, by the church's, by a nationalism that flatters it, or by an alliance that concedes its land hunger and dignifies its experience, but it will be led, and the contest for its allegiance is decided by which leadership pays the agrarian question's price. The finding travels far beyond its Italian home. In every society where a numerous class is dispersed by its work, small traders, domestic workers, the informally employed of the great cities, political leadership is supplied from outside the class's own ranks, and the hegemony test applied to such a class's supposed representatives asks the Gramscian question with special sharpness: are these representatives its organic intellectuals, formed by its life and accountable to its organisations, or are they a traditional caste renting it a voice? The peasantry's lesson is that the second arrangement is the historical default, and that the first has to be built, patiently and at cost, by movements that understand why the default exists.

## The national idiom: why a doctrine must become national to lead

A late notebook theme completes the account of how hegemony is achieved, and it explains why the concept's author, the most internationalist of thinkers, insisted on the national as the necessary form of every universal claim. A conception of the world becomes hegemonic in a country only when it has been translated into that country's idiom, not linguistically alone, though Gramsci the linguist meant that too, but historically: when it has metabolised the nation's particular past, its Risorgimento or its revolution, its religious settlement and its agrarian structure, and can speak of the general future in the accents of the specific inheritance. Doctrines imported as finished foreign systems remain, in his analysis, the property of a cosmopolitan caste, admired in reviews and inert in the nation's life, and his histories of Italian culture return obsessively to the damage done by intellectual formations that lived mentally in Paris or in the Renaissance courts while the people outside the window made a different history unrecorded. The national popular, treated earlier as a literary test, is this same requirement at the level of high culture: the hegemonic class's intellectuals must be of the nation, formed in its schools of experience, or their universal programme will arrive in the provinces as one more instruction from the capital.

The requirement disciplines internationalisms of every kind, including the one Gramsci served. A movement present in many countries does not possess one hegemony replicated; it faces as many hegemonic contests as there are national formations, each with its own common sense, its own church or its equivalent, its own agrarian question and its own dead to bury, and its universal programme is real only as the family resemblance among national constructions each built from local materials. The insight has lost none of its edge in an age of instantaneous communication, since the velocity of messages has not abolished the sedimentation of national histories, and the formations that announce a global common sense from a single metropolis reliably discover, at the first crisis, that their consent was a subscription rather than a settlement. Gramsci's word for the discovery is written across the notebooks' margins: cosmopolitan, his term of reproach for every leadership, revolutionary ones emphatically included, that floats above the national terrain it was supposed to organise.

## Decay from within: bureaucratic centralism and the closed caste

Hegemony's most dangerous enemy, in the notebooks' political analysis, is not the rival outside the gates but a deformation inside the leading group itself, which Gramsci named in the party context as bureaucratic centralism and opposed to the democratic kind. A leadership formation suffers the deformation when its cohesion ceases to be produced by a living relation with its base, by argument, promotion from below, and the continuous testing of its programme against the led's experience, and is instead produced administratively, by the apparatus's control of careers, information, and sanctions. The formation's surface can remain impressive for a long time after the deformation sets in: discipline holds, congresses vote unanimously, the press speaks in one voice. But the consensual circuit has been quietly disconnected, and the leadership is now governing its own organisation by the methods of political society, command and exclusion, while imagining it still leads a civil society that has in fact withdrawn into privacy, irony, and the practical consciousness that no longer reports upward. Gramsci's criterion for the healthy alternative was exact: in a vital formation, the relations between leaders and led resemble those of an organism whose parts move together, and the demand for discipline is itself a product of shared formation rather than of the file and the dossier.

The deformation is worth a section in a concept guide because it operates identically in states, parties, churches, unions, and universities, and because it explains a recurring astonishment of the historical record: the collapse, apparently sudden, of formations that every observer had rated monolithic. The astonishment is an artefact of measurement. Observers measured the formation's command surface, its unanimity and its reach, when the theory instructs them to measure its circuits: whether criticism travels upward and is answered, whether the base's ablest are promoted or expelled, whether the leaders can still state the led's grievances in a form the led recognise. Where the circuits have closed, the formation's hegemony has already ended and only its domination persists, on borrowed time whose term is set by the first crisis requiring reserves of loyalty that were spent, unrecorded, in the years of the great unanimity. The notebooks' author watched exactly this closure consume the movement of his youth from inside a cell provided by its enemies, and the analysis of bureaucratic decay is written with the controlled fury of a man auditing the accounts of his own defeat.

## Journalism: the daily bread of hegemony

Gramsci had edited newspapers before he theorised them, and his notebook typology of journalism is among the most practically detailed writing in the whole corpus, a working editor's classification of periodicals by the relation each establishes with its public. At one pole stands the paper of pure information, which treats its reader as a customer for facts and forms nothing; at the other, the paper of formation, which treats its reader as a participant to be educated into a conception of the world, and whose every rubric, the correspondence column, the popular science box, the serialised novel, is an instrument of intellectual organisation. Between them he sorts the party press, the trade press, the confessional press, the illustrated magazine, and he attends, as no theorist of propaganda has done, to the economics underneath: a newspaper is an industrial enterprise, capitalised, staffed, and advertised, and its editorial line is set within margins fixed by its balance sheet as surely as by its proprietor's opinions. The analysis yields a research instruction this guide endorses for every institution of civil society, not only the press: follow the formation, but audit the firm. Who owns the school, the journal, the platform of formation, on what revenue does it run, whose careers does it make, and what does its survival require it never to say? Hegemony's institutions are not embarrassed by having material bases; they are explained by them, and the analyst who stops at their doctrines has read the menu and skipped the kitchen.

The typology also supplies the concept's most intimate scale of operation, the scale at which consent is actually transacted day by day. A ruling order's hegemony is not rewon in its solemn declarations but in the ten thousand small pedagogies of its press and its platforms: the framing that makes one kind of event a scandal and another a statistic, the expert whose authority is never introduced because it never needs to be, the reader's letter selected to represent the public to itself. Gramsci's counter project, the workers' press of the Ordine Nuovo years, was conceived at the same scale, a paper whose readers were its correspondents and whose factory chronicles trained the writers as much as the readers, formation running upward as well as down. The general lesson closes the circle with the contradictory consciousness doctrine: the instruments of hegemony are not a one way technology of the powerful, since the same typology that describes the order's press describes the counter formation's, and the question is always the comparative one, which side is building the denser, better financed, more honestly reciprocal machinery of formation, and on that comparison, more often than on any clash of doctrines, the outcome of hegemonic contests silently depends.

## What the concept cannot do: scope and limits

A guide that has spent its length defending a concept's precision owes a final section to its boundaries, the questions hegemony does not answer and the work it should never be asked to perform. It is not a theory of events. Hegemony describes the slow constitution of the terrain on which events occur, the prior organisation of consent and coercion that decides which crises become transformations and which are absorbed; asked why a particular ministry fell in a particular month, it answers with conditions, not causes, and the conjunctural analysis of personalities, accidents, and decisions remains a separate craft that the relations of force checklist frames but does not replace. It is not a psychology. Contradictory consciousness describes a social distribution of conceptions, not the interior of any individual mind, and biographical applications that diagnose a person's false ideas from their class position commit exactly the vulgarity the notebooks' pedagogy forbids. It is not a substitute for political economy: the decisive nucleus doctrine assigns the economy the founding role, and an analysis of a settlement that cannot state the settlement's material basis, who produces, who appropriates, who pays for the concessions, has not begun, however refined its reading of the period's novels.

It is also, emphatically, not a universal solvent for the phenomenon of violence. The framework treats coercion as hegemony's reserve and its confession of failure, and there are historical formations, the slave plantation in its classic form, the extermination camp, the pure occupation regime, whose reproduction runs through terror so completely that the vocabulary of leadership and consent describes nothing in them but their thinnest administrative veneers. Gramsci's categories classify such formations, as domination at the limit, precisely in order to mark what they lack, and an application that discovers hegemony inside them has confused the map with a compliment. Holding the boundaries has a final, reflexive benefit for the concept's users. A theory that knows what it cannot explain can be surprised by evidence, corrected by archives, and improved by its rivals, which is to say it can behave as an instrument of knowledge rather than as a badge of belonging. The notebooks' author, who revised his own formulations across six years of enforced leisure and left the revision visible in the manuscripts, modelled that behaviour on every page, and his readers honour the model best by imitating it rather than by quoting it.

## The family: hegemony's most intimate institution

The smallest institution of civil society is the first one every person enters, and Gramsci's scattered notes on the family and on what he called the sexual question show the concept operating at its most intimate scale. The Fordism essay's attention to marriage, drinking, and domestic economy was not prudery, his own or Ford's. It registered that a new regime of production requires a new regime of reproduction: stable households that raise punctual workers, budgets disciplined to instalment purchases, sexual energies channelled into the monogamous family whose economics the wage is calculated to support. The puritan campaigns of the industrial epoch, temperance, Sabbatarianism, the policing of dance halls and of women's dress, appear in this light as a hegemonic project conducted jointly by employers, churches, and the state upon the private lives of the subordinate, with the family's internal authority, the father's wage, the mother's management, doing daily and unpaid the work that no factory inspector could. Gramsci's notes are careful to treat the resulting morality neither as conspiracy nor as mere hypocrisy. It was a real settlement, in which respectability paid tangible dividends to the families that achieved it, in credit, in housing, in the marriageability of daughters and the employability of sons, and its concessions are legible in the coin of the whole framework: security and status, granted to those who internalised the discipline, withheld from those who would not.

The intimate scale completes the concept's answer to a question the manipulation misreading always raises in its crudest form: if hegemony is not brainwashing, why do the subordinate so often defend the arrangements that subordinate them? The family notes suggest the honest reply. People defend the arrangements in which their real achievements are banked: the respectable household built across a generation, the child lifted by schooling, the status that distinguishes them from the street below. Those achievements are not illusions, and a criticism that treats them as such will be heard, correctly, as an attack on the achievers. What hegemony names, at this scale as at every other, is the organisation of those genuine stakes into the defence of the order that granted them on its terms, and what counter-hegemony requires, correspondingly, is not the exposure of the stakes as fake but the construction of institutions in which the same human goods, security, dignity, a future for one's children, can be banked on different terms. The insight is the furthest reach of the concessions condition, and it is why the concept, properly used, makes its user more rather than less respectful of the populations it describes: they appear in it neither as dupes nor as heroes in waiting, but as parties to settlements whose terms can be renegotiated only by those who first understand what the settlements are worth to the people living inside them.

## The philosophy of praxis: the doctrine hegemony belongs to

Hegemony is a part machined to fit a larger engine, and the engine has a name in the notebooks: the philosophy of praxis, Gramsci's designation for Marxism itself, part censorship evasion and part genuine restatement. The restatement's core is an absolute historicism: human beings make their own history within inherited conditions, their conceptions of the world are themselves historical products and historical forces, and a philosophy is real, in the only sense that finally matters to the notebooks, when it becomes the common sense of an epoch, gripping masses of people and organising their practice. On that premise, hegemony is not an ornament added to a materialism that could manage without it. It is the mechanism by which the premise operates. If ideas become material forces when they are organised in institutions and embodied in settlements, then the study of how that organising is done, by whom, at what price, and against what resistance, is not a cultural supplement to the theory of society. It is the theory of society at the point where structure passes into action, the exact passage Gramsci named catharsis and placed at the centre of his politics.

The parentage explains the concept's double resistance, to economism on one flank and to idealism on the other, which casual readers often mistake for inconsistency. Against economism, the doctrine that history is moved by economic forces working behind men's backs and that superstructures are shadows, the notebooks insist that men become conscious of their conflicts on the terrain of ideologies and fight them out there, so that the terrain is real and its organisation decisive. Against idealism, the doctrine that ideas move themselves, they insist with equal force that conceptions of the world are carried by classes, financed by settlements, and founded on the decisive nucleus of economic activity, so that a worldview without a class and a budget is a ghost. Hegemony is the hinge term that lets both insistences be true at once, and its working parts are the hinges' pins: intellectuals, because ideas need carriers with salaries and schools; concessions, because consent needs a material signature; the historic bloc, because the union of the two orders must be given a name that refuses to dissolve either. A reader who grasps the parentage can predict, for any new Gramsci text put in front of them, roughly where the analysis will go: toward the organisation of a conception, the class that pays for it, and the counter organisation gathering in its shadow.

The parentage also fixes the concept's emotional register, which interpreters sentimentalise at their peril. The philosophy of praxis, in the notebooks' presentation, is the self consciousness of a humanity that makes itself, and hegemony's analysis is written throughout in the grammar of construction: blocs are built, consent is organised, intellectuals are formed, positions are entrenched and stormed. Nothing in the framework is found ready made, neither domination nor liberation, and the famous pairing that posterity extracted as a motto, pessimism of the intellect and optimism of the will, is simply the register's compression: the intellect's duty to grade every formation by the test without flattery, the will's licence to build the counter formation that the grading shows to be possible. A guide that has kept both halves of the pairing in view, grading incumbents and challengers by the same inventory and refusing both the cynicism that finds every order identical and the romance that finds every protest a dawn, has used the concept as its parent doctrine intended, and may now let the concept's later interpreters speak, with their extensions marked against the construction they inherited.

## The armour examined: consent inside the apparatus of coercion

The armour of coercion has been treated so far as hegemony's reserve, the hard shell behind the consensual body, and the notebooks support a refinement that completes the picture: the apparatus of coercion is itself a social formation, staffed by subaltern men, penetrated by the same common sense it polices, and subject to the laws of consent in its own ranks. An army is recruited from the peasantry and the urban poor; a police force is a career offered to the sons of the subordinate classes; the judiciary reproduces itself through the law faculties of the universities. Each of those facts is a hegemonic variable. A ruling bloc that can offer its coercive servants honour, advancement, and the sense of embodying the nation's order possesses an apparatus whose loyalty is itself a form of organised consent, and such apparatuses have historically been granted concessions of their own, in pay, in prestige, in corporate privilege, by the very logic the concessions condition describes, since no group is exempt from the price of leadership, not even the group one leads through a barracks. Conversely, the classic symptom that a hegemony has entered organic crisis is the apparatus's infection by the crisis: soldiers' councils in the garrisons, police fraternising with crowds, junior officers reading the opposition's newspapers, conscripts voting with their feet. The revolutions Gramsci studied turned on such moments, when the armour discovered it had opinions.

The refinement carries a methodological instruction parallel to the others in this guide. Analysts divide their attention between an order's opinion pages and its ministries of the interior, as if consent were manufactured in the first and coercion warehoused in the second. The notebooks' integral state forbids the division. The recruiting poster is an instrument of hegemony addressed to the coercive arm; the military school is an institution of civil society wearing uniform; the amnesty and the decoration list are concessions paid through the apparatus to the population it is drawn from. Even the decision of where coercion falls is hegemonically priced: orders husband their armour, spending it on the margins, on colonised peoples, on the unorganised, on those whose repression the consenting population will not count as a cost, and the map of where an order dares to coerce and where it must negotiate is among the most honest documents of its true classification. A state that negotiates with its capital and coerces only its periphery has told the analyst, in its practice, exactly where its hegemony runs and where it has always been domination in the armour's own person.

## The concept among its interpreters: extensions, and where they leave Gramsci

No concept survives its author unchanged, and hegemony's later career is unusually crowded, since the term was carried into cultural studies, international relations, education, and post-Marxist theory by readers who each needed it to do different work. A complete guide owes its readers a map of the principal reinterpretations, marked clearly where each extension departs from the construction assembled in this article, because the misreadings corrected at the outset usually enter through a respectable interpreter's door. The point is not to police an orthodoxy. It is to keep the distinctions priced: a reader who knows which Gramsci is being used in a given argument can tell what evidence that argument needs, and what its conclusions are actually worth.

Perry Anderson's critique, first set out in the essay on the antinomies of Gramsci and deepened across his later work on the concept's career, remains the most formidable internal challenge from within the Marxist tradition. Anderson showed that the notebooks' usages slide: hegemony sometimes names consent as against coercion, sometimes the union of the two, sometimes a balance tilted differently in East and West, and he argued that the sliding conceals a strategic oscillation in Gramsci's own politics, between the united front's courting of allies and the insurrectionary horizon the courting was meant to serve. The descriptive part of the critique is sound, and this guide has built on it: the double formula of leadership and domination is precisely the stable structure that Anderson's sliding usages oscillate around, and the concessions condition is the element that does not slide, present whenever the concept is doing its proper work. Where Anderson's reading ends in suspicion of the concept itself, the test proposed here ends differently, in a demand that each application declare its balances and its failures, which converts the antinomies from an objection into a set of variables.

Raymond Williams, in the cultural theory of the nineteen seventies, gave the concept its most influential humanisation. Hegemony for Williams is a lived process, not a system imposed on life from outside: a whole body of practices and expectations, saturating society to such a depth that it constitutes the substance of ordinary experience, yet never total, never exhaustive, always pressured by residual formations surviving from earlier orders and emergent formations announcing later ones. The dominant, residual, and emergent triad is a genuine enrichment, and it disciplines the analyst against treating any epoch as ideologically uniform. Williams's version pays for its reach, however, by loosening the concept's joints: concessions, in his pages, recede behind the saturation metaphor, and leadership as an organised activity blurs into culture as a general condition. The test in this guide can be read as an attempt to keep Williams's sensitivity to lived process while restoring the harder questions his version lets the analyst skip.

Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe carried the concept furthest from its source, in the work of the nineteen eighties that refounded hegemony as a logic of discourse. In their construction, hegemony names the articulation of floating elements into a contingent unity, classes dissolve as necessary subjects, and the concessions condition disappears entirely, since articulation costs nothing material and a discourse can be hegemonic while its bearers concede nothing to anyone. Readers are free to find the construction fruitful, and its influence on the study of populism is a fact of intellectual history. But the construction is a successor concept wearing the ancestor's name, and the differences are exactly the ones this guide exists to mark: in Gramsci, the subjects of hegemony are classes and their alliances, formed in production before they are formed in discourse, and leadership is invoiced in concessions that a discourse analysis cannot even register. Confusing the two usages produces arguments that cite Gramsci's authority for conclusions his framework would classify as ideology talk, the very conflation the hegemony test was built to catch.

Stuart Hall's use of the concept, in the analysis of the British nineteen seventies and the authoritarian populism he diagnosed in the governing project that followed, stands nearer the source, and its nearness is instructive. Hall's ruling bloc wins consent not by deceiving a passive electorate but by constructing a popular programme out of real grievances, law and order, resentment of bureaucracy, the promise of property, and it pays for its leadership in the coin Gramsci specified: concessions to fractions of the subordinate population, selectively and divisively distributed, while coercion is reserved and expanded for the populations excluded from the settlement. Whatever a reader's verdict on the politics Hall analysed, the analysis is recognisably Gramscian by this guide's instruments: leadership before and through power, institutions mapped, concessions itemised, domains specified. It shows the concept working as an instrument of concrete history fifty years after the notebooks, and it shows, by contrast, how much thinner the word becomes in mouths that use it to mean only that somebody's ideas are in the ascendant.

Joseph Femia's scholarly reconstruction, finally, deserves mention because it codified the interpretation against which looser ones define themselves: hegemony in Gramsci as the achievement of consent through intellectual and moral leadership, resting on a decisive economic nucleus and on the leading group's willingness to compromise its corporate interests up to the limit of the essential. Femia also catalogued the concept's dangers with a clarity this guide adopts: that in careless hands it becomes a functionalist compliment, in which whatever survives is pronounced hegemonic and the pronouncement explains the survival by relabelling it. The guard against that degeneration is not reverence for the text. It is the set of defeaters built into the four questions, and it is to those defeaters, and to the strongest form of the objection they answer, that the guide now turns.

## The strongest objection: a concept that explains everything

Every serious treatment of hegemony owes its readers one section written against the concept, and the objection to be faced is not the vulgar one that the word is jargon. The objection, stated at full strength, is that the concept is unfalsifiable, and therefore empty as an instrument of knowledge. Consider its apparent elasticity. If subordinate groups comply with an order, their compliance is consent, and hegemony is confirmed. If they resist, their resistance is counter-hegemony, and the framework is confirmed again, since it predicted contestation all along. If the rulers concede, concessions prove hegemonic negotiation; if the rulers repress, repression proves the armour behind the hegemony; if the rulers do nothing, trasformismo and passive revolution stand ready to explain the stillness. A schema under which every possible observation counts as confirmation is not a theory of anything. It is a vocabulary for redescribing outcomes in a tone of insight, and its predictive content is zero. Critics from several traditions, liberal, positivist, and Marxist, have pressed versions of this charge, and the charge has force because a great deal of published hegemony talk is guilty exactly as specified: the word is applied after the fact, to arrangements the writer dislikes, with none of the concept's conditions ever checked.

The answer begins by accepting the charge against the usage and rejecting it against the concept, and the rejection rests on identifying what the concept forbids. Hegemony, as this guide has reconstructed it, makes a definite and risky claim about any arrangement it is applied to: that the arrangement's stability is being reproduced through active consent organised in civil society and underwritten by material concessions from the leading group to the led. That claim can be false, and the ways it can be false are enumerable. An order may persist for generations while conceding nothing and organising no consent, reproducing itself by coercion, by the fragmentation of its subjects, by their exit, or by rents distributed to a narrow guard; the concept, applied honestly, must call that order domination, and if the historical record showed that most durable orders were of that kind, hegemony would shrink to a rare special case and its theory to a footnote on the exceptions. An order may concede generously and organise busily yet never win the identification of its subordinates, who take the benefits and keep their allegiance elsewhere; the concept must call that patronage without hegemony, and must predict, what mere redistribution talk cannot, that such an order will find no reserves of loyalty when its capacity to pay is interrupted. These are not verbal manoeuvres. They are classifications that evidence settles, and they are the reason the four questions of the test are phrased as questions rather than as praises.

### When does consent begin to fail?

When the settlement's terms go unmet: when concessions are withdrawn or devalued, when the leading group's representatives are seen to serve the interests they had pledged to subordinate, and when events break the identification between the led and their leaders faster than the institutions of consent can repair it.

The answer continues with a concession of its own, and the concession is owed because it is true. Most applied uses of the term hegemony in journalism, in activism, and in a good deal of academic writing do not meet the standard the concept sets. They use the word as a synonym for cultural influence, they specify no domain, they itemise no concessions, they treat contest as an embarrassment to the classification rather than as one of its conditions, and they would fail the test this guide administers in the form in which they are actually stated. A scholar who values the concept should say so plainly, since a term defended at every application is a term being spent. The right defence is narrower and stronger: the concept, disciplined by its conditions, sorts the historical record in ways its neighbours cannot, predicts the brittleness of concessionless orders and the specific failure modes of hegemonic ones, and gives researchers, teachers, and citizens an inventory to draw before they reach for conclusions. Elasticity is the disease of the word's career. It is not the property of the construction Gramsci left in the notebooks, provided the construction is used whole, with its price tag attached.

One further objection, quieter than the falsifiability charge, deserves its sentence before the guide moves to its closing layers. It is sometimes said that the concept romanticises its object, that to call an order hegemonic is to pay it the compliment of depth, and to call an order dominant is to excuse oneself from understanding it. The sections on passive revolution and trasformismo should have disposed of the worry in substance: the concept-cluster contains more instruments for describing hegemony's failures, shells, and counterfeits than for celebrating its successes, and its finest historical applications, Guha's among them, are verdicts of absence. What the concept does refuse is the lazy symmetry in which every durable order is the same kind of fact. Durable orders differ in how they reproduce themselves, and the differences decide how they can be changed and how they will end. A guide that has kept that refusal at its centre has kept faith with the notebooks' own austere purpose, which was never to decorate domination with an Italian synonym, but to find, in the anatomy of how the led are led, the points where a different leadership could be built.

## The yield: a definition that can be wrong

The construction is complete, and its yield can be stated in the space of a page, because everything in the preceding sections exists to make one definition usable. Hegemony is rule secured principally through the organised consent of subordinate groups: leadership exercised by a group that has made itself representative of interests wider than its own, before power and through it, on a foundation in the decisive nucleus of economic activity, through the institutions of civil society where common sense is formed, and at the continuing price of real concessions to the led. The definition's virtue is not elegance. It is exposure. Every clause of it can fail against evidence, and the hegemony test merely enumerates the failure points: consent absent, concessions unpaid, contestation extinguished rather than organised, institutions hollow, the economic foundation gone or never present. An arrangement surviving all five checks has earned the concept's name and the predictions that travel with it, about its resilience in crisis and its manner of ending. An arrangement failing any check is handed to the neighbouring concepts, domination, patronage, propaganda, passive revolution, closed administration, each of which predicts differently, and the sorting is the knowledge.

One sentence of the yield belongs to the cluster this article sits in, since the series has used the same distinguishing condition method for exploitation and for alienation before arriving at hegemony. Each of those concepts had been blurred, in the usage the series corrects, into a general complaint about modern life, and each was recovered by finding the one condition its neighbours do not share: for exploitation, the extraction of unpaid labour time inside a formally free exchange; for alienation, the worker's separation from the product, the process, and the activity itself; for hegemony, the payment of real concessions by a leadership that organises consent. The recurrence of the method is itself a finding about how concepts decay. They decay by losing their conditions, shedding the clauses that make them expensive to apply, until only the applause or the indignation remains. Precision, in the series' thesis, is the restoration of the clauses, and a reader who has watched the restoration performed on the cluster's central concept now owns the instrument for the concepts that surround it, several of which the companion articles of this cluster take up in turn.

That is the standard this guide has applied to its subject and invites its readers to apply to the guide. The concept it reconstructs was left unfinished by a prisoner writing against time, censorship, and the ruin of his movement, and the reconstruction has had to choose, at a dozen points, between the notebooks' tensions: between consent as the concept's centre and the armour that never leaves the formula, between the Russian grammar of alliance and the Western analysis of institutions, between the concept as an instrument for incumbents to be audited and for challengers to be built. The choices made here are marked where they were made, in the treatment of Anderson's antinomies, in the naming of the concessions condition, in the test's fourth question, and a reader who disputes them disputes within the concept's own discipline, armed with its evidence families and its defeaters. What no reader is licensed to do, after this many pages, is return to the small definition with which the guide began, in which hegemony is domination through ideas and the four neighbouring terms are one. That definition has been weighed against the notebooks, against the history the notebooks analyse, and against the cases the test grades, and it has failed on every question the test knows how to ask. The word deserves better, and so does everyone whose consent is the word's subject.

## For students and exam candidates

Hegemony questions are lost, in the main, before they are begun, by candidates who define the term as cultural domination and then illustrate the definition with whatever media examples come to hand. Markers are trained to spot the substitution, because a definition that cannot distinguish hegemony from ideology, propaganda, or false consciousness makes every subsequent paragraph ungradeable. Build the answer this guide builds the concept: give the canonical definition with its conditions, consent organised through civil society, leadership exercised before power, and concessions that cost the leading group something real, and name the concessions condition in your first page as the distinction your answer will enforce. Keep a card of the four-question test among your revision materials; the companion study notes at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built for exactly this kind of portable instrument, and the test converts directly into essay structure, one question per analytical paragraph. Then apply the test to a case you know well, grade it honestly, including the domain where it fails, and close with the falsifiability objection and the reply this guide gives, since evaluation credit on this topic lives in the objection handled at full strength. The full question bank and marking treatment are in [gramsci exam answers](/2015/05/15/gramsci-exam-answers/): your task in the hall is narrower, to be the candidate whose definition still works on the last page.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach hegemony against its misreading, and let the students perform the correction themselves. A reliable opening exercise gives the seminar the manipulation definition, in the confident form in which journalism supplies it, and asks the group to find the case it cannot classify: the order that concedes nothing yet is called hegemonic, the population that resists daily yet is said to consent, the concession whose withdrawal nobody can explain. The definition fails in their hands within the hour, and the working parts can then be introduced as repairs to named failures, which is how the cluster's correction method works at its best. The hegemony test converts well into a second session: issue it blank, assign each group an order from a different century, and require the grading to cite evidence of the kinds the evidence section inventories, budgets and negotiation records before editorials. Guard the seminar's neutrality with the symmetry rule used elsewhere in this series: every group must run the test over a formation it sympathises with before grading one it opposes, and the grades are compared for method, never for verdict. Students leave with a concept they can operate and with the rarer skill of suspecting their own fluency, which is the closest a classroom comes to teaching catharsis.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The concept will repay you in proportion to the bookkeeping you bring to it. Before the word hegemony appears in a draft, the four questions should have working answers in the notes: consent evidenced from participation and from the framing of demands, concessions itemised from fiscal and legislative records with their withdrawals dated, contestation mapped to named rival formations with institutions of their own, and the whole claim bounded by domain and period. Journalists, whose deadlines punish exactly this patience, should treat the word as a claim to be sourced rather than as colour: a politician's hegemony, asserted in a column, needs the same inventory in miniature, and the absence of concessions in the record is a finding worth printing in the sentence where a vaguer writer would print the adjective hegemonic. Historians will find the notebooks' own evidentiary habits the best guide: Gramsci graded formations he admired as severely as formations he fought, classified his own tradition's failures of leadership without anaesthesia, and left the theoretical testament of a man who expected his categories to be corrected by archives. The textual cautions in this guide apply a fortiori to quotation in research: check the formulation's status in the critical edition, prefer the construction to the slogan, and let the concept's defeaters, domination, patronage, closed administration, be live options in the write up. A literature in which the test sometimes fails its subjects is the only literature in which its passes mean anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is hegemony?

Hegemony is the form of rule in which a social group leads rather than merely dominates: it secures the active consent of subordinate groups through the institutions of civil society, represents their interests inside its own programme, and pays for that leadership with real concessions from its own immediate advantage. Coercion does not disappear; it stands behind the settlement, reserved for those who refuse it. The concept therefore names a relationship with conditions attached, not a synonym for power, influence, or popularity, and it grades arrangements by how much of their stability runs through consent and how much must be supplied by force.

### Q: Did Gramsci invent the term hegemony?

No. The word descends from the Greek for leadership and entered Marxist vocabulary through Russian social democracy, where Plekhanov and Axelrod used it for the working class's claim to lead the peasantry and the democratic movement against the autocracy, and where Lenin made it central to his strategy of nineteen five. The Communist International then carried the term through the parties of Europe. Gramsci received an existing strategic word and transformed it, generalising leadership from the road to power into the analysis of how power, once held, is reproduced daily through institutions, concessions, and common sense.

### Q: How is hegemony maintained?

Maintenance is continuous work on four fronts. The leading group's intellectuals renew its conception of the world and carry it through schools, press, churches, and associations. Its negotiators service the settlement, bargaining with organised subordinate groups and delivering the reforms and protections that keep them inside the order's procedures. Its institutions absorb or promote the ablest members of the subordinate groups, the process named trasformismo. And its representatives repair the identification between leaders and led after every shock, war, slump, or scandal that strains it. Where the work stops, consent decays into habit, habit into indifference, and the order begins its drift toward rule by the coercive apparatus alone.

### Q: Does hegemony require real concessions?

Yes, and this requirement is the concept's sharpest edge. Gramsci's notebooks hold that leadership presupposes an equilibrium of compromise in which the leading group sacrifices interests of an economic and corporate kind, while refusing to surrender what it counts as essential. A factory act enforced against employers, a franchise widened under pressure, an insurance system financed by the propertied: such transfers are the receipts of hegemony. An arrangement that persuades brilliantly and concedes nothing is, by this standard, conducting propaganda in support of domination, and the withdrawal of established concessions marks a hegemonic order spending its reserves rather than consolidating its rule.

### Q: What is counter-hegemony?

Counter-hegemony is the construction of an alternative leadership by subordinate groups: their own intellectuals raised from within, their own institutions of formation and negotiation, their own alliances with neighbouring strata purchased by concessions they are prepared to pay, and a conception of the world elaborated from the fragments of good sense into a teachable programme. It is measured by the same inventory as the rule it contests. A protest, however large, is not yet a counter-hegemony; the term is earned when the challenger can show the schools, newspapers, organisations, and settlement offers through which a different order is being rehearsed before it is proclaimed.

### Q: What is contradictory consciousness?

It is Gramsci's description of the normal mental condition of subordinate people: two conceptions of the world held at once. One is inherited verbally from past ruling groups and affirmed on formal occasions, a sediment of religion, folklore, and official doctrine. The other is implicit in practical activity, in what work, cooperation, and daily friction actually teach about how the world runs. The two coexist in the same mind without felt scandal, because they answer different occasions. Contradictory consciousness is therefore neither ignorance nor deception; it is the raw material from which hegemony governs through the inherited conception, and counter-hegemony builds by giving the practical one language and organisation.

### Q: What is common sense in Gramsci?

Common sense is not the sound judgement the English phrase praises. It is the uncritical deposit left in a population by every worldview that has ever ruled over it: fragments of religions, proverbs, obsolete science, and ruling class doctrine, jumbled together without system and absorbed as the obvious shape of things. Because it is a sediment, it is conservative in effect, presenting existing arrangements as natural. Yet it contains a healthier nucleus, good sense, the practical criticism generated by people's own activity. Hegemony works on common sense as its terrain; philosophy, in Gramsci's sense, is common sense criticised and made coherent, which is why the struggle over what goes without saying is a political struggle.

### Q: What is a historic bloc?

A historic bloc is the concrete unity, in a given epoch, of an economic structure with the institutions and worldview that sustain it: a social base, the civil society formations that organise its life, and the intellectual leadership that articulates its interests as a general programme, all fitted together so that each element reinforces the others. The term names more than a coalition. In a bloc, the partners are partly remade by their membership, and the formation's solidity is the solidity of those joints. When the elements begin to move separately, base against institutions, institutions against doctrine, the bloc enters the condition Gramsci called organic crisis.

### Q: What is passive revolution?

Passive revolution names transformation accomplished from above, without the popular participation that would make the population its subject. An existing elite, pressed by demands it cannot simply refuse, satisfies them in measured doses through administrative reform, modernising the economy and the state while keeping initiative monopolised and the masses in the role of spectators. Gramsci's paradigm was the Italian Risorgimento, national unification achieved by diplomacy and limited war rather than by a Jacobin mobilisation of the countryside. The resulting formations characteristically bank little loyalty, since their concessions were gifts rather than negotiated settlements, and they meet crises with a thinness of reserves that distinguishes them from hegemonic orders.

### Q: How does hegemony differ from false consciousness?

The two terms answer different kinds of question. False consciousness, a phrase Gramsci himself rarely used, describes a state of awareness: a mismatch between a group's position and its understanding of that position, located inside individual minds. Hegemony describes a social machinery: institutions, settlements, concessions, and intellectuals through which a leadership is organised and renewed. A population can be perfectly lucid about its grievances and still live under a hegemony it has no organised means to replace; conversely, muddled awareness can persist under naked domination. Substituting the vocabulary of mental error for the analysis of machinery is the characteristic move of the manipulation misreading this guide corrects.

### Q: How does the concessions condition stop hegemony becoming a synonym for influence?

Influence is free of charge. A fashion, a celebrity, a well placed editorial can all be influential while their sponsors surrender nothing and risk nothing. The concessions condition attaches a price tag to leadership: the group claiming hegemony must have transferred real advantages, protections, income, time, or standing to the groups it leads, at measurable cost to its own immediate interests, and must go on paying to keep the settlement alive. Influence with no invoice is reclassified as ideology or publicity. The condition thus gives the analyst a falsifiable checkpoint, a column of figures and statutes to inspect, where the loose synonym offers only an atmosphere to admire or deplore.

### Q: What should a student separate between hegemony and domination in an answer?

Separate them by instrument and by evidence. Domination rules antagonists through the coercive apparatus and asks nothing of their agreement; its evidence is the prison, the prohibition, the dismissed assembly. Hegemony leads allies and the wider population through consent organised in civil society and paid for with concessions; its evidence is participation, negotiation, and a settlement the led can describe as partly theirs. Most real orders combine both, directed at different groups, so the strong answer assigns each instrument its domain rather than awarding the whole case to one term, and it notes that a shift from the first instrument toward the second, or back, is itself the movement the concept exists to track.

### Q: Why does common sense matter to the concept's working parts?

Because common sense is the material on which every other part works. Concessions are judged inside it, since a settlement succeeds when its terms pass into what goes without saying; institutions exist to form it, school by school and sermon by sermon; intellectuals are valued by their capacity to criticise it into coherence or to keep it comfortably incoherent; and crisis announces itself when the led stop finding their experience described by it. Without the category, hegemony would be a theory of organisations floating above a population of blank minds. With it, the theory has its psychology, a divided, sedimented, practical consciousness that explains both the durability of settlements and the suddenness of their collapse.

### Q: How is a historic bloc different from an alliance?

An alliance is a bargain among partners who remain what they were: each calculates its price, pays it while the terms hold, and leaves when they change. A historic bloc is a fusion at the level of structure and culture, in which a leading group's programme has been built into law, schooling, production, and everyday expectation, and the allied groups have been partly remade by the membership, their intellectuals formed inside the bloc's institutions and their interests redefined in its language. Alliances dissolve at a change of prices. Blocs survive their founders and their founding bargains, and they end not by resignation but by the slower catastrophe of organic crisis, when the fitted elements come apart.

### Q: What would a failed hegemony test look like in a case?

It looks like an inventory of absences where the publicity claims presence. The researcher asks after consent and finds compliance without participation, grievances phrased as indictments of the order's right to exist. The concessions column shows gifts to individuals, revocable and unnegotiated, or nothing at all beside the extraction figures. Contestation exists only as crime, its organisations banned and its leaders jailed or bought. The institutions of civil society turn out to be departments of the state or shells without members. Colonial India in the analysis of the Subaltern Studies historians is the classic worked example: dominance documented in full, hegemony failing on every question, and the failure itself the finding.
