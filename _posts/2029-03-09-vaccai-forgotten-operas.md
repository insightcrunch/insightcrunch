## Vaccai and the Forgotten Operas: A Crowded Field Behind Two Famous Names

The forgotten operas of the Verona story outnumber the famous ones by a wide margin, and Nicola Vaccai stands at the centre of that hidden repertory as its most instructive case. Ask a concertgoer to name an operatic treatment of the lovers and the answer rarely travels beyond two titles: the Bellini setting of 1830 and the Gounod setting of 1867. Between and around those landmarks, however, a whole shelf of scores once filled European stages, from a German Singspiel of 1776 that let the lovers live to an Italian tragedy of 1865 that held respectable houses for a generation. The verdict argued through this article is plain: the vanished settings did not disappear because the tale failed them. They disappeared because repertory is a narrow gate, and the gatekeepers were singers, publishers, and the slow hardening of a canon that admits only a handful of works per story. Vaccai proves the point with unusual clarity, because his opera both lost and won at the same time. The complete score slipped out of the repertory, while its tomb scene, detached and transplanted into the Bellini opera, enjoyed a stage life of sixty-five years inside another composer's work. A fragment outlived its parent by decades. That paradox, examined closely, explains more about how operas survive than any success story can.

The scale of the forgetting deserves emphasis at the outset. The Verona tale attracted composers in every operatic nation and in every decade from the 1770s onward. German Singspiel, French opera with spoken dialogue, Italian dramma per musica, and grand opera all took their turn at the feud, the ball, the balcony, and the tomb. Most of those scores were performed, applauded, printed in vocal score, and then shelved as taste moved on. Their libretti sit in library catalogues. Their music survives in archives, in a few cases in modern editions, in fewer cases still on recordings. Yet the standard concert and opera repertory behaves as though the tale had been set only twice. Restoring the forgotten settings to view changes the picture of the story's musical appeal. The tale was not a rare inspiration seized by two geniuses. It was a standing resource of the European stage, a property that managers reached for whenever a company needed passion, youth, and a tomb scene that could be relied upon to draw tears. The crowded field is the fact; the two survivors are the exception that requires explanation.

### What does the phrase forgotten operas actually mean in this article?

Forgotten operas, in the strict sense used here, are complete stage scores on the Verona tale that once reached professional performance and publication yet hold no place in the standard international repertory. The category excludes sketches, unfinished projects, and works known only from a title page.

It includes scores that survive entire, scores that survive in part, and scores whose music is lost while libretti and reviews remain. The shelf examined here runs from Georg Benda in 1776 to Filippo Marchetti in 1865, with Vaccai's setting of 1825 as the central exhibit. Each entry earns its place by documentation: a dated premiere, a named house, a librettist, and a traceable performance record.

That definition matters because loose talk about lost masterpieces helps nobody. Some of the vanished scores were routine products of their seasons, competent and forgettable in the ordinary way of repertory filler. Others were events of their decade, discussed across Europe, and their disappearance says nothing simple about quality. The argument here never claims that every forgotten opera is a buried treasure. The claim is narrower and stronger: the pattern of survival follows the mechanics of the repertory trade, the demands of star singers, the availability of printed material, and the accident of which scores a later generation chose to revive. Quality plays a part, as it should, but quality alone neither saved the strong nor doomed the weak. The shelf, read as a system, shows a market at work.

The central exhibit rewards the attention. Nicola Vaccai was born at Tolentino in 1790 and died in 1848, a composer of some seventeen operas and, in his later years, one of the most respected singing teachers in Europe. His Giulietta e Romeo reached the stage of the Teatro alla Canobbiana in Milan on 31 October 1825, with a libretto by Felice Romani drawn from the tragedy of the same name that Luigi Scevola had published in 1818, itself descended from the novella of Luigi da Porto. The premiere cast placed Joséphine de Méric in the title role of Giulietta and Isabella Fabbrica, a contralto in travesty, as Romeo. The opera travelled: Barcelona heard it in May 1827, Paris in September of the same year, London in April 1832, and Mexico in 1841. By any contemporary measure the work was a success, the last major success of its composer's career, although nine further operas followed it. Then the Bellini setting arrived in 1830, on a libretto that Romani reworked from his own Vaccai text, and the older opera began its long slide out of the repertory, even as its final scene began a second and stranger career.

## The Tomb Scene That Outlived Its Opera

The substitution at the heart of this article is among the best documented practices of nineteenth-century opera, and its outline can be stated with precision. Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi, first performed at La Fenice in Venice on 11 March 1830, ends with a tomb scene in which Romeo takes poison, Giulietta wakes, and the lovers die in a compressed final exchange. On 27 October 1832, at Bologna, the singer Maria Malibran, appearing as Romeo, caused the tomb scene of the Vaccai opera to be substituted for the Bellini ending. The practice spread. Through the middle and later decades of the century, Italian houses in particular presented the Bellini opera with the Vaccai finale as a matter of routine. The borrowed scene held the stage in that hybrid form until 8 September 1897, when a performance at Hamburg gave the last documented instance of the substitution. Sixty-five years elapsed between the Bologna evening and the Hamburg one. For most of that span, audiences who believed they were hearing Bellini complete were hearing Bellini with a Vaccai heart at the close.

The mechanics of the swap explain its durability. Both operas set librettos by Romani on the same Scevola source, so the dramatic situation at the tomb matched almost joint for joint. The Vaccai scene gives Romeo an extended lament at the opened tomb, built around the romanza beginning "Ah! se tu dormi, svegliati", and then grants the waking Giulietta and her lover a fuller duet of recognition and farewell than the Bellini close allows. A Romeo of Malibran's gifts gained minutes of exposed, expressive singing at the exact point of maximum audience feeling. The scene also suited the travesti tradition: the contralto or mezzo-soprano Romeo, a woman in the hero's dress, carried the finale as a solo display framed by grief. Bellini's ending, by contrast, moves swiftly and ends in a blaze of collective lament. Singers preferred the scene that lingered. Managers preferred the scene that singers preferred. The substitution perpetuated itself through the ordinary selfishness of the theatre, which is to say through the strongest force in repertory history.

### Why did singers prefer the Vaccai tomb scene to the Bellini ending?

Singers preferred the Vaccai scene because it enlarged the Romeo part at the moment of greatest emotional return. The romanza "Ah! se tu dormi, svegliati" offers a long, arching lament over the opened tomb, and the awakening that follows gives both principals a sustained duet rather than a swift collapse.

For a travesti Romeo of the Malibran type, whose art centred on expressive intensity in extended cantabile, the scene functioned as a final aria in all but name. The Bellini close, compressed and choral, offered no equivalent platform. Preference hardened into custom, and custom outlived every singer who had created it.

A comparison with the play clarifies what the substituted scene restores. Shakespeare's tomb sequence gives the hero a long solo over the body, from "Eyes, look your last" through the farewell to the "detestable maw" of death (5.3), and then grants the waking Juliet only a brief exchange with the Friar and a solitary death. The operas invert the proportions. In both Italian settings the heroine's potion speech and tomb awakening carry weight that the spoken play distributes elsewhere, because an opera house sells the soprano's grief as surely as the contralto's. The Vaccai scene, however, tilts the balance furthest toward shared lament: the lovers recognise each other, sing together, and die in sequence with the music allowed to dwell. Nineteenth-century audiences, raised on the duet as the emotional summit of an evening, received in the borrowed finale the shape they had been trained to want. Bellini, composing at speed for Venice, had given them a sterner and quicker close. The market corrected him with another composer's pages.

The episode also exposes a truth about authorship in the period. A score was a working document, not a sacred object. Singers carried favourite arias from opera to opera in the famous suitcase tradition, impresarios ordered cuts and insertions, and a successful scene could migrate across titles without scandal. The Vaccai substitution was an extreme case only in its scale and longevity: an entire closing scene, by a living rival's near-contemporary, bolted onto the most admired bel canto treatment of the tale. Later generations, taught to revere the integrity of the work, read the practice as vandalism. The participants read it as service to the evening. Both readings capture something, and the disagreement between modern scholars over exactly this point is taken up in a later section of this article, where the verdict must be earned rather than assumed.
## Nicola Vaccai: The Composer Behind the Borrowed Finale

Vaccai's career explains both the strength of his tomb scene and the fragility of his fame. Trained in Rome, where he studied counterpoint with Giuseppe Jannaconi, he earned his early living in Venice writing ballets and teaching singing, and he reached his first operatic success with I solitari di Scozia at Naples in 1815. A commission at Parma produced Pietro il grande in 1819, a production in which the composer himself appeared among the soloists. Zadig e Astartea followed at Naples early in 1825, and then, in the October of that year, the Verona opera at the Canobbiana. The house stood second in Milan only to La Scala, and a success there carried across Italy on the network of impresarios who leased theatres season by season. The Vaccai setting rode that network for seven years as a repertory piece in its own right. Its composer, meanwhile, was building the second career that would define his name for posterity: the singing studio.

The London chapter deserves a paragraph of its own. The opera reached the King's Theatre in April 1832, in the same season that made the composer a fashionable teacher in the English capital. Vaccai settled into the pedagogy that produced his Metodo pratico di canto italiano, published in 1832 and translated, adapted, and transposed for every voice type across the following century. Generations of singers learned Italian legato from its short exercises, each a compact lesson in a single technical problem, and many who mastered the method never learned that its author had written a Verona opera whose finale once displaced Bellini's. The teacher swallowed the composer. Reference works file Vaccai under vocal pedagogy first and opera second, and the tomb scene, when it is mentioned, appears as a curiosity attached to the Bellini literature rather than as the climax of a complete score with a performance history of its own.

### How many operas did Vaccai write besides the Verona setting?

Vaccai wrote some seventeen operas across a career that began in 1815, of which the Verona setting of 1825 was the last major success, with nine further stage works following it. The earlier titles include I solitari di Scozia, Pietro il grande, and Zadig e Astartea.

None held the stage as the Verona opera did, and none produced a scene with an independent afterlife. The composer's output also includes sacred music, cantatas, and the celebrated singing method of 1832, the publication through which his name survived in studios long after his operas left the theatres.

The eclipse by Bellini was not a simple contest of scores, and treating it as one distorts the record. The younger composer, born in 1801, eleven years after Vaccai, arrived with a melodic gift that contemporaries heard as a new order of expressiveness, and his setting benefited from the same Romani libretto reworked with the benefit of five years of stage experience. Romani knew which scenes had played well in 1825 and shaped the 1830 text accordingly. Bellini also composed for voices he knew, with Giuditta Grisi as his travesti Romeo, and he plundered his own failed Zaira for material under extreme time pressure, a month and a half for the Venice carnival season. The result conquered the Italian stage and then Europe. Against that ascent, the older opera had no defence except its finale, and the finale promptly defected to the winning side. The metaphor of defection is exact: the scene changed allegiance while keeping its identity, and programmes went on printing the name Vaccai in small type beneath the Bellini title, an acknowledgement that the evening's emotional climax belonged to the loser of the contest.

A fair hearing of the complete Vaccai score, in the rare modern revivals and the complete recording that circulates among specialists, supports a measured judgement. The opera is a well-made dramma of the Rossinian aftermath, strong in ensemble writing, conservative in harmony, and generous to its two principals. Its tomb scene is its summit because Romani and Vaccai concentrate there the whole machinery of recognition that the tale promises: the opened tomb, the lament, the waking, the duet, the double death. The earlier acts, by contrast, divide their interest among feud choruses and a Tebaldo whose music lacks the menace that the part requires. The shelf verdict offered at the end of this article returns to that unevenness. A single great scene could travel; a whole evening with weak stretches could not hold a repertory slot against a leaner rival. The fragment's survival and the whole work's disappearance are two faces of one judgement that audiences delivered nightly for decades.

## The Shelf Before Vaccai: Benda and the Eighteenth Century

The forgotten shelf opens in Gotha. Georg Benda, a Bohemian composer in the service of the Saxon court, supplied the music for Romeo und Julie, a Singspiel in three acts to a libretto by Friedrich Wilhelm Gotter, first performed on 25 September 1776 at the Ekhof Theatre in the Friedenstein Palace. Gotter worked from the Shakespeare translation of Christian Felix Weisse, and his adaptation follows the habits of its moment with instructive fidelity. Characters vanish: the Nurse, Mercutio, and the comic servants have no place in a text that observes the unities of time and place and concentrates the action on the lovers and the father. Comedy drains away. Julie herself becomes the strongest figure in the piece, a shift that Benda's score reinforces by giving her the musical centre of gravity. Most strikingly, the ending reverses the tale. Gotter supplied a lieto fine, a happy resolution in which the lovers live, in accordance with the operatic convention of the period that expected catastrophe to relent at the close.

That happy ending is the single most revealing document on the early shelf. It demonstrates that the tale's tragic close, treated by later centuries as sacred, was negotiable property in 1776. An audience at Gotha received the feud, the secret marriage, the potion, and the tomb, and then watched the machinery of death dismantled in time. The spoken stage of the same decades made similar adjustments, as the production history of the play in the period shows, and the opera merely followed the theatre's lead. Benda's score, admired in its day and revived in the twentieth century with a recording under Hermann Breuer and a British stage premiere by Bampton Classical Opera in 2007, survives as the earliest complete operatic treatment of the tale that performers can mount. Its disappearance from the repertory for two centuries had mundane causes: the Singspiel form fell from fashion, German courts changed their musical establishments, and the score's idiom, poised between baroque inheritance and classical clarity, pleased neither the admirers of Handel nor the followers of Mozart. The work did nothing wrong. Its world ended around it.

The French stage answered in the following decade. Daniel Steibelt, a Berlin-born keyboard virtuoso of formidable reputation and equally formidable self-regard, composed Roméo et Juliette to a libretto by Joseph-Alexandre de Ségur, first performed at the Théâtre Feydeau in Paris on 9 October 1793, in the middle of the Revolution's most violent season. The work is an opéra in three acts with spoken dialogue between the musical numbers, the form that the French stage called opéra comique regardless of subject. Steibelt restored the tragic ending that Gotter had removed, and his score, published by the composer in Paris in the year of the premiere, circulated in the arrangements and extracts through which most contemporaries encountered new opera. The piece held the Feydeau stage respectably and then faded as its composer's fame migrated to the piano: Steibelt the virtuoso, famous for a celebrated and possibly apocryphal contest with Beethoven in Vienna in 1800, eclipsed Steibelt the opera composer, a pattern that the Vaccai case repeats in a different key.

Between the Paris work and the Milan one stands the Italian entry that dominated the tale's operatic life before Vaccai. Niccolò Antonio Zingarelli, a Neapolitan master of the old school, set Giulietta e Romeo to a libretto by Giuseppe Maria Foppa, first performed at La Scala on 30 January 1796. The score won a reputation out of proportion to its composer's later standing: accounts describe a composition of remarkable speed, and the opera held the Italian repertory well into the nineteenth century. Its Romeo became a favourite vehicle for Malibran herself in the years around 1830, which gives the singer a unique place in this history. The same artist who championed the Zingarelli setting in her youth later ordered the Vaccai tomb scene into the Bellini opera, a one-woman thread connecting three of the shelf's principal exhibits. Zingarelli's setting faded as bel canto taste hardened around Rossini and his successors; its idiom, rooted in the eighteenth century, came to sound like the past. The pattern repeats across the shelf: each generation's setting is displaced not by failure but by the next generation's style, and the tale itself, indifferent to style, passes to new hands.

Read as a system, the catalogue refutes the lazy assumption that the tale waited for genius to notice it. A court Singspiel, a revolutionary Paris opera, two Milanese settings a generation apart, a Trieste score of the Risorgimento decades, and the two survivors make a continuous chain. Every link in the chain was, in its season, a plausible candidate for permanence. Benda had court prestige. Steibelt had a virtuoso's fame and a published score. Zingarelli had La Scala and three decades of Italian performances. Vaccai had the finest tomb scene of the age. Marchetti had the earnest national manner that Italian audiences of the 1860s rewarded. Permanence went elsewhere, to a Venetian score written in six weeks and a Paris score by a composer whose Faust had already conquered the world. The catalogue therefore poses the article's central question in its sharpest form: not why the forgotten works were written, but what machinery decides which of several admired works a later century keeps.

One further discipline governs the catalogue, and honesty requires it to be stated inside the article rather than left to a footnote. The shelf of settings on this tale is longer than any single table can hold, and reference works disagree about the census: minor scores, provincial productions, and works that survive only as libretto titles blur every count. The catalogue confines itself to settings whose premieres, houses, and librettists stand on firm documentation, and declines to inflate the shelf with titles that cannot be verified to that standard. A shorter shelf, honestly built, carries more argumentative weight than a long one padded with ghosts. Where a setting's record divides, the division is reported rather than smoothed away, in this table and throughout the article.

## The Italian Succession: From Zingarelli to Marchetti

The Italian line of the shelf shows the tale functioning as a kind of national property long before Shakespeare's name dominated its marketing. Da Porto's novella was Italian before the play existed, Bandello and Scevola kept the story in Italian letters, and Romani could draw his libretto from Scevola without touching the English text at all. That lineage matters for the music. The Italian settings treat the feud as civic tragedy: the chorus of Capuleti and Montecchi is not decoration but a political body, and the Prince's authority, marginal in the play after the first scene, haunts the operas as the guarantor of a public order that private passion destroys. Zingarelli's score of 1796, formed in the conventions of opera seria, gives the feud its full choral weight. Vaccai inherits that weight and adds the concentrated tomb machinery. Bellini, in the companion treatment of [Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi of 1830](/2028/11/03/bellini-capuleti-montecchi/), thins the public frame and intensifies the private one, which is part of why his score travelled beyond Italy while the heavier civic settings stayed home.

Filippo Marchetti closes the Italian shelf. His Romeo e Giulietta, to a libretto by Marco Marcello, reached the stage at Trieste on 25 October 1865 and was revised for Milanese performance in the following years. Marchetti, born in 1831, belonged to the generation of Italian composers working in the long shadow that Verdi cast over the national stage, and his setting shows the strain and the ambition of that position: an earnest, through-composed manner, an attempt to give the feud scenes a weight worthy of the middle-period Verdi model, and a tomb scene that must compete, inevitably, with the memory of two famous predecessors and with the hybrid Bellini-Vaccai finale still circulating in Italian houses. The opera won respect in Italy and travelled little beyond it. Its disappearance follows the cruelest version of the shelf's law: a competent national work, arriving after the international slots for its subject had closed, could be admired at home for a generation and still leave no trace in the wider repertory. By 1865 the tale's operatic identity abroad was already fixed as Bellini for Italy and, two years later, Gounod for France. Marchetti's opera was competing for a chair that had been taken before its premiere.

### What distinguishes the Italian settings from the German and French ones?

The Italian settings descend from the native novella tradition through Scevola and Romani, so the feud carries civic and choral weight, and the story reads as public tragedy invaded by private passion. The German Singspiel of Benda concentrates on the heroine, strips the cast to a handful of figures, observes the unities, and releases the lovers alive.

The French setting by Steibelt keeps the tragic close inside a form built on spoken dialogue, while the later French survivor adds the grand opera apparatus of ballet and massed spectacle. National habits of theatre, more than fidelity to any single source text, shape each version.

The civic reading has a textual root in the play that the operas honour more faithfully than many spoken productions do. The Prince opens the action with a sentence of death against further brawling (1.1) and closes it with judgement, "All are punish'd" (5.3), framing the love story inside a failure of public order. The Italian libretti keep that frame audible through their choruses. When the Capuleti and Montecchi sing as factions, the audience hears Verona itself as a character, and the lovers' private music sounds, by contrast, like a secession from the city. The forgotten Italian settings understood something about the tale that the star-centred nineteenth century gradually unlearned and that the substituted tomb scene, for all its beauty, helped to obscure: the tragedy is double, a city killing its children by the feud's logic while the children kill themselves by love's. The shelf's civic choruses are among the losses that the repertory's narrowing genuinely cost.


## Why Operas Vanish: The Machinery of Repertory Survival

The causes of disappearance sort into five, and the shelf supplies an exhibit for each. The first cause is stylistic succession. Opera renews its language faster than any other theatrical form, and each renewal renders the previous generation's scores expensive to perform and strange to hear. Zingarelli's idiom sounded antique beside Rossini within twenty years of the Scala premiere. Steibelt's score belonged to a revolutionary decade whose musical rhetoric the restored monarchy preferred to forget. Style alone executed more settings of this tale than any failure of craft.

The second cause is the star system, which giveth and taketh away with the same hand. A setting built around a particular voice lives exactly as long as that voice, or as long as successors willing to adopt an unfamiliar score can be found. Malibran's championship kept the Zingarelli Romeo in view and created the Vaccai substitution, yet the same system, once it fixed its affections on the Bellini score as the tale's Italian vehicle, had no need of the complete Vaccai opera. Stars canonise fragments and orphan wholes. The third cause is material: performance requires parts, and parts require publishers. A score printed in full and sold across Europe, as the Gounod setting was, can be revived by any house with a library. A score circulating in manuscript copies, hired from a single theatre's archive, dies when the archive disperses. Much of the forgotten shelf survives in precisely that fragile state, complete on paper in one or two libraries, impractical for a house that must budget rehearsal time against certainty.

The fourth cause is the closing of national slots. By the later nineteenth century the international repertory had hardened into a small canon in which each famous literary property carried one, at most two, accredited musical settings. The tale's slots filled early: Bellini for the Italian house, Gounod for the French, and the concert hall took the tale in orchestral form instead, through the fantasy-overture whose love theme became the story's most famous musical signature of all, a subject owned by the companion treatment of [Tchaikovsky's Fantasy-Overture and Its Theme](/2028/11/17/tchaikovsky-fantasy-overture/). An orchestral survivor matters to this argument because it shows the gate narrowing by medium as well as by nation: twenty minutes of concert music could hold the tale's place in programmes where a full opera could not. Marchetti, arriving in 1865 with an Italian setting of a tale whose Italian slot Bellini had occupied for thirty-five years, faced a gate already shut. Zandonai's Italian setting of 1922 faced the same gate two generations later and met the same fate abroad, a parallel examined in the treatment of [Zandonai's Opera Giulietta e Romeo of 1922](/2029/03/02/zandonai-giulietta-e-romeo/), which owns the later score whole.

The fifth cause is the accident of revival. Repertories forget by default and remember by effort: a conductor's enthusiasm, a recording label's gamble, a festival's theme. Benda's Singspiel found its effort in the twentieth century, with scholars of the pre-classical stage and a Bampton production that proved the piece stageworthy in English. The Vaccai opera found a partial effort in the bel canto revival that restored so much Italian opera of the 1820s and 1830s to view, though the revival centred on the Bellini score and treated the Vaccai finale as an appendix to be recorded for completeness. Revival is thus not a court of appeal that corrects the repertory's injustices; revival follows fashion as obediently as the original canon did, and its choices ratify most of the old verdicts while overturning a photogenic few. The forgotten operas that return tend to be those with a story attached, a substitution, a happy ending, a famous singer's name in the cast list. The shelf's plainest citizens remain forgotten, because forgetting, once complete, leaves nothing for curiosity to grip.

### Which cause of disappearance mattered most for the Vaccai opera?

Stylistic succession mattered least for Vaccai, whose language stood close to the Bellini idiom that replaced him, and the star system mattered most. The opera's complete form depended on houses choosing an unfamiliar full score over a famous rival on the same libretto, a choice managers declined once the Bellini setting became the accredited Italian vehicle.

Its finale survived precisely because the star system extracted the scene singers wanted and discarded the frame. Material fragility completed the work: without wide publication of the full score, revival required archival effort that the bel canto revival spent on bigger names.

Set against the spoken play's own survival, the operatic pattern sharpens. The play endured every century because print carried it cheaply and any company of actors could mount it. An opera is capital-intensive: orchestra, chorus, principals, and a score that exists in few copies. The tale on the spoken stage could afford a hundred local failures and still live; the tale in opera needed institutional commitment in every generation, and institutions commit to the familiar. That asymmetry, more than any judgement on quality, is the deep structure beneath the catalogue. The story's musical appeal in the nineteenth century was enormous and continuous. The machinery for preserving its musical settings was weak, local, and hostage to taste. Appeal and preservation are different variables, and the forgotten shelf is what their divergence looks like over ninety years.

## Critics on the Shelf: Three Positions and a Verdict

The literature on the forgotten settings divides into three broad positions, and naming them precisely matters more than the courtesy of vague agreement. The first position belongs to the bel canto scholarship associated with Philip Gossett, whose work on nineteenth-century performance practice, above all in Divas and Scholars, treats singer-driven alteration as a legitimate creative order rather than a corruption. On that view, the Vaccai substitution was the system working as designed: performers with superior knowledge of their own instruments reshaped an evening toward its strongest material, and the composite Bellini-Vaccai finale, ratified by sixty-five years of audiences, has a claim to be considered a genuine work of the period, authored by a tradition rather than by a single composer. The second position is the work-concept severity associated with the editorial tradition that restored the Bellini score to its composer's text in modern critical editions: the substitution was a commercial deformation, the borrowed scene is objectively weaker in construction than the close it displaced, and the duty of modern performance is restitution. Charles Osborne, in his survey of the bel canto operas, gave the popular form of this severity, treating the Vaccai scene as a mellifluous intrusion that softened Bellini's sterner ending. The third position, represented by the repertory historians who study canon formation, sidesteps the aesthetic quarrel entirely: survival patterns track institutions, publishing, and pedagogy, and the substitution interests them as evidence about how nineteenth-century opera actually functioned, whatever a modern listener prefers.

The disagreement that requires adjudication is the first against the second: was the substituted finale a creative achievement of the performance tradition or a deformation that modern practice rightly reversed? The evidence supports a split verdict with a clear tilt. On the historical question, Gossett's side wins decisively. The substitution was no freak. It arose from the ordinary, documented mechanics of the period, it satisfied singers, managers, and audiences across two generations and several countries, and condemning it by the standards of a later work-concept is anachronism dressed as principle. On the aesthetic question, however, the severity of the editors has the better of the argument in one specific: the Vaccai scene changes the ending's meaning. Bellini's compressed close denies the lovers time; the catastrophe outruns their farewell, and the effect is of a machinery of death too fast for love to answer. The Vaccai scene grants the farewell its full duet, consoles the audience, and converts haste into ceremony. Consolation is a real value, and Malibran's audiences were not fools to want it, but the sterner ending is the more honest reading of a tale whose horror lies in minutes missed and messages delayed. The play itself ends the tomb sequence in haste and interruption, with the Friar urging flight and the watch approaching (5.3). Bellini, perhaps by instinct under time pressure, preserved that haste. The tradition that replaced his ending preserved the period's taste instead. Modern performance is therefore right to restore the Bellini close as the default, and right also, on Gossett's evidence, to present the Vaccai scene in concert and in recordings as a document of equal historical dignity. Restitution for the stage, honour for the archive: that is the adjudicated conclusion.

A second disagreement, quieter but genuine, concerns the worth of the complete Vaccai opera. The Grove tradition, in entries shaped by the reference scholarship of which Julian Budden was a leading bel canto exponent, files the composer chiefly as a pedagogue with one once-popular opera, a filing that doubles as a judgement. Against that filing stands the evidence of the opera's own record: a seven-year international performance life before the Bellini setting existed, a London production in the composer's presence, and a finale that the toughest audience of all, professional singers protecting their reputations, preferred to the most admired score of the age. A composer whose scene beat Bellini in open competition for sixty-five years cannot be filed as a footnote without the filing itself becoming suspect. The measured conclusion is that the complete opera is a strong period work with an exceptional finale, neither a lost masterpiece of the first rank nor the negligible thing its reference-entry reputation implies. The shelf is full of such middle cases, and the habit of grading repertory history in superlatives, masterpiece or oblivion, is itself one of the forces that keeps the middle invisible.

For researchers assembling the evidence on substitution practices across houses and decades, the discipline of the archive is everything: cast lists, printed libretti with pasted-in replacement scenes, and publishers' catalogues must be kept in order before any pattern can be trusted. Scholars building a private research collection for this work often organise session notes and source files with a personal vault tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the catalogue of a single season's substitutions can be kept beside the correspondence that explains it. Order matters because the evidence is scattered by nature: a substitution that every Italian house adopted may survive in only a handful of annotated scores, and the scholar who cannot retrieve the Hamburg programme of 1897 cannot date the practice's end.


## The Substitution in Detail: Bologna 1832 to Hamburg 1897

The documented arc of the substitution deserves a slower telling, because its dates carry the argument. The Vaccai opera came first, in 1825, and spent its early years as a complete work in the Italian system, with Barcelona and Paris productions extending its reach. Bellini's setting followed in March 1830, and its success was immediate enough that within two years the older opera's independent life had effectively ended; houses wanted the tale in the Bellini version. The substitution began on 27 October 1832 at Bologna, when Malibran, singing Romeo in the Bellini opera, had the Vaccai tomb scene performed in place of the Bellini final act material. The detail that the initiative was the singer's is recorded consistently and fits everything known of Malibran's authority: a star of her magnitude, engaged at enormous fees, could dictate the shape of an evening, and impresarios who resisted such demands lost their attraction to a rival house.

From Bologna the practice radiated along the routes that Italian companies travelled. The borrowed finale became the expected form of the opera in Italy, the form in which a generation of listeners formed their idea of the work. Singers who had never performed the complete Vaccai opera sang its tomb scene as a matter of course. The scene's romanza circulated in sheet music, detached from both operas, as a concert piece for contralto and mezzo-soprano voices. Meanwhile the Bellini score in its genuine form receded into a kind of scholarly rumour, known to connoisseurs, restored only when the twentieth century's editorial conscience took the work in hand. The Hamburg performance of 8 September 1897 closed the documented record, sixty-five years after Bologna. By then the practice was a survival from a theatrical world that the new century was dismantling: fixed scores, conductor authority, and the printed critical edition were displacing the singer's prerogative everywhere. The substitution did not die because audiences stopped enjoying the scene. It died because the system that had authorised it died.

### What exactly was substituted at Bologna in 1832?

The substituted material was the tomb scene of the Vaccai opera, the scene built around Romeo's romanza "Ah! se tu dormi, svegliati" at the opened tomb, with the awakening of Giulietta and the lovers' final duet. The borrowing was printed, billed, and reviewed under the host opera's title from the first evening onward.

Accounts describe the borrowing variously as the opera's next-to-last scene or as its last act material, since the scene carries the whole dramatic weight of the close. It replaced the corresponding final scene of the Bellini opera, in which the catastrophe moves at a brisker pace. The host opera remained Bellini in title, billing, and the whole of its earlier acts; only the ending changed hands.

A distinction must be drawn between this substitution and the common aria-swapping of the period, because the scale differs in kind. A suitcase aria replaced one closed number with another of similar function, leaving the drama's architecture intact. The Vaccai borrowing replaced the ending of the drama, the sequence toward which every earlier scene had been aiming, and it replaced that ending with material by a different composer working from the same librettist's earlier draft of the same source. No other case in the bel canto repertory matches its combination of scale, duration, and documentary clarity. That uniqueness is why the episode anchors this article and why it serves historians as the limiting case in debates about authorship: if a sixty-five-year tradition of performance can attach a Vaccai ending to a Bellini opera without the theatrical world sensing a fraud, then the modern idea of the inviolable score is a later invention, however valuable, and not a law of operatic nature.

The scene's musical character explains its portability. Romani's tomb text is a self-contained sequence: entrance and lament, the crisis of the poison, the waking, the duet, the deaths. It presupposes nothing from earlier scenes except the potion, which every version of the tale supplies. Vaccai's setting builds the sequence as a graduated arc from solo grief to shared ecstasy-in-death, with the romanza's long-breathed melody doing the work that recitative and ensemble do elsewhere. A house could lift the sequence entire, rehearse it as a unit, and attach it to the Bellini frame with minimal friction at the joint. Portability is an unglamorous virtue, rarely listed among the causes of artistic survival, yet the history of music is full of its effects: excerpts travel, wholes stay home. The Vaccai finale became the tale's most travelled operatic fragment of the century, heard by thousands who never saw its parent opera billed, a condition of fame so complete and so anonymous that the scene's authorship faded from common knowledge even while the scene itself was being sung.

## Malibran and the Singer's Prerogative

Maria Malibran, born in 1808 into the Garcia dynasty of singers and dead at twenty-eight in 1836 after a riding accident, compressed into a short career an authority that later singers have rarely matched. Her Romeo, in the Zingarelli setting first and then in the Bellini, was among her signature assumptions, and the travesti convention suited her dramatic temperament: contemporary accounts dwell on the ferocity and tenderness she combined in the part, and on her habit of treating each performance as an act of personal possession. The Bologna substitution bears her signature in every sense. A singer of the second rank could not have imposed a rival composer's ending on the most successful new opera in Italy. Malibran could, and the record shows the imposition becoming custom so quickly that within a few seasons the borrowed ending was simply how the opera went.

Her case exposes the double edge of the prerogative she wielded. The same authority that inserted the Vaccai scene into the Bellini opera also preserved, through her advocacy, music that the market might otherwise have dropped at once: the Zingarelli Romeo owed its late currency in part to her performances. Singer power was neither vandalism nor guardianship in itself; it was a force, and like every force in theatre history it preserved what pleased the powerful. The historian's task is to trace its effects without importing a later morality. Malibran wanted a finale that displayed her art at the grave's edge, in the long line and the sustained duet, and she wanted it in 1832 because her voice and fame were at their height and the Bellini ending, for all its merits, gave the evening's biggest moment to speed and chorus. The Vaccai scene gave the moment to her. Sixty-five years of imitation followed, because the logic that produced the choice remained valid for every successor who sang the part in the same tradition.

The prerogative extended beyond a single evening's shape. Singers of the period owned their cadenzas, negotiated their aria lists in contracts, and carried printed music between engagements as personal property. An impresario bought a voice and accepted the repertory decisions that came with it. Read against that background, the substitution appears less as an anomaly than as the system's most durable monument, the one case where a singer's preference fossilised into a text that publishers eventually printed as an alternative ending, available to houses that wished to follow the custom. The printed alternative is the crucial stage in the story: once the Vaccai finale could be ordered from a catalogue as an authorised supplement, the practice no longer depended on any singer's memory or insistence. It had become infrastructure. Infrastructure outlives its makers, which is why a scene imposed by a singer dead in 1836 was still being performed in Hamburg in 1897, by artists for whom Malibran was a legend of their grandparents' generation.


## Two Endings Compared: What the Borrowed Scene Changes

Placing the two closes side by side, as scores, converts the substitution debate from anecdote into analysis. Bellini's final sequence begins with the Capulet tomb and the mourning of the Montecchi party, gives Romeo a romanza of farewell, "Deh! tu, bell'anima", and then accelerates: poison, waking, a brief recognition, deaths, and the choral judgement of the families. The design is Verdian before Verdi in its refusal to linger, and it honours the tale's own mechanics, in which every disaster arrives a few minutes too late or too soon. The Friar's letter miscarries by hours; the waking misses the poison by moments. An ending that hurries repeats that rhythm at the level of form. The audience is denied the consolation of a long farewell because the tale itself is about farewells denied.

Vaccai's tomb scene, drawn from the same Romani source at an earlier stage of its shaping, expands the recognition into the opera's emotional summit. The romanza over the tomb dwells on the single image of the sleeping face: wake, if sleep holds that face, is the burden of "Ah! se tu dormi, svegliati", a lover addressing beauty that he believes to be death. The poison works more slowly in dramatic time, the waking comes earlier relative to its action, and the duet that follows allows the pair the sustained mutual address that the spoken play grants them only in the orchard and the dawn parting. In effect the scene imports the balcony's reciprocity into the tomb. For an audience formed by the duet tradition, the gain felt enormous: the lovers, separated by the plot since the third act's dawn, are restored to each other in music before death divides them. The loss is the tale's timing, the cruelty of near-miss that makes the catastrophe feel fated rather than merely sad. Both endings are coherent. They are coherent about different things, and the nineteenth century, given the choice nightly, chose coherence about love over coherence about fate.

The play's own text arbitrates part of the dispute. Shakespeare's Romeo dies before Juliet wakes; the pair never exchange a word in the tomb, and the Friar's account to the Prince must reconstruct what the audience witnessed in silence and separation. The operas, all of them, soften that desolation by letting the heroine wake in time for recognition, a liberty that descends from the Italian novella tradition and that even the sterner Bellini close retains in compressed form. The difference among the operas is therefore one of degree within a shared liberty: how long the recognition may last. Vaccai's scene represents the maximum of the period, Bellini's near the minimum. A modern listener who knows the play may find the maximum sentimental and the minimum bleakly faithful, yet both stand at a fixed distance from the source, and the distance is the price of opera itself, an art that cannot let its soprano lie silent through the finale. The forgotten shelf teaches that lesson with unique clarity, because the same librettist calibrated the same scene twice, five years apart, for two composers of neighbouring style, and the market then chose between the calibrations.

## The Happy Ending and the Negotiation of the Close

Benda's lieto fine of 1776 belongs in this discussion because it proves that the ending of the tale was contested ground for as long as the tale has been staged in music. Gotter's rescue of the lovers offended no convention of its moment; the Saxon court audience expected the catastrophe to relent, and the Singspiel's Julie, established as the strongest figure in the piece, earns her survival as the drama's reward for constancy. What changed across the following century was not the tale but the ethics of adaptation. By the 1820s a tragic close had become the mark of seriousness, and Romani's libretti for Vaccai and Bellini both end in the tomb without rescue. By the time of the Hamburg substitution's last performance, a happy ending for the lovers would have been received as a burlesque. The shelf thus records a complete cycle: rescue, tragedy, and then the subtler negotiation of the Vaccai scene, which keeps the deaths and rescues only the farewell. Each generation kept the part of the ending it could bear to lose least.

The spoken stage ran the same cycle in parallel, with altered endings, restorations, and adaptations succeeding one another across the centuries, and the operatic record cannot be understood apart from that wider traffic. Opera borrowed the theatre's habits along with its story. When the spoken stage treated the text as material for the evening's needs, opera did the same with scores. The modern reverence for fixed texts in both arts arrived together, with the scholarly edition and the recording, and it froze the repertory at the moment of its arrival, canonising the Bellini opera as written and stranding the Vaccai scene as an appendix. Freezing has benefits that this article does not dispute: the Bellini score in its integrity is a greater experience than the hybrid, taken whole. Freezing also has costs that the shelf makes visible: an entire ecosystem of settings, each a record of how its age heard the tale, fell silent at once when the machinery of performance stopped tolerating alternatives.

### Did any forgotten setting besides Benda's spare the lovers?

No other setting on the documented shelf spares the lovers. The happy ending belongs to the Benda and Gotter Singspiel of 1776 alone among the principal exhibits, a product of its period's convention that tragedy at the opera house should relent at the close.

Steibelt restored the deaths in 1793, Zingarelli kept them in 1796, and every Italian setting after that point ends in the tomb. The singularity of the Gotha rescue is itself evidence: the tragic close hardened into an expectation so complete that later composers competed only over how the deaths should be paced and sung.

The negotiation over the close also shaped the tale's concert life. Orchestral treatments could select the love music and discard the tomb, or dwell on the feud and the deaths, according to the programme's needs, and the freedom of selection made instrumental versions durable in a way that fixed stage works were not. The ballet stage exercised the same freedom across a full evening. Opera, bound to a libretto's sequence, had the least freedom of all, which is another reason its settings multiplied and vanished in such numbers: each composer had to solve the whole tale, ending included, and each solution dated with its decade's taste. The shelf is crowded because the problem was compulsory and perishable at once.


## The Nineteenth-Century Appeal: Why Composers Kept Returning

The tale offered nineteenth-century opera a combination that no other literary property matched so completely. It supplied two young principals of equal weight, a soprano and a travesti contralto in the Italian system, or soprano and tenor in the French, so that a house could feature two stars rather than one. It supplied a public frame of feuding choruses that filled the stage and justified spectacle. It supplied a sequence of guaranteed situations that audiences came expecting and composers could pace like stations: the ball, the balcony, the wedding, the duels, the potion, the tomb. And it supplied an emotional register, youthful passion against civic hatred, that suited the century's self-image, in Italy above all, where the feud of two houses in a divided city carried meanings that no censor needed spelled out. A composer setting the tale bought a pre-sold evening. The wonder is not that settings multiplied; the wonder would be a century that left such property unexploited.

The travesti Romeo deserves particular stress, because the convention shaped the music of the Italian shelf at its core. A woman's voice in the hero's part lifts the love duets into a register of two high voices intertwining, a sonority that audiences prized and that composers from Zingarelli through Vaccai to Bellini wrote for with full knowledge of its effect. The convention also explains the tomb scene's design: the finale belongs to the Romeo because the Romeo was the evening's vocal athlete, the artist whose name sold the tickets. When the convention faded and tenors reclaimed the part in the French manner, the Italian settings lost their casting logic, one more mechanism by which style change executed the shelf. A modern house reviving the Vaccai opera must decide whether to honour the travesti casting, with a mezzo-soprano hero, or to transpose for a tenor and falsify the vocal design. Either choice costs money and rehearsal courage, and the difficulty itself helps keep the work forgotten: revival is easiest for operas whose casting maps onto the modern ensemble system, and the shelf's Italian entries map badly.

The appeal persisted because each nation could read its own concerns into the feud. In the German lands the tale arrived through translation as a test of the Singspiel's ambition to carry serious passion. In revolutionary Paris the lovers' defiance of family authority carried a charge that required no commentary in 1793. In Italy the divided city spoke to a peninsula of divided states, and the Prince's failed peace read as a parable of authority too weak to reconcile its factions. In the France of 1867 the tale became grand opera, with the ball as spectacle and the duets as the evening's merchandise. A property that every market can localise will be set again and again, and each setting, being local, will date when its moment passes. The crowded shelf and the empty repertory are the same fact viewed from different ends of the century.

## Verdict on the Forgotten Operas

The verdict promised at the outset can be delivered in full. The forgotten operas of the Verona tale vanished through the ordinary operation of the repertory system: style succeeded style, stars fixed their affections on single vehicles, unpublished scores became impractical, national slots closed, and revival followed fashion rather than justice. Within that system, individual quality mattered at the margin and decided little. The strongest single scene on the shelf, the Vaccai tomb scene, survived by deserting its opera. The most original conception on the shelf, Benda's heroine-centred Singspiel with its rescued lovers, survived as a scholarly exhibit and a festival rarity. The most complete period success before Bellini, the Zingarelli setting, vanished almost entire. Survival and merit correlate so weakly across the catalogue that no honest account can present the modern repertory as a judgement of history. The repertory is a residue. Residues deserve study precisely because they are not verdicts.

For the listener, the practical conclusion follows. The complete Vaccai opera merits the occasional revival it receives and repays attention in recording, above all for the tomb scene in its native context, where its arrival after two acts of feud and separation carries a weight that the borrowed position inside the Bellini opera, paradoxically, both proved and diluted. The Benda Singspiel merits its festival life as the tale's strangest and most revealing early mirror. The Steibelt and Marchetti scores merit the archival performances and scholarly editions through which forgotten opera is honestly served, rather than the inflated rediscovery rhetoric that promises a masterpiece in every archive. And the substitution itself merits its place in every account of the Bellini opera, not as a scandal to be tutted over but as the period's own criticism of the ending it was given, delivered by the only critics whose opinions the nineteenth century fully trusted: the singers who had to bring the house down at the tomb, night after night, in city after city, for sixty-five years.

The series thesis finds its confirmation here. The cliché knows two operas and imagines a tale set twice by genius. The record shows a crowded field in which genius was one factor among many and rarely the deciding one. Restoring the forgotten settings does not demote the survivors; the Bellini and Gounod scores keep their stature under any honest comparison. Restoration changes the meaning of their survival, from inevitability to contingency, and contingency is the beginning of understanding. The lovers died in the tomb in every generation. The operas that carried them there died in the archive, each for reasons that its own century would have recognised as business. Business, not oblivion, is the true subject of the forgotten shelf, and Vaccai, the teacher whose finale outlived his opera inside a rival's masterpiece, remains its most eloquent witness.


## The Teacher's Afterlife: Vaccai Beyond the Stage

The strangest part of the Vaccai story is that the composer never vanished at all; only the operas did. The Metodo pratico of 1832 travelled further than any of his scores, into studios across Europe and the Americas, translated and transposed until the name on the title page detached from any biography and became a brand of exercise. Singers who would never hear the tomb scene in a theatre practised their legato on Vaccai's graduated lessons, and teachers assigned the method as a matter of routine into the twentieth century and beyond. The phenomenon has few parallels: a composer remembered by practitioners of an adjacent craft, in daily use, while the works on which his contemporary fame rested sit unperformed. It complicates any simple story of forgetting. The repertory forgot the operas and kept the man, in the one form, the teaching text, that the repertory system does not control, because pedagogy transmits hand to hand and needs no impresario, no chorus, no orchestra, and no slot in a canon.

The method also preserves, in miniature, the aesthetic that the tomb scene displays at full scale. Its exercises prize the long line, the even scale, the messa di voce discipline by which a singer swells and diminishes a single tone without breaking its thread. Those are exactly the virtues that the romanza at the tomb demands: a melody that must be sustained over the opened grave at a grief-stricken tempo, swelling on the beloved name, diminishing into the recognition that no answer comes. Malibran's generation learned that discipline from teachers formed in the same school, and the fit between the scene's requirements and the era's vocal training was not accidental. Vaccai wrote his finale for the technique he taught. When the technique changed, when voices grew heavier and orchestras louder across the later century, the scene's demands came to seem fragile, a further quiet cause of its retirement in 1897 alongside the editorial conscience that displaced it.

A theatre in Tolentino, the composer's birthplace, carries his name, the Teatro Nicola Vaccaj, a monument of the kind that Italy grants its musical sons. Monuments, however, commemorate rather than perform. The honest summary of the afterlife is that Vaccai survives everywhere as a teacher, in one city as a name over a door, and in the opera house only as a documented episode inside another composer's history. The episode's documentary richness is the compensation: because the substitution touched a canonical work, its traces were preserved in editions, memoirs, and programmes with a care that the independent histories of Steibelt or Marchetti never received. The forgotten shelf's central exhibit is, by the accident of its fragment's fame, the best evidenced of them all.

## Reading the Play Through the Forgotten Scores

The forgotten operas repay the reader of the play as well as the listener, because each setting is a dated act of interpretation, a record of what one decade heard in the tale. Benda and Gotter heard a heroine's drama and a catastrophe that decency should avert. Steibelt and Ségur heard a tragedy of authority defied, staged while a revolution tested every authority in France. Zingarelli heard a civic tragedy in the opera seria manner, in which the feud's choruses frame private passion. Vaccai and Romani heard a tomb scene waiting to be expanded until it held the whole meaning of the story. Marchetti heard a national drama in the Verdian shadow. None of these hearings is the play itself, and each is evidence about its hearers. A shelf of adaptations functions as a reception history in music, more candid than reviews because a composer must commit: every cut is a judgement, every expansion a confession of what the age found wanting in the source.

Two textual moments show the process at work. The first is the potion scene, in which the heroine debates the vial's terrors before drinking (4.3), a solo of fear that the play builds from questions addressed to absent figures. Opera seizes such a moment instinctively, since the aria is the form's native instrument for a mind arguing with itself, and the Italian settings give the potion scene a prominence that spoken productions, eager for the tomb, often hurry past. The second moment is the dawn parting (3.5), the lark and nightingale debate, which the operas convert into the great duet of separation that their audiences expected before the catastrophe. In both cases the settings redistribute the play's weight toward the heroine's solitary resolve and the pair's mutual address, away from the feud's public machinery and the Friar's plotting. Read across the shelf, the drift is unmistakable: the nineteenth century progressively privatised the tragedy, until the tomb duet could stand for the whole. The substituted Vaccai finale, the most privatised ending of all, is the drift's logical end. Modern spoken productions that restore the civic frame, the Prince's judgements and the families' public cost, are arguing, knowingly or not, with the operatic century's reading of the play.

### How do the forgotten operas change the reading of the tomb scene in the play?

The forgotten operas expose the tomb scene's openness by filling its silences in contrary ways. The play gives the hero a long solo, the heroine no farewell with him, and the explanation to the Friar and the Prince after the deaths.

Vaccai expands the recognition into a full duet, Bellini compresses it toward the play's haste, and Benda abolishes the deaths outright. Each solution reveals a pressure the text exerts: grief demands address, timing denies it, and decency in one century demanded rescue. The scene in the play reads differently once its musical afterlives have shown how many endings its silences can hold.

That openness has a documentary root. The early texts of the play differ at the tomb as elsewhere, and the operas, descending through the novella tradition as often as through the play, never faced a single fixed original to betray. Fidelity, the charge most often laid against forgotten adaptations, dissolves on inspection: the adapters were faithful to the tale as their culture transmitted it, through Scevola, through translations, through the stage practice of their own theatres. Judged by transmission rather than by a modern edition, the shelf's liberties shrink and its continuities grow. The lieto fine remains a liberty of the first order, yet even that liberty had spoken-stage precedent in the adaptation culture of its century. The forgotten operas were not careless with the tale. They were careful in currencies that later ages stopped accepting, which is a different failing, and a more interesting one.


## Evidence and Editions: How the Forgotten Shelf Is Studied

The study of forgotten opera rests on paper. Libretti printed for first nights carry cast lists, dedications, and the Argomento that summarises the source, and because libretti were sold at the door and kept as souvenirs, they survive in numbers that scores cannot match. The Vaccai libretto of 1825 and the Bellini libretto of 1830, both from Romani's hand, allow the tomb scenes to be compared word by word, and the comparison shows the later text redistributing lines and compressing exchanges that the earlier text had spread at leisure. Printed vocal scores form the second stratum: the Steibelt score published by its composer in 1793, the extracts and arrangements through which the Vaccai romanza circulated, and the alternative-ending editions that fossilised the substitution. The third stratum is the press: reviews that record not what a work contains but what an audience heard, including the uncomfortable fact that many reviewers of the hybrid Bellini performances praised the ending without remarking its authorship, because the audience's experience, not the title page, was the object of criticism.

Modern scholarship has rebuilt the shelf from those strata. The bel canto revival of the later twentieth century, driven by singers seeking vehicles and by editors producing performable texts, restored the Bellini opera to the international stage and dragged its Vaccai appendix into the light of recording studios. Grove's dictionary entries and the catalogues of the Italian theatres supply the premiere data on which the catalogue in this article rests. The Hamburg date of 8 September 1897, which closes the substitution's record, survives because German theatrical documentation of the period was systematic; equivalent performances in smaller Italian houses may have escaped record entirely, and the honest phrasing is therefore that 1897 marks the last documented instance, the point where the paper trail ends. Forgotten opera studies live with that humility throughout: absence of evidence describes archives as often as it describes stages. The claims made in this article have been confined to what the paper trail supports, and the shelf is shorter for it. That confinement is deliberate. A catalogue that admits only the verified can be trusted when it surprises, and its central surprise, sixty-five years of documented substitution, needs no embroidery.

## What Revival Would Require

A serious revival of the complete Vaccai opera faces practical hurdles that illuminate the whole problem of the forgotten shelf. The casting must solve the travesti Romeo: the part lies for a mezzo-soprano or contralto in the modern ensemble system, and houses that revive the Bellini opera in its original casting possess the template, yet the Vaccai score asks its Romeo for the sustained tomb cantabile that made the finale famous, a speciality within a speciality. The edition must be prepared from sources, with the labour costs that preparation entails and the risk that textual surprises await in any score unperformed for a century and a half. The marketing must sell a name that the public does not know, attached to a story the public knows in two other operatic forms, and the evening must survive comparison with the Bellini setting in the memory of every critic in the hall. Festivals, which exist to absorb exactly those risks, are the natural home of such revivals, and the festival circuit has indeed been the shelf's lifeline: Benda at Bampton, the bel canto rarities at specialist Italian and German festivals, the concert performances that test a score before staging commits to it.

The deeper requirement is a change in how the repertory tells its own history. As long as programme notes present the Bellini opera as an isolated masterpiece and the Gounod as its French counterpart, the shelf stays invisible and each revival arrives as a curiosity without a context. Place the same works inside the crowded field, as this article's catalogue does, and the curiosity becomes a document: the Vaccai performance explains the Bellini ending that audiences elsewhere will hear in its genuine form, and the explanation enriches rather than threatens the survivor. Survivors gain from the shelf's restoration. A canon that knows its own contingency is sturdier than one that must pretend to inevitability, because contingency can be argued and inevitability can only be asserted. The forgotten operas, restored to view, give the famous ones a history. That exchange, memory for company, is the final reason the shelf matters, and it costs the survivors nothing but a myth they never needed.


## The French and German Shelves Reconsidered

The northern entries on the shelf reward a second look, because their forgetting followed different mechanics from the Italian line and the contrast disciplines the argument. Benda's Singspiel belonged to a court culture: a Kapellmeister composing for a named theatre, a resident company, and a duke's taste. When court establishments contracted and the Singspiel's spoken-dialogue form yielded prestige to through-composed German opera, the work lost its institutional home entire, not merely its audience. Its survival on paper in court archives, and its twentieth-century exhumation by scholars of the pre-classical stage, followed the archive's logic rather than the market's. Steibelt's opera, by contrast, belonged to the commercial Paris of the revolutionary decade, was published by its composer for the open market, and died of biography: the composer's fame migrated to the keyboard, his later years were spent in St Petersburg, and no institution owned the opera's memory. Two northern works, two distinct deaths, one by institution and one by reputation, neither by demonstrable inferiority to the Italian settings that also died.

The comparison disciplines any temptation to treat the Italian story as universal. In Italy the tale was national property with a native novella lineage, and its settings competed inside one dense theatrical system where a single substitution could propagate from Bologna to Hamburg along company routes. In the German and French cases the settings stand more nearly alone in their national records, isolated peaks rather than a range, and their afterlives depended on individual champions: a scholarly conductor for Benda, a publisher's print run for Steibelt. The lesson generalises across the forgotten repertory of every subject, not this tale alone. Works survive inside systems, not inside judgments. Where a system exists, as in Italy, even a fragment can ride it for sixty-five years. Where only a document exists, as with Steibelt's printed score, survival means a library shelf and the hope of a curious editor. The catalogue's neat rows conceal that difference in kind, and the prose of this article has tried to restore it at every entry.

A final northern observation concerns translation. Gotter's libretto descends from Weisse's German rendering of the play, Ségur's from the French prose tradition, Romani's from the Italian novella line that bypasses the play altogether. The forgotten operas therefore preserve, in their texts, three distinct national Shakespeares of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, each filtered through a translation culture with its own decorums. Scholars of translation study those libretti as witnesses; scholars of opera study the scores; the shelf sits at the crossing of the two disciplines and is fully owned by neither, one more structural reason for its neglect. Interdisciplinary orphans are the repertory's most reliably forgotten children.


## A Note on Numbers: How Crowded Was the Field?

Any census of settings on this tale must be offered with its method attached. Counting only complete stage scores with documented premieres, the period from 1776 to 1867 yields the shelf catalogued in this article plus a penumbra of minor works: provincial settings, one-act reductions, and scores known from a libretto and a review rather than from surviving music. Extending the count across the following century adds the Zandonai opera of 1922 and a scatter of later settings in several countries, most as absent from the stage as their predecessors. Reference works that offer totals differ because their criteria differ: some count every title page, some only performed works, some only surviving scores. The disagreement is not a scandal; it is a reminder that the field's crowdedness, the premise of this article, rests not on any single total but on the density of the documented core. Six major settings in ninety years, three of them in Milan and Venice alone within a single generation, constitute a crowd by the standards of operatic production, where a literary subject that attracts two settings in a century counts as fortunate. The tale attracted a setting roughly every fifteen years across the period, each from a different national system or a different Italian generation. No other Shakespearean subject of the period, not even the Scottish play or the Moor of Venice, sustained such a rate of operatic return before the twentieth century. The crowd is the fact; the forgetting is the puzzle; the machinery described in this article is the solution on offer.


## The Romani Libretti Compared: One Poet, Two Tomb Scenes

No other pair of operas in the century allows so controlled a comparison as the two Romani settings, because nearly every variable except the composer and the date is held constant: the same poet, the same source in Scevola, the same city of the tale, the same voice types, the same theatre system. The differences therefore isolate what five years and a younger composer changed. The Vaccai text of 1825 distributes its interest across the opera's full span, with the feud given its civic due and the tomb scene reserved as the climax of a balanced structure. The Bellini text of 1830, reworked under pressure of a six-week commission, tightens the public material, hurries the middle acts, and arrives at the tomb with its energies conserved for the principals. Romani, revising his own work, behaved as his own first critic, and the revision's judgements are legible: keep the potion machinery, keep the heroine's resolve, cut the civic sprawl, and let the ending strike fast. Bellini's music then executed the revision's logic so completely that the earlier setting's virtues, its breadth, its patience, its great finale, came to look like the faults of an older dispensation.

Yet the comparison cuts the other way at exactly one point, and the point is the finale. Romani's revision compressed the tomb recognition; the market, through Malibran, expanded it again with the discarded material's music. The episode suggests that the revision had cut below the audience's requirement at the single moment where requirement was absolute. A tragedy of missed minutes still needs its farewell to be witnessed, and the nineteenth-century audience, whatever the tale's logic said, demanded to watch the lovers recognise each other before the poison finished its work. Vaccai's scene is that demand's monument. Its music is not superior to Bellini's in invention, bar for bar, and no claim of that kind is made here; its dimensions were superior to the occasion's appetite, and in the theatre, dimension is a quality. The controlled comparison thus ends in a draw with different winners on different counts, which is why the hybrid that history actually produced, Bellini's opera with Vaccai's ending, satisfied two generations completely: the evening gave them the younger man's score and the older man's farewell, and only a later age, armed with editions, insisted on choosing.

That insistence had its own justice, recorded in the adjudication offered earlier in this article, and the modern settlement, Bellini entire on stage and Vaccai honoured in the archive and the concert hall, is accepted here as the right one. Acceptance should not be confused with amnesia. A listener who knows that the stern Bellini close was once found wanting by the very audiences the composer wrote for hears that close more sharply, as an artistic risk that happened to fail commercially and succeed posthumously. Risks of that kind are the life of repertory, and the forgotten shelf, which preserves the evidence of the risk, earns its place in the education of anyone who would understand how the famous operas became famous.

## Listening to the Shelf: A Practical Path

A listener approaching the shelf for the first time faces the material problem that defines the subject: the famous tomb scene can be heard in recordings of the Bellini opera's alternative ending and in specialist collections, the complete Vaccai opera can be heard in its rare complete recording, and the Benda Singspiel can be heard in the performance under Hermann Breuer with the Gotha forces, while the Steibelt, Zingarelli, and Marchetti scores remain, for most listeners, paper music, present to the eye in libraries and absent to the ear. That gradient of availability is itself a lesson in how forgetting works in the age of recording: a work that has been recorded exists for the world, and a work that has not been recorded effectively does not, whatever its archival state. The shelf's modern map is therefore drawn by record labels and festival programmers as surely as its nineteenth-century map was drawn by impresarios, and the same caution applies to both maps. Neither is a judgement of worth. Both are records of decisions taken under constraint by institutions with budgets.

One caution governs every stage of that path. Recordings and editions present the shelf through modern performance habits, heavier voices, larger orchestras, and tempos shaped by later taste, so the listener hears each score through two filters at once, its own century's intentions and the present century's technique. Allowing for the double filter is part of the exercise: the Vaccai finale sung at modern weight still discloses its design, and the Benda ensembles played by a modern period band disclose theirs, yet the margin of difference should be kept in mind whenever a forgotten score is pronounced dull or slight on the evidence of a single modern hearing. Many a repertory staple would fail the same test under the same conditions.

The practical path runs from the known to the unknown. Begin with the tomb scene in its borrowed home, where its virtues explain themselves against the Bellini close in a single sitting. Proceed to the complete Vaccai opera, where the scene's arrival after the full arc of feud and separation restores the weight that transplantation dilutes. Then take the Benda Singspiel as the shelf's opposite pole, the rescue instead of the tomb, and hear how completely a change of ending re-educates every earlier scene. The paper scores reward the reader who follows with a libretto, particularly at the points where the settings diverge from the play: the expanded potion debate, the dawn duet, the civic choruses. Heard and read in that order, the shelf stops being a list of failures and becomes what it was in its own century, a conversation among theatres about how the tale should sound, carried on in the only medium that the participants fully trusted, which was music performed before an audience that had paid to weep.

One further exhibit belongs in any honest account of the shelf, precisely because its documentation is thinner than the rest and its fate therefore the most typical. Across the decades covered by the catalogue, provincial Italian and German houses mounted settings of the tale by local composers whose names survive in theatre almanacs and libretto imprints but whose music, in most cases, does not survive at all. Those works cannot be catalogued to the standard this article requires, and they are not smuggled into the table. Their collective existence still matters to the argument, because it shows the tale operating at the base of the theatrical pyramid as well as at its summit: a story that provincial Kapellmeisters and house composers reached for, season after season, as reliable material for local singers and local audiences. The famous settings crown a pyramid whose base was broad, busy, and almost entirely unrecorded. Forgetting, at that level, was total and unlamented, and no revival will disturb it. The shelf that scholarship can rebuild is the pyramid's upper storeys, and humility about the missing base should govern every generalisation built upon the survivors, including the generalisations in this article, which are offered for the documented core and for that core alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Nicola Vaccai and why does his name survive?

Nicola Vaccai (1790 to 1848) was an Italian composer of some seventeen operas and, in his later career, one of Europe's most respected teachers of singing. His name survives chiefly through two channels. The first is pedagogy: his Metodo pratico di canto italiano of 1832, a set of graduated exercises in Italian legato singing, remained in studio use for generations and carried his name into countries where none of his operas was ever staged. The second channel is a single scene: the tomb finale of his Giulietta e Romeo of 1825, which singers substituted into Bellini's rival opera for sixty-five years. The composer of a once-popular complete opera is therefore remembered as a teacher and as the author of a fragment, a division of fame that the repertory system imposed and that no quality in the complete score could prevent.

### Q: When and where was Vaccai's Giulietta e Romeo first performed?

The opera was first performed at the Teatro alla Canobbiana in Milan on 31 October 1825. The Canobbiana stood second among Milanese houses only to La Scala, and a success there carried a work across the Italian theatre network. The libretto was by Felice Romani, drawn from Luigi Scevola's tragedy of 1818 and ultimately from Luigi da Porto's novella. The premiere cast placed Joséphine de Méric as Giulietta and Isabella Fabbrica, a contralto performing in travesty, as Romeo. The production began a genuine international life for the score: Barcelona heard the opera in May 1827, Paris in September of the same year, Lisbon in 1828, London in April 1832, and Mexico in 1841, a record that establishes the work as a success of its decade rather than a failure rescued by a single famous scene.

### Q: What is the Vaccai tomb scene and why is it famous?

The tomb scene is the closing sequence of Vaccai's Giulietta e Romeo, built around Romeo's romanza at the opened tomb, beginning "Ah! se tu dormi, svegliati", followed by Giulietta's awakening and the lovers' final duet. Its fame rests on transplantation: from 1832 onward, singers performing Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi substituted the Vaccai scene for Bellini's own ending, and the borrowed finale became the customary close of the Bellini opera in Italy for the rest of the century. The scene is famous, in other words, less as part of its parent opera than as the most durable example of singer-driven substitution in the bel canto repertory, a fragment that outlived its whole work by decades and was heard by generations of listeners who believed they were hearing Bellini complete.

### Q: Who first substituted the Vaccai ending into Bellini's opera?

The substitution was made by the singer Maria Malibran, who performed Romeo in Bellini's opera at Bologna on 27 October 1832 and caused the Vaccai tomb scene to be given in place of the Bellini ending. Malibran, the most commanding singer of her generation, possessed the contractual and artistic authority to dictate the shape of an evening, and the borrowed scene served her art directly: it enlarged the Romeo part at the moment of greatest emotional return, exchanging Bellini's compressed close for an extended lament and a full farewell duet. Her initiative became custom with remarkable speed. Within a few seasons the hybrid ending was the expected form of the opera in Italian houses, adopted by singers who had never met its originator and perpetuated by printed editions that offered the Vaccai finale as an authorised alternative.

### Q: Why did the same librettist stand behind both rival operas?

Felice Romani wrote the libretto for Vaccai's Giulietta e Romeo in 1825, from Luigi Scevola's tragedy of 1818, and five years later reworked his own text for Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi at La Fenice. The reuse was practical rather than treacherous: Romani was the leading Italian librettist of the period, theatres engaged him as a matter of course, and Bellini's Venice commission allowed only a month and a half for composition, which made an existing, stage-tested text a necessity. The shared libretto explains the substitution's feasibility, since the two tomb scenes occupy identical dramatic ground, and it sharpens the episode's irony. The older opera was displaced by a younger setting of substantially its own words, and then took its revenge by supplying the ending that audiences of the younger opera preferred for sixty-five years.

### Q: What was the earliest opera on the Verona tale?

The earliest complete operatic treatment of the tale with a documented premiere is Georg Benda's Romeo und Julie, a Singspiel in three acts first performed at the Ekhof Theatre in Gotha on 25 September 1776. Its libretto, by Friedrich Wilhelm Gotter, descends from Christian Felix Weisse's German translation of the play. The Gotha work concentrates the action on the heroine, removes the comic figures and most of the supporting cast, observes the unities of time and place, and ends with the lovers alive, in accordance with the period's operatic convention of the happy ending. The score survives complete, has been recorded, and received a British stage premiere from Bampton Classical Opera in 2007. Earlier isolated musical treatments of episodes may exist in the record of court entertainments, but Benda's Singspiel stands as the shelf's firm starting point.

### Q: How does Steibelt's French setting differ from the Italian ones?

Daniel Steibelt's Roméo et Juliette, to a libretto by Joseph-Alexandre de Ségur, was first performed at the Théâtre Feydeau in Paris on 9 October 1793. The formal difference is structural: the French work is an opera in three acts with spoken dialogue between its musical numbers, the opéra comique framework, whereas the Italian settings proceed in continuous recitative and closed numbers within the dramma per musica tradition. The dramatic difference is equally sharp. Steibelt restored the tragic ending that Benda's Gotha version had reversed seventeen years earlier, returning the lovers to the tomb from which the German Singspiel had rescued them. The score, published by the composer in Paris in the year of the premiere, circulated in print while the work's stage life faded, an inversion of the Vaccai case, in which a scene circulated while its score stayed home.

### Q: What place does Zingarelli's setting hold on the shelf?

Niccolò Antonio Zingarelli's Giulietta e Romeo, to a libretto by Giuseppe Maria Foppa, was first performed at La Scala on 30 January 1796 and held the Italian repertory well into the nineteenth century. Its place on the shelf is that of the dominant setting before Vaccai: a work in the opera seria inheritance whose Romeo became a favourite vehicle for leading travesti singers, among them Maria Malibran in the years around 1830. That casting connection gives the Zingarelli opera a unique thread into the later history, since the same singer who sustained its currency also imposed the Vaccai finale on the Bellini opera. The setting faded as taste hardened around Rossini and the bel canto generation; its idiom came to be heard as belonging to a closed century, and its disappearance illustrates stylistic succession in its purest form, a success retired by fashion rather than by failure.

### Q: What is Marchetti's Romeo e Giulietta and why did it vanish?

Filippo Marchetti's Romeo e Giulietta, to a libretto by Marco Marcello, was first performed at Trieste on 25 October 1865 and was revised for Milanese performance in the following years. The opera belonged to the earnest Italian manner of the Risorgimento generation, written under the long shadow of Verdi, with weighty feud choruses and a tomb scene that faced comparison with two famous predecessors. Its vanishing illustrates the closing of national slots. By 1865 the tale's Italian place in the international repertory had belonged to Bellini for thirty-five years, and the French place was about to be sealed by Gounod. A respected national work, admired at home for a generation, found no chair free abroad, and its absence from the international repertory records that arithmetic rather than any verdict delivered by the audiences who heard it.

### Q: Why did Italian houses accept a rival composer's ending for so long?

Italian houses accepted the Vaccai ending because the theatrical system of the period treated a score as working material rather than as an inviolable text. Singers held contractual prerogatives over the shape of their evenings, impresarios depended on star satisfaction for their seasons, and audiences judged the performance in the theatre rather than the title page. The substitution also solved a felt problem: Bellini's ending is compressed, and the Vaccai scene supplied the extended lament and farewell duet that the period's audiences expected at a tragedy's close. Once publishers issued the borrowed finale as an authorised alternative, the practice became infrastructure, independent of any single singer's memory. Sixty-five years of acceptance therefore require no conspiracy and no lapse of taste to explain; they require only the ordinary operation of a system whose values differed from the values of the editorial age that succeeded it.

### Q: How long did the substitution practice last?

The documented substitution practice lasted from 27 October 1832, when Maria Malibran introduced the Vaccai tomb scene into Bellini's opera at Bologna, until 8 September 1897, when a performance at Hamburg gave the last recorded instance of the borrowed ending. The span is sixty-five years. The Hamburg date should be read as the end of the paper trail rather than as a certified final performance anywhere: German theatrical documentation of the period was systematic, and smaller houses elsewhere may have continued the custom without leaving records that survive. Even taken as a minimum, the span is extraordinary. It covers the entire mature career of the bel canto tradition, the rise of Verdi, and the arrival of the editorial culture that finally displaced the singer's prerogative and restored Bellini's own close as the expected text.

### Q: Is the complete Vaccai opera ever performed?

The complete Vaccai opera is performed rarely, chiefly in festival and specialist contexts devoted to bel canto rarities, and it circulates among listeners through a complete recording rather than through regular stage life. The obstacles are practical as much as artistic. The score requires editorial preparation from surviving sources, the travesti Romeo must be cast for a mezzo-soprano voice trained in the sustained cantabile that the finale demands, and the marketing must introduce an unknown name attached to a story that the public already knows through two famous settings. Within those constraints, hearings of the whole work consistently support the judgement that its tomb scene is its summit and that its earlier acts, while well made, lack the concentration of the close. The opera's rarity is a fact about repertory economics, not a sentence passed on its quality by any court whose verdict survives.

### Q: What does the happy ending of the Benda Singspiel reveal?

The happy ending of Benda and Gotter's Romeo und Julie of 1776 reveals that the tale's tragic close was negotiable property for the eighteenth-century stage. Gotter rescued the lovers in accordance with the operatic convention of the period, which expected catastrophe to relent, and the Gotha audience received the feud, the potion, and the tomb machinery with the deaths dismantled at the final moment. The rescue also reflects the libretto's concentration on the heroine: Julie, established as the strongest figure in the piece, earns survival as the drama's reward for constancy. Later centuries treated the double death as the tale's sacred core and the rescue as a curiosity, yet the spoken stage of the same era made comparable adjustments to the play. The Singspiel's ending is therefore evidence about its century's ethics of adaptation, in which a classic text was material for the evening's needs rather than an object of fidelity.

### Q: Why did repertory slots close against later settings?

Repertory slots closed because the international opera system hardened, across the later nineteenth century, into a small canon in which each famous literary property carried one accredited setting per national tradition. The Verona tale's Italian slot was filled by Bellini from 1830 and its French slot by Gounod from 1867, while the concert hall absorbed the story through orchestral treatments that required no staging at all. A new setting thereafter competed not against a vacancy but against an incumbent with publishers, performing materials, star associations, and audience memory all in place. Marchetti in 1865 and Zandonai in 1922 both faced that arithmetic in Italy. The closing of slots is a property of institutions rather than of scores: it explains why admiration at home, which both later settings earned, could coexist with complete absence abroad, and why the forgotten shelf grew longest exactly where the tale was loved most.

### Q: How did Vaccai's singing method shape his fame?

Vaccai's Metodo pratico di canto italiano, published in 1832, shaped his fame by outliving his operas entirely. The method's short, graduated exercises, each addressing a single technical problem of Italian legato singing, were translated, transposed for every voice type, and assigned by teachers for generations, so that the composer's name became a fixture of the studio while his stage works left the theatre. The afterlife is double-edged. It preserved the man and buried the composer, filing him in reference works as a pedagogue who had also written operas. It also preserves his aesthetic in practical form, since the method's prized virtues, the long line, the even scale, and the controlled swelling of a single tone, are precisely the virtues that his tomb scene demands of its Romeo. The teacher's text and the opera's finale are one art at two scales.

### Q: What do scholars disagree about concerning the substitution?

Scholarship divides over whether the substituted Vaccai finale was a legitimate creation of the performance tradition or a commercial deformation of Bellini's work. The performance-practice position, associated with Philip Gossett's studies of nineteenth-century opera, holds that singer-driven alteration was the period's normal creative order and that a composite ending ratified by sixty-five years of audiences has a genuine claim to historical authorship. The editorial position, embodied in modern critical editions of the Bellini score and voiced in surveys such as Charles Osborne's account of the bel canto operas, treats the borrowed scene as a mellifluous intrusion that softened a deliberately stern close. The adjudicated view taken in the accompanying article splits the question: the practice side wins on history, since the substitution was ordinary mechanics rather than vandalism, while the editorial side wins on the ending's meaning, since Bellini's haste is the more faithful reading of a tale built on missed minutes.

### Q: Why did the travesti Romeo matter to these operas?

The travesti Romeo, a hero sung by a contralto or mezzo-soprano woman, mattered because the convention shaped the Italian settings' vocal design at every level. Two high voices in the love duets produce the intertwining sonority that Italian audiences prized, and the finale of an evening built around a star in travesty naturally gravitates toward that singer's display, which is one reason the tomb scene, with its extended lament for the Romeo, became the tale's operatic centre of gravity in Italy. The convention's decline therefore damaged the Italian shelf structurally: as tenors reclaimed the part in the French manner, the Italian scores lost their casting logic, and a modern house reviving them must choose between honouring the original voice types and transposing for tenor at the cost of the written sonority. Casting difficulty is an unglamorous but decisive cause of forgetting.

### Q: What evidence survives for the forgotten settings?

The evidence survives in three strata. Libretti printed for first nights preserve cast lists, dedications, and plot summaries, and their survival in library collections allows the Vaccai and Bellini tomb scenes, both by Romani, to be compared word by word. Printed music forms the second stratum, from Steibelt's self-published score of 1793 to the arrangements through which the Vaccai romanza circulated and the alternative-ending editions that fossilised the substitution. The press forms the third: reviews record what audiences actually heard, including the instructive fact that reviewers of the hybrid Bellini performances often praised the ending without remarking its authorship. Modern catalogues and theatre archives supply the premiere data that anchor the field, while the Hamburg date of 1897, which closes the substitution's record, survives through systematic German documentation. Gaps in the trail are reported as gaps, since silence in an archive describes record-keeping as often as it describes stages.

### Q: How does the forgotten shelf change the story of the tale in music?

The forgotten shelf changes the story by replacing a tale of two geniuses with a record of continuous traffic. The standard account imagines the Verona story waiting for Bellini and Gounod to notice it; the documented record shows a German Singspiel in 1776, a Paris opera in 1793, settings at La Scala in 1796 and the Canobbiana in 1825, the Bellini score in 1830, a Trieste setting in 1865, and the French survivor in 1867, a new operatic treatment roughly every fifteen years across the period. Each setting records what its decade heard in the tale, from heroine-centred rescue to civic tragedy to the privatised tomb duet. The survivors keep their stature under the comparison, yet their survival reads as contingency rather than inevitability, and that change of meaning is the shelf's chief gift: the canon acquires a history, and the forgotten works acquire the dignity of evidence.

### Q: What is the verdict on the forgotten operas?

The verdict is that the forgotten operas vanished through the ordinary machinery of the repertory system rather than through any collective failure of quality. Style succeeded style and retired the Zingarelli setting; the star system extracted the Vaccai finale and orphaned its parent; unpublished or fragile materials made revival expensive; national slots closed against Marchetti and later against Zandonai; and revival, when it came, followed fashion as obediently as the original forgetting had. Within that machinery, the shelf's strongest scene survived by desertion, its most original conception survived as a scholarly exhibit, and its most complete early success vanished almost entire. Survival and merit correlate too weakly across the catalogue for the modern repertory to be read as history's judgement. The forgotten operas are best understood as a residue of business decisions taken nightly for a century, and their study restores to the tale's musical history the crowd that the cliché of two famous settings conceals.
