Logistics is usually discussed as a matter of efficiency, as though the movement of goods were a technical problem that better planning might one day solve outright. A Marxist analysis begins somewhere else entirely. It asks what kind of activity the movement of goods actually is, and it answers that question by dividing the work gathered under the single word logistics into kinds that do different things to value. Some of that work produces value in the strict sense the tradition gives the term. Some of it produces nothing of the kind and is paid for out of value produced elsewhere. The division is not a pedantic refinement. It decides how the industry is understood, where its costs truly fall, and what sort of power the people who perform the work can plausibly claim.

![Crates, pallets and a freight yard arranged for an essay on logistics and value, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-112.webp)

The headline of the analysis can be stated before any of its detail. Transport, on Marx's own account, is productive labour, because the thing it sells is a change of place, and a change of place is exactly what the buyer of transport wants to obtain. Storage, by contrast, together with the labour of buying, selling, and keeping accounts, belongs to the costs of circulation, which add nothing to the value of the goods they handle and are met from surplus value created in production. An industry that contains both kinds of activity under one roof and one balance sheet therefore sits on both sides of the productive and unproductive distinction at once. Any sentence that pronounces on logistics as a whole, declaring it productive or dismissing it as mere circulation, has already gone wrong, and the correction of that error is the foundation on which everything else in this article is built. Readers who want the underlying distinction itself, between labour that produces surplus value and labour that does not, will find it set out in the [surplus value complete guide](/2010/11/01/surplus-value-complete-guide/), to which this article links rather than returning over ground already covered there.

## Logistics and the question the distinction asks

The productive and unproductive distinction is frequently misheard as a judgement about usefulness, and clearing away that mishearing is the first task. Labour can be indispensable and still be unproductive in the technical sense, just as labour can be trivial in its object and still be productive. A night watchman guarding a warehouse performs work without which the goods inside might vanish, yet the watching adds nothing to the value of a single crate. A singer employed by an impresario who sells tickets performs work whose object evaporates in the hearing, yet the singing is productive because it is exchanged against capital and returns more value than it costs. The distinction tracks a relation, not a dignity. It asks whether labour is set to work by capital in a process whose product carries new value, surplus value included, or whether labour is instead occupied with the changes of form, of ownership, of location in the ledger, through which already produced value is realised, guarded, transferred, or counted.

Once the question is posed in that relational way, the peculiar position of logistics comes into view. Moving goods looks, at a glance, like the purest case of circulation, since nothing in the crate changes while it travels. The timber that enters a wagon is the timber that leaves it, chemically and physically unaltered, and an observer who defines production by visible transformation will classify the journey as an interval between real processes, a cost to be minimised and nothing more. Marx's analysis refuses that observer's definition. Production, in the second volume of Capital, is the creation of a use-value for a purchaser, and there are use-values that consist in a change of state rather than in a new object. A good in the wrong place is, for the person who needs it, not yet the good at all. The coat in a distant warehouse does not warm anyone. The machine part sitting far from the stalled line it would repair performs no function. Place is a property of a use-value as surely as shape or weight, and altering place is therefore a productive act whenever the alteration is what is bought.

That single insight reorganises the whole field. It means the analyst cannot classify firms, and cannot classify industries, and cannot even classify whole occupations. The unit of classification is the activity, the concrete stretch of labour considered by what it does to the value of the thing it touches. A single enterprise may employ drivers whose labour produces value, clerks whose labour realises it, guards whose labour protects it, and sales staff whose labour transfers its ownership, all within one fence and one payroll. The industry's aggregate accounts blend these into a single margin, which is precisely why aggregate accounts cannot settle the theoretical question. The question has to be put to each activity in turn, and the answers assembled into something like a ledger of kinds. That ledger is what this article calls the productive and unproductive table, and constructing it carefully is the article's central exercise.

### Does transport produce value in Marxist theory?

yes. Marx argues in the second volume that the transport industry is productive because what it sells is the change of location itself, which is the use-value the purchaser wants. Storage and the expenses of purchase and sale are treated as deductions from surplus value instead.

The answer deserves its emphasis, because the opposite assumption is widespread even among readers sympathetic to the tradition. Survey treatments of circulation often absorb transport into the sphere of exchange, treating the journey as part of the metamorphosis of commodities rather than as production continued by other means. Marx's text does not permit that absorption. The treatment in the second volume places the transport industry among the branches of production, with its own peculiarity stated plainly: its product is consumed in the very act of being produced, since a journey cannot be stored and sold later, yet the product is a genuine use-value for all that. The purchaser of freight capacity buys displacement. When the displacement is accomplished, the purchaser has received the thing paid for, in the same sense in which the purchaser of a coat receives the coat. The labour expended in accomplishing it stands in the same relation to capital as the labour of the tailor, and the value it adds is governed by the same determinations, the wear of vehicles and fuel entering as transferred value and the living labour of crews entering as new value, part replacing wages and part constituting surplus.

Holding that point firmly also disciplines the opposite exaggeration. Because transport is productive, it does not follow that everything travelling under the flag of logistics shares the status. The exception is an exception precisely because the surrounding territory is circulation. The same enterprise that moves a consignment also holds it, insures it, invoices for it, advertises the service of moving it, and negotiates the contract under which the moving occurs. Each of those surrounding activities has to be tried separately against the criterion, and most of them fail it. The result is neither a celebration of the industry as a hidden engine of value nor a dismissal of it as parasitic overhead. The result is a rule for proceeding, activity by activity, and a warning that the industry's self-description, which naturally presents every cost as essential service, is no guide to the classification at all.

## The transport exception in the second volume

The transport exception is the namable claim at the centre of this article, and it repays slow statement. In the second volume of Capital, in the discussion of the costs of circulation, Marx pauses over an industry that appears to belong wholly to circulation and argues that it does not. The useful effect produced by the transport industry, he observes, is the change of place of persons and things, and that change of place is the use-value which the industry sells. The formulation matters because it locates the product neither in the vehicle nor in the cargo but in a relation between the cargo and the world: the cargo's position. Position is invisible in the object and decisive for its use, which is why the exception surprises readers who expect production to leave a mark on the thing produced. A journey leaves no mark on the crate. It leaves the crate somewhere else, and somewhere else is the entire product.

From that location of the product, the rest of the analysis unfolds by ordinary steps. The capital advanced in transport divides as capital elsewhere divides. One portion purchases means of production, vehicles, fuel, track, warehousing at the point of transfer, and the value of that portion reappears in the price of the service, transferred rather than created, diminished by wear exactly as the value of a loom reappears piecemeal in cloth. Another portion purchases labour power, and the labour set in motion adds value beyond its own price whenever the working day extends past the hours needed to reproduce the wage. The surplus so created belongs to the same category as surplus created in a mill. There is nothing honorary or metaphorical about calling the driver's labour productive. It satisfies the definition in full, and denying it the title while granting the title to the mill hand requires a definition of production based on the physical transformation of objects, a definition Marx's own treatment of services had already set aside.

The exception also clarifies what productivity is not, by contrast with its neighbours in the same chapter. Immediately around the transport discussion, the second volume handles the work of purchase and sale, the keeping of books, and the formation of hoards, and assigns all of them to the costs of circulation. The clerk who records a transaction performs labour that is necessary, skilled, and exhausting, and the labour creates no value, because the transaction it records is a change in value's shape, from commodity into money or money into commodity, and changes of shape, however laborious, are not production. The juxtaposition is the lesson. Within a few pages, labour that moves a crate across a continent is productive and labour that moves its title across a ledger is not, and the difference lies not in effort, intelligence, social esteem, or even necessity, but in whether the labour's effect is a use-value delivered to a buyer or a metamorphosis undergone by value already in existence.

### Why does the location of a good count as part of its use-value?

because place governs whether a thing can be used at all. Grain held far from any mill feeds nobody, and a component distant from the line awaiting it performs no function. Altering where a thing stands therefore alters what it can do, which is why a purchaser of transport buys a genuine effect and not a courtesy.

The philosophical weight of the exception should not be understated. It demonstrates that the category of production in this tradition is functional rather than material. What counts is whether an activity completes a use-value for exchange, not whether it resembles the stereotyped image of making, with raw stuff at one end and a finished object at the other. Whole regions of the economy that a material image would exile to circulation return, under the functional criterion, to production: any service whose effect is itself the commodity sold, consumed in the moment of its performance yet purchased as a definite useful result. Transport is the case Marx works through in detail because it arises inside the analysis of circulation and threatens to blur the boundary of the whole volume. If the boundary holds even here, with an industry whose workplace is the road between other workplaces, it holds with some confidence elsewhere.

There is a further subtlety in the treatment that careful readers notice. Marx observes that the value added by transport is governed by the same laws as value added elsewhere, which means it is governed, in the final analysis, by the labour time socially necessary for the displacement. A route traversed slowly, with redundant handling and idle vehicles, does not thereby produce more value than the same route traversed efficiently, any more than a slow weaver produces more valuable cloth. The market enforces a standard of necessary labour for movement as for making, and enterprises whose costs exceed the standard surrender the difference from their margin. This point will matter later, when the article turns to the competitive dynamics of chains, because it explains why the pressure toward tightly coupled, inventory-lean movement is not a fashion in management but a compulsion written into the value relation itself. Capital that lingers in transit is capital not yet realised, and capital that moves goods at above the necessary cost is capital donating its surplus to rivals. The exception, properly understood, contains the seed of the entire modern organisation of movement, which is a considerable yield from a few pages of a posthumous volume.

## Storage, keeping, and the boundary inside the warehouse

Storage is where the exception meets its limit, and the limit is drawn with a fineness that the secondary literature often flattens. The second volume distinguishes, within the act of holding goods, between a stock that functions as a condition of production or of continuous sale, and a stock that exists because sale has failed or because commodities are being withheld. The distinction is not between warehouses but within the same warehouse, between portions of the same pile, considered by the role each portion plays. A reserve of components held so that a production line need never pause for want of an input belongs, in the analysis, to the production process itself, since the continuity it purchases is a condition of producing at all, and the labour of maintaining that reserve shares, to that extent, in the character of productive labour. A stockpile accumulated because the market will not take the goods at the price asked is a different matter. It is circulation arrested, value frozen in commodity form, and every hour of guarding, insuring, and warehousing it is a deduction from the surplus the frozen value will yield if it is ever realised at all.

This internal boundary is the most practically useful element of the whole analysis for anyone studying a distribution operation, because distribution buildings are exactly the places where the two characters of stock coexist indistinguishably to the eye. The same worker, in the same shift, may replenish a flow-through bay whose contents turn over continuously and then move to a slow bay where seasonal goods wait out their season. The first activity leans toward production, as an auxiliary of the transport and supply function. The second is a cost of circulation in nearly pure form. An analysis that assigns the building a single character, or the occupation a single status, erases the very distinction the theory exists to preserve. The correct procedure is proportional and functional: ask of each stream within the building what would happen to production and to sale if the stream ceased, and classify by the answer.

### Is storage ever productive in this analysis?

in one bounded case only, where the stock held is a genuine condition of an uninterrupted process, so holding it functions as part of producing, or of keeping sale continuous. Storage that merely waits, for a buyer, a season, or a better price, creates nothing, and its costs fall on surplus value as a growing charge.

The treatment of buying and selling completes the map of the boundary. Commercial labour, the work of negotiating, invoicing, corresponding, and accounting, is unproductive in the strict sense while being, under developed conditions of exchange, utterly indispensable. Marx's handling of this point is worth emulating for its refusal of moralism. The commercial worker is exploited, in the analysis, in a manner specific to unproductive labour: the worker's unpaid hours do not create surplus value but reduce the share of surplus value that the employer must surrender to the costs of realisation, which is why extending the commercial working day enriches the merchant without adding a particle of value to the social product. The warehouse clerk, the sales representative, the bookkeeper of a freight enterprise, all stand in this relation. Their labour is real, their exploitation is real, and their product, in the value sense, is nil. Holding those three propositions together, without letting any one of them bully the others out of the analysis, is the discipline the distinction demands.

It follows that the growth of logistics employment, considered by itself, says nothing whatsoever about the growth of value production, since the growth may be concentrated on either side of the line. An expansion of driving and of the movement function enlarges the sphere in which surplus value is produced. An expansion of the apparatus of selling, recording, and guarding enlarges the deductions from a surplus produced elsewhere, and can perfectly well accompany stagnation or decline in the productive core. Commentators who treat the swelling of the sector as straightforward evidence of economic dynamism, and commentators who treat it as straightforward evidence of parasitism, are making the same mistake from opposite directions. Both are reading a total where the theory instructs them to read a composition.

## The productive and unproductive table

The productive and unproductive table is this article's findable artifact: the classification of logistics activities by their relation to value, set out so that a reader can apply it to any operation without first mastering the whole theory. Its rows are the activities that the industry itself distinguishes in its own organisation of work. Its columns record whether the activity adds value, the reasoning by which that verdict is reached, and what follows for the analysis of an enterprise performing the activity. The table is a tool rather than a decoration. Each row compresses an argument made at length in the surrounding sections, and a reader who disputes a row should dispute the argument, since the table asserts nothing the prose has not earned.

| Activity | Adds value | Reasoning | What follows for the firm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transport | Yes | Displacement itself is what the buyer purchases, so the labour completes a product the buyer wants | Its crews stand in the same value relation as production workers, and its margins contain surplus value of its own making |
| Storage | Generally no | Holding that merely waits for sale, season, or price is circulation arrested, and its costs are met from surplus value | Warehousing charges are deductions to be minimised against a surplus produced elsewhere |
| Handling | Divided | Handling that loads, unloads, and transfers between vehicles is an auxiliary of movement; handling that counts, checks, and records is circulation work | The same dock can contain both kinds of labour in a single shift, and headcount alone settles nothing |
| Sorting | Divided | Sorting that directs goods toward their destinations continues the movement function; sorting for inspection, display, or sale prepares a change of ownership | Classification follows the purpose of the sort, not the gestures of the sorter |
| Sales | No | Negotiating and concluding a purchase changes the form of value from commodity to money without altering the commodity | Sales labour reduces the employer's realisation costs rather than adding to the product |
| Administration | No | Accounting, invoicing, and correspondence record and arrange metamorphoses of value that have occurred or are awaited | Administrative expansion enlarges deductions even when it is skilful, necessary, and well paid |

Reading the table well requires resisting two temptations. The first is to treat its verdicts as verdicts on the worth of the people performing the activities, a slide the theory explicitly forbids, since unproductive labour in this vocabulary can be highly skilled, socially essential, and bitterly exploited, while productive labour can be directed to objects of no social merit whatever. The second temptation is to treat the verdicts as properties of job titles. They are properties of functions. A person employed as a driver who spends a season selling the enterprise's services has crossed the line without changing desks or uniforms, and a clerk reassigned to dispatch, directing vehicles toward destinations, has crossed it in the other direction. The table classifies stretches of labour, not the biographies of labourers, and applying it to an actual operation means following the work through a day and marking where each stretch falls.

The divided rows carry the table's most instructive content. Handling and sorting are the activities in which the industry's two natures are physically interleaved, often within arm's reach of one another. Loading a vehicle is unintelligible except as part of the movement that the vehicle will perform; the labour of the loader is a moment of the transport labour process, divided among hands for efficiency. Counting the load, checking it against a manifest, and recording discrepancies makes sense only as part of the transaction the movement serves; it belongs to the paperwork of ownership. That one worker frequently does both, in alternation, is an accident of staffing. The theory's instruction is to separate in analysis what the shift combines in practice, and to refuse the lazy inference from a mixed job to a mixed verdict on either component.

### How should the table be applied to an operation that mixes activities?

by following the work rather than the job titles, separating each stretch of labour by the function it performs, and assigning it to movement, to holding, or to the paperwork of ownership as its purpose dictates. A mixed shift yields a mixed classification in proportions. The enterprise as a whole receives no single verdict.

What follows from the table, at the level of the enterprise, is a particular way of reading its accounts. The margin of a transport enterprise is a composite: surplus value produced by its movement labour, minus the circulation costs its own apparatus imposes, plus or minus whatever it gains or loses in competition with rivals whose necessary labour for the same displacement is lower or higher. Two enterprises with identical margins may have arrived there by opposite routes, one producing much and spending much on its commercial apparatus, the other producing modestly and spending little. The table gives the analyst the questions that distinguish those cases, which aggregate figures cannot. It also explains a standing puzzle of the industry, the coexistence of relentless pressure on driving labour with relentless expansion of coordination staff. The first pressure defends the surplus the enterprise produces. The second growth enlarges the deductions from it. Both movements are rational for the individual firm and contradictory for the whole, which is a pattern readers of this series will recognise as characteristic rather than exceptional.

## Circulation costs and the pressure they exert

Circulation costs occupy a peculiar place in the theory: they create nothing, yet capital cannot escape incurring them, and the manner of their incurring shapes the organisation of production itself. The second volume's treatment divides them into the labour of buying and selling, bookkeeping, and the costs bound up with holding stock, and then observes that because these costs are deductions, every enterprise labours under a permanent incentive to shift them, shrink them, or socialise them. The history of commercial organisation can be read as the record of those three strategies succeeding one another. Costs are shifted when a seller makes the buyer perform the labour of collection or inspection. Costs are shrunk when bookkeeping is mechanised and a single ledger replaces an army of clerks. Costs are socialised when the state supplies the roads, the dredged channels, and the standardised measures without which private movement would pay far more for far less.

The deduction character of circulation costs also explains why their growth is compatible with their cheapening per unit. As the scale of production grows, the mass of transactions grows with it, and the absolute sum surrendered to realisation can swell even while the cost of each transaction falls. An economy can therefore exhibit, simultaneously, an apparatus of circulation that is historically efficient and a burden of circulation that is historically heavy, and both observations can be true of the same figures read in different ways. Polemics that seize on one of the two readings and suppress the other are not analysis. The theory's contribution is to hold the two together by naming the relation between them: efficiency in circulation economises on a deduction, and the deduction grows with the product from which it is deducted, so the two trends are expressions of one development rather than rivals for the truth.

### What did Marx say about circulation costs in the second volume?

that they are the costs of buying and selling, of bookkeeping, and of holding stock, incurred because commodities must change their form to be realised, and that they add nothing to the worth of the goods whose metamorphoses they serve. They are paid from surplus value already created in production, and capital accordingly treats them as a deduction to be pressed down.

There is a corollary about time that the analysis of logistics cannot do without. Value in the commodity form is unrealised value, and the interval between production and sale is an interval during which capital is locked in a shape that yields nothing. Circulation time therefore enters the calculations of every enterprise as a pure loss to be compressed, distinct from the costs of the labour that circulation employs. The compression of circulation time is the deep driver behind the reorganisation of movement in the modern economy: the preference for speed over slack, for flow over stock, for schedules knitted so tightly that a good is sold, in effect, before it has finished travelling. When the article later examines tightly coupled chains and the leverage they create, the reader should remember that the coupling is not an aesthetic preference of managers. It is the form taken by capital's standing war against its own circulation time, waged with the deduction logic of the second volume as its charter.

The war has a limit, and the limit is instructive. Circulation time can be compressed toward the time physically required for movement and no further, and as it approaches that floor, the remaining costs stand revealed as the irreducible price of realising value under a system in which production and consumption are separated by ownership, distance, and time. Attempts to pass below the floor do not abolish the costs but displace them, onto suppliers made to hold the stock, onto workers made to wait unpaid at the edges of shifts, onto public infrastructure made to absorb the wear. Tracing where displaced circulation costs come to rest is one of the most revealing exercises the framework affords, because the resting place is rarely the balance sheet that claimed the saving.

## Supply chains as objects of analysis

A supply chain, stripped of the managerial aura the phrase has acquired, is a sequence of production and circulation processes through which a commodity passes on its way from raw material to final purchaser, with ownership changing hands at some of the joints and not at others. The Marxist analysis approaches the chain with a question the managerial literature does not ask. The managerial question is how the chain is coordinated: who decides what is produced where, who sets the standards, who bears the risks. The Marxist question is what happens to value along the chain: where it is produced, where it is transferred without being produced, and where it is captured by parties whose contribution was the ownership of a position rather than the performance of labour. The two questions are not rivals, and a complete account needs both, but they are different questions, and much confusion in the literature comes from answering the first while believing one has answered the second.

The chain is a natural object for this analysis precisely because the productive and unproductive distinction, and the distinction between producing value and capturing it, operate at every joint. A component crosses a border and its recorded price changes. A design is supplied from one place and the assembled article sold from another, with the margin settling where the accounts are kept rather than where the hands were busy. Transport between the stages is productive, storage at the stages is largely a deduction, and the contractual apparatus that binds the stages together is circulation labour on a grand scale. To describe a chain as efficient or as exploitative without decomposing it in this way is to gesture at a total. The decomposition is laborious, and it is also the only route by which the chain yields explanations rather than impressions.

There is a historical dimension that the framework keeps in view. Chains lengthen when the gains from dividing production across places exceed the circulation costs of joining the places together, and they shorten when the balance reverses. The transport exception is implicated in that balance at every point, because cheapening movement cheapens the joining, and the cheapening of movement is itself governed by the socially necessary labour for displacement, pressed downward by competition among carriers. The geography of production is therefore not a backdrop to the value analysis but one of its products. Where things are made is decided, in part, by what it costs in labour to undo the consequences of making them there, and the unmaking of distance is a productive industry with its own surplus, its own deductions, and its own workforce standing at the joints.

### Why does the analysis decompose a chain instead of judging it whole?

because every joint performs a different operation: value is produced at one stage, transferred at another, captured at a third. A verdict on the whole conceals precisely the facts that explain the chain. Decomposition assigns each stage its character and lets the analyst say where the margin originates, which is what judgements about chains are usually trying and failing to ask.

The chain perspective also corrects a habit of thought inherited from national accounting, in which production is imagined as occurring inside borders and trade as occurring between them, with movement as a service rendered to both from outside. In a developed chain the movement is inside the production process, as much a phase of making the final article as any machining performed along the way, since the article is not the article, as a use-value, until it is where its purchaser is. The statistician's boundary between manufacturing and services cuts across the labour process at a place the labour process does not recognise. Marxist analysis, following the function rather than the statistical convention, treats the chain as one extended process of production interleaved with acts of circulation, and insists on marking which is which at each step rather than inheriting the marks from a classification drawn for other purposes.

## The chain literatures and how they differ

Three overlapping bodies of work dominate the scholarly analysis of cross-border production, and a reader entering the field needs their differences stated cleanly, since the literatures cite one another constantly while asking different questions. The first, associated above all with Gary Gereffi and developed in his Global Value Chains and Development, published in 2018, asks how chains are governed. Its central concept is governance: the capacity of a lead firm to set the terms on which other firms participate, specifying what is made, to what standard, at what price, and with what division of the gains. The framework distinguishes forms of governance by the complexity of the transactions involved, the extent to which the relevant knowledge can be codified, and the capabilities of the supply base, and it has been used extensively to advise governments on how firms in their territories might upgrade within chains, moving from assembly toward design and branding, where the margins are thicker.

The second body of work, the global production networks approach set out by Jeffrey Henderson, Peter Dicken, Martin Hess, Neil Coe, and Henry Wai-Chung Yeung in their 2002 article on global production networks and the analysis of economic development, widens the lens. Where the governance literature centres the chain and its lead firm, the networks approach centres the territory and asks how production embeds itself in places, drawing on local institutions, labour markets, and histories, and how places in turn are shaped by the embedding. Its category of strategic coupling describes the meeting between a region's assets and a network's needs, and its temper is geographical where the governance literature's temper is managerial. The two frameworks overlap in their objects and differ in their sympathies: one begins from the firm that coordinates, the other from the place that hosts.

The third body of work is the Marxist critique, and its complaint against both predecessors is that coordination and embedding, however well described, leave the movement of value unexamined. Benjamin Selwyn's work on poverty chains, including his 2019 study of labour in global production, argues that chains are constituted by class relations and that the poverty of the workers at their base is not a failure of upgrading but a product of the same relations that generate the margins at their apex. John Smith, in Imperialism in the Twenty-First Century, published in 2016, presses the measurement argument hardest: the statistics by which trade is recorded attribute the full final value of an article to the place of its last assembly or of its sale, so that value produced by labour in one place appears, in the accounts, as value added in another, and the transfer vanishes from the record precisely because the record was designed for other purposes. Intan Suwandi, in Value Chains, published in 2019, examines the labour process inside supplier firms and argues that the pressure transmitted down a chain is, at its base, pressure on the length and intensity of the working day, so that the chain's economics terminate in the oldest categories of the critique of political economy.

### How do the supply chain literatures differ in what they take as their object?

the governance literature asks who sets the terms of participation and takes coordination as its object. The networks literature asks how production joins itself to places and takes embedding as its object. The Marxist critique asks where value originates, moves, and is retained, and takes that movement as its object. Same chains, different explanations.

The critique's charge deserves its full weight, because it is not that the other literatures are wrong in what they describe but that what they describe is incomplete in a specific and consequential way. A governance analysis can establish, correctly, that a lead firm captures the largest share of a chain's margin, and can explain the capture by the firm's control of design, standards, and market access. What it does not ask is how much of that margin represents value produced under the lead firm's own roof, how much represents value produced by supplier labour and transferred through prices, and how much represents the lead firm's success in making others bear circulation costs that the accounts then credit to its efficiency. Those are different sources of margin with different implications, and a literature that does not distinguish them cannot say whether the lead firm's position rests on production, on transfer, or on the shifting of deductions. The Marxist critique connects here to the older dependency literature, which made an analogous charge against trade statistics at the level of nations, and readers who want that lineage pursued will find it in [dependency theory explained](/2016/07/01/dependency-theory-explained/), while the application to chains as such is pursued in [imperialism and global supply chains](/2017/06/01/imperialism-and-global-supply-chains/).

A fair statement of the field must also record what the critique owes its rivals. The governance literature established that chains are not markets, that the relations between their stages are relations of power exercised through specification and exclusion, and that finding was a precondition of the critique's own questions, since value cannot be transferred through a joint that has not first been shown to be a joint. The networks literature established that places are not passive, that the same chain settles differently in different territories, and any Marxist account that ignores the point will mistake a local settlement for a universal law. The critique is best read as standing on both literatures and objecting to their stopping point, not as dismissing them. Scholars in the field increasingly write across the boundaries, and the boundaries are clearest, as boundaries often are, in the work of those who cross them deliberately.

## Value added and value captured

No distinction in this article does more work, relative to the space it occupies, than the distinction between value added and value captured. Value added, in the accounting sense, is a residual computed within a set of books: the difference between what an enterprise receives for its output and what it paid for its inputs, recorded where the enterprise keeps its accounts and under the conventions those accounts obey. Value produced, in the theoretical sense, is a quantity determined in production, by the labour expended under the prevailing conditions and at the prevailing intensity, wherever that labour was performed. The two quantities are related, and in a simple economy of independent producers they roughly coincide, which is why the accounting term borrows the theoretical one's authority. In a chain, they come apart, and the manner of their coming apart is the whole subject.

They come apart through prices. A chain settles its internal prices by negotiation under unequal power, and nothing in the settlement requires the price paid for a stage's output to equal the value that stage's labour produced. A supplier pressed by a powerful buyer sells below the value produced, and the difference does not evaporate; it reappears in the buyer's books as margin, indistinguishable there from margin the buyer's own labour created. A supplier protected by scarcity or by a monopoly of some necessary skill sells above, and captures value produced elsewhere in the chain or outside it. The accounts at each stage faithfully record what was captured. They are silent, by construction, about what was produced. A literature that reads the accounts as a map of production is therefore reading a map of settlements and calling it a map of labour, and the Marxist critique of the chain literatures is, at bottom, the insistence on that sentence.

The point has a second edge, directed at the concept of upgrading. In the governance literature, upgrading names the movement of a firm or a territory toward functions with higher recorded value added, toward design, branding, and coordination and away from assembly. The critique does not deny that such movement raises what the mover captures. It denies that the capture measures a rise in what the mover's labour produces, and it notes that the functions upgraded into are, in the terms of this article's table, heavily weighted toward the circulation side of the line: design for a market, management of a brand, coordination of others' production. A territory can therefore upgrade its way into a larger share of the world's deductions while its productive labour stagnates, and the statistics will applaud. Whether that outcome is desirable is a political question. That it is misdescribed by the vocabulary of production is an analytical one, and the analysis is this article's business.

### How do value added and value captured come apart inside a chain?

they come apart wherever bargaining power moves a stage's price away from the worth its labour created. The books at each stage faithfully record the settlement, what was retained after inputs were paid. They are silent about what was produced. A supplier squeezed by a strong buyer hands over the difference, which then sits in the buyer's margin disguised as the buyer's own achievement.

The distinction also disciplines the use of the word efficiency in chain analysis. A lead firm that lengthens its payment terms, requires suppliers to hold the chain's stock, and reserves to itself the right to cancel orders at short notice will show admirable figures: lean inventories, rapid turnover, high returns on the capital it has chosen to commit. The chain as a whole may be no leaner at all. The stock sits in the suppliers' warehouses instead of the lead firm's, the waiting is done by the suppliers' workers instead of its own, and the risk of cancellation rests on balance sheets too small to bear it. What the figures record is the successful export of circulation costs and circulation time to parties with less power to refuse them. None of this requires bad faith. It requires only the ordinary operation of the deduction logic set out earlier, in which every enterprise presses its own deductions downward and outward, and the chain's architecture settles where the relative power of the parties leaves it. Analysis that stops at the lead firm's ratios will call the settlement efficient. Analysis that follows the costs to their resting place will call it something else, and will have the better claim to be describing the chain.

There is a methodological moral here that extends beyond logistics. Whenever a quantity is defined by an accounting convention, the analyst should ask what the convention was designed to record and what it systematically cannot. Trade and production statistics were designed to record transactions at borders and residuals in books. They were not designed to record the expenditure of labour or its location, and no ingenuity in rearranging them will make them do so. The Marxist tradition's long suspicion of official statistics is sometimes caricatured as contrariness. In this instance it is nothing more exotic than respect for what a measurement is, and the chain literature's measurement debate is the tradition's suspicion arriving, by an empirical route, at the same destination.

## The chokepoint argument stated

The chokepoint argument is the most discussed claim in the contemporary Marxist analysis of logistics, and it begins from an observation about the physical organisation of movement. A chain organised for flow rather than for stock, in which inventories are held lean and stages are timed to one another with little slack, acquires a distinctive vulnerability: its continuity depends on a small number of nodes through which a large share of its traffic must pass, and on the labour performed at those nodes. Distribution hubs, transfer terminals, and the haulage links between them are not interchangeable with the spaces around them. A stoppage at such a node does not merely subtract the node's own output. It starves every stage downstream of inputs and chokes every stage upstream with output it cannot move, so the effect of the interruption multiplies as it propagates, and the numbers of workers involved can be small relative to the disruption their absence causes.

From that observation the argument draws a conclusion about the strategic position of labour. Beverly Silver, in Forces of Labor, published in 2003, gave the underlying concept its standard form by distinguishing the bargaining power workers derive from their position in a production process, which she called workplace bargaining power, from the power they derive from organisation. Erik Olin Wright's earlier distinction between structural power, arising from location in the economic system, and associational power, arising from collective organisation, makes the same cut with different tools. The chokepoint literature applies the concept to the reorganised geography of production and argues that the reorganisation has, unintentionally, manufactured structural power at the nodes. Deborah Cowen, in The Deadly Life of Logistics, published in 2014, traced how the science of movement became a science of security, with the protection of circulation elevated into an object of state concern. Jake Alimahomed-Wilson and Immanuel Ness, introducing the collection Choke Points, published in 2018, gathered case studies arguing that logistics workers occupy the strategic high ground of a system that had been assumed to have dispersed such ground beyond recovery.

The conclusion is usually stated as a reopening. For a long period, the decline of large-scale manufacturing employment in the wealthy economies was widely taken to have dissolved the classical basis of working-class power, since the massed workers of the great plants, whose structural position the older labour movements had relied upon, were shrinking as a share of employment and scattering across small workplaces and services. The chokepoint argument replies that the same reorganisation which scattered production concentrated circulation, and that power dispersed at the point of making has reappeared at the points of moving. The claim is not that logistics workers are numerous, nor that they are organised, nor that their conditions make them militant. The claim is narrower and stronger: that the system's own pursuit of leanness has placed a small number of workers where their inaction costs more, per worker and per hour, than the inaction of far larger groups elsewhere, and that this asymmetry is a fact about the architecture rather than about anyone's intentions.

### What is the chokepoint argument in its precise form?

that tightly coupled chains concentrate systemic dependence on a handful of nodes, so the workers stationed there hold structural power out of proportion to their numbers, since a stoppage propagates to every stage the node feeds. The claim concerns position rather than organisation or consciousness, and it holds that leanness manufactures the leverage it exposes the system to.

Edna Bonacich and Jake B. Wilson, in Getting the Goods, published in 2008, supplied much of the argument's empirical scaffolding in their study of how the reorganisation of retail supply reorganised the labour that serves it, showing how the drive to strip inventory and time out of distribution rebuilt the warehouse sector as a concentrated, low-wage, strategically placed workforce. Their work is valuable to the argument precisely because it was not written as a celebration of leverage. It documents the degradation of the jobs at the nodes alongside their positional importance, and thereby poses the question the rest of the literature has to answer: what follows, for strategy, when the worst-paid and least secure workers in a chain are also the workers whose stoppage the chain can least afford? The chokepoint literature is the attempt to answer that question without flinching from either of its terms.

## The objection to the chokepoint argument, at full strength

That case must be stated at full strength, because the argument's popularity has outrun its evidence and a reader is owed the strongest version of the other side. The objection begins by granting the premise and denying the conclusion's durability. Yes, the objector says, coupling concentrates dependence, and dependence at a node confers leverage at a moment. But the same coupling that creates the leverage creates, in the parties exposed to it, an overwhelming incentive and a considerable capacity to abolish it. Leverage that everyone can see is leverage that everyone is working to remove. The strategic position of the node worker is therefore not a possession but a wasting asset, and a strategy founded on it is founded on the one feature of the system that the system's owners are most motivated to engineer away.

The removal proceeds by several routes, each documented in the literature that studies how circulation is secured. The first route is redundancy: the construction of alternative capacity, duplicate facilities, and reserve arrangements whose whole purpose is to ensure that no single node is indispensable, purchased precisely because the cost of the duplication is smaller than the cost of the dependence. The second route is rerouting: the development of the informational and contractual capacity to divert flows around an interrupted node, so that a stoppage becomes a detour rather than a paralysis. The third route is the automation or relocation of the node itself, replacing the concentrated workforce with machinery that does not stop, or dispersing the function across sites too numerous to act together. The fourth route is legal: the designation of circulation functions as essential, with stoppages in them restricted, pre-empted, or subjected to compulsory settlement, a route whose availability Cowen's own work on the securitisation of logistics documents from the state's side. Kim Moody, writing in On New Terrain, published in 2017, pressed a version of this scepticism from within the labour movement's own strategic debate, cautioning against readings of the reorganised economy that discover decisive leverage wherever a diagram shows a narrow passage, and insisting that positional advantage without deep organisation has repeatedly proved perishable when tested.

The objection then turns from durability to the other side of concentration. A node is a place where workers are gathered, but it is also a place where they can be watched, and the same informational systems that coordinate the flow coordinate the surveillance of the people performing it. Concentration cuts both ways for replacement as well: an employer facing a stoppage at one site among many must bargain, but an employer who has concentrated a function has also concentrated the prize for automating it, and the investment that would be irrational across a hundred scattered sites becomes rational at a single node. The objector concludes that the chokepoint literature has mistaken a snapshot for a structure. It photographs the system at the moment when leanness has outrun the countermeasures, and reads a permanent redistribution of power into what is better described as a lag.

### What would defeat the chokepoint argument outright?

a record showing that stoppages at such nodes are, as a rule, soaked up quickly and cheaply, by diverting flows around the affected point and by drawing on stocks held against the risk. That is the counter-argument's empirical form, and it can be checked. If soaking up is the norm, the celebrated leverage belongs to diagrams rather than to the system.

The answer available to the argument's defenders is narrower than the argument's usual statement, and honesty requires marking the difference. What would have to be true for leverage at a node to be durable is a conjunction: the node must be hard to duplicate, because of geography, capital cost, or the time construction requires; the flows through it must be hard to reroute, because alternatives lack capacity rather than merely lacking use; the workforce must be hard to replace, because its skills are specific or its labour market is tight; and organisation must convert the positional advantage into associational strength faster than the countermeasures erode the position. Where that conjunction holds, the leverage is real and can persist. Where any term fails, the leverage is a wasting asset on the objector's schedule. The defenders' weakness, which the objection correctly identifies, is that the literature has generally asserted the leverage from the architecture and left the conjunction unexamined, treating the first term, position, as though it entailed the rest. Position is a precondition of durable power. It is not a synonym for it, and the distance between the two is where serious analysis of particular nodes has to live.

There is also a concession to be made about the evidence base. The case literature is rich in instances where stoppages at nodes imposed costs out of proportion to the numbers involved, and thin in instances followed long enough to show whether the position survived the response it provoked. A stoppage that wins its immediate demands and triggers, over the following years, the duplication of the facility and the automation of its core function is a more ambiguous exhibit than the literature's celebratory mode admits. None of this dissolves the argument. The architectural fact stands: leanness concentrates dependence, and dependence is the raw material of structural power. What the objection dissolves is the inference from raw material to result, and an article that presented the argument without its objection would be recruiting for a conclusion rather than analysing a claim. The verdict of this article is therefore conditional in form. The chokepoint argument identifies a real and previously underweighted source of potential power, whose conversion into actual and durable power is an open empirical and organisational question, and the argument would be defeated, as a claim about the system rather than about moments within it, by evidence that absorption of interruptions is routine. That defeater is stated here so that the reader can hold the article to it.

## The labour process inside the node

Whatever the fate of the chokepoint argument, the work performed at the nodes has a character of its own, and describing it precisely matters more than the fashionable vocabulary that usually surrounds it. Warehouse and delivery work is characterised, in its developed form, by three features that recur across employers and territories because they follow from the work's position in the chain rather than from any firm's culture. The first is continuous measurement: the labour process generates, as a by-product of coordinating the flow, a record of each worker's pace, pauses, and errors, at a granularity that older systems of supervision could not approach. The second is allocation by system: tasks arrive assigned by an optimising procedure that sequences work according to the state of the whole operation, so the worker's next task is determined neither by a foreman's instruction nor by the worker's own judgement of the floor, but by a calculation performed elsewhere and delivered as an instruction to be executed. The third is pace-setting by that same system: the standard against which a worker is measured is not a rate negotiated or inherited but a rate computed from the flow's requirements and revised as the flow changes, so the effort bargain is struck with a procedure rather than with a person.

The correct theoretical placement of these features is as a development of scientific management, not as a rupture with it. Frederick Winslow Taylor's programme, set out in The Principles of Scientific Management, published in 1911, proposed the separation of conception from execution, the decomposition of work into timed elements, and the transfer of knowledge of the labour process from the worker to management. Harry Braverman, in Labor and Monopoly Capital, published in 1974, argued that the programme's logic extended far beyond the factories Taylor observed, into every corner of capitalist work. The warehouse governed by allocation systems is that argument's vindication in a specific register: the knowledge of the whole operation, of where everything is and what must happen next, has been gathered into the system, and the worker's contribution has been narrowed to execution at a measured pace. What is new is the granularity and the continuity of the measurement, and the fact that the measuring apparatus is the same apparatus that coordinates the flow, so supervision no longer needs a separate machinery of overseers. What is not new is the relation: conception on one side, execution on the other, and a standard of effort set by the side that conceives.

### Is warehouse work a new labour process or an old one refined?

an old one refined, on the evidence of its structure. Separating planning from doing, timing the elements, and setting pace from the planning side are scientific management's defining moves, and the warehouse performs all three with better instruments. The measurement is continuous where the stopwatch was episodic, but the relation between worker and standard is the relation Taylor specified.

Precision here is not antiquarian. Treating the warehouse labour process as unprecedented invites the conclusion that resisting it requires unprecedented means, and that the accumulated knowledge of how workers have contested pace, measurement, and the effort bargain under scientific management is obsolete. Treating it as a development invites the opposite conclusion: that the contests are old contests on new ground, that the system's record of each worker's pace is also a record workers can demand to see, that the concentration of conception in a procedure makes the procedure's assumptions a contestable object, and that the tightness of the flow, which the employer experiences as efficiency, is experienced on the floor as the absence of the slack in which informal organisation once lived. Both the employer's advantage and the workers' difficulty follow from the same features, and the analysis that names the features correctly serves whoever acts on it better than the analysis that reaches for novelty.

The delivery round extends the same process into public space. The driver or courier works alone and is supervised more completely than the factory hand of the stopwatch era, since the vehicle's position, the sequence of calls, the duration of each stop, and the interval between them are all recorded as the work proceeds. Solitude and surveillance, which an older imagination treated as opposites, are combined: the worker is unobserved by any person and observed in every particular by the system. The parallel with the algorithmic allocation examined in [platform capitalism marxist analysis](/2020/11/15/platform-capitalism-marxist-analysis/) is direct, and the labour-process analysis is the same in both settings, since in both the procedure that assigns the work also grades it, and the grading is the supervision.

## Comparing logistics work with manufacturing work

Comparisons between logistics work and manufacturing work are made constantly in the literature and are worthless unless the dimension of comparison is stated, because the two kinds of work differ along several dimensions at once and a verdict reached on one dimension does not travel to the others. This article states its dimensions explicitly and keeps them separate. The first dimension is leverage: the cost imposed on the wider system by a stoppage, per worker and per hour. On that dimension, work at the nodes of a tightly coupled chain scores high and has risen as coupling has tightened, while manufacturing work in a plant holding its own stock of inputs and finished goods scores lower than its historical reputation suggests, since the plant's buffers absorb the first stretch of any stoppage. The second dimension is skill composition: the distribution of training, certification, and tacit knowledge across the workforce. On that dimension the comparison runs the other way in many settings, since manufacturing retains concentrations of craft and technical skill that the warehouse labour process has been designed, deliberately, to minimise, by narrowing tasks until they can be taught in hours. The third dimension is replaceability: the time and cost an employer needs to restore a function after losing the workers who perform it. Replaceability tracks skill composition partly and labour market conditions partly, and it can diverge from both leverage and skill, since a workforce of modest skill in a tight labour market may be harder to replace than a skilled workforce in a slack one.

Keeping the dimensions separate dissolves a standing confusion in strategic discussion. The chokepoint argument is a claim about the first dimension only. Its critics frequently answer it on the second or third, pointing to the low skill thresholds of warehouse work or to the ease with which individual workers are substituted, and both sides then talk past each other, since high leverage and high replaceability are perfectly compatible: they describe a workforce whose collective absence is catastrophic and whose individual members are treated as interchangeable. Indeed the combination is the normal one at the nodes, and it is strategically treacherous, because the power exists only in the collective act and evaporates in every individual transaction. A worker who can be replaced by Friday has no individual bargaining power at all, while the same workers acting together on a Monday can halt a chain. Any strategy, and any analysis, that fails to specify which of those two situations it is describing will mislead whoever relies on it.

### On which dimension does the chokepoint claim rest, and on which does it not?

on leverage alone: the cost a stoppage imposes on the system relative to the numbers who stop. It does not rest on the workforce's skill, which the labour process is designed to keep low, nor on the difficulty of replacing individuals, which is often slight. Confusing the dimensions lets an argument and its refutation both be correct about different things.

The comparison has a further dimension that discussion often smuggles in unnamed: the visibility of the work to the public and to the state. Manufacturing stoppages interrupt production, which the public experiences later and indirectly, as shortages. Logistics stoppages interrupt distribution, which the public experiences immediately, as empty shelves and undelivered goods, and that immediacy is double-edged for the workers involved. It multiplies the pressure a stoppage exerts and it multiplies the pressure exerted in return, since the disruption is legible to everyone as disruption, and the political demand for restoration arrives quickly and with broad support. The essential-services route of the objection, discussed earlier, draws its strength from exactly this visibility. A complete comparison therefore runs on at least four dimensions, leverage, skill composition, replaceability, and public visibility, and the honest summary of the logistics position is that it is extreme on the first and fourth and weak on the second and third, a profile unlike the classical manufacturing profile on every dimension at once. That is why analogies from the older labour movement, in either direction, optimistic or dismissive, should be treated as hypotheses to be tested dimension by dimension rather than as inheritances to be claimed or refused wholesale.

## The cheapening of movement and the geography of production

The transport exception contains, as noted earlier, the seed of an account of how the geography of production is made. Because the value added by movement is governed by the labour socially necessary for the displacement, competition among carriers presses that necessary labour downward without rest, and every reduction redraws the map of what can profitably be made where. A displacement that once consumed so much labour that only goods of high value relative to their bulk could bear it becomes, as the labour required falls, available to goods of progressively humbler value, and production of those goods can then migrate toward cheaper labour, cheaper land, or cheaper materials, with the journey's cost no longer forbidding the separation. The history of the standardised container is the emblematic case, and it can be described without a single figure: by making the transfer between ship, rail, and road a single mechanical gesture rather than a day's work of individual handling, standardisation collapsed the labour consumed at the joints of the journey, which had been the most expensive part of it, and thereby collapsed the penalty that distance imposed on production.

Deborah Cowen's account of the container and of the military origins of logistical science is instructive here, because it refuses to treat the cheapening as a neutral technical drift. The systems that made movement cheap were designed, in significant part, to make movement governable: to render cargo legible, countable, and routable by procedure, and to reduce the discretion of the workforces at the joints, whose local knowledge had once been a condition of the work and therefore a source of their power. Cheap movement and the disciplining of the movement workforce were one project with two faces. That observation connects the geography of production to the labour process in a way the purely economic account misses. Distance was not merely shortened; it was shortened by methods chosen partly for their effects on the people stationed along it, and the chokepoint literature's question, where power now resides in the system, has to be asked against a history in which the system's designers asked it first and built accordingly.

The cheapening has a direction of benefit that the value framework makes visible. When the necessary labour for a displacement falls, the value added by performing that displacement falls with it, and the gain accrues not to the carrier, whose margin is competed back to the ordinary level, but to the capitals whose goods move, in the form of a smaller deduction from, and a faster realisation of, the value their production creates. Cheap transport is thus a subsidy from the transport sector's productivity to every other sector's circulation, delivered through competition and invisible in any single set of accounts. It also follows that the gains from cheapening movement are exhausted asymmetrically: each successive reduction matters less than the one before, while the vulnerability created by organising production around the cheapness does not diminish at the same rate. A system built on the assumption that distance costs almost nothing has no economical way to reprice distance when the assumption is disturbed, which is the deep reason interruptions at nodes propagate as far as they do. The chokepoint argument and the cheapening of movement are not two topics. They are one development read from its two ends, from the side of the gain and from the side of the exposure, and the article's insistence on treating them together is deliberate.

## Turnover, circulation time, and the annual rate

The second volume's analysis of turnover gives the logistics question a quantitative skeleton, qualitative though this article keeps it. Capital advanced for a production process returns only when the product is sold, and the time of that return is the sum of production time and circulation time. Two capitals of equal size, exploiting labour at the same rate within their production periods, will therefore realise different masses of surplus value across a year if their turnover times differ, since the faster capital begins new cycles sooner and sets its advanced value in motion more often. Movement sits inside this arithmetic as the greater part of circulation time for any production whose inputs and outputs are geographically separated, which is to say, for nearly all developed production. The speed of the chain is not a logistical nicety. It is a determinant of the annual yield of every capital the chain connects, and the pressure to accelerate it is correspondingly indifferent to the comfort of the parties along the way.

That arithmetic explains a feature of chain governance that purely managerial accounts describe without explaining: the lead firm's intense interest in the internal affairs of firms it does not own. A supplier's slow dispatch, a carrier's unreliable schedule, a warehouse's leisurely turnaround are not, from the standpoint of the capital waiting on the chain, inefficiencies in someone else's business. They are extensions of its own circulation time and reductions of its own annual rate, and they will be policed with the instruments governance provides, timetables, penalties, and the standing threat of replacement. The chain's famous discipline is the turnover arithmetic wearing contractual clothes. It also explains why the policing intensifies as margins thin: when the surplus available in production narrows, the portion of the annual yield that depends on speed grows in relative importance, and the chain's joints are squeezed for hours that would once have been beneath notice.

### Why does circulation time matter to a capital that does not itself perform the movement?

because its advanced value returns only when the product finishes travelling and is sold, so every interval in transit or in store is an interval in which that capital opens no new cycle and gathers no fresh surplus. Across a year the count of completed cycles governs the mass realised on a given advance, which makes other firms' speed a direct determinant of this capital's return.

The turnover analysis also locates the true function of inventory with a clarity the management literature, which treats stock as a self-evident evil, rarely achieves. Stock is capital in suspended animation, advanced and unproductive of new value while it waits, and the campaign against it is a campaign to release advances for new cycles. But stock is also the buffer that decouples the stages of a chain from one another's accidents, and releasing the advance means accepting the coupling. The lean chain is therefore not a chain that has abolished the costs of waiting. It is a chain that has converted the certain, financeable cost of holding buffers into the uncertain, catastrophic cost of interruption, and has done so because the certain cost appeared in its accounts while the uncertain one, until it falls due, appears nowhere. Accountancy's time horizon is written into the architecture of the physical system. When the article's verdict on the chokepoint argument insists that the leverage is real but its durability unproven, part of what is meant is that the architecture embodies a bet, that interruptions will be rarer than the buffers were expensive, and bets of that kind are settled by events rather than by arguments.

There is a final turn of the turnover screw that falls on labour directly. A capital pressing its circulation time downward presses, among other things, on the intervals in which the movement workforce rests, waits, and hands over between tasks and shifts, since those intervals are, from the standpoint of the flow, circulation time in human form. The scheduling of warehouse shifts to the minute, the elimination of paid waiting for drivers, the design of rounds that leave no unmeasured gap, are the turnover arithmetic applied to the working day of the people who perform the movement. The labour process described earlier in this article and the turnover analysis of this section are the same pressure observed at different scales: at the year's scale it figures as a rate of return; at the shift's scale, as the abolition of the pause. Theory earns its keep when it can show that two phenomena a reader took for unrelated are one phenomenon, and the connection between annual rates and abolished pauses is among the most useful this article has to offer.

## Fixed capital, infrastructure, and the socialisation of movement

Movement requires a material basis that dwarfs the enterprises performing it: roads, channels, terminals, and networks of communication, together with the vehicles that use them. The value analysis of that basis raises a question the article has so far deferred. Much of the infrastructure of circulation is not provided by the capitals that use it, or is provided on terms that do not recover its full cost from them, and the difference is borne by public funds. In the vocabulary of the framework, a large share of the constant capital of the transport function has been socialised, removed from the individual carrier's advance and supplied as a general condition of production. The effect on the accounts is systematic. The carrier's necessary advance is smaller, the turnover of that advance is faster, and the rate of return on the capital actually committed is higher than it would be if each carrier had to lay the road before driving on it. What appears as the transport sector's efficiency is, in part, the invisibility of its largest cost, which has been transferred to a ledger the sector's analysts do not read.

Marx's treatment of fixed capital supplies the terms for thinking about this without resorting to complaint. Infrastructure is fixed capital of an extreme kind: enormously costly, extraordinarily durable, and useful only as a whole, since half a channel serves nobody. Such capital has never, in any developed economy, been advanced on the ordinary terms of private investment, because its turnover time exceeds the horizon across which private advances are calculated, and its benefits accrue to users who cannot be charged in proportion to their gain. The state steps in not from benevolence but because the general conditions of production must exist for any particular production to occur, and the state's provision of them is one of the standing ways in which the costs of circulation are socialised, alongside the standardisation of measures, the enforcement of contracts, and the keeping of the peace along the routes. An analysis of logistics that confines itself to firms will therefore always understate the system's true cost and misplace the residue. The residue sits in public accounts, and the politics of infrastructure spending, of what gets built and what is left to decay, is the politics of deciding which chains shall enjoy cheap movement and which shall not, conducted in a vocabulary of engineering that conceals the decision's distributive character.

The vehicles and systems of the carriers themselves present fixed capital in its ordinary form, with the ordinary consequence that the framework predicts: the capital committed to a fleet or a terminal must be recovered across the whole of its working life, through every fluctuation of demand, and the pressure to keep it in continuous use is relentless. Idle fixed capital is value draining away by wear and obsolescence while producing nothing, so the carrier's interest in flow is even more absolute than the shipper's. Schedules are built to sweat the assets, labour is arranged around the assets' requirements rather than the reverse, and the working patterns that result, with their peaks, their nights, and their abolished pauses, are the human form of a balance-sheet necessity. To say this is not to excuse the patterns. It is to locate their cause where a remedy would have to operate, in the structure of the advance, rather than in the character of the managers who administer it, which is where commentary that lacks the framework invariably locates it and where remedies proposed on that basis invariably fail.

## Commercial capital and the logistics enterprise

The third volume's treatment of commercial capital gives the logistics enterprise its second theoretical home, alongside the second volume's treatment of transport, and holding the two together explains the industry's hybrid behaviour. Commercial capital, in that treatment, is capital that specialises in the metamorphosis of commodities, buying in order to sell, and its profit is a share of the total surplus value produced in the economy, ceded to it by productive capital because the specialisation cheapens the work of realisation for everyone. The merchant produces nothing and is paid, in effect, for reducing the deduction that realisation would otherwise impose on each producer separately. The logistics enterprise of the developed kind is a descendant of that figure with a productive limb grafted on. It sells movement, which produces value, and it sells coordination, contracting, and the management of other parties' stocks and sales, which do not, and its accounts fuse the two revenues into a single return on its advance.

The fusion has analytical consequences that go beyond classification. Because the enterprise's profit is a blend of surplus it produces and surplus it is ceded, its competitive strategies blend two logics that the theory keeps apart. As a producer of movement, it gains by cheapening the labour of displacement below the social standard and pocketing the difference until competition erodes it. As a commercial capital, it gains by enlarging the sphere of realisation work it performs for others, absorbing functions that producers once performed in-house and charging for them out of the producers' surplus. The celebrated tendency of logistics enterprises to expand from carrying goods toward managing entire chains is the second logic at work: each absorbed function converts an internal cost of the producer into a revenue of the coordinator, and the producer consents because the coordinator, performing the function at scale for many clients, performs it more cheaply than the producer could alone. The third volume predicts exactly this division of labour in circulation and even supplies its limit: the saving is real only to the extent that the specialisation genuinely economises the labour of realisation, and beyond that extent the coordinator's growth is a growth of the deduction itself, dressed as a service.

### Is the profit of a logistics enterprise produced by it or ceded to it?

both, in proportions its published accounts do not separate. The portion earned by performing movement is surplus its own crews produce in the act of displacement. The portion earned by coordinating and managing other firms' circulation is a share of surplus made elsewhere, ceded for realisation work. Treating the whole margin as either kind misdescribes the enterprise.

This hybridity also clarifies the industry's relation to its clients' power. A coordinator who manages a producer's entire chain acquires, along with the functions, an intimate knowledge of the producer's costs, timings, and alternatives, and knowledge of that kind is a form of the governance power the chain literature describes, exercised here not by a lead firm over suppliers but by a circulation specialist over the producers it serves. The governance literature and the commercial-capital analysis meet at this point and can be read as describing one relation in two vocabularies: what the first calls governance, the second calls the terms on which surplus is ceded to the capital that performs realisation. Neither vocabulary is complete alone. The first lacks the theory of where the margin comes from. The second lacks the account of how the terms are actually set, contested, and revised between firms. The reader who holds both can ask, of any chain, the two questions that matter: who decides, and who is paid out of whose labour.

## Just-in-time as a doctrine of displaced cost

The family of production doctrines gathered under the heading of just-in-time deserves examination in this article's terms, because it is the point where the war on circulation time became a self-conscious philosophy of organisation. Its core prescription is the elimination of stock at every stage, with each stage producing or delivering only what the next stage requires, at the moment the requirement arises. In the vocabulary of this article, the prescription is the systematic conversion of storage, a circulation cost borne quietly and continuously, into dependence, a risk borne invisibly until it falls due. The doctrine's genuine achievement should be acknowledged first. It exposed how much of what passed for production cost in older arrangements was in fact the cost of holding, moving twice, waiting, and searching among piles, and it demonstrated that the exposure of those costs could release advances on a scale that transformed the finances of the firms adopting it. A critique that cannot state the achievement cannot explain the doctrine's conquest of the industrial world.

The critique, in this framework, has three terms. The first concerns where the eliminated stock went. In many celebrated implementations, stock did not vanish from the chain; it migrated, to suppliers required to hold finished components near the assembler's gate, to carriers required to hold goods in motion as a rolling warehouse, and to workers required to hold themselves in readiness as a reserve of labour time. The assembler's accounts recorded the abolition. The chain's total holding fell by less, and in some configurations merely changed its owner and its visibility. The second term concerns time. The doctrine treats the interval between stages as waste by definition, but some of that interval was performing the decoupling that absorbed variation in supply and demand, and abolishing it transferred the variation, undamped, to every stage and ultimately to the workforce, whose hours became the buffer of last resort. The third term concerns power. A chain with no slack is a chain in which every participant's failure is immediately everyone's emergency, and emergencies are governed by whoever has the authority to declare and direct them, which is the lead firm. Leanness is therefore also a constitution: it allocates the right to command in a crisis to the party that designed the leanness, and that allocation is a political fact about the chain that its engineering vocabulary does not mention.

None of the three terms requires the doctrine to be a fraud, and the article does not present it as one. The savings are real where the stock was genuinely redundant, and the discipline has exposed waste that complacent arrangements had normalised for generations. The framework's contribution is to insist on the follow-up questions that the doctrine's own accounting cannot ask: where did the holding go, whose time became the buffer, and who commands when the buffer fails. Those questions convert an engineering assessment into an analysis of a social arrangement, and they are the same questions, in another dress, that the value added and value captured distinction posed to the chain literatures. The recurrence is not accidental. At every level of this subject, from the single warehouse to the world-spanning chain, the analytical error to be avoided is the reading of one party's ledger as the account of the whole.

## Reading an operation with the table

Abstract distinctions earn their place by surviving contact with a concrete case, so this section applies the article's instruments to a distribution operation described in deliberately general terms: a regional enterprise that receives goods from producers, holds them, assembles orders, delivers them to retailers, and administers the whole arrangement, employing drivers, warehouse staff, sales representatives, and office workers under one roof. No actual enterprise is described, and the generality is the point, since the procedure being demonstrated is meant to transfer to any operation a reader may know. The procedure has three passes. The first pass classifies the labour. The second pass traces the costs. The third pass asks where the power lies, using the dimensions the comparison section kept apart.

On the first pass, the drivers' labour is productive in full, since the enterprise sells displacement and the drivers perform it. The warehouse staff divide. Those who unload, move, assemble, and load are performing the handling that belongs to movement, auxiliary to transport and sharing its character in the proportions the table specifies. Those who count stock, reconcile records, and investigate discrepancies are performing circulation labour, as are the sales representatives who negotiate the retailers' orders and the office workers who invoice, correspond, and keep the books. The manager sequencing the day's deliveries sits interestingly astride the line: insofar as the sequencing is the conception of the movement labour process, it is a function of production, the planning without which the execution would be chaos; insofar as it is the administration of contracts and accounts, it is circulation. The proportions in a real enterprise would have to be estimated from the work itself, and the estimate, however rough, already says more than the payroll total, which lumps every category into a single cost of labour and invites the single verdict the theory forbids.

On the second pass, the enterprise's costs sort themselves by the same division. Fuel, vehicle wear, and the drivers' wages are the costs of a productive process, returning in the price of the delivery service with surplus attached. The warehouse rent divides according to the character of the holding: the portion sheltering goods in continuous flow leans toward the conditions of the movement function, while the portion sheltering goods awaiting orders is a circulation deduction in bricks and mortar. The sales and office apparatus is a deduction throughout, necessary, skilled, and to be pressed down by every device of mechanisation the enterprise can afford, which is why such enterprises automate their paperwork with an enthusiasm they do not show for automating the loading bay until much later. The pattern of investment inside a real operation, what gets mechanised first and what last, is often the most legible public record of the classification the theory makes privately, since capital votes for the table with its money whether or not it has read the second volume.

### What does an enterprise reveal when it mechanises its paperwork before its loading bay?

that it experiences its circulation labour as a deduction to be shrunk and its movement labour as a source of surplus to be sweated, the table expressed as an investment policy. The order of mechanisation follows the theory's categories with a fidelity no manager needs to intend, because the pressure of the accounts teaches the classification to anyone responsible for meeting them.

On the third pass, the power questions arrive with their dimensions labelled. The drivers and the loading staff together occupy the operation's node: their combined stoppage halts the enterprise's entire output within a day, since nothing it sells can be delivered, which is leverage of a high order relative to their numbers. Their replaceability is another matter: the tasks have been designed for rapid training, the local labour market supplies candidates continuously, and individual replacement is cheap, so the leverage lives only in the collective act and only while the act holds. Their skill composition is low by design, their public visibility is moderate, rising sharply if the retailers they serve are themselves public-facing, and their organisation is the variable on which everything else depends and about which the architecture says nothing. The exercise's lesson is that the three passes yield a portrait no single pass could: an enterprise whose surplus is produced by a minority of its staff, whose deductions are administered by another minority, whose daily operation is planned in the narrow space between them, and whose strategic vulnerability sits exactly where its productive function is performed by its most replaceable people. That portrait, with the names changed, describes a remarkable share of the modern economy's workplaces, which is why an article about an apparently specialised topic keeps turning into an article about the whole.

## The split-category pattern and its method

This article instantiates a pattern that recurs across the series, and naming the pattern is part of the article's work. Some theoretical distinctions divide the world into kinds of thing, and the useful output of applying them is a verdict on each thing. Others divide activities, functions, or moments, and the useful output is a rule for proceeding, since every concrete case will contain several of the distinguished elements in combination. The productive and unproductive distinction, applied to logistics, is of the second kind, and the recurring error in the literature is to wield it as though it were of the first: to ask whether logistics is productive, as one might ask whether a specimen belongs to a species, and to expect the question to have an answer. It has none, and the demonstration that it has none, together with the rule that replaces the question, is the transport exception's real content. Marx's treatment does not award the industry a status. It hands the analyst a procedure: locate the use-value being sold, test each activity against it, and let the classification fall where the test puts it.

The pattern has a methodological moral that extends to the other distinctions this series handles. Wherever a category in the tradition is defined by a relation rather than by a material property, industries, occupations, and firms will straddle it, because relations run through them rather than around them. The analyst's task in such cases is not to force the straddling case into one camp, which produces the sterile controversies that fill the journals, but to map the straddle precisely and to draw the conclusions that follow from the proportions. In the present case the proportions carry the conclusions: about where surplus originates in a chain, about why paperwork is mechanised before loading bays, about why the most replaceable workers can hold the most consequential positions. None of those conclusions is available to an analysis that begins by demanding a verdict on the industry. All of them follow from an analysis that begins by refusing one.

There is a corresponding discipline for readers encountering claims about other straddling sectors, in commentary on finance, on care, on digital services, and on the state. The first question to ask of any such claim is whether the distinction being invoked is relational, and if it is, whether the claim has classified activities or merely labelled an industry. A claim of the second kind can be set aside without further examination, not because its conclusion is necessarily false but because its form guarantees that its conclusion, true or false, was not reached by the method the distinction prescribes. This is a severe standard, and it eliminates a great deal of published writing at a stroke, including some written in the tradition's name. Severity of that kind is what a distinction is for. A category that excludes nothing explains nothing, and the productive and unproductive distinction, handled as a rule for classifying activities, excludes the industry's own self-image, the statistician's sector boundaries, and the polemicist's total verdicts in a single motion, which is the measure of its continuing usefulness.

## Misreadings corrected

Five misreadings recur in treatments of this subject, including treatments sympathetic to the tradition, and correcting them in one place will save the reader repeated work elsewhere. The first is that all logistics is unproductive circulation. It rests on the glance that sees goods unchanged by their journey and concludes that nothing was produced, and it is refuted by the transport exception itself: the product of movement is a change of place, and a change of place is a use-value wherever place governs usability, which is nearly everywhere. The second misreading is the mirror image of the first within the scholarly literature: that Marx treated transport as a branch of circulation and mentioned it only to exclude it. The text of the second volume does the opposite, pausing in the middle of the circulation chapters to install the transport industry among the spheres of production and to work out the composition of its capital. Readers who cite the volume for the exclusion have usually read summaries of it rather than the chapter devoted to circulation costs, and the correction is simply to read the chapter.

The third misreading is that the chain literatures agree with one another and differ only in emphasis. They do not. They take different objects, coordination, embedding, and the movement of value, and their findings can conflict in substance: a chain can be well governed by the first literature's standards, deeply and beneficially embedded by the second's, and a machinery of value transfer by the third's, all at once, with no contradiction, because the standards measure different things. Treating the three as a single literature with a single verdict produces the characteristic confusion of policy discussion, in which upgrading is recommended as a remedy for transfer, as though moving a firm toward the functions where value is captured repaired the mechanism by which value is transferred away from the firms that remain behind. It may enrich the firm that moves. It does not repair the mechanism, and the third literature exists to say so.

The fourth misreading is that warehouse management by allocation system is unprecedented, a break in the history of work requiring wholly new categories. The labour-process section of this article has given the correction at length: the categories of scientific management, conception separated from execution and pace set by the conceiving side, describe the warehouse exactly, with the instruments improved and the relation intact. The misreading persists because novelty is flattering to everyone who trades in it, to vendors of systems, to managers who administer them, and to commentators who announce eras. The fifth misreading is that the concentration of leverage at nodes is unambiguously favourable to the workers stationed there. The objection section has given that correction at length as well: concentration concentrates the incentive to abolish the leverage, the visibility that invites legal restriction, and the prize for automating the function, so the position is an opportunity with a term attached rather than an endowment. A reader who carries the five corrections together possesses the article in miniature: transport produces, the text says so, the literatures differ, the labour process is old in its relations, and the power is real, conditional, and perishable.

### Which misreading does the most damage in public discussion of supply chains?

the first, that the whole sector is circulation and therefore a cost to be apologised for, because it licenses treating the movement workforce as overhead rather than as producers, and that treatment then justifies the wages, insecurity, and political invisibility offered to them. A classificatory error becomes, by a short road, a settlement about what many workers are worth.

The five corrections share a shape worth noticing. Each replaces a total judgement with a decomposition: of an industry into activities, of a literature into three, of a labour process into its relations, of a strategic position into its conditions. The habit of total judgement is the besetting sin of commentary on this subject, because the subject arrives in public discussion already totalised, as the supply chain, spoken of as a single thing that is fragile or resilient, efficient or broken, deserving of gratitude or of blame. The framework's unfashionable insistence is that there is no such single thing at the level where explanation happens. There are activities, relations, and settlements, and the totals are what the analysis must explain, never what it may assume. A reader who internalises that insistence has gained something transferable to every other topic this series treats, since the same totalising habit, and the same correction, recur wherever the tradition's relational categories meet a world that prefers its kinds material and its verdicts simple.

## Circulation interrupted: blockages and the revelation of structure

Interruption is the great revealer in this field, because a system organised for flow discloses its structure only when the flow stops. In ordinary operation, the chain presents itself to its participants as a sequence of local tasks, each workplace knowing its suppliers and its customers and very little else, and the whole exists for no one except the coordinators whose systems model it. When a node fails, the interdependence that the division of labour had rendered invisible becomes the only fact anyone can see: stages far from the failure discover that their inputs passed through it, enterprises that believed themselves diversified discover that their several suppliers shared a single passage, and the public discovers that the abundance of the shops rests on a choreography it never noticed. The Marxist analysis has a standing interest in such moments, since they perform, involuntarily and at scale, the demonstration that the system's unity is real and that its appearance of independence among capitals is an appearance. The relations were there all along. The interruption is merely the occasion on which they send their bill.

The analytical use of interruption requires care, however, because the same event that reveals structure also tempts exaggeration. A blockage demonstrates dependence at the node where it occurs. It does not demonstrate that the dependence is permanent, that the traffic cannot be rerouted once the first shock passes, or that the system's owners will leave the revealed vulnerability unrepaired, and the counter-argument stated earlier is precisely the enumeration of the repairs. The correct inference from an interruption is therefore double. Structurally, the event confirms that coupling has concentrated systemic risk in the manner the theory describes, and that the concentration was chosen, implicitly, when the buffers were released for the sake of the advances they freed. Strategically, the event starts a clock, since every revealed node becomes a candidate for duplication, automation, and legal protection, and the leverage it displayed begins, from the moment of its display, to waste. Analysts who draw only the first inference become the chokepoint literature's uncritical wing. Analysts who draw only the second miss the fact that the repairs are themselves constrained, by geography that cannot be duplicated, by capital costs that must be justified, and by the same deduction logic that created the leanness, since redundancy is a cost and the accounts will resist it in every quarter when the memory of the interruption fades.

There is a distributive question about interruptions that the framework insists on asking and that public discussion almost never does: who bears the cost while the flow is stopped. The capitals at the node lose revenue. The capitals downstream lose production. But the costs cascade, by design, toward the parties with the least contractual protection, and they arrive, at the end of the cascade, as unpaid waiting, cancelled shifts, and wages for work that was scheduled and then withdrawn, borne by the movement workforce and by the workforces of the stages around it. The system's fragility and the workforce's insecurity are not two features but one: a chain that holds no buffers in goods will hold its buffer in people, in their availability without guarantee, and an interruption simply makes visible, for a few days, the arrangement by which the buffer is ordinarily provided invisibly and continuously. That sentence, if the article had to be reduced to a single claim after the transport exception itself, is the one its author would keep, because it joins the theory of circulation costs to the lived experience of the workforce by a chain of reasoning in which every link has been exhibited rather than asserted.

## The state, security, and the keeping of the routes

No account of logistics in this framework is complete without the state, because the movement of goods at scale presupposes a set of public functions that the analysis has touched repeatedly and should now gather. The routes must exist, which is the infrastructure question. The measures, gauges, and standards must agree, which is a question of imposed uniformity. The contracts that bind the chain's stages must be enforceable across the whole of its extent, which is a question of law's reach. And the flow itself, once an economy's life has been organised around its continuity, becomes an object of protection in its own right, guarded against interruption with a seriousness once reserved for territory. Cowen's work is the essential guide to this last development: her argument, developed across The Deadly Life of Logistics, is that the science of circulation, born in military planning, has returned to its origins in the era of the lean chain, so that the securing of supply lines is now a standing preoccupation of states, pursued through the designation of critical infrastructure, the surveillance of nodes, and the legal preparation of emergency powers over movement. The chokepoint, in her telling, is a place where labour's structural power and the state's security apparatus meet, each aware of the other.

The framework adds a characteristic twist to that meeting. The state's protection of circulation is, in the terms of the second volume, the socialisation of a circulation cost at the highest level: the cost of guaranteeing realisation itself, borne publicly because no private capital could bear it and because every private capital requires it. This is not a corruption of the system's logic but an expression of it, of a piece with the public provision of roads and standards. It follows that the politics of logistics cannot be conducted solely at the level of the firm or the chain, since the guarantees under which firms and chains operate are set elsewhere, in decisions about which nodes count as critical, which stoppages count as emergencies, and which workforces count as essential, with the obligations that designation imposes arriving more reliably than the protections it promises. The designation of a workforce as essential is, in this light, a double act: it concedes the workforce's structural importance in the language of honour while preparing the legal instruments by which that importance may be prevented from becoming power. Workers and analysts who hear only the honour in the designation have misread it, and the misreading is consequential enough that the article states the point without softening.

### Why does the state appear in an analysis of what looks like a private industry?

because the industry's conditions are publicly supplied at every level: routes built and maintained, standards imposed, contracts enforced, and the continuity of the whole guaranteed against interruption. Remove the guarantees and the private calculations collapse, since the leanness they assume is leanness under protection. The state is a standing component of the analysis, not an intruder in it.

The state's role also completes the article's answer to a question a reader may have carried since the opening sections: if circulation costs are deductions that every capital strives to shrink, why does the apparatus of circulation grow rather than wither as the economy develops. Part of the answer was given earlier, that transactions multiply as production itself expands. The fuller answer adds that the guarantee of realisation becomes more elaborate as more of the economy's life is staked on uninterrupted flow, so the socialised portion of the deduction grows with the private portion, and the two together make circulation, in its broadest sense, one of the expanding spheres of the developed economy even as each of its unit costs falls. Development, on this account, does not abolish the distance between production and consumption. It industrialises the crossing of that distance, staffs the crossing with millions of workers divided by the productive line, and then discovers, in the crossing's vulnerability, a strategic question it believed it had left behind with the age of the great factories. The discovery is the article's subject, and the state is where the discovery is registered officially, in designations and doctrines, before it is registered anywhere else.

## What the framework cannot settle

An article in this series owes its reader a statement of its framework's limits, and the limits here are genuine. The value analysis classifies activities and traces settlements, but it does not, by itself, predict the timing or the outcome of the contests it describes. It can establish that leverage exists at a node and enumerate the conditions of its durability, and it cannot establish whether organisation will arise to use the leverage before the countermeasures erode it, because that depends on histories, leaderships, and accidents that no theory of value contains. It can establish that a chain transfers value from its base to its apex, and it cannot, without empirical work the theory only commissions, measure the transfer in any particular chain, since the measurement requires reconstructing prices against values across stages whose books were not kept for the purpose. And it can establish that the movement workforce produces surplus value in its driving and handling while administering deductions in its offices, and it cannot derive from that classification any conclusion about what the workforce will think, want, or do, since consciousness is not a column in the table.

These limits are stated not as apologies but as boundaries, because a framework that claims to settle everything settles nothing credibly. The debates the article has reported, between the chain literatures, between the chokepoint argument and its objection, are debates the framework disciplines without closing. It closes the classificatory questions, where its categories are decisive: transport produces value, storage as such does not, the literatures take different objects, the accounts record capture rather than production. It leaves open the strategic and empirical questions, where its categories are necessary and insufficient: whether the leverage at the nodes will be used and will last, how large the transfers along any given chain in fact are, and what forms of organisation fit a workforce that is collectively powerful and individually replaceable. A reader who finishes the article knowing which of its questions are closed and which are open has understood it better than a reader who finishes with a slogan, in either direction, about the power of logistics workers or the parasitism of the supply chain.

There is a last limit, of a different kind, that honesty requires naming. The framework was built in the nineteenth century, around the movement of physical goods by the means then available, and its application to a world of instantaneous information, automated handling, and services delivered without travel is an extension, argued for in this article by the functional criterion, that the effect sold is the product, rather than a quotation. Extensions of that kind are legitimate when the criterion genuinely covers the new case, as it covers transport itself, which Marx already treated as the limiting case. They are illegitimate when they proceed by analogy and metaphor, borrowing the vocabulary's authority for cases the criterion does not reach. The article has tried to stay on the legitimate side of that line, testing each application against the use-value actually sold, and readers applying the framework beyond the article's territory should hold themselves to the same test, asking of every new activity the old question in its exact form: what is the effect the purchaser buys, and is the labour that produces that effect, or the labour that arranges its sale.

## Later debates on productive labour and the place of transport

The classification this article applies was not left undisturbed after Marx, and a reader should know the shape of the later debate, because the transport case is one of the few points on which the debate's parties largely agree, and the agreement is informative. Ernest Mandel, in Late Capitalism, published in 1972, confronted the growth of employment in services, commerce, and administration and argued, against both celebrants and lamenters of the trend, that the categories of productive and unproductive labour remained the correct instruments for analysing it, provided they were applied by function rather than by sector. His treatment is the bridge between the second volume and the modern economy: the swelling of the tertiary sphere appears, under the functional test, as a swelling predominantly of the circulation apparatus, with the transport function standing out inside it as the great productive exception, expanding with the physical scale of the economy while remaining, in its driving and handling core, production of value by the movement of goods.

Other contributors pressed the debate in directions that sharpen the present article's instruments. Theorists of state expenditure asked whether labour employed by public bodies could be productive when its product never takes the commodity form, and the transport case supplies the contrast that clarifies their question, since the carrier's product does take that form even though it cannot be stored. Theorists of domestic labour asked whether work that reproduces labour power without producing a commodity for a capitalist employer fell inside or outside the category, and the debate's resolution, that the category tracks the relation to capital rather than the usefulness of the work, is the same resolution the warehouse watchman illustrated at this article's opening. In each adjacent controversy, the transport exception serves as a fixed point: an activity that looks like circulation, is classified by the founder as production, and forces every party to state their criterion in the functional form that the exception requires. A distinction that can discipline debates beyond its home territory is a distinction in good health.

### Why does the transport case matter to debates far from logistics?

because it is the limiting case that compels every party to declare a criterion. Define production by the physical transformation of objects and transport fails, making the founder's own classification wrong. Define it by the making of a use-value for sale under capital's command and transport passes, and the definition must then be applied consistently to services and to every other contested case.

The debate also produced a warning that the article endorses. Several rounds of controversy were consumed by attempts to settle, by definition alone, questions that were empirical in disguise: whether the growth of unproductive employment must depress the rate of profit, whether the circulation apparatus had grown beyond what realisation required, whether particular occupations were rising or falling as shares of the whole. The definitions could not settle those questions, because their answers depend on magnitudes the categories do not contain, and the controversies stand as a permanent exhibit of the error this article's limits section described, of asking a classificatory framework to do predictive work. Transport's contribution to the warning is its clarity about proportions. Whether the productive core of logistics is growing faster than its unproductive shell is a question for statistics gathered with the table in hand. The theory supplies the table. It does not supply the numbers, and any writer who derives the numbers from the theory has confused the instrument with the measurement, a confusion against which the whole of this series, in its various territories, has had repeated occasion to warn.

## The spatial fix and the movement of overaccumulated capital

David Harvey's work supplies the final large framework within which the article's subject sits. In The Limits to Capital, published in 1982, and in the essays that followed, Harvey developed the idea of the spatial fix: the tendency of capital, when accumulation stalls in one configuration, to seek relief in geographical expansion and reorganisation, building new landscapes of production and circulation whose fixed investments then become, in their turn, barriers to the next round of adaptation. Logistics is the spatial fix's circulatory system. Every outward movement of production toward cheaper labour or new markets is a lengthening of chains and an enlargement of the movement function, and every such lengthening raises the share of the economy's life that depends on the nodes this article has examined. The fix, in Harvey's account, never finally fixes anything, since the built landscape that relieves one period's overaccumulation becomes the rigid inheritance the next period must work around or write off, and the ports, terminals, and distribution landscapes of the movement economy are among the most rigid inheritances of all, being fixed capital on the largest scale, in the most specific shapes, at the most particular places.

Read through Harvey, the chokepoint acquires a longer history than the lean era. Concentration at nodes is not only the product of just-in-time doctrine. It is the product of the economics of fixed capital in movement, which has always favoured the great terminal over the small one, the deep harbour over the shallow, the single vast distribution landscape over a scatter of modest ones, because the fixed costs of the apparatus fall per unit as the throughput rises. Leanness intensified a concentration that scale had already ordained, and the countermeasures the objection enumerates run against the same economics: duplication is expensive precisely because the original concentration was cheap. That asymmetry, between the cost of concentrating and the cost of duplicating, is the strongest structural reason for thinking the leverage at the nodes, while perishable in any particular case, will be perpetually recreated by the system's own development, since each new landscape built for cheapness rebuilds the narrow passages through which everything must flow. The argument's defenders have not always stated this ground for their position, preferring the drama of the immediate stoppage, but it is the ground that survives the objection's best points, and the article records it as the strongest available form of the claim.

The spatial fix also explains the rhythm by which the subject enters public consciousness. In the long intervals when the built landscape serves the flows it was built for, logistics is invisible, a background utility noticed by no one outside the industry, and its workforce shares the invisibility. When accumulation shifts, when a landscape ages or a flow outgrows it or an interruption reveals the dependence the landscape created, the subject becomes suddenly and briefly central, discussed by everyone in the vocabulary of crisis, and then subsides again as the fix is applied and the flows resume. Analysts who meet the subject only in its crisis appearances mistake the rhythm for the reality, writing as though fragility were a discovery rather than a permanent property periodically disclosed. The framework's longer view, in which the building of landscapes, their concentration of dependence, and their periodic disclosure are phases of one process, is the corrective, and it is the note on which the article's analytical sections close before turning to the readers who will use them.

## The chain and the working day at its base

The chain literatures meet the oldest category of the tradition at the chain's base, and the meeting deserves its own section because it converts the article's analysis from a geography into a human arithmetic. Suwandi's research on supplier factories, cited earlier, documented the mechanism in its concrete form: a lead firm's demand for lower prices and faster turnaround arrives at the supplier as a margin that cannot be met from the supplier's own surplus, and is met instead from the working day of the supplier's employees, by its extension, by its intensification, or by the compression of the wages that define its necessary portion. The categories are those of the first volume, absolute and relative surplus value, operating at the end of a chain of contracts. What the chain adds to the first volume's picture is distance and deniability: the capital that benefits from the extension is separated from the workers who endure it by several layers of ownership, each layer able to describe the pressure as a market condition rather than a decision, so the working day is lengthened by a process in which no participant experiences themselves as lengthening it.

The transport function stands inside this mechanism as its transmission belt in the literal sense. The turnaround times the chain demands are performed by the movement workforce: the driver's day extends to meet the schedule the supplier's day was extended to produce, the warehouse shift compresses to dispatch what the factory shift was compressed to finish, and the pressure that originated in a buyer's pricing decision arrives, at each stage, as a fact about clocks. An analysis of the chain that stops at the contracts therefore misses the medium in which the contracts are executed. The chain is not only a structure of prices and governance. It is a system for synchronising working days across distances, and its synchronisation is achieved, in the last instance, by adjusting the days of the people with the least power to refuse the adjustment. That is why the labour-process section and the chain section of this article belong to one argument: the allocation system in the warehouse and the governance structure of the chain are the same discipline at different scales, the one setting the pace of a shift and the other setting the pace of a world economy's worth of shifts, with the categories of the critique of political economy legible at both scales to anyone who looks.

### Where does the pressure a lead firm exerts finally come to rest?

in the length, intensity, and remuneration of working days at the chain's base and along its routes, since every other resting place is temporary. A supplier's margin absorbs pressure for a season, a carrier's schedule for a quarter, but the worker's day can always be extended, intensified, or cheapened once more. Tracing pressure there completes the analysis.

It follows that the study of logistics and the study of the working day, often pursued as separate specialisms, are one study. The movement workforce is not an appendage to the chains it serves, interesting for its novelty. It is the medium through which the chains' economics become hours, fatigue, and wages, and its conditions are therefore evidence about the chains themselves, readable by anyone who knows the categories. A warehouse's pace standard is a document of the governance structure above it. A driver's schedule is a document of the turnover requirements of capitals the driver will never hear named. The tradition's method, which reads the surface of the economy as the expression of relations beneath it, finds in logistics an exceptionally legible surface, since the relations here are written in timetables, and timetables, unlike ideologies, can be checked against the clock.

## The movement of workers themselves

One territory remains that the article should mark, if only to define its edge. Transport carries persons as well as goods, and the carriage of persons divides, under the functional test, into kinds that the analysis can separate cleanly. Where a carrier sells a journey to a purchaser who buys it as a use-value, a holiday, a visit, a chosen displacement, the labour of carriage is productive in the same manner as freight, the change of place being the product in both cases. Where the journey is the daily reproduction of labour power, the worker's travel to and from the workplace, the analysis shifts its ground: the journey is a condition of the worker's appearance at the point of production, its cost falls, in the general case, on the worker, in time and in fares, and it belongs to the economics of labour power's reproduction rather than to the production of value by the carrier, whatever the carrier's own status in selling the seat. The same vehicle, on the same road, can thus figure in two different analyses depending on whose journey and whose purchase is under examination, which is the split-category pattern appearing once more, at the boundary of the article's territory.

The point is worth the paragraph it occupies because the daily movement of the workforce is one of the largest movements in any developed economy and one of the least examined in this framework's terms. The time it consumes is time subtracted from the worker's own portion of the day, neither labour for capital nor free disposal, and its lengthening, as production concentrates and housing disperses, is a quiet extension of the working day's shadow across the worker's life, uncompensated and largely uncounted. The framework does not make this article the place for that examination, which belongs with the analysis of labour power and its reproduction, and the companion treatment of modern capitalism in this series takes up the wider setting. It is marked here so that the reader does not mistake the article's silence for the framework's, and so that the transport exception's full reach is visible: a doctrine that begins with the value of moving a crate ends by organising questions about the hours a workforce spends moving itself, and the distance between those two applications is the measure of the doctrine's fertility.

## Standards, containers, and the legibility of cargo

Standardisation is the unglamorous hero of the movement economy, and the framework gives it a precise significance. A standard, in this analysis, is a socialisation of knowledge: the dimensions of a box, the pattern of its fittings, the codes by which its contents and destination are declared, are fixed once, publicly, and thereafter available to every participant without negotiation. The effect is to convert what would be a fresh act of skilled judgement at every transfer into a mechanical operation performable by interchangeable labour and, eventually, by machines. In the vocabulary of the labour process, standardisation is the precondition for prising conception apart from execution in movement, since only a world of uniform objects can be routed by a system that knows nothing about any particular one of them. The container is the emblem because it is the standard made steel, but the same logic runs through the pallet, the barcode, and the data format, each of which renders cargo legible to a procedure by stripping away the particularity that once required a knowledgeable handler.

The value consequences follow the pattern established throughout this article. Standardisation cheapens the displacement, reducing the socially necessary labour of transfer toward the labour of the mechanical gesture alone, and the gain accrues, through competition, to the owners of the goods moved. It cheapens, at the same time, the labour power required at the joints, since the knowledgeable handler's skill, built over years of acquaintance with irregular cargo, is made worthless by the uniformity that abolished the irregularity, and the workforce at the terminal is reconstituted at a lower level of training and pay. And it enlarges the scale at which concentration pays, since a standardised flow can be aggregated without friction at a single great terminal in a way that a heterogeneous flow cannot, feeding the nodal structure on which the chokepoint argument turns. One innovation, three consequences, in production, in the labour market, and in the geography of power, and the framework's ability to derive all three from a single change in the labour process is the kind of yield that justifies its continued use against newer vocabularies that treat each consequence as a separate topic with a separate literature.

There is a historiographical caution to attach. The standard account of standardisation tells it as a story of an invention and its diffusion, a better box spreading by its evident merits. The framework directs attention instead to the contests the standard settled: whose dimensions prevailed, whose existing equipment was rendered obsolete, which workforces' knowledge was devalued by the settlement, and what public authority underwrote the agreement where private interests could not reach one. Standards are treaties, and like treaties they record a balance of forces at the moment of their signing. Reading them as treaties rather than as inventions restores the politics that the technical narrative dissolves, and it explains a fact the technical narrative cannot, which is that the standardisation of movement proceeded in bitter disputes and by public compulsion at several points, although the outcome, once settled, benefited nearly everyone who moves goods. Benefits that arrive only through compulsion are a signature of collective action problems among capitals, and their presence in the history of the container is one more confirmation that the movement economy's foundations are socialised in fact, whatever the ideology of its operators proclaims.

## Insurance, risk, and the price of the passage

Insurance sits in the classification without difficulty, and its placement illuminates the whole category of circulation costs from a fresh angle. The labour of insuring, of assessing risks, issuing policies, and settling claims, produces no value: it rearranges the incidence of losses that production and movement incur, pooling them across many capitals so that no single loss falls with its full weight on the capital that suffered it. The premiums are a deduction from the surplus of the insured, paid for a service that is, like the work of the sales desk, indispensable under the system's conditions and unproductive under the system's categories. What insurance adds to the article's picture is the element of risk itself, which the analysis of chains has already encountered in the form of interruption. A system that concentrates its flows at nodes concentrates its risks at the same points, and the price of insuring the concentration is one of the few places where the system's exposure appears as a number in an account rather than as an argument in a journal. The analyst of chokepoints who wants evidence about the system's own estimate of its fragility could do worse than to study what the insurers charge, since the premium is the market's priced confession of the probability that the leverage described in this article will, in some form, be exercised or suffered.

Risk also clarifies the meaning of the buffer that leanness abolished. A stock of goods held against interruption is, in substance, a form of self-insurance, paid for continuously in advance, in rent, in handling, and in capital suspended from circulation. The lean chain replaced that self-insurance with two substitutes: formal insurance against the financial consequences of interruption, and the uninsurable, because unacknowledged, insurance provided by the workforce's flexibility, which absorbs the operational consequences in extended and cancelled hours. The substitution was economical in the precise sense that it moved the cost from accounts where it was visible to arrangements where it was not, and the framework's insistence on following costs to their resting place applies here with special force, because the resting place of the chain's risk is, disproportionately, the household budgets of the people who staff the chain. A complete accounting of the movement economy's efficiency would price that absorption. No existing accounting does, which is less a scandal than a datum: the accounts record what their keepers need to know, and the keepers of the chain's accounts do not need to know what the chain's risks cost its workforce, since the workforce, and not the keeper, pays.

## Competition among carriers and the equalisation of the return

The carriers compete, and their competition has consequences the framework predicts and the industry's history confirms. Because the product of transport is consumed in its production and cannot be stored, capacity unused at the moment of its availability is lost absolutely: the empty departure earns nothing and cannot be made good by a fuller one later, since the later departure's capacity is a separate and equally perishable stock. Perishability of this kind intensifies price competition beyond the norm of industries whose products wait in warehouses for better days, and it produces the characteristic pattern of movement markets, in which rates collapse whenever capacity exceeds the traffic and recover only when the excess has been bankrupted or withdrawn. The framework reads the pattern through the equalisation of the rate of profit: capitals enter movement where returns promise above the average, their entry creates the excess capacity that destroys the promise, and the exit of the ruined restores the average by the wasteful route that competition always prefers to planning. The shippers, meanwhile, harvest the intervals of collapse as windfall cheapness and complain of the intervals of recovery as extortion, experiencing as the carriers' character what is in fact the arithmetic of a perishable product.

Within that competition, the distinction between the carrier as producer and the carrier as commercial capital does further work. The large integrated enterprise, performing coordination for many shippers alongside its own movement, possesses a defence the pure carrier lacks: its coordination revenue, ceded from its clients' surplus, steadies its return across the movement market's collapses, and it can price its carriage, in a slump, at levels that ruin the pure carrier while its blended margin survives. The industry's long drift toward integration and scale is therefore not only a story of efficiency. It is a story of the commercial function insuring the productive one against the productive market's own violence, and of the pure carrier, however excellent its operations, standing exposed to a competition whose terms its excellence cannot alter. Analysts who ask why the movement of goods concentrates into ever larger enterprises, although the driving of vehicles shows no comparable economies, find their answer here, in the blend of revenues rather than in the technology of the road.

### Why do movement markets swing more violently than markets for storable goods?

because the product cannot wait. A manufacturer facing weak demand accumulates stock and produces less, spreading the adjustment across time. A carrier facing weak traffic owns capacity that expires by the hour and must sell it at whatever price the hour offers or lose it entire. The swings are the rational conduct of sellers of a perishable product, not a failure of rationality.

The equalisation has a final implication for the workforce, completing a circuit the article has traced several times. When rates collapse, the carrier's defence is the compression of its costs, and the cost most compressible at short notice is the labour of movement, in its hours, its pace, and its pay. The violence of the movement market is thus transmitted, by the ordinary operation of competition, into the working days of the crews, whose conditions fluctuate with a market they cannot see from the cab or the warehouse floor. The theory's service is to make the transmission visible as a chain of necessities rather than a series of local cruelties, since remedies addressed to the local cruelties, however well intentioned, leave the transmission intact, while remedies addressed to the transmission, to the perishability, the competition, and the blend of functions that governs who survives it, at least aim at the mechanism that produces the outcomes everyone agrees to deplore.

## The last stage: delivery and the household

The chain ends at a household, and the final stage of movement, the delivery of goods to the persons who will consume them, has grown from a minor appendage of retail into a major theatre of the article's themes. The functional test applies without strain. The carriage of a parcel to a door is transport, productive of value, the change of place being exactly what the purchaser of the delivery bought. The apparatus that surrounds it, the platforms of ordering, the labour of selling, the management of returns, divides by the familiar rule, with the handling at the depots sharing movement's character and the commercial apparatus standing as circulation cost. What is distinctive about the final stage is not its categories but its geometry: where the older chain concentrated its flows into fewer and larger movements as goods approached the consumer, the final stage disperses them again into millions of individual journeys, each carrying little, along routes that cannot be consolidated without defeating the purpose of the journey, which is arrival at a particular door at a tolerable time.

That geometry reverses several of the tendencies the article has described, and the reversals are instructive. Concentration gives way to dispersion, and with it the nodal structure weakens: a workforce scattered across a city's streets, each worker alone in a vehicle, holds leverage of a different kind from the workforce gathered at a terminal, since its stoppage interrupts a service rather than a passage, and services can be replaced provider by provider in a way that passages cannot. Measurement, by contrast, intensifies rather than relaxes, because the dispersed worker, invisible to any supervisor's eye, is rendered visible to the system in every particular of route and timing, so the labour process achieves the combination noted earlier, solitude with total surveillance, in its most developed form. And the boundary between the worker's time and the system's time blurs as the apparatus of allocation reaches into the intervals between tasks, holding the worker in readiness that is neither employment nor freedom. The strategic literature on the gig economy, to which [class analysis gig economy](/2012/01/01/class-analysis-gig-economy/) is this series' companion, works this territory in detail; the present article's contribution is to locate the final stage within the value analysis, as transport in its categories and as an inversion in its geometry, so that the reader can see both why the old concepts apply and why the old conclusions about power do not transfer without re-examination.

The household end of the chain also returns the analysis to its starting point in the theory of circulation. The delivered good completes its metamorphosis only when it is consumed, and the interval between its production and its consumption, which the whole apparatus of movement exists to shorten, is closed at the door. Behind that door the categories of this article fall silent, since consumption is not production and the household's internal labour belongs to a different analysis, of the reproduction of labour power, which the article has marked at its edge. The chain's story, for the purposes of value theory, ends where the commodity's story ends, in use. What the article has traced is the long middle of that story, the passage between the making and the using, and its claim is that the middle is not an interval but a world: of production disguised as passage, of deductions disguised as services, of power concentrated by the very arrangements that were built to make power unnecessary. A reader who can no longer see a delivery vehicle without seeing that world has received what the article exists to give.

## Smith's two definitions and Marx's resolution

The transport exception did not arise from nothing, and its ancestry in classical political economy explains why Marx handled the case with such care. Adam Smith had bequeathed the tradition two definitions of productive labour that do not coincide. By the first, labour is productive when it is set against capital and reproduces its own cost with a profit. By the second, labour is productive when it fixes itself in a durable, vendible object that outlasts the act of production. The two definitions agree across the great mass of manufacturing and agriculture, which is why Smith could hold both without noticing their divergence, but they part company precisely over services and movement: the carrier's labour satisfies the first definition completely and the second not at all, since the journey leaves no object behind it. A theory that keeps both definitions must either misclassify transport or contradict itself, and the confusion ran through the political economy of the following century, with carriers, performers, and servants sorted differently by different authors according to which of Smith's criteria each happened to be applying.

Marx's resolution, worked out in the manuscripts on the theories of surplus value and applied in the second volume, is to discard the second definition as a materialist prejudice, the mistaking of the commodity's physical durability for an economic property, and to keep the first, purified: labour is productive, in the capitalist sense that governs the whole analysis, when it produces surplus value for the capital that employs it, whatever the physical character of its product. The transport industry is the resolution's showcase. Its product is a pure effect, a change of relation between a thing and the world, with no residue that could be pointed to, weighed, or stored, and its labour is productive in the full sense, because the effect is sold and the sale returns more than the labour cost. The case thereby performs for the theory of productive labour the service that limiting cases perform in every discipline: it separates the definition's essence from its accidental associations, in this instance the association with factories, with objects, and with the visible transformation of stuff, none of which survives the journey of a crate across a continent that leaves the crate exactly as it was and its owner in possession of something genuinely bought.

### Why did the classical economists stumble over the carriage of goods?

because their inherited definitions pointed in two directions at once, one toward the relation between labour and capital and the other toward the durability of labour's product, and carriage satisfies the first while mocking the second. The stumble recurs whenever ordinary usage lets the making of things compete with production as a social relation.

The ancestry matters for a further reason. It shows that the productive and unproductive distinction was, from its birth, an instrument for criticising appearances, since its first serious use was to dissolve the impression that wealth consists in objects and to replace it with an account of wealth as a social process that objects sometimes embody and sometimes do not. The logistics application is faithful to that origin. The impression it dissolves is the impression that the economy's productive life happens in the places where things are made and that the spaces between those places are a kind of overhead the economy carries. On the analysis given here, the spaces between are workplaces, the passage between them is production in one of its purest forms, and the overhead, where overhead exists, is located not in the passage but in the apparatus of buying, selling, guarding, and recording that the passage requires under a system of separated owners. Readers who grasp the ancestry will also grasp why the article has insisted so stubbornly on the functional test: the test is not a scholastic preference but the distinction's original cutting edge, recovered from the confusion of its first formulation.

## The rate of profit and the weight of circulation

The framework's largest claim about logistics concerns not the sector but the whole: the development of the circulation apparatus, of which the movement industry is the physical core, enters the general movement of the rate of profit. The reasoning assembles pieces already on the table. Surplus value is produced in production and only there. The mass of capital that must be advanced to sustain a given mass of production includes the capital tied up in circulation, in stocks, in the apparatus of selling, and in the intervals of transit, and that portion of the advance yields no surplus of its own beyond the special case of the transport function itself. As production develops, the distances its products travel and the elaboration of its selling apparatus tend to grow with it, so the unproductive portion of the total advance grows alongside the productive, and the rate of return on the whole is pressed by the weight of the part that produces nothing. Against that pressure stand the counter-tendencies this article has catalogued: the cheapening of movement, which shrinks the transit interval and the transport advance alike; the mechanisation of bookkeeping and selling, which shrinks the commercial deduction; and the socialisation of infrastructure, which removes whole categories of the advance from private books altogether. The history of the rate of profit, on this account, is partly a history of the struggle between the growing weight of circulation and the growing efficiency with which circulation is performed, fought out in timetables and ledgers.

This is the deepest reason the subject deserves the attention of readers who will never study a warehouse. The movement of goods is where the economy's abstract tendencies acquire a physical address. The pressure to economise on circulation is felt, at the end of its transmission, in a shift's pace and in the length of a driver's day. The pressure to expand circulation is felt as the growth of the districts of warehouses at the edges of cities and the reordering of whole regions around the passages between them. And the contradiction between the two pressures, to move everything faster with an apparatus that must itself be paid for out of the proceeds, is not a problem the system solves but a tension it inhabits, generating in its inhabitation the strategic landscape this article has mapped: the nodes, the leverage, the countermeasures, and the workforce that is at once the system's most replaceable element and its most consequential one. A theory that connects the rate of profit to the abolition of a driver's pause may look like a theory that has lost its sense of scale. It is better described as a theory that has kept its sense of what scale means, since the largest abstractions of political economy are, in the end, composed of nothing but such pauses, multiplied.

## How to read a study of a chain

Readers who go beyond this article into the literature will benefit from a short discipline for reading studies of particular chains, since the field's works vary enormously in what their evidence can bear. The discipline has four questions, in the order in which they should be asked. The first is the classificatory question: does the study distinguish, within the chain it describes, between the stages where value is produced, the stages where it is transferred, and the apparatus where it is merely realised and recorded. A study that does not make the distinction may still be excellent on governance or geography, but its conclusions about exploitation, upgrading, or development should be read as conclusions about capture until proven otherwise. The second question is the accounting question: whose books supply the study's quantities, and what do those books systematically omit. Studies built on the lead firm's figures will find the lead firm efficient with the reliability of a mirror, and the reader should ask, of every striking ratio, where the corresponding cost has been put.

The third question is the labour question: does the study reach the working day. A chain analysis that terminates in contracts, prices, and logistics performance has described the transmission and omitted the load, and the reader should treat its silences about hours, pace, and wages as the place where its subject actually lives. The fourth question is the durability question, applying this article's verdict discipline to the study's strategic claims: where the study finds leverage, does it examine the conditions under which the leverage would persist, the duplicability of the node, the reroutability of the flow, the replaceability of the workforce, and the speed of organisation relative to the speed of the countermeasures. A study that asserts leverage from architecture alone has completed the first half of the chokepoint argument and omitted the half on which the conclusion depends. These four questions will not make every study good. They will make every study's goodness or badness visible at the points where it matters, which is what a reading discipline is for, and they generalise, with the nouns changed, to the literatures of every other sector where this series' readers will meet the same confusions wearing different clothes.

### What is the single most common defect in published chain studies?

the reading of one participant's accounts as the account of the chain, from which that participant's efficiency, the chain's fairness, and a territory's prospects are all inferred in turn. The defect is common because the lead participant's books are the ones that exist in usable form, while the chain's true account must be reconstructed against the grain of every participant's bookkeeping.

There is a complementary discipline for reading the strategic literature, the works that draw conclusions for organisation from the architecture of chains. Its single question is whether the work distinguishes between the existence of a position and the existence of a power. Positions are facts of engineering and geography, ascertainable from diagrams and timetables. Powers are facts of organisation, history, and law, ascertainable only from the record of attempts and their outcomes. The literature's characteristic slide is from the first kind of fact to the second kind of conclusion without passing through the evidence that connects them, and the slide is seductive because the diagrams are so much cleaner than the record. The objection section of this article was built to block that slide at the point where it matters most, and the reader who has absorbed it will find that it generalises: wherever a contemporary analysis discovers decisive leverage in a structure, in a network, a platform, a protocol, the same questions wait, about duplication, rerouting, replacement, and the relative speeds of organisation and response. The chokepoint debate is a local instance of a general temptation, and learning to resist it here is training for resisting it everywhere.

## The verdict assembled

The article's findings can now be gathered, with their conditions attached, in the form this series requires. On the classificatory question, the verdict is unqualified: logistics straddles the productive and unproductive distinction, transport producing value because displacement is the very thing purchased, and storage in its waiting character, together with the work of buying, selling, and accounting, standing as deductions from surplus value produced elsewhere. No general statement about the industry's productivity is available, and the productive and unproductive table is the right replacement for every such statement. On the chain question, the verdict is that the three literatures describe governance, territorial embedding, and the movement of value respectively, that the Marxist critique is right to insist the third is not reducible to the first two, and that value added in the accounts and value captured through prices must never be read as the value produced at a stage. On the labour process, the verdict is that warehouse and delivery work develop scientific management rather than breaking with it, the system's allocation and measurement refining Taylor's separation of conception from execution to a granularity he could not achieve.

On the strategic question, the verdict is conditional, and its condition is the article's defeater, stated at the outset and earned, it is hoped, by the sections between. The chokepoint argument establishes that tightly coupled chains concentrate structural leverage in small numbers of workers at their nodes, a genuine redistribution of potential power produced by the system's own pursuit of cheap circulation. Whether that potential becomes durable power depends on the conjunction the objection identifies: on nodes that resist duplication, flows that resist rerouting, workforces that resist replacement, and organisation that outruns the countermeasures of redundancy, automation, and legal restriction. The argument, as a claim about the system's settled character rather than about its moments of exposure, would be defeated by evidence that interruptions at concentrated nodes are routinely absorbed through rerouting and inventory within short periods. Pending that evidence, and pending the organisational record that would settle the conjunction case by case, the correct statement of the position is the one this article has defended: the leverage is real, its durability is unproven, and the proponents of the argument have generally asserted the second on the strength of the first. A reader who needs a shorter version for an examination or an argument will find the whole article compressed into that sentence, and will also find, in the sections behind it, the means to defend every clause.

## Modes of movement under one analysis

The framework treats the several modes of movement, by road, by rail, by water, and by air, as one industry wearing different technologies, and the unity is worth exhibiting because the modes' separate literatures rarely meet. In each mode the product is identical in kind: displacement, sold as a use-value, consumed in its production. In each mode the capital divides into the same portions, the portion embodied in vehicles, fuel, and way, transferred piecemeal into the price of the service, and the portion advanced for labour power, returning with surplus attached. And in each mode the surrounding apparatus, of terminals, of booking, of insurance, of record, divides by the same rule between the handling that serves movement and the paperwork that belongs to ownership. The differences between the modes are differences in the proportions and in the physics: in the ratio of the way's cost to the vehicle's, in the perishability of capacity, in the granularity with which traffic can be divided and recombined, and in the degree to which the mode's infrastructure is socialised. Those differences matter enormously to the modes' economics and not at all to their classification, and an analysis that begins from the classification can compare the modes without being captured by any one mode's self-understanding.

The comparison repays the effort at the points where the modes meet, since the modern chain is a relay among modes and its joints are the transfers between them. The transfer is where the standardisation argument of an earlier section does its work: the cost of the joint, once the dominant cost of the whole journey, was collapsed by the uniform box and the mechanical gesture, and the collapse rebalanced the modes themselves, favouring those whose vehicles could be loaded and released at the new speed and punishing those whose terminals remained organised for the old world of individual handling. The relay also concentrates the article's strategic geography, since a transfer terminal is a node by construction, a place where flows must gather to change their vehicle, and the workforces at the transfers inherit the leverage of the gathering. The history of struggle in the movement industries is disproportionately a history of the joints, of the places where cargo changes hands and mode, and the framework explains the disproportion without remainder: the joints are where the labour was, where the knowledge was, and where the dependence is, all three for the same reason, that a transfer is the one moment in a journey when the flow must stop and be handled by human beings in a particular place.

### Why do the joints between modes matter more than the stretches between them?

because the stretches have been mechanised into near pure movement, while the joint remains a place where flow halts, gathers, and passes through hands, concentrating labour, knowledge, and dependence at one point. The journey's economics were decided at the joints until standardisation cheapened them, and its politics are decided there still.

The modal comparison also disciplines a familiar exaggeration about speed. The fastest mode is not, in the framework's terms, the most productive, since productivity of transport labour is measured by the value added against the labour expended, and a mode that moves goods swiftly at enormous cost in fuel, vehicles, and crew may add less value per hour of social labour than a slow mode moving them cheaply. Speed's economic function lies elsewhere, in the turnover analysis: it shortens the circulation time of the capitals whose goods are carried, and the premium shippers pay for it is a payment out of their surplus for the acceleration of their own cycles, not a measure of the carrier's productivity. Confusing the premium with productivity leads to the spectacle of commentators praising the fastest mode as the industry's productive frontier while its economics depend entirely on the willingness of hurried capitals to surrender surplus for time. The framework's separation of the questions, what the carrier produces and what the shipper gains, dissolves the spectacle into its components, and the components, as usual in this article, are more interesting than the blur they replace.

## Casual labour, decasualisation, and the shape of the workforce

The movement industries carry a labour history that the framework illuminates and that the present workforce's condition cannot be understood without. Movement work at the joints was, for most of its history, casual in the strict sense: hired by the day or by the ship, assembled at a morning call, and dismissed when the vessel or the season ended, because the demand for it arrived in waves that no employer would pay to hold a standing workforce against. Casual hiring is, in the terms of this article, the purest historical form of the buffer held in people: the industry's irregularity was absorbed entirely by the workers' incomes, which rose and vanished with the traffic, while the employers' advances were never suspended in idle wages. The long struggles for decasualisation, for registration, for guaranteed minimums, and for the pooling of employment across an entire terminal's employers, were struggles to move the buffer back from the workers' households to the industry's accounts, and their partial victories built the stable, organised, comparatively well-defended workforces that the older movement industries were known for.

Containerisation and standardisation then reversed the settlement from the other side. The new terminals needed fewer hands, steadier flows permitted steadier employment, and the casual mass from which the old organisations had been built thinned into a smaller, more technical workforce, while the growth of the industry migrated inland, to the distribution districts, where the employment relation was rebuilt in its casual form under new names: the agency engagement, the zero-guarantee contract, the algorithmically summoned shift. The framework's contribution to this history is to insist that the two movements, the decasualisation at the old joints and the recasualisation at the new ones, are one process: the buffer follows the work, and capital's preference for holding its buffer in people rather than in wages or goods reasserts itself at each new site until organisation moves it again. The strategic literature's discovery of the distribution workforce's precarity is, in this light, a rediscovery, and the older movement industries' history is the new workforce's most relevant archive, since the questions are the same questions in a new building: who holds the buffer, who bears the irregularity, and what organisation could move the cost back to the accounts that generate it.

The point completes the article's treatment of replaceability. The individual replaceability of the node workforce, on which the chokepoint objection leans, is not a natural property of the work. It is a product of the employment forms built around the work, of hiring arrangements designed to keep a surplus of available hands attached loosely to the enterprise, so that any individual hand is one of many. Those arrangements are the reserve mechanism of the movement industries, maintained deliberately and at a cost the enterprises judge worth paying, and they stand in the analysis exactly where the objection needs them examined: as a countermeasure, continuously purchased, against the leverage the architecture creates. Leverage and its antidote are thus both artefacts, the one of the chain's design and the other of the employment relation's design, and the contest between them is a contest of designs backed by organisation, which is a considerably more precise statement of the strategic situation than either the celebration or the dismissal of the node workforce provides.

## Credit, circulation, and the appearance of abolished time

The credit system enters the analysis of logistics as the great dissolver of circulation time's appearance, and the second volume's remarks on it deserve more attention than they usually receive in treatments of movement. Credit allows the producer to treat the product as realised before it is sold, drawing against its expected price and beginning a new cycle while the goods are still travelling. The interval of circulation does not shrink by an hour; the goods move as slowly as before, and the warehouse holds them as long. What changes is whose capital waits. The waiting is transferred to the credit system, which advances against the future sale and charges interest for the service, so that circulation time, expelled from the producer's turnover, reappears as a cost of borrowing in the producer's accounts and as the business of an entire financial apparatus standing between production and consumption. The framework's reading of this arrangement is characteristic: credit does not abolish the costs of circulation but refines their form, converting dead time into interest, and concentrating the risk of the interval in institutions whose failure, when the expected sales do not arrive, transmits the blockage back through every chain that borrowed against it.

For the movement industries themselves, credit's role is the financing of the perishable. The carrier's capacity expires hourly while its costs, for vehicles, for terminals, for crews, are continuous, and the bridging of that mismatch is a financial operation before it is an operational one. The large integrated enterprises perform the bridging internally, out of the blend of revenues described earlier. The small carrier performs it at the mercy of its lenders and its clients' payment terms, and the lengthening of those terms by powerful shippers, a standard instrument of chain governance, is in substance a conscription of the carrier's credit into the shipper's service: the shipper's circulation time is shortened by exactly the interval the carrier is made to finance. Here again the article's refrain applies. The chain's accounts, read at the level of the lead firm, record an efficiency. Read at the level of the whole, they record a transfer, of waiting, of financing, of risk, from the strong to the weak, and the transfer is invisible to any method that takes the lead firm's books as its evidence. The Marxist critique of those literatures, which began in this article as a point about trade statistics, ends as a point about every quantity the chain reports about itself.

### Does credit abolish circulation time?

no. It relocates the waiting from the producer's turnover to the lender's advance, where the interval reappears as interest charged and as risk concentrated. The goods travel no faster and the sale arrives no sooner; only the identity of the party whose capital is suspended has changed, together with the form in which the suspension is paid for.

The credit point also guards the article against a misunderstanding of its own emphasis on speed. The war on circulation time, as this article has described it, is not a war that capital is winning in any final sense, since the time is a physical and social fact, the time of journeys, of handling, of the interval between a need arising and its satisfaction across a separated world. What capital wins, repeatedly, is the rearrangement of who waits and who pays for the waiting, and the history of logistics innovation is as much a history of those rearrangements as of genuine accelerations. Some innovations truly shorten the journey. Others shorten the journey's appearance in a particular set of books while lengthening it, or financing it, or staffing it with unpaid readiness elsewhere. The analyst's task is to tell the two apart, and the instruments for telling them apart are the ones this article has built: the classification of activities, the tracing of costs to their resting place, and the refusal to read any single ledger as the account of the whole.

## Rent at the node: land, location, and the distribution district

The geography of movement has a political economy of land that completes the article's map. A node is a place, and places where flows must gather acquire a value that has nothing to do with the labour performed on them and everything to do with the gathering itself. The land at the junction of routes, at the edge of the great consuming cities, at the points where modes exchange, commands rents that reflect the dependence of the whole system on passage through it, and the owners of such land capture, in rent, a portion of the surplus the system produces, without performing any function in its production or its circulation beyond the permission to occupy. The theory of rent, developed in the third volume for agricultural and urban land, applies to the distribution landscape with unusual directness: the warehouse district is a machine for converting location into income, and its landowners stand to the movement economy as the landlords of a fertile valley stood to agriculture, taxing a productivity they did not create because they hold the gate through which it must pass.

The rent analysis adds the final element to the article's account of why chains are governed as they are. The lead firm's celebrated asset-lightness, its preference for owning neither the vehicles nor the warehouses nor the land beneath them, is a preference for avoiding the advances whose returns are slow and whose risks are fixed, and for leaving those advances to others: to landlords who take rent, to carriers who take the movement market's violence, to warehouse operators who take the labour problem, while the lead firm retains the functions of coordination and sale, where the surplus is ceded and the assets are information. The chain's architecture, which the governance literature describes as a strategy, appears in the value analysis as a distribution of the system's burdens according to each party's power to refuse them, with rent, risk, and the buffer held in people settling on the parties least able to decline, and the margin settling where the refusal was possible. That is the article's whole argument in one sentence, arrived at from the landward side, and it is fitting that the sentence should be reachable from every direction the analysis has travelled, since a framework is only as good as the convergence of its routes.

## The evidence of the floor: what studies of the work itself show

Between the architecture of chains and the theory of value stands the evidence of the workplaces themselves, and a section on that evidence is owed to readers who rightly ask how the article's categories fare against observation. The ethnographic and survey literature on warehouse and delivery work, produced by sociologists, geographers, and labour researchers working inside the facilities, converges on a picture that the labour-process section predicted from the categories alone. Workers describe the pace as set by a system whose standards they cannot inspect and whose revisions arrive without negotiation. They describe the measurement as total and the discretion as minimal, confined to the micro-decisions of sequence and grip that the system leaves open because closing them would cost more than it saves. They describe the employment relation as engineered for replaceability, with induction measured in hours and the workforce's composition churned deliberately, and they describe, alongside the degradation, the solidarities that the shared pace generates, since a workforce measured by one standard acquires a common grievance with a precision that workers under individual supervision rarely achieve.

The convergence between the categories and the observation is not a coincidence to be marvelled at but a test the framework passes, and it is worth stating what would have counted as failure. If the studies had found the warehouse governed by craft knowledge, with pace set by custom and the system's role confined to recording outcomes, the scientific-management reading would have been refuted in its chosen territory and the article would have had to report the refutation. Had the studies instead turned up leverage exercised routinely and durably, the objection's scepticism would have been refuted instead. What the studies in fact find is the middle condition the article's conditional verdict describes: pace and measurement as the theory predicts, leverage as episodic and contested, organisation as the variable that decides episodes, and the countermeasures, automation of the most concentrated functions, dispersal of others, legal preparation around the most critical nodes, proceeding continuously in the background. The evidence, in short, supports the architecture of the argument while leaving its strategic question open, which is exactly the state of affairs the verdict assembled earlier announced, and a framework whose conditional claims are confirmed in their conditions and open in their outcomes is behaving as a framework should.

### What would the workplace evidence have to show to embarrass the framework?

either of two findings: that conception and execution in the warehouse remain united in the worker, with the system a mere record, which would break the labour-process reading; or that the classification fails on the floor, with movement work turning out to be disguised selling, which would break the table. Neither finding appears in the literature surveyed.

One further strand of the workplace literature deserves record, because it corrects a temptation in the article's own tradition. Studies of delivery and warehouse workers repeatedly find that the workers' own understanding of their position is more exact than the commentary written about them: they know that their individual replaceability and their collective importance coexist, they can name the nodes whose stoppage would matter, and they discount, from experience, both the celebration of their power and the announcement of their helplessness. The analyst who treats the workforce as the object of a strategic theory rather than as its first practitioners will misjudge the situation in the manner the chokepoint literature's critics diagnosed. The theory's proper relation to that practical knowledge is to organise and extend it, connecting the pace standard in one building to the governance structure of a chain and the turnover requirements of distant capitals, which are connections the floor cannot see from inside itself. That is a modest role for theory, and the article claims no larger one.

## What the analysis is for

An analysis earns its length by the uses it enables, and this article's uses can be stated plainly at the end of its analytical course. For scholarship, the analysis supplies a classification and a set of tracing rules that convert the study of chains from a description of coordination into an account of production, transfer, and capture, and it identifies the exact points, the accounts, the working day, the durability conditions, where further evidence would change conclusions. For policy, it supplies a warning and a map: the warning that the costs of circulation do not vanish when they are made invisible, only migrate to parties and ledgers where they will not be counted; the map, in the table and in the tracing rules, of where they migrate to. A policy that proposes to cheapen movement, to streamline a chain, or to secure a node can be interrogated with the article's questions before its costs are distributed by default to the parties least able to refuse them, and interrogation of that kind, conducted in advance, is the most that analysis can ever do for policy, which will decide as it decides.

For the workers inside the system, and for those who organise with them, the analysis supplies something more specific than encouragement: a location. It says where, in the architecture of the modern economy, the leverage actually sits, on which dimension it sits, and under what conditions it lasts, and it refuses the two disservices that commentary usually performs for this workforce, the flattery that calls it omnipotent and the pity that calls it helpless.

A location, properly given, includes the surrounding terrain, and the terrain around the node has been mapped in these pages with some care: the employment forms that keep the reserve of hands attached and cheap, the legal designations waiting to convert importance into obligation, the machinery being installed function by function at the most concentrated points, and the calendar that concentrates the system's need and the workforce's exhaustion on the same dates. None of that terrain abolishes the location's significance. It defines the work that significance demands, which is the work of building, inside the term the countermeasures allow, the organisation that position alone never supplies. History's verdict on earlier workforces stationed at earlier passages was written by exactly that race, between the organisation of the people at the point of dependence and the reorganisation of the dependence away from them, and there is no reason to expect a different author for the verdict this time. The analysis can name the race and mark its course. Running it belongs to the people the race is about, and a theory that respects its subject will end, as this one does, by handing them the map rather than the result. For the general reader, finally, the analysis supplies a way of seeing: the delivery at the door, the stocked shelf, the warehouse at the city's edge, resolved from background into a world of production, deduction, and concentrated dependence, in which the largest questions of the tradition, about who produces, who captures, and who can act, are posed in their contemporary form. The hub treatment in [marxism and modern capitalism](/2020/11/01/marxism-and-modern-capitalism/) sets this article among its companions on the present shape of the system, and readers who have followed the argument this far will find there the wider frame into which the movement of goods, at last, has been fitted as a central rather than a peripheral topic.

## A guided passage through the chapter on circulation costs

Readers who want to verify this article's foundation should go to the chapter of the second volume where the costs of circulation are treated, and a guided passage will help them find the argument's joints. The chapter opens by separating the costs that arise from the mere change of form, the buying and selling through which commodities become money and money becomes commodities, from the costs that arise within production processes continued into the sphere of circulation. That separation, announced almost in passing, is the chapter's whole architecture: everything that follows is the sorting of particular costs under one head or the other. The labour of purchase and sale is taken first and assigned, without hesitation, to the costs of pure form. The text insists that the time and labour consumed in selling, however necessary and however skilled, create no value, because the metamorphosis they accomplish is a change in the shape of value rather than an addition to its mass, and the insistence is repeated with a patience that suggests the author knew exactly how unwelcome the conclusion would be to the commercial employees who might read it.

Bookkeeping follows, treated as the idealised counterpart of the same metamorphosis, the recording of changes of form in a parallel register, and assigned to the same category, with the observation that its costs fall as the scale of operations rises and as the function is concentrated, an early glimpse of the economising logic that the third volume would develop for commercial capital as a whole. Then the chapter turns, and the turn is the moment this article exists to celebrate. Transport appears, introduced as an industry whose useful effect is consumed in the very process of production yet which stands as an independent sphere of investment, and the text awards it, in terms, the full status of production: the change of place is its product, the laws of value govern its prices, and its capital divides and behaves as capital in the acknowledged branches of industry. Storage follows transport, and is handled with the discrimination described earlier, the stock that serves continuity distinguished from the stock that registers failure, so that the chapter's last pages leave the reader with a complete instrument: a criterion, a set of verdicts on the standard cases, and a discrimination fine enough to divide a single warehouse against itself.

The chapter's place in the volume's economy should also be marked. It stands early, before the great analyses of turnover and reproduction, because the classification it performs is presupposed by everything later: the turnover analysis must know which intervals count as circulation time, and the reproduction schemes must know which labours replace value and which create it, before their arithmetic can begin. Readers who skip the chapter as a taxonomy of minor costs therefore disarm themselves for the volume's central arguments, and the tradition's teachers have not always warned them. This article's emphasis is, in part, a correction of that pedagogy. The few pages on transport and storage are not a detour in the second volume. They are a load-bearing wall, and the modern literature on logistics, which cites them constantly and reads them rarely, would be improved by the hour it takes to read them as the guided passage suggests, with the criterion in one hand and a particular warehouse in the other.

### How should a first reader approach the chapter on circulation costs?

by watching for the single separation on which everything depends, between costs of changing value's form and processes of production continued into circulation, and by testing each verdict against the criterion rather than memorising verdicts. The same separation silently governs the volume's later analyses of turnover and reproduction, which cannot be followed without it.

There is a textual caution to add for the reader who goes to the chapter in a modern edition. The second volume was assembled by Engels from manuscripts of different dates, and the treatment of circulation costs bears the marks of its composition, with formulations from different drafts standing side by side. The transport passage is among the most finished in the volume, which is one reason it has borne the weight later writers placed on it, but the reader should still expect the texture of a workshop rather than a monument, and should treat the discrimination on storage, in particular, as a direction of analysis to be completed rather than a schedule of verdicts to be applied mechanically. That expectation is, in the end, the correct one for the whole framework. The texts supply criteria and exemplary applications. The application to the reader's own cases is the reader's work, and an article like this one is best understood as a worked example of that work being done, open to correction wherever a finer discrimination or a better piece of evidence requires it.

## Edge cases at the table: maintenance, returns, and packaging

A classification proves itself on its edge cases, and three deserve treatment before the article leaves the table. The first is maintenance: the labour that keeps vehicles, terminals, and handling equipment in working order. Such labour produces no displacement and sells nothing directly, yet it belongs, in the framework, to the production whose instrument it maintains, since the repair of fixed capital is a condition of the capital's functioning and its cost enters the value transferred by the instrument across its life. The mechanic in the carrier's workshop therefore stands on the productive side of the line, as an auxiliary of the movement labour process, in the same way that the millwright stands inside manufacturing production although no cloth passes through the millwright's hands. The case matters because it blocks a crude reading of the table, in which only the worker touching the cargo counts. The criterion was never touch. It was function within a process whose product is sold, and maintenance satisfies it at one remove, exactly as handling does.

The second case is the movement of returned goods, the flow that runs the chain backwards as purchasers send back what they will not keep. The carriage of a return is transport by every test the article has used: displacement is performed and, where a carrier is paid for it, displacement is sold. Yet the return's place in the economy of the chain is peculiar, since it realises nothing and un-sells what was sold, and its costs have the character of a deduction from the original transaction's proceeds even while its labour is productive in form. The framework holds both truths without strain, because its categories answer different questions: the classification of the labour is one question, and the incidence of the cost is another, and a mature analysis keeps them separate. The growth of the backward flow is, in the article's terms, the growth of a productive activity in the service of a circulation failure, the failure of the first sale to be final, and the spectacle of an expanding industry devoted to undoing completed transactions is one of the purest expressions of the developed economy's tendency to industrialise its own frictions.

The third case is packaging, which straddles the line with a fineness that rewards attention. Packaging that protects a good through its journey, that makes it stackable, countable, and fit for the standardised handling the chain requires, is a continuation of production into circulation by the chapter's own logic, since the good is not the good, as a deliverable use-value, until it can survive its passage. Packaging that dresses the good for the moment of sale, that persuades at the shelf and differentiates at the point of choice, belongs to the costs of selling. The same material, applied in the same building, divides by its purpose, and the division is not a pedantry: the two kinds of packaging face opposite pressures in the enterprise's accounts, the first being a cost of the movement function to be engineered for adequacy, the second a cost of competition to be engineered for effect, and confusing them produces the familiar muddle in which protective packaging is gold-plated for appearance and display packaging is stripped until the goods arrive damaged. The table, applied with its purposes kept straight, is a practical instrument even here, at the level of the cardboard and the film, which is where classifications either earn their keep or confess their uselessness.

## The social division of labour between making and moving

Behind the article's classifications stands a fact about the social division of labour that gives the subject its scale. In an economy of separated producers, the movement of goods is not an afterthought to production but one of its two great organising systems, the other being production itself, and the proportion of the social working day devoted to moving, holding, and administering the movement of things is a measure of how far the separation of producers has gone. A household economy moves almost nothing. A local economy moves its products across short distances by the hands that made them. A developed economy of chains moves everything, repeatedly, through the hands of specialists, and supports, in the movement and its administration, a workforce comparable in scale to the workforces of its celebrated industries. The framework's categories let that proportion be read correctly: not as the economy's overhead, since the core of the movement is production, and not as its productive triumph, since the shell of administration around the movement is a deduction that grows with the same development. The proportion is the economy's signature, the physical record of its choice to organise production through separation and exchange rather than through direct allocation, and the costs and powers this article has traced are the price and the politics of that choice.

The division of labour between making and moving also has a direction of development that the framework predicts and observation confirms. As production is mechanised, the labour required to make a given mass of goods falls, while the labour required to move, sort, sell, and account for that mass falls more slowly, since the goods must still be handled one by one at the joints whatever the factories achieve. The movement share of the social working day therefore tends to rise even under relentless mechanisation of movement itself, a tendency visible in the landscape of every developed economy, where the districts of distribution grow as the districts of manufacture shrink. Polemicists read that landscape as decline, the replacement of real work by circulation. The framework reads it as arithmetic: the necessary consequence of cheapening making faster than moving, within a system that multiplies the journeys each product takes. Neither reading is available to an analysis that cannot separate the movement core from its administrative shell, since the landscape contains both in proportions the eye cannot judge, and the article's table is, once more, the instrument the judgement requires.

### Does the growth of the movement workforce mean the economy is becoming unproductive?

not by itself, since the movement core is productive labour whose growth enlarges the sphere of surplus production, while the administrative shell around it enlarges the deductions. The aggregate figure conceals the composition, and the composition is the whole question. Both kinds of growth show as the same rising sector in the statistics.

The division of labour has a final consequence, for the composition of the working class itself, that the series' wider concerns make relevant. A class increasingly employed in movement and its administration is a class whose daily experience is of flow, of interdependence, of systems that span the world and fail together, rather than of the bounded workplace producing a bounded product. What that experience teaches, and what forms of organisation and imagination it sustains, are questions the framework poses and history answers, and they are posed with special sharpness at the nodes, where the experience of interdependence is most concentrated and where this article began: with a small number of workers, standing where everything passes, in possession of a knowledge of the system's unity that the system's own ideology denies and that its interruptions periodically confirm to everyone. Whether that knowledge becomes power is the open question the verdict recorded. That it exists, as a standing feature of the developed economy's daily operation, is among the few certainties the analysis affords.

## The warehouse district as a social world

The nodes have a social geography that the analysis should register, because the workforce at a node does not appear by magic at the start of a shift. Distribution concentrates where land is cheap, routes converge, and a large pool of labour can be reached within a tolerable journey, and those conditions select, in practice, for particular kinds of place: the edges of great cities, the corridors between them, the districts where housing is affordable precisely because the work on offer is the work this article has described. A social world grows around the node with a characteristic shape. Employment agencies cluster where the hiring is done, since the employment relation's engineered looseness requires intermediaries to hold the reserve of available hands. Transport for workers runs at hours the public network does not keep, because the shifts begin when the flow requires rather than when the city wakes. Housing, schooling, and the whole apparatus of reproduction arrange themselves around schedules that the chain sets at a distance, by parties who will never see the district their timetables govern.

The framework reads that world as the reproduction of labour power under the node's specific demands, and the reading adds a term to the chokepoint calculation that the strategic literature rarely prices. A workforce drawn from a district built around the node is a workforce whose households, debts, and caring arrangements are all calibrated to the node's employment, which raises the cost of action for the workers and simultaneously raises the cost of the node's failure for the whole local economy, since the district has no second industry to absorb the shock. Concentration, once again, cuts both ways, and at the scale of the district the two edges are visible in the same street: the node is the district's livelihood and its single point of failure, the source of its wages and of its vulnerability, and any politics of the node that ignores the district will misunderstand both the loyalty and the anger it encounters there.

There is also a temporal shape to the district's life that follows from the flow's calendar. Movement work swells and slackens with the seasons of consumption and production, and the district absorbs the swelling in its households before it appears in any statistic: in the overtime that floods a family's winter and the short weeks that starve its spring, in the agency's list lengthening and shortening, in the second jobs taken and surrendered. The buffer held in people, which this article has traced as an abstraction through the theory of circulation costs, has an address. It lives in the district, in the gap between the hours the chain wants and the hours a household needs, and the gap is bridged, week by week, by arrangements that no account records. A complete political economy of logistics would map the district as carefully as the chain, since the chain's celebrated flexibility is, in the end, the district's inflexibility about rent, food, and debt, and the two facts are one fact observed from opposite ends of the wage.

## The military genealogy of the science of movement

The word logistics entered the language of business from the language of war, and the genealogy is more than a curiosity, because the science was formed, in its military home, around exactly the problems this article has analysed in their civilian form. An army in the field is a chain made visible: a consuming body at a distance from its sources, supplied across a passage that must not fail, with the costs of the passage counted in the same currency as the costs of the fighting, since a force that cannot be fed cannot fight whatever its other virtues. Military thought learned early that the passage could decide campaigns that battles merely ratified, and it built, over generations, the apparatus of calculation, the timetables, the depots, the standardised loads, that civilian commerce later adopted entire. Cowen's history traces the transfer in detail, and her point is that the transfer carried the military frame with the military techniques: the treatment of circulation as a problem of security, of flows to be guaranteed against an enemy, where the enemy, in the civilian translation, becomes interruption in any form, including the interruption threatened by the workforce at the nodes.

The genealogy explains a tone that students of the subject often notice without placing it. The literature of logistics speaks, even in its most commercial registers, the language of command: of theatres and deployments, of strategic stocks and critical nodes, of resilience as a virtue of systems under threat. That language is not a metaphor laid over the subject from outside. It is the subject's native tongue, inherited from the institutions that first made movement a science, and it shapes what the science can see. A discipline formed to guarantee supply against an enemy sees the workforce at the node, when it acts, as a threat to be planned around, and sees the state's guarantee of the flow as the natural completion of its own work. The Marxist analysis, arriving from outside that inheritance, sees the same facts in the opposite arrangement: the flow's guarantee as a socialised cost, the node's workforce as the producers of the movement the discipline administers, and the security frame itself as an ideology in the strict sense, a picture of the system that serves the system's continuation by misdescribing the relations inside it. Neither discipline is lying about the tonnages. They are answering different questions about them, and the genealogy teaches the reader to hear, in the civilian literature's most technical passages, the accent of its origin, and to ask whose security the passage is securing.

For the historian, the genealogy also supplies the missing link between the article's two discussions of the state. The state's guarantee of circulation is not an improvisation of the lean era. It descends from the era when the guarantee was openly military, when the movement of goods and the movement of armies shared their planners, their infrastructures, and their science, and the modern doctrines of critical infrastructure are that descent wearing civilian dress. The chokepoint, in consequence, is a place where three histories meet: the history of a theoretical distinction about value, the history of a workforce's organisation and defeat and reorganisation, and the history of a science of war that became a science of commerce without surrendering its founding assumption, that the flow must not stop. Articles in this series rarely find so many of their themes converging on a single site, and the convergence is the final justification for the length this one has taken: the node is where the modern economy keeps its secrets in the open, and reading it correctly requires every instrument the tradition has built.

## Automation at the node: what machinery changes and what it does not

Automation is the countermeasure the chokepoint objection mentions most confidently, and it deserves a section of its own, because the framework both confirms its power and locates its limit. Machinery at the node substitutes constant capital for living labour in the handling and sorting functions first, since those are the functions standardisation had already rendered mechanical in form. The substitution changes the composition of the node's workforce profoundly: the mass of handlers thins, and around the machinery there grows a smaller workforce of technicians, maintainers, and supervisors of the system, whose labour differs from the handlers' in every dimension the comparison section distinguished, being scarcer in skill, harder to replace, and stationed at points where the system's dependence is, if anything, more concentrated than before. Automation does not abolish the node. It rebuilds the node with a different workforce inside it, and the leverage question must then be asked again of the new workforce, whose small numbers would once have been read as weakness and whose position athwart an automated flow may prove, on the leverage dimension, stronger than the mass it replaced.

The framework predicts this shape, because it treats machinery as it treats every development of the productive forces: as a change in the technical composition of a process whose social relations persist until they are separately changed. The automated warehouse still sells displacement, still divides its labour by the table, still holds its buffer in the people who remain, in the technicians summoned at any hour and the reduced crews whose absence the machinery cannot cover. What automation changes decisively is the arithmetic of the employer's choice. A function performed by hundreds can be contested by those hundreds with the leverage of their numbers at a passage. A function performed by a system and a handful of its keepers can be contested only by the handful, but the handful's withdrawal may stop more than the hundreds' ever did, since the system without its keepers is a sculpture. Whether that concentration of consequence in fewer hands makes action more or less likely is a question about organisation and law, not about machinery, and the literature that announces automation as the end of the node's politics has confused a change in the workforce with a change in the structure, which is the same confusion, in a new costume, that the article corrected in the literature on the labour process.

There is a further limit, of political economy rather than of engineering. Automation is an advance of fixed capital, and fixed capital must be recovered across its life through every fluctuation of the traffic, which makes the automated node a hostage to the flow's continuity in a way the labour-intensive node was not. The enterprise that replaces its flexible wage bill with an inflexible capital stock has traded a cost it could shed in a slump for a cost it cannot, and has thereby deepened its own dependence on the uninterrupted operation that the chokepoint argument describes. The countermeasure against the workforce's leverage thus manufactures a new exposure of the employer's, to interruption from any source at all, and the system's long argument with itself, between flexibility and efficiency, between the buffer in people and the buffer in machines, continues on the new ground without approaching a settlement. Analysts should therefore treat each wave of automation neither as the objection's triumph nor as its refutation, but as the relocation of the question this article has taught them to ask: where the dependence now sits, in whose hands, and under what conditions it could be used.

## The seasons of flow and the calendar of circulation

Circulation has a calendar, and the calendar disciplines everything this article has said about buffers, leverage, and the working day. The demand for movement is not level across the year. It swells with the harvests, with the seasons of manufacture, and above all with the great festivals of consumption toward which the retail chain aims its year, and it slackens in the intervals between. A system organised for flow must meet the swell with capacity that the slack will leave idle, and the manner of meeting it is the buffer problem in its annual form. Capacity held in buildings and vehicles is paid for across the whole year. Capacity held in people, in the seasonal engagement, the agency list, the overtime banked against the peak, is paid for, in the main, only when used, and the difference explains the calendar's human shape: the movement workforce's year is a tide, of frantic weeks in which every hour is sold and fallow weeks in which the hours cannot be given away, and the household economy of the warehouse district, described earlier, is built on that tide's shore.

The peak is also the moment when the chokepoint argument is most nearly true, and the qualification is worth stating exactly. At the height of the flow, the system's slack is consumed by the traffic itself, rerouting capacity is committed, inventories are drawn down by design, and an interruption falls on a chain with nothing left to absorb it, so the leverage of the node's workforce reaches its annual maximum in the very weeks when its own members are most exhausted and least able to forgo wages. The coincidence is not a conspiracy. It is the arithmetic of a system that concentrates its dependence and its demands on the same dates, and it explains a rhythm observable across the movement industries' history, in which the great contests cluster at the peaks and are settled, by exhaustion on both sides, before the slack returns. Strategy that ignores the calendar will misread both the contests and the settlements, attributing to courage or betrayal what the tide table explains.

For the analyst, the calendar supplies a methodological rule to add to the reading discipline given earlier: date every piece of evidence. A study of a node's resilience conducted in the slack describes a different system from the one that operates at the peak, and a study of its workforce's power that does not specify the season has omitted the variable on which its conclusion most depends. The structural-not-current discipline of this article forbids it to trade in particular seasons' events, but the structural point itself is a point about time: that the system's properties vary across its own calendar in a regular and predictable way, and that the variation is not noise around the structure but part of the structure, as much as the node's geography or the chain's governance. The reader who carries away that single refinement, to ask of every claim about this industry in which weeks it is true, will read the whole literature, and a good deal of the rest of political economy, with sharper eyes.

## How the national accounts miscount the movement economy

The article's measurement argument has a final application, to the national accounts themselves, which classify the economy's activity for the state's own purposes and thereby fix the terms in which public discussion is conducted. The accounts divide production by the industry of the establishment performing it, so that the transport performed inside a manufacturing enterprise, by its own fleet and its own drivers, vanishes into manufacturing's totals, while the identical transport performed by a specialist carrier appears as the output of a transport sector. The boundary between the sectors is therefore a boundary between organisational forms, not between activities, and it moves whenever enterprises outsource or absorb their movement functions, generating apparent growth and decline of sectors that record nothing but the redrawing of firms' edges. A decade in which manufacturers shed their fleets to specialists will show, in the accounts, a booming transport industry and a stagnating manufacturing one, although the same goods moved the same distances by the same methods throughout, and commentary built on the accounts will invent explanations, about the economy's transformation from making to moving, for an event in corporate organisation.

The framework's correction is the one applied throughout this article: classify activities, not establishments, and treat every sectoral total as a question rather than an answer. The correction has teeth beyond the transport case. The growth of the services in the national accounts, the master fact of modern economic commentary, is partly the same artefact at a larger scale, the record of functions once performed inside producing enterprises, of cleaning, of catering, of computing, of carriage, being expelled to specialists and thereby becoming visible as sectors. Some of the growth is real, the genuine expansion of the circulation apparatus that the rate-of-profit section described. Some is the artefact. The accounts cannot separate the two, because the separation requires the functional classification the accounts do not perform, and the analyst who wants the truth of the matter must reconstruct it from below, establishment by establishment and function by function, in the manner the reading discipline prescribed for chain studies. That labour is rarely done, which is why the artefact passes, in nearly all public discussion, for the transformation of the economy itself.

There is a lesson here about the relation between theory and statistics that the series has occasion to repeat in every territory it enters. Statistics are not the raw material of analysis, innocent of the categories that organise them. They are analysis already, performed by institutions with purposes, and the categories baked into them, of sector, of industry, of the product itself, decide in advance what can be seen. A theory like the one in this article earns part of its keep by supplying rival categories, under which the same world sorts differently and different facts become visible: the fleet inside the factory reappears as transport, the warehouse's two kinds of labour separate, the lead firm's efficiency dissolves into capture and transfer. Readers who have followed the argument will recognise that the article's apparently scholastic opening question, whether moving a crate produces value, was the thin end of this wedge, and that the wedge's thick end is a standing instruction for reading every number the economy publishes about itself.

## Uneven development and the siting of chains

The chain's geography, finally, is a geography of uneven development, and the framework connects the siting of production and movement to the oldest pattern in the tradition's account of the world economy. Chains settle their stages where the combination of wages, skills, infrastructure, and political conditions offers the lead firm its best terms, and they settle their nodes where the routes and the markets dictate, and the two settlements together write a map on which some territories host the chain's conception and capture, others its production, and others merely its passage. The territory of passage is the map's most easily overlooked figure: the place the chain crosses without enriching, whose land carries the terminals, whose workforce staffs the nodes, whose environment absorbs the traffic, and whose share of the chain's margin is the wage bill of the movement workforce and the rent of the corridor's owners. Such territories appear in the governance literature as locations and in the accounts as costs. In the value analysis they appear as participants whose contribution, the indispensable one of passage, is compensated at the rate of the least regarded function in the chain, which is a precise description of a familiar injustice in the geography of the world economy.

The siting decisions are not made once. They are remade whenever the terms shift, and the movement landscape's rigidity, its vast fixed capital in terminals and corridors, means that the remaking lags the terms by years, so the map at any moment is a palimpsest of past settlements, with chains flowing through landscapes built for the wages and politics of an earlier round. That lag is a further source of the system's periodic surprise: the dependence concentrated at a node was rational when the node was built, under conditions that no longer hold, and the leverage discovered there in a later round is the inheritance of a decision nobody now in authority would repeat. History, in this field, is not background. It is the explanation of why the passages run where they run and why the power sits where it sits, and the analyst who reads the map without its dates will mistake inheritances for choices and choices for necessities, the two characteristic errors of the literature this article has tried to correct.

## Circulation costs and the price the consumer pays

Every cost traced in this article is finally paid by someone, and the consumer price is where the payments gather, so a section on the passage of circulation costs into prices completes the analysis's circuit. The price of a finished good must, in the aggregate and over time, cover the value produced in its making, the value transferred by its movement, and the deductions its circulation imposed, together with the shares of surplus claimed at each stage by the capitals that handled it. The consumer, standing at the end of the chain with the price in view, sees a single number in which production, transport, storage, selling, rent at the nodes, insurance against the passage, and the financing of the intervals are fused beyond separation. The fusion is the final form of the invisibility this article has fought throughout: at the point of consumption, the whole world of the chain collapses into a figure that invites only two judgements, cheap or dear, both of them blind.

The framework's decomposition has a practical consequence for the politics of prices. When the price of a good rises, public argument assigns the rise to whichever stage of the chain is most visible or most disliked, to the producers' wages, the carriers' rates, the retailers' margins, in rotation, and each assignment is a guess dressed as an explanation. The decomposition replaces the guess with a procedure: establish which component moved, the value produced, the necessary labour of the movement, the deductions, the rents, the financing, and let the assignment follow the evidence. The procedure will rarely be completable from public data, since the components are exactly what the accounts fuse, but even its partial application disciplines argument, because it forces every claimant to name the component their story requires and to confront the components it ignores. A rise assigned to carriers' rates that coincides with a fall in the movement's necessary labour is a rise in capture, not in cost, and the distinction decides whether the remedy proposed, against the carriers, against the chain's governance, or against the rents at its nodes, is aimed at the mechanism that actually moved.

A counterpart exists for the economy entire. The share of the social product absorbed by circulation, in its broadest sense of movement, holding, selling, and administering, is a deduction from what is available for consumption and accumulation, and its growth, which the earlier sections established as a tendency of development itself, sets a standing problem for every economic policy that promises simultaneously cheaper goods, higher wages in production, and an expanding apparatus of distribution. The promises are not impossible together, since the cheapening of movement and the mechanisation of the apparatus work continuously against the tendency, but they are in tension, and the tension is structural rather than a failure of anyone's competence. Political economy's service is to state the tension in its exact form, as this article has tried to do for its territory, so that the argument about prices can be an argument about the real quantities in dispute rather than a competition of impressions about a fused and silent number.

## The reproduction schemes and the place of movement

The second volume closes with the reproduction schemes, the great analysis of how the economy's two departments, producing means of production and means of consumption, exchange their outputs so that production can repeat itself on the same or an enlarged scale. Movement is the schemes' silent presupposition, and making the presupposition audible is a fitting late exercise for this article. Every exchange the schemes describe, of machines for the wage goods that sustain the machine makers, of consumption goods for the materials that replenish the consumption industries, is an exchange between separated producers at separated places, and each requires the physical passage of goods from the department that made them to the department that needs them. The schemes abstract from the passage in order to exhibit the proportions that must hold between the departments' values, and the abstraction is legitimate. But the passage is where the proportions are executed, and its costs, its time, and its workforce are the material form of the interdependence the schemes state algebraically.

Reading the schemes with the movement restored changes their emotional register, if not their logic. The smooth reproduction they describe, in which every output finds its purchaser and every input its source, appears, once the passages are imagined, as a choreography of staggering complexity, performed daily by the workforce this article has described, under the discipline of the turnover arithmetic, with the buffers held where the system's design has put them. The schemes' famous fragility, their demonstration that reproduction succeeds only when exact proportions hold and fails into crisis when they do not, acquires in the movement a physical mechanism: the proportions must not only be right in value but must arrive, at the right places, in the right order, within the intervals the buffers allow. A disproportion in the schemes is an entry in a table. In the world it is a queue of vehicles at a terminal that lacks what the queue behind it is carrying, and the crisis theory of the tradition is incomplete until its algebraic disproportions are followed into the passages where they become visible as the system's ordinary life breaking down in its ordinary places.

That is the last and largest sense in which logistics is not a special topic within the critique of political economy but one of its central scenes. The reproduction of the whole, the process by which the economy continues to exist from one day to the next, is executed in the movement of goods between its parts, and the categories that govern that movement, of production and circulation, of value made and value captured, of buffers held in goods and buffers held in people, are the categories in which the whole's continuation can be understood. An article that began with a crate and a journey ends, without strain, at the reproduction of society, and the absence of strain is the tradition's signature: its concepts were built to travel exactly that distance.

## The driver's day: time, fatigue, and the limits set by law

The working day of the driver is where the article's abstractions meet the human body most directly, and the meeting has a legal history that illustrates the framework's account of the state. Movement labour is performed in public space, with vehicles whose misuse endangers strangers, so the length and shape of the driver's day have long been objects of public regulation of a kind that factory labour won only after generations of struggle: maximum hours, mandated rests, records that must be kept and produced. The regulation is usually explained as a safety measure, and so it is, but the framework hears in it a second note. The driver's hours are the point where the turnover arithmetic, pressing for the abolition of every pause, meets a limit that the individual employer cannot be trusted to observe, since the employer who rests a driver while rivals do not surrenders the traffic to the rivals, and the industry therefore requires, and eventually demands, a rule imposed on all alike. The pattern is the one the first volume exhibited in the factory legislation: a limit that capital in general needs and capital in particular resists, supplied by the state as the general interest of the class against the ruinous competition of its members.

The records the regulation requires have had the double career that records in this industry always have. Kept to enforce the rest, they become, in the enterprise's hands, the instrument of the pace, the continuous account of the driver's day from which the allocation system learns and against which the driver is graded, so the protection and the surveillance are one document read by two parties for opposite purposes. The driver's day is thus the most documented working day in the economy and among the least rested, since the documentation serves the flow first and the limit second, and the intervals the law carved out are pressed, by scheduling that loads the permitted hours to their legal edge, into a shape the law's authors would recognise only formally. None of this is an argument against the regulation, which stands between the driver and a worse condition. It is an argument for reading the regulation as the framework reads every settlement in this field: as a balance of forces at a date, inscribed in rules whose text is stable and whose effect is contested daily in the scheduling office.

Fatigue, the body's own accounting, closes the section. The turnover arithmetic counts time in hours of circulation saved. The labour process counts it in tasks per shift. The body counts it in attention, which fails on its own schedule and announces the failure without negotiation. A system that organises the movement of goods around the minimisation of every interval will meet the body's schedule as it meets every other limit, by pressing against it until the accidents price the pressing, and the long public argument about drivers' hours is, in the framework's terms, the argument about where that price is set and who pays it in the meantime. The article records the argument without pretending its categories settle it. They locate it: at the joint between the annual rate of return and the length of a human day, which is the joint at which the whole of this subject, pursued far enough, always arrives.

## Logistics as the economy's confession

It remains to say, in closing the analytical course, why this subject repays the attention of readers whose interests lie far from warehouses. Every economy has a story it tells about itself, and in the story the developed economy tells, production happens in places of innovation and consumption happens in places of choice, while between them stretches a frictionless medium through which goods pass as information passes, without labour, without cost, and without politics. Logistics is the refutation of that story by the economy's own practice. The medium turns out to be a world: staffed by millions, governed by the oldest categories of political economy, organised around a distinction between producing value and arranging its realisation that the story cannot even state, and vulnerable, at its narrow passages, to the withdrawal of the labour the story renders invisible. To study the movement of goods is therefore to catch the economy in the act of confessing what its self-image denies, that it rests on work, that the work is divided between making and realising by a line with consequences, and that the interdependence its ideology celebrates as a network is also a dependence, with a politics, concentrated at points where a small number of confessedly replaceable people are, in the only sense that finally matters, irreplaceable together.

That confession is the article's deepest finding, and it is the reason the transport exception, a few pages in a posthumous volume, has carried the weight this article placed on it. Marx noticed, in the middle of the least read of his books, that the journey of a crate is production, and in noticing it he supplied the key to a world that his century had barely begun to build: a world that would organise itself around movement, staff its passages with a new working class, concentrate its dependence at nodes its designers would then have to guard, and rediscover, in the guarding, the question of working-class power it believed it had buried. The reader who has followed the argument from the crate to that rediscovery possesses the article, and possesses, in the table and the tracing rules and the defeater, the instruments to test every future claim the subject produces, including the claims of this article itself, which asks for no other treatment.

## Fictitious capital and the assets of movement

The terminals, fleets, and networks of the movement economy circulate in a second sense besides the physical one: as assets, bought and sold, securitised and leased, in transactions whose prices are capitalisations of expected revenue rather than measures of the labour embodied in the assets themselves. The third volume's category for such prices is fictitious capital, value that exists as a claim on future revenue and trades at whatever the expectation of that revenue, discounted, will bear. The category matters for this article because the movement landscape's ownership has been progressively separated from its operation, so that the land at the node, the terminal upon it, the vehicles that serve it, and the enterprise that coordinates them may each belong to a different owner, each drawing a different form of revenue from the same flow: rent for the land, interest and lease payments for the equipment, profit of enterprise for the operator, and the cession for coordination to the chain's governor. The flow's surplus is thus divided among more hands than the chain's own accounts display, and each division is a claim whose holder will defend it, in any contest over the chain's future, as though it were the whole.

The separation of ownership from operation also changes the system's behaviour in ways the framework predicts. An operator who owns the terminal must recover its cost across decades and therefore plans in decades. An operator who leases it, from an owner who must satisfy investors quarterly, inhabits a shorter horizon inside a longer asset, and the mismatch expresses itself in the maintenance deferred, the capacity sweated, and the buffer refused, since every party's rational horizon is shorter than the life of the thing they share. The landscape's periodic decay, its terminals aging past the flows they were built for while their revenues are drawn off in forms that cannot be recalled for rebuilding, is the spatial fix's financial face: the fix is applied, the claims on it are sold, and the proceeds of the sale are consumed elsewhere while the fix itself remains, aging, as the inheritance the next round must work around. Analysts of the movement economy who confine themselves to operations will miss this entire stratum, in which the system's future is mortgaged in the literal sense, and the article marks it as the point where its subject joins the theory of finance, a junction the reader can pursue through the hub article's wider treatment of the modern system's forms.

## The chain and the consumer: demand as a command

The chain is usually described from its origins, in raw materials and components, toward its end in consumption, and the description's direction conceals the direction of command, which runs the other way. In the developed chain, it is the anticipated act of consumption that organises production: the retailer's forecast becomes the assembler's schedule, the assembler's schedule becomes the supplier's order book, and the supplier's order book becomes the working days at the base, each stage translating the command into a tighter and less negotiable form as it travels. The movement system is the command's nervous system, carrying the signal in one direction as instructions and in the other as goods, and its speed determines how late the command can be given and how exactly it can be revised, which is why the cheapening and acceleration of movement have been accompanied, paradoxically, by the growth of forecasting as an industry: the faster the chain can move, the more its governors are tempted to govern it by prediction, and the cost of a prediction's error is exported, by the mechanisms this article has traced, to the buffers held in people at every stage.

The consumer at the chain's end is therefore not the passive terminus the marketing vocabulary describes. The consumer's anticipated desire is the chain's constitution, the authority in whose name every schedule is set and every day at the base is lengthened, and the anticipation is itself manufactured by the apparatus of selling that the framework counts among the deductions. There is a bleak completeness in the circuit: surplus is produced at the base, transferred upward through the prices, captured at the apex, and spent in part on the manufacture of the desires that will command the next round of production, while the movement workforce, which executes the circuit's physical half, appears in its accounting as an overhead to be minimised and in its ideology as a service to be thanked. The article's framework does not exist to generate indignation at that circuit, which is merely the system working as the system works. It exists to make the circuit visible as a circuit, with its directions of command and transfer marked, since no one can act intelligently inside a machine they experience as weather.

## A short exercise in tracing a single commodity

The article's method can be compressed into one exercise, which readers may perform on any commodity whose biography they can partially know, and performing it once teaches more than rereading the table three times. Take a simple manufactured article, of the kind whose production is divided among places: a garment, a toy, a small appliance. Begin at its sale and walk backward. At the point of sale, separate the price into what the accounts would show and what the framework asks: the retailer's margin is capture and deduction together, the realisation apparatus paid from the whole. Behind the retailer stands the final carriage, productive transport, whose cost in the price is value genuinely added by the crews who performed it, small beside the total and absolutely real. Behind the carriage stands the holding, the distribution centre where the article waited, a deduction in rent and wages except for the handling that moved it through. Behind the holding stands the long carriage from the place of making, again productive, and at the place of making the production proper, where the article's value was principally created by the labour the chain's prices will least reward. Alongside every stage, place the paperwork, the orders, the inspections, the financing of the intervals, the insurance of the passages, and mark each as the deduction it is.

Now walk forward again with the quantities reversed, asking at each stage not what the stage cost but what it retained, and the biography changes its moral without changing a single fact. The stage that created the most retained the least. The stages that created nothing retained, in several cases, the most defensible margins in the chain, defensible because they are protected by the assets, the information, and the governance positions the earlier sections described. The exercise does not require the reader to invent figures. Its force is qualitative and structural: it shows that the two walks, backward through production and forward through capture, describe one object in two incompatible vocabularies, and that public discussion of the object is conducted almost entirely in the second vocabulary while believing itself to be using the first. A reader who has performed the exercise on one commodity will find it difficult to unsee, and will begin performing it involuntarily on others, which is the definition of an analytical habit and the proper success of an article of this kind.

The exercise also locates the exact point where the tradition's analysis and the reader's daily experience touch. Everyone has stood at the end of such a chain holding the finished article, and everyone has felt, without the vocabulary, the disproportion the exercise formalises, between the price in the hand and the imagined reward of the hands that made the thing. The framework does not create that feeling and does not need it. What it creates is the feeling's explanation: the chain of transfers, deductions, and captures, each link lawful, each link accounted, whose sum is the disproportion. Explanations of that kind are the tradition's whole claim on a reader's attention, in this territory as in every other the series treats.

## What ten years will not change in this subject

This article was written under a discipline that forbade it to name the passing scene, and the discipline's justification can be given, to close, as a list of the things that will still be true when the scene has changed beyond recognition. Transport will still produce value, because place will still be a constituent of use, and no technology on any credible horizon moves goods without labour, capital, and time. Storage will still divide by its function, and the apparatus of selling and recording will still be paid from the surplus it helps to realise. Chains will still be governed, will still transfer value toward the positions that govern them, and will still report the transfer as efficiency in the governors' accounts. The pressure on circulation time will still abolish pauses at the scale of the shift and still be celebrated as a rate of return at the scale of the year. Nodes will still concentrate dependence, workforces at them will still be collectively consequential and individually replaceable, and the contest between the leverage and its countermeasures will still be decided by organisation and law rather than by architecture. And the state will still guarantee the flow, socialising the costs that no capital can bear and designating as essential the workforces whose importance it is the guarantee's business to neutralise.

A reader who doubts any item on that list is invited to specify the mechanism by which it would change, and will find, in the specifying, that every candidate mechanism runs through the categories the article has used rather than around them. That is the sense in which the analysis claims durability: not that the world will stand still, but that its movement will be legible in these terms, because the terms name relations that the system's own operation continually reproduces. The article's particular claims, its readings of literatures, its conditional verdict on the chokepoint, are all open to correction by evidence, and its defeater stands where it was placed, at the head of the strategic argument, as a standing invitation to correct it. Its framework asks only to be used, and to be discarded, if it ever comes to that, for a better one, by the standard it has applied to everything else in its pages: not whether it is old or new, celebrated or unfashionable, but whether it separates what the accounts fuse and follows the costs to where they rest.

## The information of the chain: knowledge as a circulation cost

A chain must know itself to function, and the labour of that knowledge deserves classification, because it has grown into one of the largest components of the modern circulation apparatus. Tracking a consignment, forecasting a demand, reconciling the records of a hundred stages into a single picture on which decisions can be made: this is labour, much of it highly skilled, and under the functional test nearly all of it falls on the unproductive side of the line, since it records, predicts, and arranges the metamorphoses of value rather than producing the use-values whose metamorphoses they are. The classification surprises the information workforce as it once surprised the commercial clerk, and the article repeats, for their benefit, the distinction's standing disclaimer: unproductive is a relation, not a rank, and the analyst who designs the chain's information systems is as exploited, in the specific manner of unproductive labour, as the clerk whose bookkeeping the systems mechanised away. What the classification does predict, correctly, is the pressure on this labour: as a deduction, it will be mechanised without rest, its own practitioners building, in the ordinary course of their work, the systems that cheapen and then abolish their functions, a fate the framework reads not as irony but as the deduction logic working through a new material.

Yet the information of the chain has a second face that the classification must not be allowed to flatten. Insofar as knowledge of the whole operation is a condition of the movement labour process itself, of the sequencing that sends each vehicle to the right place with the right load, it belongs to the conception side of production, the planning that the labour process separates from execution and concentrates. The same system, and often the same worker, therefore stands on both sides of the line within a single shift: productive as the brain of the movement process, unproductive as the memory of the ownership process. The split-category pattern could ask for no purer instance, and the instance carries a strategic corollary that the literature has begun to notice. The workers who hold the chain's knowledge hold, in their heads and their systems, the map of the whole that no other participant possesses entire, and knowledge on that scale amounts to a form of the structural position this article has analysed in its physical form. Whether it can be converted into power is subject to the same conditions and the same defeater as the leverage of the node, and the article leaves it, as it leaves the larger question, in the conditional form the evidence supports.

## Waste, loss, and the shrinkage of the passage

Not everything that enters a chain leaves it, and the losses of the passage, the goods damaged, spoiled, stolen, misdirected, and written off, form a category the framework handles with characteristic dryness. Loss in transit is value destroyed without being realised, and its cost falls, by the arrangements of insurance and contract traced earlier, on whichever party the chain's governance has assigned it to, which is rarely the party whose carelessness caused it. The analytical interest of shrinkage is that it prices the passage's imperfection. A perfect circulation, instantaneous and lossless, is the limiting fiction toward which the system's innovations strain, and the actual rate of loss measures the distance from the fiction in the only units that finally matter to the accounts. The labour devoted to reducing the loss, the checking, the sealing, the surveillance of cargo and of the workforce alike, is circulation labour in its most defensive form, value spent to protect value, and its growth alongside the flow's growth is one more instance of the article's standing observation, that the apparatus of circulation expands with the production it serves even as each of its operations is cheapened.

There is a human corollary that the dry treatment should not conceal. In the moral economy of the warehouse and the depot, shrinkage is the charge under which the workforce lives: the suspicion of theft, organised into procedure, is one of the oldest instruments of discipline in the movement industries, and the searches, the sealed bags, the cameras over the packing bench are its architecture. The framework does not adjudicate the suspicion. It observes its function, which is to convert a statistical property of the passage, that some goods will always vanish, into a standing relation between the enterprise and every individual it employs, in which each worker is treated as the probable address of the loss. A workforce so addressed experiences the system's imperfection as a personal accusation renewed daily, and the experience belongs in any complete account of the labour process at the node, beside the pace and the measurement, as the third term of its discipline. The passage loses goods. The accounts assign the loss. The worker carries the suspicion. The three sentences describe one arrangement, and the arrangement, like everything else in this article, becomes visible only when the costs are followed to the place where they finally rest.

## The port of the imagination: why the node grips the public mind

Interruption at a node produces a public reaction out of proportion to the goods delayed, and the disproportion is itself evidence about the subject. The spectacle of a passage blocked, of vessels waiting, of shelves thinning, touches an anxiety that ordinary economic news does not reach, because it reveals, to people who never think about political economy, the fact on which this whole article rests: that the abundance surrounding them is not a stock but a flow, maintained by continuous labour at continuous risk, and that the margin between the stocked shelf and the empty one is measured in days of other people's work. The public's fascination with the great terminals, with the largest vessels and the vastest distribution sheds, has the same root. These are the places where the flow becomes visible as a flow, where the scale of the interdependence can be photographed, and the photographs circulate because they show the citizen of a developed economy the true size of the machine that feeds them, which the machine's ordinary operation is designed to conceal.

The tradition has a term for what happens in such moments of revelation, and the article will use it carefully. The relations of production, ordinarily hidden behind the relations between things, become briefly visible as relations between people: between the crews and the consumers, the schedulers and the suppliers, the state and the workforce it designates essential. Political economy's whole method is the attempt to make that visibility permanent and analytical rather than episodic and spectacular, to see in the calm what the crisis shows in the storm. A reader who has understood why the blocked passage grips the imagination has understood the method's starting point, and a reader who can then perform the analysis in the calm, classifying the activities, tracing the costs, and locating the leverage without waiting for an interruption to pose the questions, has acquired the method itself. The article's length is justified, if it is justified, by that acquisition, since methods are not transmitted by summary but by exercise, and the sections behind this one have been the exercises.

## The cooperative counterfactual: what the classification says to planners

Every analysis in this series is eventually asked what it implies for the organisation of things otherwise, and the question deserves a direct answer here, since the classification this article defends has a planning implication that is rarely drawn. If a society were to organise the movement of its goods deliberately, by a plan rather than by the competition of separated owners, the distinction between the costs of changing form and the processes of production would not merely survive the transition. It would become, for the first time, fully visible in the society's own accounts, because most of the costs of form exist only where ownership changes hands: the buying and selling, the competitive bookkeeping, the insurance against one another's failures, the apparatus of capturing value at the chain's joints, all presuppose the separation of owners that the plan would abolish. The transport would remain, and the storage that serves continuity would remain, and the handling at the joints would remain, because goods would still need to be where their users are. What would vanish is the shell, and the size of the shell, which the present system's accounts cannot isolate, is precisely what the classification measures. The counterfactual thus returns a quantity the present cannot give itself: the portion of the social working day that the separation of owners costs, over and above the movement the goods themselves require.

The counterfactual also disciplines a utopian error in the opposite direction. Planning would not abolish circulation time, the geography of production, or the vulnerability of nodes, since distance and dependence are physical before they are social, and a planned chain that held no buffers would be as exposed as a commercial one, with the difference that its exposure would be a chosen policy rather than an artefact of competing accounts. Nor would planning abolish the strategic question at the nodes, though it would change its form, since a workforce stationed where everything passes would hold its position under any system that moves goods at scale, and the terms on which that position is held, honoured, and limited would remain a political question of the first order. The tradition's founders understood this, which is why their remarks on the society to come are so much more reticent than their analysis of the society that exists. The analysis travels. The reticence should travel with it.

For the reader whose interest is practical rather than speculative, the counterfactual has a nearer use. It supplies the standard against which any particular circulation cost can be interrogated in the present: ask whether the cost would survive the abolition of separated ownership, and the answer separates the costs of distance from the costs of the market with a clarity no other test provides. The driver's journey survives the test. The duplicate bookkeeping of buyer and seller does not. The insurance of the passage against nature survives in altered form; the insurance of capitals against one another does not. Running the test across the table's rows gives the reader a second classification laid over the first, of the costs that moving goods entails in any society and the costs that belong to moving them under this one, and the second classification is, in the end, the tradition's whole critique of the present arrangement stated as an accounting exercise. An article that began with the question of whether a crate's journey produces value concludes, by that route, with an instrument for imagining the journey performed by a society that had abolished the question's conditions, and the gap separating the two is the distance the critique of political economy exists to measure.

## The household's own logistics: the unpaid passage

The chain's last passage is performed, in large part, without wages, and the analysis is incomplete until it has looked at the unpaid movement with which every household supplements the delivered economy. Goods that arrive at a shop rather than a door must be carried the final distance by their purchaser, in journeys whose time is counted in no account and whose labour appears in no sector's totals. Goods delivered to the door displace that labour onto the paid workforce this article has described, and the choice between the two arrangements, made afresh in every retail format, is a choice about where the final stage's cost shall rest: in the price, where it is visible and shared among all purchasers, or in the purchaser's own time, where it is invisible and distributed according to each household's endowment of hours, vehicles, and strength. The framework's category for the unpaid passage is the reproduction of labour power, within whose economics the journey to the shops belongs with the cooking, the cleaning, and the care that the wage does not pay for and cannot do without. The passage's invisibility in the accounts is therefore not an oversight specific to logistics. It is the general invisibility of the household's contribution to the economy's continuation, appearing at the chain's end in its most literally mobile form.

The development of delivery can be read, in this light, as a long negotiation over the boundary between the paid and unpaid passages, with each retail innovation moving the boundary and re-pricing the crossing. The self-service format moved the passage's labour, and much of the shop's internal movement labour besides, onto the customer, and charged less for the goods in exchange for the customer's work, a bargain whose terms the framework states exactly: circulation labour was not abolished but transferred to the one party who performs it without wages and records it nowhere. The return of delivery reverses the transfer, at a price, and the price's level relative to the value of the customer's time decides, household by household, which side of the boundary each purchase falls on. None of this requires new categories. It requires the old ones, applied past the point where the wage relation ends, and the exercise confirms the article's recurring lesson in its final form: that the costs of circulation are never abolished, only assigned, and that the assignments the accounts cannot see are the ones that fall, by default and by design, on the time of households and the hours of the replaceable. The chain that began in a distant factory ends, as it was always going to, in somebody's arms, carrying the goods the last few hundred yards, and whether those arms are paid is a question the price tag does not answer and the framework insists on asking.

## What the people who move things know

There is a body of knowledge about the system that exists nowhere in its literature, because it is held by the workforce in the form of practice rather than print. A driver learns which loading bays waste an hour and which turn a vehicle around in minutes, which schedules are honest and which are written to be failed, where a route's official geography diverges from the geography of weather, roadworks, and habit. A warehouse hand learns the true order of the building, which aisles jam, which goods arrive damaged whatever the paperwork claims, which of the system's instructions must be quietly disobeyed if the shift's work is to be finished at all. This knowledge is not folklore decorating the real process. It is a working model of the process, in many respects more accurate than the model held by the apparatus that plans the work, since it is corrected daily against outcomes while the apparatus's model is corrected only when its failures become too large to hide.

The labour-process analysis gives that knowledge its theoretical address. Where conception has been separated from execution, the knowledge of how the work actually goes migrates, in part, into the heads of those who execute it, because no plan survives contact with a wet road and a broken seal, and the gap between the plan and the day is bridged, invisibly, by the workforce's judgement. Management's systems perpetually attempt to harvest that judgement, encoding the drivers' discoveries as route data and the hands' discoveries as procedure, and each harvest narrows the gap in which the workforce's irreplaceability quietly lives. The contest over knowledge is therefore a second front in the same struggle this article has followed through pace and measurement: the system needs what the workers know, the workers' security partly consists in knowing it exclusively, and every improvement in the system's legibility is also a transfer of an asset from the floor to the office. Analysts who ask only about wages and hours miss the front on which the long war is actually fought.

For the strategic question, the knowledge point cuts in a direction the chokepoint debate has neglected. The leverage of a node rests partly on the flow's physical concentration, but it rests also on the concentration of operational knowledge at the node, on the fact that the people who could keep the passage working through an improvisation are the same people whose withdrawal is under discussion. Replacement labour can be hired to stand in the right places. It cannot be hired already knowing which bay floods, which customer must be called before arrival, which pallet will collapse if stacked as the system directs. That is one reason stoppages at nodes bite faster than headcounts suggest, and one reason the recovery from them lags the formal resumption of work. The system's dependence, in short, is epistemic as well as physical, and a complete account of where its power relations lie has to map what is known, and by whom, with the same care it maps what is owned. The tradition's old insistence that the worker is not a pair of hands but a bearer of capacities finds, at the node, one of its sharpest modern confirmations.

## The exam of the senses: a closing walk through a terminal

End with a walk, since the article's abstractions were all drawn from places a reader can picture, and returning to the picture with the instruments in hand is the surest test of whether the instruments work. Imagine arriving before dawn at a large distribution shed on the rim of a city, of the kind that has multiplied wherever routes converge and land is cheap. The yard is already lit and loud. Vehicles queue at the gates in an order nobody visible has decreed, yet the order holds, because each arrival was scheduled by a system that knows the state of every dock inside. On the floor, goods stream from the inbound doors toward the outbound ones along paths marked in paint, pausing in bays whose occupants turn over so quickly that the building is less a store than a river with a roof. Along the stream stand the people this article has been about, each with a device that speaks the next task, each moving at a pace set elsewhere, each, in the intervals the system cannot use, briefly and completely idle in the manner of a machine awaiting its instruction.

Now apply the table, as the reading exercise taught. The drivers at the gates and the hands along the stream are producing, in the strict sense, since what the enterprise sells is the onward journey of everything in the building and they are performing it. The clerks reconciling the night's discrepancies in the glass office above the floor are not, though their work is skilled and their absence would be felt within the hour. The stock in the slow bays at the far end, waiting out its season, is a deduction with a roof over it, while the reserve staged beside the outbound doors, without which the morning's loading would stall, belongs to production's own conditions. The manager walking the floor with a screen is doing both kinds of work in alternation, conceiving the movement in one minute and administering its paperwork in the next. Nothing about the scene has changed since the walk began. Everything about its meaning has, because the building can now be read, and a building that can be read can be argued about precisely, which is all that theory has ever promised anyone.

The walk ends where the article's strategic question lives. When the shift changes, the yard empties and refills within minutes, and the ease of that exchange is the employer's comfort and the workforce's warning, since hands that can be swapped so smoothly hold their power only together and only at the moments when the flow cannot wait. Yet the same building, viewed from the road at the height of the season, with the queue at the gates stretching out of sight and every dock committed, tells the other half of the truth: that the whole arrangement, for all its systems and substitutions, still passes, quite literally, through those hands, and that no one has yet built the version of it that does not. Between the comfort and the warning stands the open question this article has refused to close, and the reader who can now state it, with its dimensions separated and its defeater named, has finished the course the article set.

One habit, above all, is worth carrying out of the yard and into every other territory where this series works. Whenever a total is presented, in an account, a headline, or a theory, ask what the total is made of and whether its parts obey the same law. The economy's most consequential deceptions are rarely falsehoods about parts. They are truths about parts silently extended over wholes to which they do not apply: a margin that is partly production and partly capture, quoted as though it were either; a workforce that is partly indispensable and partly interchangeable, described as though it were one or the other; an industry that is partly a maker of value and partly an apparatus for realising it, praised or condemned as though it had a single nature. Against that habit, the distinction defended in these pages is a small and stubborn instrument. It asks, of each activity in turn, a plain question about what the activity does, and it refuses to be hurried past the asking by the size of the enterprise, the authority of the accounts, or the impatience of the audience. Readers who keep the instrument will find that it opens more than warehouses. It opens balance sheets, policy papers, and manifestos, anywhere that a fused total is doing quiet work on behalf of an interest. That is the use of a distinction, and it is why a few pages about the carriage of goods, written a century and a half ago in a volume few complete, still repay the closest reading this article could give them.

## For students and exam candidates

Students meeting this material in an examination setting should organise their preparation around the article's three portable instruments, since questions on logistics and value reward the application of instruments rather than the narration of the industry. The first instrument is the transport exception itself, which should be learned as a claim with its ground attached: transport is productive because the buyer of carriage purchases the displacement itself, and the ground, not the slogan, is what earns marks, since a marker can distinguish instantly between a candidate who can say why the crate's journey produces value and a candidate who has memorised that it does. Practise the ground until it can be stated in two sentences under pressure, and practise its edge at the same time: the same enterprise's storage, selling, and bookkeeping fall on the other side of the line, so the complete answer always has two halves, and a script that gives only the transport half has answered the easier question and left the discriminating one untouched.

The second instrument is the productive and unproductive table, which converts the distinction into a procedure an answer can perform in public. When a question presents an activity, a firm, or a sector, the strong script classifies activity by activity, states the criterion it is applying, and lets the mixed verdict fall where it falls, remarking that the mixture is the finding. That performance is exactly what the distinction was built for, and it separates candidates who possess the concept from candidates who possess a verdict about the sector. The third instrument is the pair of distinctions that govern the chain material: governance against value transfer, in the literatures, and value added against value captured, in the accounts. A script that can say what each literature takes as its object, name Gereffi for governance, Henderson and his co-authors for networks, and Selwyn or Smith for the critique, and then state the measurement point in one sentence, has covered the field at the level examinations reward, and the chokepoint material should be handled with the same discipline: state the argument, attribute it, state the objection at full strength, and close with the defeater, the evidence about absorption that would settle the matter. Scripts that present the chokepoint as a settled triumph read as advocacy; scripts that carry the defeater read as judgement, and the difference is visible to markers within a paragraph.

Two practical instruments support the preparation. The Marxist study notes tool at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) lets the productive and unproductive table be turned into a set of revision cards, one activity per card with its verdict and reasoning, which is the right grain for drilling a classification until applying it becomes reflex. For candidates in the civil service stream, the previous year question explorer at [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) allows the instruments to be tested against questions examiners have actually set on production, circulation, and the services, which is a sterner and more useful rehearsal than any invented question bank. Candidates continuing to the assessment of the whole cluster should proceed to [modern capitalism exam answers](/2021/03/15/modern-capitalism-exam-answers/), where the treatment of the present system is organised for timed conditions. The warning that closes every section of this kind in the series applies here with force: cite no figure for the movement of goods, for the stocks held in chains, for the employment of the sector, or for the costs of delay. The analysis in this territory is structural, the figures in circulation are unreliable in the specific ways the measurement section described, and a script that argues from the structure will outscore a script that decorates itself with numbers it cannot defend.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teachers will find in this article a teaching asset of an unusual kind: a theoretical distinction whose correct application contradicts the first intuition of nearly every student in the room, which makes it a diagnostic instrument for the quality of a seminar's thinking. The transport exception is the clearest available illustration of what the productive labour distinction actually turns on, because it separates, in a single case, the two intuitions that students habitually confuse, the intuition that production means making things and the doctrine that production means producing value under the command of capital. A seminar that begins by asking whether a driver produces anything will surface the confusion within minutes, in the students' own words, and the second volume's passage then lands as a resolution to a problem the room has felt rather than as a doctrine to be transcribed. Leaders should resist the temptation to give the answer first. The exception teaches itself, provided the question is allowed to divide the room before the text is opened.

The table is the seminar's second instrument, and it works best as an exercise rather than as a handout. Give the room an operation, a distribution enterprise of the kind described in the reading section, and require the classification of its activities in pairs, with the criterion stated for each verdict and the divided rows argued rather than assigned. The arguments over handling and sorting, over the manager who sequences the day's work, over the packaging that protects and the packaging that persuades, are the seminar's real content, since they force the criterion to do work in the students' hands and expose, in their disagreements, the exact points where the criterion needs sharpening. A leader who lets those arguments run, intervening only to return the room to the criterion when it drifts toward verdicts about the worth of jobs, will find that the distinction has been learned in the only form in which it is worth having, as a procedure the students can carry to cases the course never mentioned.

The chokepoint material serves a different pedagogical purpose: it teaches the handling of a live, contested argument with a named defeater. Set the argument and the objection as opposing briefs, require each side to attribute its claims to the literature's actual authors, and require the closing statements to specify what evidence would change the speaker's mind. Students trained on settled material often experience the exercise as a revelation, that an argument can be strong, well evidenced, and genuinely undecided, and that the undecidedness is a property of the evidence rather than a failure of the seminar. The transferable skill is the one this series most wants its readers to carry away: the habit of asking, of any confident structural claim, what the world would look like if the claim were false, and whether anyone has looked. Teachers who want the wider frame for the cluster will find it in the hub article already linked, and the present article's sections on the chain literatures can be set, profitably, against a governance textbook's own account of itself, so that students watch two literatures describe one chain and learn to ask what each description is for.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Researchers entering this field should begin from the article's measurement warning, since it determines what the available evidence can and cannot establish. The statistics of trade and production record transactions and residuals, not the expenditure of labour, and any research design that proposes to measure value transfer along a chain from published accounts alone has assumed away its central difficulty. The workable designs in the literature share a shape: they reconstruct a chain from the labour process outward, using workplace study, wage and hours data gathered at the stages, and the contracts that set the prices between them, and they treat the published accounts as evidence about capture rather than about production. Suwandi's factory research and Smith's reconstruction of trade figures are the models, in different registers, and the chokepoint literature's case studies supply the corresponding method for the strategic questions, following particular nodes through interruptions and, crucially, through the responses that followed, which is the follow-through the celebratory literature omits and which future research should treat as obligatory.

Historians will find the subject's archive unusually instructive, because the movement industries were early and thorough recorders of themselves. The systems that coordinate flow generate, as their by-product, a continuous account of what moved, when, by whose labour, and at what pace, and the historian of the future will inherit a record of the economy's daily operation finer than any previous age produced. The historian of the present, working backward, faces the opposite condition: the records of the casual era at the joints, of the great terminals and their hiring halls, survive in the archives of unions, of port and railway administrations, and of the public inquiries that interruptions periodically provoked, and those inquiries, with their sworn testimony about dependence and its costs, are among the richest sources for the social history of circulation. The framework's questions give that archive its research programme: to establish, for particular places and periods, where the buffer was held, in goods, in wages, or in people; how the classification of the workforce shifted as standardisation proceeded; and what the state guaranteed, and on what terms, at each stage of the landscape's building.

Journalists covering the subject, usually at the moment of an interruption, can take from this article a checklist that will distinguish their reporting from the standard crisis account. Establish what the node actually carries and for whom, rather than repeating that it is important. Establish where the buffer was held before the interruption and who is holding it now, in cancelled shifts and unpaid waiting as much as in empty shelves. Establish what the interruption is costing each party along the chain, and notice how the costs are being assigned while the emergency lasts, since emergencies are when the chain's constitution is rewritten in practice. Ask the system operator what duplication and rerouting exist and how long they take to engage, which is the defeater question in operational dress, and ask the workforce's representatives what organisation exists at the node, which is the durability question in its human form. Report the answers with the same prominence as the spectacle of the stoppage itself, and the resulting piece will still be serving its readers when the flow has resumed and the standard accounts have been forgotten, because it will have described the structure that the interruption revealed rather than merely the interruption. That is, in the end, the use of the framework for every kind of reader this article addresses: it converts events into evidence about arrangements, and arrangements, unlike events, are still there to be examined, and changed, after the headlines move on.

One habit of mind unites the three readerships this closing group of sections has addressed, and it can serve as the article's parting instruction to all of them. Distrust the aggregate. Behind every smooth total in this field, a sector's output, a chain's efficiency, a workforce's headcount, there stand unlike things performing unlike operations on value, and the first move of competent work, whether in a study, an archive, or a newsroom, is to break the total open and sort its contents by function before drawing any conclusion the total was designed to suggest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Does transport produce value in Marxist theory?

Yes, and the point is argued rather than assumed. Marx installs the carrying trade among the spheres of production in Capital's second volume, on the ground that a commodity's usefulness depends on where it is, so carrying it to the place of need finishes the work of making it serviceable. The wages of crews and drivers therefore behave like the wages of any productive workforce: part of the value they create replaces their own cost, and the remainder is surplus appropriated by the capital employing them. Freight is not an errand performed around production. On this account it is production, carried on by other means and in other places.

### Q: Is storage productive labour?

As a rule, no. Keeping goods in a building while they await a purchaser leaves every property of those goods untouched, and labour that changes nothing about a commodity cannot be adding to what the commodity is worth. Rent, guarding, insurance, and the wages of the keeping are accordingly paid out of gains made elsewhere. One qualification matters in practice: where a reserve of materials is what allows a productive process to run without pausing, holding that reserve is woven into production itself, and the labour involved borrows the productive character of the process it serves. The same shed can thus house both kinds of stock, and only the role of each pile decides its classification.

### Q: What is the chokepoint argument?

It is an argument about where fragility and bargaining strength coincide in a lean distribution system. When firms strip out spare stock and time their stages tightly together, the whole arrangement comes to depend on a few junctions where goods are transferred, sorted, and dispatched. The people working at those junctions are not numerous, but a refusal of work there does not stay local: stages waiting for inputs idle, stages holding outputs jam, and the costs mount far beyond the junction's own payroll. Writers in this vein argue that the pursuit of lean operation has quietly rebuilt a form of workplace leverage that the decline of the great factories was supposed to have ended.

### Q: What is the difference between value added and value captured?

The first phrase belongs to bookkeeping, the second to political economy, and confusing them is the error this article most wants to prevent. A firm's books show a gap between its sales and its bought-in costs, and that gap is labelled its value added, but the label describes a settlement, not an act of creation. Where along a chain the gap settles depends on bargaining strength: a buyer with many alternative suppliers can force prices down until much of the worth created by supplier labour shows up in the buyer's gap instead. Capture names that outcome, the retention of value made by hands other than the retainer's. Production is where labour works; capture is where the proceeds come to rest.

### Q: How do the supply chain literatures differ?

They differ first of all in the question each one asks. One tradition, linked with Gary Gereffi, studies how a dominant firm organises its suppliers, laying down specifications, prices, and deadlines, and how weaker firms might climb toward more rewarding functions. A second, linked with Henderson and his collaborators, studies how production settles into particular territories and what a place gains or loses by hosting it. A third, the Marxist line running through Selwyn, Smith, and Suwandi, complains that both of the others stop short of asking where the proceeds originate and whose labour they reward. Governance, settlement, and the flow of value are three objects, not one, and the literatures are best read as partial maps laid over the same terrain.

### Q: Is warehouse work a new labour process?

The honest answer is that its instruments are new and its logic is old. A handset or scanner now assigns each task, times it, and grades the performance against a standard the worker did not set and cannot inspect, which feels like a departure from every earlier workplace. Structurally, though, the arrangement is the one Frederick Taylor prescribed and Harry Braverman analysed: knowledge of the job gathered upward into a planning apparatus, execution left below in simplified, timed fragments. What the digital instruments add is continuity and granularity of measurement, not a new relation between the planner and the planned. Calling the warehouse unprecedented flatters its vendors and misleads its analysts, since it discards a century of accumulated understanding about how such a regime is experienced and resisted.

### Q: What did Marx say about circulation costs?

He treated them as the price an economy of separated owners pays for turning goods into money and money back into goods. Negotiating sales, keeping the ledgers that track them, and holding stock through the interval between making and selling all consume labour and resources, yet none of them improves the goods or enlarges what they are worth, because a transfer of ownership is not an act of making. Such outlays are therefore recovered from the surplus generated in production proper, and every firm feels them as a weight to be lightened, shifted to a trading partner, mechanised away, or handed to the public purse in the form of roads and standards. Transport stands apart in his treatment, for reasons the opening answer gave.

### Q: Do logistics workers have strategic power?

In one precise sense yes, in several others no, and the article insists on keeping the senses apart. Their positional strength is considerable: a coordinated stoppage at a busy junction idles far more capital than the strikers' own wages represent, because so much else waits on their work. Their individual strength is slight: tasks are designed to be learned quickly, hiring pools are kept deep, and any single worker can be swapped out with little loss. Skill levels, deliberately kept low by the design of the jobs, offer a third and weaker ground again. So the power is real but collective and perishable, depending on organisation arriving before employers finish duplicating the junction, automating its core tasks, or securing legal protection for the flow.

### Q: Where does logistics fall in the productive distinction?

It straddles the line, and the straddle is the finding. Hauling goods, and the loading and sorting that hauling requires, belong on the productive side, because being in the right place is part of what makes a thing useful and somebody is paid to put it there. Warehousing that is mere waiting, together with selling, invoicing, record keeping, and insuring, belong on the other side, as charges against gains made in production. Since one firm, one building, and often one job combine both sorts of activity, the only honest procedure is to sort task by task, which is what the productive and unproductive table in this article is for. Any verdict pronounced on the sector as a whole has answered a question the theory does not ask.

### Q: What would defeat the chokepoint argument?

A demonstrated pattern of painless absorption. If the record of stoppages and breakdowns at major junctions showed, case after case, that freight simply flowed around the obstruction or drew briefly on spare stock and that schedules recovered within a short span at modest expense, then the supposed leverage would be an artefact of drawing diagrams, and the argument would collapse into the observation that narrow passages look important on maps. The defeater is deliberately framed in observable terms, because the argument's friends and its critics alike should be able to agree on what evidence would settle their dispute, and because a claim that no record could embarrass is not a claim about the world at all.

### Q: How does the transport exception grade the logistics literature?

It works as a sieve with two meshes. The first mesh catches any treatment that assigns the whole sector a single character, productive or parasitic, since Marx's own handling forbids the shortcut by splitting the industry against itself. The second catches subtler work that classifies correctly but then reads firms' published margins as though they measured where value was made, forgetting that margins also register bargaining strength and shifted costs. Writing that passes both meshes, sorting activities by function and tracing proceeds to their origins in labour, earns the article's confidence whatever conclusions it reaches. Writing caught in either mesh may still be informative about management or geography, but it has not yet engaged the question of value.

### Q: What should a student separate between adding value and capturing value in a chain?

An examiner wants to see three operations distinguished and never blurred. Making value is the expenditure of labour under capital's direction, wherever in the chain that happens. Moving value is what prices accomplish when they settle above or below the worth a stage created, handing gains from one participant to another without any new creation. Keeping value is what the final accounts display, the residue each participant held when the settling stopped. The student who can point at a figure in a case study and say which of the three it represents, and who refuses to let a retention masquerade as a creation, has grasped the distinction the whole topic is built to teach, and can defend the grasp under follow-up questioning.

### Q: Why is transport productive when storage is not?

The contrast turns on what happens to the commodity in each case. Under transport the commodity gains the one property it lacked: availability where it is wanted, without which its other properties are useless to anyone. The buyer of carriage is buying that gain, and labour that produces a bought gain is productive by the theory's standard. Under storage the commodity gains nothing; it merely persists through a delay imposed by the gap between making and selling, and the labour of guarding its persistence is a charge on the proceeds, like the labour of selling it. Effort is not the criterion, nor is necessity, since both activities are necessary. The criterion is whether the activity completes a use-value or only shepherds one through the interval before its sale.

### Q: How does the productive and unproductive table order the activities?

It orders them by the job each one does, not by prestige, pay, or skill. Haulage stands first among the productive entries because displacement is the service being sold. Loading, unloading, and the sorting that steers goods toward their destinations follow as haulage's auxiliaries, while sorting done for inspection or display crosses to the other side. Keeping stock sits among the charges, apart from the narrow case of reserves that keep production running. Selling and office administration close the list as the pure costs of transferring ownership and recording the transfer. Each row also states the reasoning and the consequence for reading a firm's accounts, so the table can be applied to an unfamiliar operation without further theory.

### Q: What separates a chokepoint from a bottleneck anyone can route around?

The availability of a real alternative at the moment of failure. A bottleneck slows traffic and raises its cost, but the traffic still moves, because another passage exists with room to spare or because waiting is tolerable. A chokepoint offers no such release: the alternatives are absent, too small, too distant, or too slow, so pressure builds on both sides of the blockage until the whole arrangement stalls. The status is not a permanent property of a place, since building a second passage demotes a chokepoint into a bottleneck, and that demotion is exactly the countermove employers and states pursue once a junction's importance has been advertised by a stoppage. Hence the article's warning that leverage at a node is a wasting asset.
