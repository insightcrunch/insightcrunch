On the night between 22 and 23 December 1949, idols of Ram Lalla were placed beneath the central dome of the Babri Masjid in Ayodhya, and the 1949 Babri Masjid idol placement became, within a single week, something larger than a local crime or a local devotion. The claim defended in this article is that the episode established the religious-place-state-administration template through which India would manage contested sacred sites for the next seventy-five years. The template had three working parts, each fixed in the last week of December 1949: the state would administer a disputed religious place rather than adjudicate the rights at stake; a receivership would freeze the physical situation exactly as the administration found it, idols included; and the pendency of civil suits would supply the standing justification for never reaching a decision. Every later station of the Ayodhya dispute, from the lock opening of February 1986 to the demolition of 6 December 1992 and the Supreme Court judgment of 9 November 2019, operated inside arrangements whose architecture was drawn in Faizabad in December 1949 by named officials acting on dated orders that survive in the record.

## The Ground Before December 1949: A Mosque, a Platform and a Fence

The structure that Faizabad's officials locked in December 1949 had been disputed ground for generations, and the older history explains why the administration of 1949 reached for management rather than judgment. The mosque was raised in 1528 or 1529 by Mir Baqi, a commander of the Mughal emperor Babur, and Hindu belief held the site to be the birthplace of Ram, the janmabhoomi. For centuries the two claims coexisted in practice, with Hindu worship conducted at a platform in the outer courtyard, the Ram Chabutra, and Muslim prayer conducted inside the domed building. The coexistence was periodically violent. In 1855, fighting between Hindus and Muslims connected with the Hanumangarhi temple and the mosque left dozens dead, and in 1859 the British administration of Faizabad drew the arrangement that December 1949 would inherit: a fence separated the inner courtyard, reserved to Muslim worship at the mosque, from the outer courtyard, where Hindus worshipped at the Ram Chabutra. The fence was an administrative device, not a legal determination. No court had decided who owned the site. The colonial state had decided only who would stand where, and it policed the line.

The centuries before the fence had already taught the town the dispute's grammar. Ayodhya's place in the Ram tradition made the site sacred in Hindu devotion long before any mosque stood on it, and the claim that the mosque occupied the janmabhoomi, the birthplace itself, runs through the town's religious memory in forms older than the colonial record. The mosque's construction in 1528 or 1529 by Babur's commander Mir Baqi is documented in the building's own inscriptions as the record was read by later courts, and the tradition that a temple was destroyed for its raising became, across the following centuries, the Hindu claim's foundation, contested by historians on the evidence of the texts and the ground. The first recorded violence of the modern pattern came in 1853 and again in 1855, in the last years of Awadh's nawabi rule, when fighting around the Hanumangarhi and the mosque showed the provincial power of that day the same arithmetic Faizabad's magistrates would face in 1949: two congregations, one sacred geography, and a state whose authority depended on not being seen to surrender either. The British settlement of 1859, the fence, was the colonial answer, and it is worth pausing on its character, because 1949 inherited it whole. The fence decided nothing about God, truth or title. It decided where bodies would stand, and it put the state's force behind the standing places. For ninety years that sufficed, which is to say that the town's peace in those decades was an artefact of administration in exactly the sense December 1949 would renew.

The courts of the nineteenth century were asked for more and declined to give it. In 1885 Mahant Raghubar Das sued in the Faizabad court for permission to build a temple on the Ram Chabutra, and the suit travelled to the district judge, F. E. A. Chamier, who dismissed the appeal on 18 March 1886 with the observation that it was most unfortunate a mosque should have been built on land held sacred by Hindus, but that the event had occurred centuries earlier and could not be remedied by his court. The pattern of 1886 is the pattern of 1949 in embryo: a judge recording sympathy, a legal system declining substantive determination, and an administrative arrangement left to carry the weight. In December 1934, riots in Ayodhya damaged the mosque's domes, and the colonial administration repaired the damage with funds recovered through fines imposed on Hindu rioters, an early instance of the state absorbing the material consequences of the dispute without adjudicating it. Litigation between Shia and Sunni Muslims over the mosque's management produced a judgment on 23 March 1946 in which the civil judge held that the mosque, though built by a Sunni patron, had been used by both sects. By the winter of 1949, therefore, the site carried a layered inheritance: a fence from 1859, a dismissed suit from 1886, a state-funded repair from 1934, and a sectarian judgment from 1946 that concerned Muslim claimants alone. None of it had settled title. All of it had taught officials that the site's peace was an administrative artefact, maintained by arrangement and force, and that any disturbance of the arrangement would be answered by arrangement again.

The mosque also had an institutional life in the decades before the crisis, conducted through the waqf administration that governed Muslim endowments in the United Provinces, and that life matters because it shows the site's Muslim character as a matter of administrative routine rather than of assertion. An enquiry by the Commissioner of Waqfs in 1936 recorded the mosque as a Sunni waqf founded by Babur's commander, and the endowment's affairs, its mutawallis, its income and its repairs, ran through the waqf board's registers in the ordinary way of endowed property. When Shia claimants asserted a share in the mosque's character in the 1940s, the question went where endowed-property questions went, to a civil judge, whose judgment of 23 March 1946 held the founder Sunni and the usage shared. The litigation is a small thing beside what followed, and it is recorded here for what it proves about the pre-1949 order: the mosque's status was administered, registered and adjudicated within the normal machinery of the colonial and successor states, and Muslim prayer within its walls was an established, documented usage of the inner courtyard down to the week the idols entered it. The ouster of December 1949 was therefore not the interruption of a dormant claim. It was the termination, by night entry and administrative freeze, of a functioning religious use that the state's own registers and courts had recognised within living memory, and the Muslim plaintiffs who eventually sued in 1961 came to court as the representatives of an endowment in possession, not as petitioners for a favour.

The founding settlement of the republic was being drafted in the same months, and its religion-state architecture forms the backdrop against which Faizabad's improvisations must be read. The Constituent Assembly had debated the place of religion in public life through 1948 and 1949, producing the settlement traced in [the founding-period account of the Assembly's three tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) and in [the Assembly debates on secularism](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/). The Constitution that emerged protected religious freedom as a fundamental right, permitted state regulation of secular activity associated with religion, and said nothing that told a district magistrate what to do when idols appeared inside a mosque in the last week of December. The gap between the constitutional text and the district file is the space in which the 1949 template was built. What happened in Ayodhya also belonged to the unsettled aftermath of Partition, whose constitutional and demographic consequences are traced in [the account of Partition's constitutional consequences](/2019/01/08/partition-constitutional-consequences-india/): a republic two years from a subcontinental wound, governing a town where Hindu and Muslim residents lived with the memory of 1947's violence fresh, could not treat a mosque's desecration, as Muslim residents named the placement, or a deity's appearance, as Hindu devotees named it, as an ordinary question of trespass.

### What stood on the site when the crisis came?

The site held a domed mosque in its inner courtyard used for Muslim prayer, a Hindu platform called the Ram Chabutra in the outer courtyard, a fence line dating to 1859 separating the two usages, and no judicial determination of ownership, because every court asked since 1885 had managed the question rather than decide title.

That inheritance matters because it disciplines the claims that can honestly be made about December 1949. The officials who acted in that week were not confronting a settled property that a single night's trespass disturbed. They were confronting a site whose legal condition had been undefined for a century, whose physical condition was a managed partition of space, and whose peace had always depended on the state's willingness to hold the line the fence represented. The architectural reading defended in this article begins from that record. It asks not who behaved well or badly in the week of the placement, though the record permits judgments on that question and this article will render them where the documents permit. It asks what institutional machinery the week's decisions built, and it finds the machinery still running in 1986, in 1992, in the courtrooms of 2010 and 2019, and in the consecration of January 2024.

## The Night of 22 and 23 December 1949 and the Paper It Produced

The documentary spine of the episode is unusually firm for an event wrapped in devotional and accusatory retelling, because the Supreme Court's Constitution Bench, delivering its judgment on 9 November 2019, reconstructed the week from the district administration's own files, which the Allahabad High Court had called for and placed on record in May 2009. The Bench recorded that in the days before the placement, a large presence of bairagis, Hindu ascetics, had built up in the outer courtyard, and that the Superintendent of Police of Faizabad had expressed his apprehension in writing that Hindus would seek forcible entry into the mosque to install idols. The Bench recorded that the deputy commissioner and district magistrate, K. K. Nayar, whose name the judgment's extracts also spell Nayyar, paid no heed to the written intimations and rejected the superintendent's apprehension as baseless. The apprehension was borne out. On the night between 22 and 23 December 1949, a group of fifty to sixty persons scaled the walls and installed idols of Ram, Sita and Lakshman on the pulpit beneath the central dome of the mosque. The men most consistently named in the accounts of the placement are Abhiram Das, a mahant of the Hanumangarhi tradition who led the group that entered, and his associates Ramsevak Das Shastri and others of the Ayodhya ascetic orders. The organisation that had prepared the ground, holding a nine-day recitation of the Ramcharitmanas at the site in the preceding days and campaigning for the temple cause in Ayodhya through 1949, was the Akhil Bharatiya Ramayan Mahasabha.

The police paper began at once. On 23 December 1949, Sub-Inspector Ram Dubey of the Ayodhya police station lodged the first information report, naming Abhiram Das and recording that fifty to eighty persons had entered the mosque, placed the idols, and damaged the structure's doors and locks in the process. The report charged offences of rioting, trespass and defiling a place of worship under the Indian Penal Code. A criminal case, in other words, existed from the first morning: the placement was, on the state's own paper, an unlawful act by identifiable men, and no official at any level in December 1949 described it as anything else in writing. Nayar himself, in his reports to the provincial government, acknowledged the installation to have been an illegal act. What the officials disputed was never the act's legality. What they disputed was the remedy, and the dispute over remedy is where the template was born.

The discovery on the morning of 23 December drew crowds of Hindu devotees to the mosque gates for darshan, the sight of the deity, and the administration's first moves were crowd moves: the gates were secured, Muslim worshippers could no longer enter the inner courtyard, and the district's senior officials converged on the site. Among them, alongside Nayar, stood the city magistrate Guru Datt Singh, an officer whose devout sympathies were a matter of local knowledge and whose later accounts of the week, like Nayar's, defended the administration's course. Muslim residents of Ayodhya protested the placement to the administration as a desecration and demanded the idols' removal and the mosque's restoration. The demand was legally straightforward, since the criminal case conceded the entry's illegality, and it was the demand that the Prime Minister of India would take up within three days. The administration on the spot declined it. In its place, over the six days from 23 to 29 December, the Faizabad and Lucknow officialdom constructed the arrangement that the rest of this article anatomises: the idols would stay, worship at them would continue under official management, the building would pass into a receiver's custody, the gates would be locked against both congregations' free entry, and the questions of right would be referred to civil courts that had not been asked a title question in sixty-four years and would not answer one for seventy.

### Who entered the mosque that night, on the surviving record?

A group of fifty to sixty men entered on the night between 22 and 23 December 1949 and installed the idols beneath the central dome. The first information report named Abhiram Das among the entrants, and the Supreme Court's 2019 judgment, working from the district files, recorded the same scale and the same night.

The devotional counter-account must be stated at its strongest, because the stance of this series requires every position to be rendered as its holders render it. For the devotees who gathered on 23 December and for the ascetic orders that had campaigned in Ayodhya, the idols had not been placed but had appeared: Ram Lalla had manifested himself at his birthplace, and the language of prakat, of divine self-revelation, entered the movement's vocabulary from that morning and never left it. The strength of the account, as an account of belief, is not in question, and the political weight of the belief across the following decades is a fact of the record. What the documentary record establishes, and what the Supreme Court in 2019 held as fact, is the human sequence: written police warnings before the night, a dismissed apprehension, an entry by dozens of men, a first information report naming accused persons, and an administration that conceded the entry's illegality in its own correspondence. The article's architectural argument does not require any reader to surrender a theology. It requires only that the state's machinery be described from the state's paper, and on that paper the placement was an act of men, recorded as a crime, whose consequences the state chose to administer rather than reverse.

## The Refusal and the Letters: Nayar, Pant and Nehru in the Last Week of December

The week's decisive act was a refusal. The Government of Uttar Pradesh, led by Chief Minister Govind Ballabh Pant, sought the views of the man on the spot, and K. K. Nayar reported that the idols could not be removed without provoking grave disorder, that the depth of Hindu feeling behind the movement should not be underestimated, and that the administration should instead take the property into custody and let the courts decide. The reasoning deserves statement in its strongest form, because it was the reasoning of an experienced district officer in a province two years from Partition, in a town crowded with devotees, with a police force he judged insufficient to force the mosque against a mobilised crowd without bloodshed. Nayar's position, maintained in his official reports and defended in his later writings, was that a magistrate's first duty in such a week is the preservation of life and public order, that the removal of the idols by force in the last days of December 1949 would have ignited violence in Ayodhya and beyond it, and that the lawful course open to him was custody, prosecution of the offenders, and reference of the civil questions to the civil courts. His critics' position, also deserving statement at its strongest, was A. G. Noorani's: that the district officer had been warned in writing, had dismissed the warning, had then declined to enforce a law he conceded had been broken, and that custody on such terms rewarded the trespass and taught every future mobiliser the lesson that a fait accompli, once accomplished, would be administered rather than undone. The documentary record supports the factual core of both statements. The warnings existed and were dismissed. The illegality was conceded and the removal refused. What the two positions divide over is the evaluation, and the evaluation is marked disputed in this article wherever it appears.

Delhi's answer came from the Prime Minister. Jawaharlal Nehru wrote to Pant on 26 December 1949, three days after the placement, in terms the correspondence preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library has made canonical: the affair distressed him deeply, a dangerous example was being set in Ayodhya which would have bad consequences, and the mischief should be undone. Nehru's position was constitutional before it was tactical. A republic that had just written religious freedom and equality before law into its founding text could not, in his stated view, permit the seizure of a minority's place of worship to stand as an accomplished fact, whatever the local cost of reversal, because the precedent would instruct every district in India in the profitability of such seizures. He pressed the point in the correspondence that followed into January 1950, asking what steps the provincial government proposed to take and making plain that the Union government regarded restoration of the position before 23 December as the required outcome. The exchange is the founding document of the centre-state tension that the brief for this article names, and it belongs to the larger Nehruvian settlement whose institutional baseline is traced in [the account of the Nehruvian era's three tensions](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/).

Pant's replies defended the provincial course. The chief minister, himself a figure of the founding generation and no communal partisan by record or reputation, reported the district assessment upward: feeling in Ayodhya ran too deep for removal without serious violence, the property was being taken into official custody, the offenders faced prosecution, and the rights of the parties would be determined by the courts, whose processes the attachment would protect. Pant's position, at its strongest, was the position of a government responsible for order in a province that had absorbed Partition's refugee movements and communal killings within living memory of every official concerned: the state would not shed blood to reverse in a week what the courts existed to decide in law, and custody was not surrender but the ordinary legal instrument for property in dispute and in danger. The outcome of the exchange is a matter of record. Nehru's view did not prevail. The idols were not removed. The provincial course, custody and courts, became the republic's course, and the Prime Minister's letters entered the archive as the road not taken. Historians have argued the counterfactual ever since: whether removal in December 1949 would have settled the Ayodhya question at the cost of a week's disorder, or merely displaced the conflict into a martyrology with a longer reach. The counterfactual is undecidable and is marked as such. What is decidable is the institutional residue, and the residue is the subject of the three sections that follow.

The letters also had an afterlife in the Prime Minister's own doctrine, which the record of his fortnightly letters to chief ministers through the early 1950s documents in his hand. Nehru used those letters, the founding decade's standing instrument of centre-state persuasion, to warn the provinces repeatedly against the pattern Ayodhya had set: local officers bending before majoritarian pressure, provincial governments sheltering behind process, and minorities learning that their rights held only where no crowd objected to them. He treated such episodes, in the letters' recurring argument, as the seedbed of a communalised administration, the one corruption the republic could least afford in its first decade. The doctrine failed at Ayodhya in his own tenure and under his own signature's weakness, since the letters to Pant changed nothing on the ground at Faizabad, and the gap between the doctrine and the outcome is the founding era's honest epitaph on the episode. Delhi knew the right answer in December 1949, wrote it down within three days, filed it, and governed otherwise for seventy years. The template's first choice was made in Faizabad, but it was ratified at the centre by acquiescence, and the ratification, not the refusal alone, is what later custodians inherited as precedent: proof, at the highest level of the founding state, that a prime minister's stated constitutional position could be left unenforced against a province's plea of order, at no political cost to anyone who left it so.

The Union home minister's part in the exchange completes the founding triangle and explains why Delhi's pressure spent itself in letters. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, who held the home portfolio and the Congress organisation's discipline in the same hands, was informed of the Ayodhya events as they unfolded, and his counsel to the province, expressed in his correspondence of the winter, ran against forcible removal: the dispute, in his stated view, was one for settlement by negotiation between the communities and determination by the courts, not for the bayonet, and he cautioned Lucknow about the conduct of officials whose sympathies might compromise the administration's neutrality. Patel's position deprives the week's outcome of any simple partisan reading. The strongest figure of the Hindu traditionalist wing of the Congress counselled process over restoration just as the secular Prime Minister demanded restoration over process, and Pant, situated between his two seniors, chose the course his home minister's counsel legitimised. Patel's death on 15 December 1950, a year after the lock-up, removed the one authority in the ruling party who might have revisited the settlement from within, and the arrangement he had counselled as an expedient hardened, in his absence, into the permanence this article describes.

One further feature of the week deserves emphasis before the anatomy begins, because it conditions everything the template would later do. No politician ordered the week's outcome in the form it took. The outcome was assembled by administrators: by a district magistrate who refused a remedy, by a city magistrate who managed worship at the locked gates, by an additional city magistrate who would sign the attachment order on 29 December, and by a chief minister and a prime minister whose disagreement was conducted in letters and resolved by the letters' failure to move the province. The template was therefore bureaucratic in its authorship even where it was political in its effects. That authorship mattered to its durability. An arrangement made by politicians can be unmade by politicians at the next election. An arrangement made by magistrates, recorded in orders, and wrapped in pending litigation acquires the inertia of procedure, and procedure, in the Ayodhya matter, would outlast every government that inherited it.

## Architectural Choice One: The State Administers, the Court Does Not Decide

The first architectural choice of December 1949 was jurisdictional. Faced with a seized place of worship, the Indian state had two instruments available. It could treat the questions at stake, ownership, worship rights, the legality of the idols' presence, as questions for prompt judicial determination, using the criminal case to restore possession to the congregation that had held it on 22 December. Or it could treat the questions as matters for administrative management, taking the property into state custody and leaving the rights to whatever litigation the parties chose to bring at whatever pace the courts chose to keep. The Faizabad administration, ratified by Lucknow and acquiesced in by Delhi, chose the second instrument completely. No official sought an urgent judicial ruling on possession. No court was asked to decide, in December 1949 or January 1950, whether the idols' presence was lawful, although the administration's own correspondence conceded it was not. The civil judge who entered the matter in January 1950 entered it because a devotee, Gopal Singh Visharad, sued for the protection of his worship, not because the state sought a determination of the rights it was administering.

The consequence of the choice was to relocate the dispute's centre of gravity from law to custody. Whoever held the keys held the dispute, and the state held the keys. For the Muslim residents of Ayodhya and for the Sunni Central Waqf Board that would sue in 1961, the relocation meant that the recovery of their mosque depended on persuading either the custodian who had declined to restore it or a court system that had been handed the question without urgency and without a state plaintiff. For Hindu devotees and the ascetic orders, the relocation meant that the worship established by trespass acquired an official manager, and that the object of future politics would be the terms of management: how often the gates opened, how near the devotee might approach, whose priest would serve the deity. The politics of the Ayodhya dispute for the next forty years was exactly that politics of managed access, conducted through magistrates' orders, receivers' arrangements and interim injunctions, and it was the politics the first architectural choice created. A dispute over title would have had plaintiffs, defendants and a decree. A dispute over custody had applicants, permissions and a lock.

Administration also distributed responsibility in a way adjudication would not have. A judgment names a loser. Custody names no one. The Faizabad arrangement of December 1949 allowed every actor in the chain to describe his own part as provisional and procedural: the district magistrate had preserved order, the province had preserved the peace, the Union had been assured that the courts would decide, the courts in time would observe that the parties had not pressed for decision. The template's first choice thus built deniability into the structure, and the deniability proved to be among its most inherited features. When the locks were opened in 1986, the opening would be executed by officials within hours of a district judge's order, and governments at Lucknow and Delhi would describe themselves as mere implementers of a judicial direction. When the mosque was demolished in 1992, the state government of Uttar Pradesh would describe its assurances to the Supreme Court as having been overwhelmed by a crowd. The grammar of those later exculpations was composed in December 1949, in the decision that the state would hold the place and the question, and answer for neither.

### Why did no court decide possession in December 1949?

The state never asked one to. The Faizabad administration chose custody under the criminal procedure's preventive provisions over seeking a judicial restoration of the mosque, the criminal case addressed the trespass alone, and the first civil suit came a month later from a Hindu devotee protecting worship at the idols, not from any authority seeking a ruling on title.

The choice also set the terms on which the dispute could be known by the wider republic. A title suit would have generated pleadings about 1528, about waqf law, about the archaeology of belief, of the kind that eventually filled the Allahabad High Court's record six decades later. Custody generated instead a vocabulary of order: status quo, attachment, receiver, injunction, words that convert a question of right into a question of management and allow officials to speak of the dispute without ever stating what it is about. Readers of the Indian press across the 1950s and 1960s met the Ayodhya matter, when they met it at all, in that vocabulary, as a locked property under a receiver with suits pending, a formulation that concealed the fact that a congregation had lost its mosque by night entry and that the state had declined to give it back. The concealment was not a conspiracy. It was the natural product of the first architectural choice, and it is one reason the dispute could sleep for a generation and wake with its grievances intact.

## Architectural Choice Two: Receivership and the Frozen Moment

The second architectural choice was executed on 29 December 1949, six days after the placement, in an order whose text survives and repays attention. Markendey Singh, Magistrate First Class and Additional City Magistrate of Faizabad-cum-Ayodhya, recorded himself satisfied from police reports and other credible sources that a dispute between Hindus and Muslims over rights of proprietorship and worship in the building claimed variously as Babri Masjid and Janmabhoomi Mandir was likely to lead to a breach of the peace. Acting under section 145 of the Code of Criminal Procedure of 1898, the preventive provision for disputes over immovable property likely to cause such a breach, and treating the case as one of emergency, he attached the building and directed the station officer of Ayodhya to place the attached property in the charge of Priya Dutt Ram, chairman of the Municipal Board of Faizabad-cum-Ayodhya, who would be receiver and would arrange for the care of the property in dispute. The parties were directed to appear on 17 January 1950 and file written statements of their claims to actual possession. With that order, the week's improvisation acquired legal form. The mosque became, in the eye of the state that administered it, an attached property under a receiver, and the congregation that had prayed in it on 22 December became one claimant party among two, invited to state its claim to a magistrate.

Three features of the arrangement built the second architectural choice. The first is that the freeze preserved the situation of 29 December, not of 22 December. The idols placed by trespass remained beneath the central dome, in the receiver's custody, maintained and served. The status quo the law protected was the status quo the trespass had created, and the asymmetry was not incidental: a freeze at the moment of custody is a decision about which moment counts, and the decision of December 1949 counted the moment after the entry. The second feature is that worship continued under management. The receiver arranged for a priest to perform the daily service of the idols inside the locked structure, and Hindu devotees were permitted darshan from outside the gates, at a distance and through the grille, on the pattern that would persist, with variations of access, until 1986. Muslim entry to the inner courtyard ceased entirely, and the mosque's use for prayer, which the record shows had continued into December 1949, ended. The third feature is that the arrangement had no term. Section 145 proceedings are designed as a bridge to civil adjudication of possession, swift and interim by intention. In Ayodhya the bridge became the residence: the receiver's custody, renewed and defended through decades of interlocutory litigation, lasted until the demolition swept the structure away in 1992, and the priest serving an idol in a locked mosque under a magistrate's receiver became one of the most durable institutional images of the Indian state's management of religion.

The receivership's daily operation deserves a closer look, because the template lived in its details. The attachment of 29 December fastened on the inner structure and its courtyard, the ground the entry had seized; the outer courtyard, where the Ram Chabutra stood and Hindu worship had proceeded for generations, continued its established use outside the receiver's charge, so that the freeze's geography reproduced the old fence of 1859 with a new interior fact. The receiver held the attached property's keys, engaged and paid the priest who served the idols, supervised the devotees' darshan at the gates, and answered to the magistrate for the building's care, rendering the accounts and reports of a custodian whose ward was a crime scene the state had decided to furnish as a shrine. Repairs, festivals, the arrival of ascetic parties, the occasional attempt by Muslim residents to test the gates: each generated its file, its application, its order, and the pile of such paper in the Faizabad record rooms is the material residue of choice two. Administration had not merely frozen a building. It had built a small, durable bureaucracy around the freeze, with a budget, a staff of one priest and a succession of receivers, and bureaucracies of custody, once built, develop the custodian's instinct for their own continuance, which in Ayodhya meant that the arrangement's administrators became, across the decades, a constituency for the arrangement.

The arrangement's defenders, in 1949 and in the historiography since, rest on its even-handed surface. Both congregations were locked out of free entry; neither party's claim was recognised; the building was preserved from destruction at a moment when its destruction by crowd action was a stated fear of the administration; and the receiver was a municipal notable rather than a partisan of either congregation. Sushil Srivastava, in The Disputed Mosque: A Historical Inquiry of 1991, the scholarly treatment most sceptical of the administration, answers that the even-handedness was formal only: the Hindu claimants had gained by the freeze the one thing they had sought and could not lawfully win, the deity's installed presence and continuing worship, while the Muslim claimants had lost by the freeze the one thing they had lawfully held, their mosque's use, and that a custody which conserves an accomplished wrong is a judgment in everything but name. Defenders of the Faizabad officers answer in turn that the alternative to the freeze was not a courtroom but a riot, that the mosque stood intact for forty-three years under the arrangement, and that the courts to which the parties were referred were open to the Muslim claimants throughout, as their own suits of later years demonstrate. The factual elements of the arrangement are not in dispute. The evaluation of a freeze that conserved an entry conceded to be illegal is evaluative ground on which reasonable readers of the same record divide, and this article marks it disputed while noting that the architectural consequence is independent of the evaluation: whatever its motives, the freeze taught the template's second lesson, that the state in a religious-place conflict would conserve the physical facts on the ground at the moment of intervention, and that those who wished to change a sacred geography should therefore change it first and negotiate the conservation afterwards.

The instrument Markendey Singh used had a pedigree that shaped its Ayodhya career, and the pedigree deserves a paragraph of its own. Section 145 descended from the criminal procedure codes of the nineteenth century, drafted for an empire that expected its magistrates to smother disputes over land, water and shrines before they flowered into affrays, and its design assumptions were the empire's: possession for the moment, not right for the record; a receiver's cool custody in place of a crowd's hot seizure; the civil courts, in their own time, for everything else. Magistrates across India had applied it to contested temples, mosques, burial grounds and processional routes for generations, and in the ordinary run of cases the design worked as designed, because the parties to an ordinary dispute accepted the bridge and crossed it to a decree within years. Ayodhya exposed the design's hidden condition. The bridge holds only where both parties want the far bank. In Ayodhya the party advantaged by the freeze had every incentive to live on the bridge, the party dispossessed by it lacked the political weight to force a crossing, and the state that owned the bridge collected its toll in quiet. Section 145 did not fail in December 1949. It succeeded at exactly what it was built to do, prevent a breach of the peace in the week of application, and its success, renewed month by month for forty-three years, is the measure of how completely a preventive instrument can swallow the adjudication it was built to serve.

## The Three Architectural Choices of December 1949: Table 1

The argument of this article can be carried in a single table, which the surrounding prose has built and which later sections will use. Table 1, the three-architectural-choice map of December 1949, sets out each choice, the instrument that executed it, the immediate arrangement it produced, and the form in which later decades inherited it.

| Architectural choice | Instrument of December 1949 | Immediate arrangement | Inheritance in later decades |
|---|---|---|---|
| State administration rather than judicial determination | District and provincial executive action, 23 to 29 December 1949; Nehru's restoration demand declined | Custody of the disputed structure by the Faizabad administration; no urgent ruling on possession sought from any court | Managed access as the dispute's standing politics; governments of 1986 and 1992 describing themselves as implementers of circumstance and court order |
| Status quo preserved through receivership | Section 145 attachment order of Markendey Singh, 29 December 1949; Priya Dutt Ram appointed receiver | Idols installed by the entry of 22 and 23 December conserved under official custody; priest-served worship within, darshan from the gates, Muslim prayer ended | The frozen geography defended as neutral for forty-three years; the 1986 opening and the 1989 foundation ceremony negotiated as adjustments to the freeze |
| Legal-suit pendency as deferral mechanism | Civil suits from 16 January 1950; interim injunction restraining removal of the idols | The rights questions referred to litigation without a state plaintiff and without expedition; the injunction conserved the idols' presence pending a trial that kept receding | Suits consolidated, transferred and heard across 1950 to 2019; pendency cited by every government as the reason decision lay elsewhere, until the Supreme Court's judgment of 9 November 2019 |

Read as a map, Table 1 states the article's namable claim in tabular form. The 1949 Babri Masjid lock-up established the religious-place-state-administration template, and the architectural choices shaped the post-1949 trajectory through 2024, because each later crisis in the Ayodhya matter was processed by one of the three mechanisms the table records, and none was processed outside them until the Constitution Bench closed the litigation in 2019 on reasoning that itself indicted the second choice's product: the Bench held that the placement of the idols in 1949 and the ouster of Muslim worshippers that followed were acts done otherwise than by due process of law, a judicial verdict, delivered seventy years late, on the arrangement the freeze had conserved.

## Architectural Choice Three: Pendency as a Standing Answer

The third architectural choice was completed not by the administration but by the litigants and the courts, in the weeks after the attachment, and it is the choice that most requires the correction of a widespread misremembering, including one carried in the brief from which this article is written. The civil litigation that would justify seventy years of deferral began on 16 January 1950, when Gopal Singh Visharad, a Hindu devotee, filed the first title suit at Faizabad, praying for a declaration of his right to worship the installed deity and for a permanent injunction restraining the removal of the idols and interference with his worship. Within days the civil judge granted an interim injunction in those terms: the idols were not to be removed, and worship was not to be obstructed, until the suit was decided. Mahant Paramhans Ramchandra Das filed his own suit later in 1950, numbered 25 of that year, seeking the continuation of worship and darshan without check or interference; he withdrew it in time, its object largely secured by the Visharad injunction. Muslim plaintiffs came to the litigation far later than the standard summary suggests, and the correction matters to the architecture's meaning: the Sunni Central Board of Waqfs, with nine Muslim residents of Ayodhya among its co-plaintiffs, filed the fourth suit only on 18 December 1961, days inside the twelve-year limitation period, claiming the mosque and its land and demanding the idols' removal. Hashim Ansari, the Ayodhya tailor who would become the litigation's most dogged Muslim litigant and its oldest, entered as a plaintiff in that 1961 suit, not in January 1950. The pendency that deferred the Ayodhya question was therefore constructed, in its first decade, almost entirely by Hindu plaintiffs conserving the freeze and by a state that, having appealed the interim injunction in April 1950, let its resistance lapse and settled into the role of custodian.

The deferral mechanism's genius, if an emergent property of aligned incentives can be called genius, was that it required no one's continuing decision. The injunction conserved the idols. The receiver conserved the building. The suits, which multiplied with the Nirmohi Akhara's claim of 17 December 1959 to possession as the site's traditional custodian and the deity's own suit of 1 July 1989, conserved the question. Any official asked, in any year between 1950 and 1989, why the Ayodhya matter remained unresolved could answer truthfully that the matter was sub judice, that the courts were seized of it, that the government's hands were tied by the very pendency its own custody sustained. The answer was available to Congress governments and to their successors alike, and it was used by all of them. Litigation that in design is a path to decision became in operation a substitute for decision, and the substitution was the third architectural choice, ratified afresh by every adjournment, every transfer, every decade in which the Faizabad and later Allahabad courts carried the Ayodhya files without a hearing on the merits. When the Allahabad High Court finally decided the title suits on 30 September 2010, dividing the site in three, and when the Supreme Court heard the appeals and delivered its unanimous judgment on 9 November 2019, the litigation closed a question that the pendency mechanism had kept open for sixty-nine years, a span longer than the lives of most of the original plaintiffs, Hashim Ansari dying in July 2016 with the appeals still unheard.

The pendency acquired a fifth and decisive plaintiff in its fortieth year, and the addition illuminates the mechanism's deepest property, its hospitality to new conservators. On 1 July 1989 the deity itself, Bhagwan Sri Ram Virajman, sued through a next friend, Deoki Nandan Agarwal, a retired judge of the Allahabad High Court, claiming the site as the deity's own by the juristic personality Hindu law accords an installed idol. The fifth suit changed the litigation's metaphysics and its strategy at once: the plaintiff could not die, could not be substituted away, and held a claim that no settlement among the human parties could extinguish, since the deity's next friends could always disown a compromise as unauthorised. The suits were withdrawn that same year from Faizabad to a special bench of the Allahabad High Court at Lucknow, where their consolidation formalised what the deferral mechanism had long practised, the treatment of five causes as one permanent condition. A mechanism designed by no one had acquired, by 1989, a plaintiff designed to be immortal, and the suits' subsequent pace, evidence through the 1990s, excavation in 2003, judgment in 2010, reflected a litigation whose structure rewarded continuation over conclusion at every joint.

The Faizabad phase of the suits, across the 1950s, framed the questions the litigation would carry for its whole life, and the framing itself served the deferral. Issues were struck on whether the building was a mosque and waqf property, on whether the Hindu plaintiffs held enforceable rights of worship at the installed deity, on the effect of the 1949 entry upon possession, and on the parties' respective user of the inner and outer courtyards across the preceding century. Evidence was recorded in the manner of a title suit of the old school, witness by witness, document by document, through commissioners and translations, in a court whose list carried the matter as one file among thousands. Nothing in that procedure was corrupt, and little in it was swift, and its very ordinariness was the mechanism's protection: a question that had convulsed a district and divided a cabinet was processed, year after year, as routine civil business, its urgency dissolving into the list. By the time the Nirmohi Akhara and the Waqf Board had joined the queue, the suits had achieved the condition the third architectural choice describes, a litigation whose continuation required no decision from anyone and whose conclusion no one's tenure required.

### What did the January 1950 injunction actually restrain?

The interim injunction restrained removal of the idols from beneath the central dome and restrained interference with the worship conducted at them under the receiver's arrangement. It decided nothing about ownership, nothing about the mosque's character in law, and nothing about the legality of the entry, yet by conserving the position it governed the site for decades.

The third choice completes the template, and its interaction with the first two explains the system's stability. Administration without adjudication (choice one) meant the state held the site. Receivership (choice two) meant the state held it in the configuration the trespass had made. Pendency (choice three) meant the state could hold it indefinitely while attributing the holding to the judiciary. Each choice covered the others' exposure. A government pressed on custody pointed to the suits. A government pressed on the suits pointed to the courts' independence. A court pressed on delay pointed to the parties and the record's immensity. The Ayodhya dispute accordingly became the exemplary Indian case of a conflict that every institution was processing and no institution was deciding, and the template's exportability, the lesson other religious-place disputes would draw, was that processing is a stable political condition, cheaper in every currency than decision. The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act of 1991, which froze the religious character of every other Indian place of worship as it stood on 15 August 1947 and exempted Ayodhya alone from its freeze, is the template's legislative monument: Parliament generalised the freeze for the whole republic while carving out the one site where the freeze of 1949 had already failed to settle anything.

## The Template at Work: The Lock Opening, the Demolition and the Judgment

The decades in which the template merely conserved are quickly told, because conservation was their content. The receiver's priest served the idols; devotees took darshan at the gates; the suits aged; Faizabad's courts heard interlocutory matters; and the Ayodhya question, frozen in the posture of December 1949, played almost no part in the national politics of the 1950s, 1960s and 1970s. Its stillness is itself evidence for the architectural reading. A dispute that the long-arc framing treats as a fuse burning steadily toward 1992 in fact lay inert for thirty-five years, and its inertness was manufactured by the template: so long as custody, freeze and pendency held, there was nothing for politics to grip. The movement that eventually gripped it had to be built outside the template, by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's campaign from 1984 and the mobilisation traced in [the account of the Rath Yatra and the Ayodhya movement](/2019/10/10/advani-rath-yatra-ayodhya-movement/), and its first demand was precisely a change in the terms of custody: the opening of the locks.

The opening, when it came on 1 February 1986, displayed the template's mechanics in their purest later form. A Faizabad lawyer, Umesh Chandra Pandey, applied for the locks' opening; the district judge, K. M. Pandey, ordered the gates opened for Hindu worship; the order was executed by the district administration within the hour, with television cameras admitted to record the first darshan; and the government of Rajiv Gandhi at Delhi and the government of Uttar Pradesh at Lucknow each presented the event as a judicial act which the executive merely implemented. Muslim organisations answered by organising the Babri Masjid Action Committee, and the political symmetry that followed, in which the same Congress dispensation that opened the locks also legislated on the Shah Bano judgment to Muslim applause, taught both constituencies that access to the frozen site was a currency of electoral management. Note what the 1986 episode did not contain: any determination of rights. The lock opened because a judge varied the terms of custody, in a proceeding about access, while the title suits slept. The template's first choice, administration over adjudication, had acquired a judicial dialect without changing its substance.

The foundation ceremony of 9 November 1989, the shilanyas at which the Vishwa Hindu Parishad laid the first consecrated brick on land adjacent to the disputed structure with the acquiescence of the Rajiv Gandhi government, extended the same logic: the freeze was preserved in form, its boundary negotiated in fact, and the negotiation conducted as administration. Then, on 6 December 1992, the template met the event it could not process. A crowd of kar sevaks, assembled at Ayodhya by the movement's organisations after assurances of symbolic service, climbed the domes and demolished the mosque over the course of that day, while the Uttar Pradesh government of Kalyan Singh, which had assured the Supreme Court that the structure would be protected, failed to protect it. The demolition is treated in its own place in this series, and here it matters for one architectural reason: it was the violent repudiation of the 1949 settlement by the constituency the settlement had half satisfied. Custody had conserved the idols but denied the temple; pendency had deferred the title past two generations; and the movement built on those frustrations concluded that the frozen geography would yield only to the method that had created it, an accomplished physical fact. The state's response to the demolition reverted at once to template: the Union government acquired 67.7 acres at Ayodhya by the Acquisition of Certain Area at Ayodhya Act of 1993, took the site into central custody, froze access afresh, and referred the title back to the courts, where the suits, revived and consolidated, ground forward to 2010 and 2019. Even the destruction of the structure could not destroy the architecture built around it. The receiver's logic simply changed landlords.

Between the reimposition and the end stands the Allahabad High Court's judgment of 30 September 2010, the template's strangest fruit and its partial repudiation. A three-judge special bench, after years of evidence that included an Archaeological Survey excavation ordered by the court itself in 2003, divided the disputed site in three shares among the deity Ram Lalla, the Nirmohi Akhara and the Sunni Central Waqf Board. The majority's reasoning treated the 1949 placement with a leniency that critics, Noorani among the most unsparing, named as the judgment's original sin: the idols' installed presence figured in the division as a fact of possession to be accommodated, rather than as a trespass to be disregarded, and the ouster of the mosque's congregation receded behind the arithmetic of shares. Defenders of the 2010 verdict answered that a court decreeing after sixty years of accomplished facts could not pretend the facts away, and that partition of the site at least converted custody into ownership and ended the receiver's century. The Supreme Court stayed the judgment in May 2011, calling the division's basis surprising, since no party had prayed for it, and the appeals that followed carried the whole record, the 1949 file at its heart, to the Constitution Bench. The episode confirms the architectural reading from the judiciary's side: even the court that finally heard the merits in 2010 could escape the template only by splitting the frozen geography, and it took the republic's highest bench, nine years later, to do the thing the template existed to prevent, which was to decide the title outright and to name 1949 a wrong in the deciding.

The Supreme Court's judgment of 9 November 2019, delivered by a unanimous five-judge Constitution Bench, closed the litigation the template had sustained. The Bench awarded the disputed site for the construction of a Ram temple under a trust to be created by the Union government, directed the allotment of five acres at Ayodhya to the Sunni Central Waqf Board for a mosque, and decided the title on the balance of the archaeological, documentary and worship evidence across centuries. For this article, the judgment's treatment of 1949 is its pivotal feature. The Bench held that the Muslims had been ousted from the mosque in December 1949 otherwise than by due process of law, that the placement of the idols beneath the central dome was an act of desecration of the mosque, and that the wrongs of 1949, like the destruction of 1992, which the Bench called an egregious violation of the rule of law, could not be rewarded even within a judgment that awarded the site to the Hindu claimants on the title evidence. The republic's highest court thus closed the deferral by condemning the fait accompli the deferral had conserved, and in doing so supplied the architectural reading with its strongest documentary support: the arrangement of December 1949 had conserved an illegality for seventy years, and the illegality, not the conservation, is what the law of 2019 saw when it finally looked. The consecration of the Ram temple at the site on 22 January 2024, performed by the Prime Minister as chief patron of the ritual, completed the trajectory the template had channelled: a dispute the state declined to decide in 1949 was decided in the end by litigation, legislation and electoral politics in combination, each operating through channels whose courses the Faizabad week had dug.

Even the settlement's execution bore the template's fingerprints, in the administrative forms through which the judgment was delivered into fact. The temple rose not by a congregation's private act but under the Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra, the trust the Union government created under the judgment's directions in February 2020, a state-chartered body holding a state-awarded title, so that the site's final disposition was administered into being by the same species of instrument, official custody under public authority, that had governed it since the receiver took the keys in 1949. The mosque's side of the decree moved through the counterpart channel: the Sunni Central Waqf Board accepted the five acres allotted at Dhannipur, some twenty-five kilometres from the disputed site, constituted the Indo-Islamic Cultural Foundation to hold them, and proceeded by application, approval and fundraising toward a mosque whose foundation was laid in January 2021 and whose construction advanced at the pace of administered projects rather than of congregational urgency. Nothing in the post-2019 landscape was left to the unmediated action of either community. The republic that had declined to decide in 1949 decided at last in 2019, and then, true to the habits of seventy years, administered its decision through trusts, boards and allotments, so thoroughly that the template may be said to have survived its own supersession: adjudication arrived, and administration, its elder sibling from the Faizabad week, carried the judgment home.

## Prelude, Procedure or Template: The Historians' Disagreement

Three readings of December 1949 contend in the literature, and the stance of this series requires each to be rendered at its strongest before adjudication. The long-arc-prelude reading, carried by much journalistic history and by the movement's own historiography in mirror image, treats 1949 as the first act of a drama whose last act was 1992: the idols' placement lit a fuse, the lock-up stored the explosive, and the demolition was the detonation toward which the whole post-Independence history of Ayodhya tended. The reading's strength is narrative truth of a kind: the actors of 1989 and 1992 consciously invoked 1949, the deity whose darshan the movement demanded was the deity installed that December, and the site's frozen condition was the movement's founding grievance. Its weakness is the thirty-five years of stillness it must explain away, and its tendency to read the choices of 1949 backward from the choices of 1992, as though Nayar and Pant were drafting for an audience of kar sevaks unborn in their week.

The legal-procedural reading, whose most distinguished carrier is A. G. Noorani in The Babri Masjid Question, 1528 to 2003, his documentary history of 2003, treats December 1949 as a failure of legal process by identifiable officials: a warned magistrate who would not act, a province that would not enforce, a centre that would not compel, and a judiciary that conserved the trespass by injunction and then slept. Noorani's reading is built from the record itself, file by file, and its strength is that almost every document he prints supports his sequence of defaults. Where the prelude reading dissolves agency into destiny, Noorani restores it to named men with named duties, and any honest treatment of the week must absorb his demonstration that the placement was foreseeable, foreseen, and unprevented by the officer charged with preventing it. Sushil Srivastava's The Disputed Mosque of 1991 stands adjacent to Noorani, an academic inquiry that reached the district record earlier and judged the administration's conduct with similar severity, while Hilal Ahmed's Muslim Political Discourse in Postcolonial India of 2014 turns the lens around, examining how the Ayodhya matter, 1949 included, functioned within the political speech of Indian Muslims, as an emblem of the minority's conditional citizenship in the secular republic. Ahmed's contribution disciplines both other readings: the meaning of the lock-up was never only administrative or only Hindu; it was also a text read by Muslim India for what it disclosed about the state's protection, and that reading shaped Muslim politics for generations.

A fourth body of writing must be placed in the disagreement before adjudication, the movement's own historiography, because the stance of this series extends to it the same strongest-form courtesy. Writers sympathetic to the temple cause, among them Koenraad Elst in his 1990 study of the dispute, argued that the evidence of a temple beneath the mosque was compelling, that the Nehruvian state's treatment of the Hindu claim was the era's real partisanship, and that the idols' installation, whatever its legal character, expressed a civilisational claim that the secular republic lacked the vocabulary to hear. The strength of that writing, at its strongest, is its exposure of the founding state's selective decisiveness, its willingness to rebuild Somnath by executive act in the same years it pleaded helplessness at Ayodhya, a contrast this article's Somnath section has already pressed into service. Its weakness is the mirror of the prelude reading's: it needs the night's entry to be an epiphany rather than a trespass, and the district file, the police warnings, the named accused, will not carry that transfiguration. The devotional account of the appearance belongs to faith and is honoured as faith in this article's pages. As historiography of the state's conduct it fails on the state's own paper.

The architectural-template reading defended in this article does not deny the documents of the second reading or the invocations of the first. It subsumes them as the account of what the week's choices built. The prelude reading cannot explain the stillness of 1950 to 1984, because a fuse does not wait thirty-five years; the procedural reading cannot explain the stillness either, because a mere failure of process predicts drift and accident, not the systematic reproduction of the same custody, freeze and pendency across changes of party, generation and regime. The template reading explains the stillness as the system's normal operation, explains 1986 as a variation of custody terms, explains 1989 as a boundary negotiation of the freeze, explains 1992 as the template's violent overthrow from outside, and explains the post-1992 decades as the template's reimposition under central custody until a court, at last, decided. It also explains the export of the form: the Places of Worship Act of 1991 generalised the freeze, and the disputes of Kashi and Mathura have been processed since 1991 through the identical grammar of custody, character-freeze and pendency. An interpretation that explains the sequence, the stillness and the export is to be preferred to interpretations that explain the sequence alone, and on that criterion the architectural reading adjudicates the disagreement in its favour. The framework it extends is the one this pillar opened with: the contested settlement of Indian secularism described in [the pillar's account of secularism as a contested settlement](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/), in which the state's management of religion proceeds by arrangements that defer the constitutional question they administer.

## The Strongest Objection: Improvisation, Not Architecture

The objection that must be answered, and answered fairly, is that December 1949 produced no architecture at all: that frightened and exhausted men, governing a partitioned subcontinent with a skeletal police, in a province raw with refugee movement and communal memory, improvised a week's expedients, and that to call their expedients a template is to mistake the historian's pattern-making for the actors' design. On this objection, the stillness of the following decades needs no architectural explanation, only the ordinary inertia of Indian litigation and the ordinary reluctance of politicians to spend capital on a locked building in a provincial town. The objection is the strongest available because much of its premise is true. Nothing in the record suggests that Nayar, Pant or Nehru intended to design a seventy-year system. Nayar intended to get through the week without a massacre. Pant intended to hold his province. Nehru intended the opposite outcome and lost. If architecture requires intention, there was none.

The answer is that institutional architecture, in the sense this article uses the term, is a property of consequences, not of intentions, and the test is behavioural: did later actors, facing later crises at contested religious places, reach for the instruments of December 1949 because those instruments lay ready, legitimised and staffed? The record answers that they did, at Ayodhya in 1986, 1989, 1992 and 1993, and across India in the legislation of 1991. A district judge varying custody in 1986, a Union government acquiring and freezing in 1993, a Parliament freezing religious character nationwide in 1991: these are not coincidences of improvisation but the reuse of a repertoire, and a repertoire that reliably reproduces itself through the hands of actors who did not invent it is an architecture by any functional test. The men of December 1949 built better than they knew, and worse. What they built kept a disputed building standing and a town largely peaceful for four decades, and it also kept a congregation dispossessed for seven, conserved an avowed illegality as a civic fact, and taught every observer that in a conflict over sacred ground the Indian state would process, conserve and defer far longer than it would decide. Both columns of that ledger belong to the template, and the article's adjudication, rendered on the primary record of the week and its seventy-year operation, is that the architectural-template reading is the one under which the whole evidence, the crime and the custody, the letters and the lock, the stillness and the storm, coheres.

A word, in fairness, about the officers' own later testimony, since the objection from improvisation often rests on it. Nayar defended his December decisions for the rest of his life, in statements and interviews that repeated the district file's reasoning: the warning had seemed to him exaggerated in a season of rumours, the entry had been a wrong he prosecuted, and removal would have lit the province. Guru Datt Singh's memory in Ayodhya's tradition casts the city magistrate as the deity's quiet protector within the administration. These testimonies are evidence of what the officers believed about themselves, and the article has used them as such. They are not evidence that no template formed, because a template is indifferent to the self-understanding of its builders: the receiver's priest kept his hours for decades after both officers had left Faizabad, under governments that had never heard their names, which is the behaviour of an institution and not of a memory. The improvisation objection, absorbed at full strength, thus refines the claim without defeating it. December 1949 was an improvisation. It was also, in the strict institutional sense, a founding: the week in which the Indian state, meaning to survive until January, built the machine that would manage its most dangerous sacred dispute until the century's children pulled the machine down and a court, seventy years on, finally did the deciding the machine existed to postpone.


## The Week in the Town: Ayodhya's Residents Between the Entry and the Lock

The district file speaks in the state's voice, and the town that the file governed deserves a hearing in its own, because the template's costs and mercies were distributed among named streets and households. Ayodhya in December 1949 was a pilgrim town of temples, akharas and mosques, its economy woven around the Saryu's ghats and the calendar of festivals, its Hindu ascetic orders among the most organised religious bodies in northern India, its Muslim residents a minority of artisans, traders and mosque servants who had lived through Partition's alarms in a province that had seen refugee columns and retaliatory killings within two years' memory. The nine-day recitation the Ramayan Mahasabha staged before the entry filled the town with visitors and expectation. When the news of the idols spread on the morning of 23 December, the devotees who streamed to the mosque gates came from this town and its hinterland, and the fear that governed the administration's week was not abstract: it was the fear of what a crowd in festival temper and a minority in festival streets might do to each other before the district's thin police could interpose. That fear, whatever use officials later made of it, was not invented, and the week's mercy belongs in the ledger beside its wrong: no riot consumed Ayodhya in December 1949, and the town's Muslims, though dispossessed of their mosque, were not massacred in their lanes, an outcome that Partition's recent history had taught every resident to price above almost any legal restitution.

The Muslim residents' own conduct in the week is the record's rebuke to the charge that the administration faced two mobs. The congregation and the mosque's custodians responded to the entry with petitions, deputations and demands for restoration addressed to the district and provincial authorities, the constitutional repertoire of a citizenry invoking its rights, and the paper trail of their representations runs through the same files that record the refusal. Figures like Baba Raghav Das, the Gandhian ascetic who represented the Faizabad region in Parliament and whose authority crossed the town's communities, worked the channels of Congress politics for a settlement in the week's aftermath, and the failure of those channels is part of the story: the settlement channels existed, were used, and produced custody rather than restitution, because the men staffing them shared the administration's pricing of order above decision. The town thus supplied the template with its social foundation. A minority that petitioned rather than rioted could be deferred at low cost. A majority's devotees whose crowd temper set the week's price could be conserved at low cost. The residents of Ayodhya learned the lesson their rulers taught, and the bitterness that later decades harvested in the town's politics was sown in the discovery, made by both communities in the same week, that the state answered paper with custody and crowds with conservation.

## The Officers of the Week: What the District File Shows About the Men Who Decided

The template's authorship was bureaucratic, and the district file lets the bureaucrats be seen as men rather than as functions. K. K. Nayar arrived at Faizabad as district magistrate in 1949, an officer of the Indian Civil Service formed in the late colonial service, posted to a district whose twin towns carried a sacred geography that every subdivisional officer learned on arrival. His surviving paper from December 1949 shows a consistent official: the reports concede the entry's illegality without hesitation, estimate the crowd's temper as the governing fact, and recommend custody as the instrument that keeps both the peace and the question. What the file also shows, and what the Supreme Court's 2019 recital fixed in the judicial record, is the warning he set aside. The superintendent of police had reported the bairagis' build-up in the outer courtyard and the intention to enter the mosque and install idols, in writing, before the night. Nayar's rejection of the warning as baseless was the week's first consequential decision, taken before any idol moved, and it converted the placement from a prevented crime into an accomplished one. His defenders observe that a magistrate who sealed the mosque against the district's ascetics in the week before Christmas 1949 would have needed forces he did not command and would have fought the same crowd at the fence instead of at the gate. His critics observe that the warning's specificity, a named intention against a named building on an imminent night, made the dismissal a choice rather than an error of judgment under uncertainty. The record carries both observations. It does not carry any evidence that Nayar ordered, planned or welcomed the entry, and accounts that promote him from negligence to conspiracy exceed the paper they cite; the strongest honest charge on the file is the one Noorani pressed, foreknowledge unacted upon and illegality unreversed.

The city magistrate, Guru Datt Singh, occupies a different place in the record and in the town's memory. The arrangements for worship at the locked structure in the days after 23 December, the priest's service within and the devotees' darshan at the gates, were administered through his office, and Ayodhya's Hindu tradition remembers him with gratitude as an officer sympathetic to the deity's cause, a memory later retellings enlarged into claims of active complicity in the placement itself. The documentary record supports sympathy and administrative facilitation of the post-entry worship. It does not document participation in the night entry, and this article treats the stronger claim as unproven on surviving paper, attributed where it appears to the movement's hagiography rather than to the district file. The distinction matters to the architectural argument. The template did not require conspirators. It required only officers whose sympathies, cautions and order-first instincts aligned with the fait accompli at each decision point, and December 1949 supplied such officers at every point where the week turned.

Above the district stood the province, and the provincial file shows the same alignment operating through constitutional forms. Pant's government asked its district officers for the facts, received Nayar's assessment, and adopted it as the province's policy, defending it to Delhi in the language of order and of courts. The chief secretary's office and the home department processed the week as a law-and-order file with a judicial sequel, which is to say they processed it in the only grammar the template needed. Scholars and students who reconstruct the week from its paper commonly lay the documents in sequence to watch the grammar form, the police warning, the magistrate's dismissal, the first information report, the refusal of removal, the attachment order, the prime ministerial protest arriving after the custody it protested; teachers of the episode often keep the sequence assembled in a [presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) so that each document can be read against the order it produced. Read in that sequence, the week's most striking feature is the absence of any document in which an official proposes deciding the question. Restoration is proposed, by Nehru. Removal is refused, by Nayar. Custody is ordered, by Markendey Singh. Decision is proposed by no one, because decision was the one output the machinery was not built, that week, to produce.

## The Managed Geography: Ayodhya Between the Lock-Up and the Lock Opening

The thirty-four years between the attachment of December 1949 and the mobilisation of 1984 test the architectural reading better than any argument, because they show the template in its steady state. The geography the receiver took charge of on 29 December 1949 settled into a routine that residents of Ayodhya could set their calendars by. Inside the locked gates, the receiver's priest performed the daily round of service to the idols beneath the central dome, waking, bathing, feeding and resting the deity in the rhythm of temple worship conducted in a mosque's shell under a magistrate's authority. At the gates, Hindu devotees received darshan through the barred entrance at permitted hours, a darshan at a distance that the ascetic orders accepted as custody's terms and resented as custody's insult in roughly equal measure across the decades. On Ram Navami and other festivals the crowds at the gates swelled and the administration reinforced the lock it described as temporary. Muslim residents of Ayodhya did not enter the inner courtyard again. The mosque's congregation dispersed to the town's other mosques, and the building whose name the suits carried stood silent as a place of Muslim prayer from December 1949 until its destruction, a fact the Supreme Court in 2019 recorded as the ouster that the placement had effected.

The litigation that was supposed to resolve the arrangement instead administered its longevity. The criminal case arising from the first information report of 23 December 1949, the one proceeding that addressed the entry itself, languished in the Faizabad courts and was never pursued to a judgment on the entrants' guilt, so that the state which had conceded a crime in its correspondence declined for decades to prove it in its courtrooms. The civil suits advanced at the pace the deferral mechanism set. The Visharad injunction, granted in days in January 1950, stood unshaken as the site's effective constitution. The province's appeal against it, filed in April 1950, faded from prosecution. Paramhans Ramchandra Das withdrew his suit, its object secured by the injunction. The Nirmohi Akhara's 1959 suit added a third Hindu claimant family to the queue without disturbing the arrangement, and the Waqf Board's 1961 suit, the Muslim claim's arrival twelve years after the ouster it challenged, joined a docket whose hearing on the merits no participant expected within a political lifetime. Orders passed, commissioners recorded evidence, parties died and were substituted, and through all of it the receiver's successors held the keys and the priest kept the hours. An arrangement designed in section 145 as a bridge measured in weeks had become, by the 1970s, the oldest continuous institution of the Ayodhya dispute, older than the republic's Constitution itself by a matter of days.

The steady state also shaped what the dispute could mean to the town that lived inside it. For Ayodhya's Muslim families, the locked mosque stood in the town's centre as a daily, visible instruction in the terms of their citizenship: a place of worship taken by night, conceded as taken in the government's own paper, and withheld for a lifetime by processes no neighbour could be blamed for and no election could alter. Hilal Ahmed's work on Muslim political discourse traces how Ayodhya entered Muslim political speech across these decades as the standing example of the secular state's protective promise unkept, long before the demolition made the example national. For the town's Hindu residents and its ascetic orders, the same locked gates taught the reciprocal lesson: that the deity installed in 1949 was held on sufferance, worshipped through bars at a magistrate's pleasure, in a building the state called disputed and the devotee called a prison, and the language of the deity's imprisonment that the movement of the 1980s would make famous was coined in the experience of the managed geography. Custody, in short, did not pacify the dispute's meanings. It incubated both grievances at low temperature for a generation, and it guaranteed that when politics returned to Ayodhya it would return as a quarrel over the terms of custody, which is exactly the quarrel the lock opening of 1986 ignited.

Politics returned by a route the template's authors could not have foreseen and the template's logic fully explains. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad, founded in 1964, adopted the recovery of Ram's birthplace as a national campaign at its Dharma Sansad of 1984, and the demand it carried to Ayodhya was framed entirely within the managed geography's terms: not a decree of title, which the courts had withheld for thirty-five years, but the opening of the locks, a variation of custody. The movement's genius, or its opportunism, the evaluation divides opinion and is marked disputed, was to see that the template had made access the dispute's real currency and to demand payment in that currency. The shilanyas of 1989, the rath yatra of 1990 and the assault of 1992 each escalated the same demand past the point at which custody could satisfy it, and the demolition stands in this article's argument as the moment the claimant of 1949's method, the accomplished physical fact, was turned against the settlement of 1949 itself. The men who climbed the domes on 6 December 1992 had learned from the record what the district file taught: that in Ayodhya, facts made on the ground were conserved, and law arrived afterwards to describe them.

## The Contrast of Somnath: A Founding Decision the State Did Take

That the template was a choice rather than a necessity of the founding years is proven by the founding state's opposite performance at the other end of the sacred map, at Somnath in Gujarat, in the very years of the Ayodhya lock-up. The Somnath temple, sacked in the eleventh century and revered in Hindu memory as Ayodhya's twin in grievance, lay in Junagadh, whose nawab's attempted accession to Pakistan in 1947 brought Indian troops and a provisional administration. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, the Union home minister, visited Somnath on 13 November 1947 and announced on the spot that the temple would be rebuilt. The decision was executive, immediate and unembarrassed: the Government of India associated itself with the reconstruction, K. M. Munshi carried the project as its political organiser, a trust executed the work without litigation over the site's title, and on 11 May 1951 the President of India, Rajendra Prasad, attended the installation of the deity against Nehru's expressed misgiving about official participation. No receiver held Somnath. No injunction froze it. No suit deferred its future to a court that never sat. The founding state, confronted with a ruined temple on undisputed ground freed by a princely accession's reversal, decided, built and consecrated within four years of independence, in the same seasons in which it was declining to decide a mosque's possession two provinces away.

The contrast is instructive precisely because the two cases differ in the one respect that explains the divergence, and the difference vindicates the architectural reading rather than the partisan ones. At Somnath there was no living congregation dispossessed by the decision, no rival worship continuing on the site, and no crowd whose temper a magistrate had to price into his orders; the costs of decision were ceremonial and diplomatic, and the state paid them gladly. At Ayodhya the costs of decision were the costs Nayar itemised: a mobilised crowd, a minority's rights, a province's peace. The founding state's religion-place policy was therefore never a policy of decision or a policy of deferral in the abstract. It was a pricing mechanism, deciding where decision was cheap and deferring where decision was dear, and December 1949 is the founding document of the deferral price. Later decades repriced the same calculation repeatedly: the lock opening of 1986 was purchased when its price fell, the Places of Worship Act of 1991 froze every other site's character at its 1947 posture precisely to keep their prices from being quoted, and Ayodhya itself, the exempted site, was decided in the end only when the demolition had destroyed the status quo whose conservation had been the deferral's object and a five-judge bench could distribute a decision's costs across a unanimous judgment, a trust, and five acres of alternative land. Somnath shows the state could decide. Ayodhya shows what it built when it would not, and the building is the template this article has anatomised.

The template's grammar has since been applied, clause by clause, at the two sites the temple movement named after Ayodhya, Kashi and Mathura, and the application confirms that Faizabad's repertoire became the republic's default software for sacred disputes. At Varanasi, the Gyanvapi mosque standing beside the Kashi Vishwanath temple has been processed through custody and managed access for decades, its character frozen by the Places of Worship Act of 1991, its disputes channelled into suits over worship rights and, in time, into court-ordered surveys and inspections that vary the terms of knowledge without disturbing the terms of possession. At Mathura, the Shahi Idgah beside the Krishna Janmabhoomi has run the same course: suits filed, the 1991 Act's bar pleaded, interim arrangements conserving the standing geography. In neither city has the state decided the underlying question. In both, it administers, conserves and defers, in the forms December 1949 composed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What happened at Babri Masjid in 1949?

On the night between 22 and 23 December 1949, a group of fifty to sixty men entered the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya and installed idols of Ram Lalla beneath its central dome. A police report named accused persons that morning, crowds gathered for darshan, and the Faizabad administration secured the site. The district magistrate, K. K. Nayar, declined to remove the idols, judging removal a danger to public order, and on 29 December 1949 the property was attached under section 145 of the criminal procedure code and placed with a receiver, Priya Dutt Ram. The gates were locked, Hindu worship continued inside under official arrangement, Muslim prayer in the mosque ended, and the questions of right were referred to civil litigation that began in January 1950.

### Q: Who placed the idols in Babri Masjid?

The first information report lodged at Ayodhya police station on 23 December 1949 by Sub-Inspector Ram Dubey named Abhiram Das, a mahant associated with Ayodhya's ascetic orders, among a group of fifty to sixty persons who entered the mosque during the night and installed the idols beneath the central dome. The organisation that had campaigned at the site through 1949 and held a nine-day Ramcharitmanas recitation in the days before the entry was the Akhil Bharatiya Ramayan Mahasabha. Devotees described the event not as a placement but as the deity's appearance, and that devotional account became the movement's own telling. The criminal case arising from the police report never produced convictions that settled individual responsibility, so the record identifies the named accused and the entering group rather than a judicially determined list of entrants.

### Q: Who was K.K.K. Nayar?

K. K. Nayar was the district magistrate and deputy commissioner of Faizabad in December 1949, the officer on whose decisions the Ayodhya outcome turned. An officer of the Indian Civil Service, he received written warnings from his superintendent of police that Hindus intended to enter the Babri Masjid and install idols, and he rejected the apprehension as baseless. After the entry he acknowledged in his reports that the installation was an illegal act, yet refused to order the idols' removal, reporting to the Government of Uttar Pradesh that the depth of Hindu feeling made removal a danger to public order. His refusal overrode the restoration demanded by Prime Minister Nehru, and the custody arrangement he favoured became the site's governing settlement. He later left the civil service, joined the Bharatiya Jana Sangh and sat in the Lok Sabha.

### Q: What did Nehru say about the Babri Masjid?

Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru wrote to Uttar Pradesh Chief Minister Govind Ballabh Pant on 26 December 1949, three days after the idols were placed in the Babri Masjid, stating that the affair distressed him deeply and that a dangerous example was being set at Ayodhya which would have bad consequences. He held that the mischief should be undone and the position before 23 December restored, on the ground that a republic founded on religious freedom and equality before law could not let the seizure of a minority's place of worship stand as an accomplished fact. He pressed the demand in correspondence continuing into January 1950. The letters, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, record the founding era's clearest statement of the restoration position, and they failed: the provincial government's course of custody and litigation prevailed over the Prime Minister's.

### Q: When was the Babri Masjid first locked up?

The Babri Masjid was first locked up in the last days of December 1949, in the week after the idols were placed beneath its central dome. The administration secured the gates as crowds gathered on 23 December, and the lock became legal form on 29 December 1949, when Additional City Magistrate Markendey Singh attached the property under section 145 of the Code of Criminal Procedure of 1898 and placed it with a receiver. From that date neither congregation entered freely: a priest served the idols inside under the receiver's arrangement, Hindu devotees took darshan from outside the gates, and Muslim worship in the mosque ceased. The locked condition, varied only in its terms of access, endured until a Faizabad court ordered the locks opened for Hindu worship on 1 February 1986.

### Q: What is the 1949 receivership arrangement?

The 1949 receivership arrangement was the legal form the Faizabad administration gave to its custody of the disputed Ayodhya site on 29 December 1949. Acting under section 145 of the Code of Criminal Procedure of 1898, Additional City Magistrate Markendey Singh attached the building, finding a dispute likely to cause a breach of the peace, and appointed Priya Dutt Ram, chairman of the Faizabad-cum-Ayodhya Municipal Board, as receiver to care for the property. Under the arrangement the idols installed on 22 and 23 December remained in place and were served daily by a priest engaged under the receiver, Hindu devotees worshipped from outside the locked gates, Muslim prayer in the mosque ended, and both parties were directed to file their claims to possession before the magistrate. The receivership, designed as an interim bridge to civil adjudication, governed the site for decades.

### Q: How did the 1949 episode shape later events?

The 1949 episode shaped later events by fixing the machinery through which the Ayodhya dispute was processed for seventy years. Its custody arrangement kept the idols in place and the title undecided, so the politics of the dispute became a politics of managed access: the lock opening of 1 February 1986 varied the custody terms, the foundation ceremony of November 1989 negotiated the freeze's boundary, and the demolition of 6 December 1992 was the frozen settlement's violent overthrow by the movement it had half satisfied. After 1992 the Union government reimposed the template, acquiring the site, freezing access and returning title to the courts. The Supreme Court's judgment of 9 November 2019 closed the sequence, holding the 1949 placement an act of desecration and the ouster of Muslim worshippers a wrong done otherwise than by due process of law.

### Q: What did the first information report of 23 December 1949 record?

The first information report lodged at Ayodhya police station on the morning of 23 December 1949 by Sub-Inspector Ram Dubey recorded that a group of fifty to eighty persons had entered the Babri Masjid during the night, placed idols of Ram, Sita and Lakshman beneath the central dome, and damaged the mosque's doors and locks in forcing entry. The report named Abhiram Das among the accused and charged offences under the Indian Penal Code covering rioting, house trespass and defiling a place of worship. The document matters beyond its charges, which never ended in convictions settling the entrants' identities. It is the state's first paper on the episode, and it records the placement, from the first morning, as a criminal act by identifiable men, the characterisation every later official inquiry and the Supreme Court's 2019 judgment worked from.

### Q: Did the district administration receive warnings before the idols were placed?

Yes. The Supreme Court's Constitution Bench, reconstructing December 1949 from the district administration's own files in its judgment of 9 November 2019, recorded that a large presence of bairagis had built up in the mosque's outer courtyard in the days before the placement, and that the Superintendent of Police of Faizabad had expressed his apprehension in writing that Hindus would seek forcible entry into the mosque to install idols. The judgment recorded that the district magistrate, K. K. Nayar, paid no heed to the written intimations and rejected the superintendent's apprehension as baseless. The forewarning is a settled documentary fact, not a retrospective accusation, and it is the foundation of the strongest criticism of the Faizabad administration: that the entry of 22 and 23 December was foreseen by its own police and unprevented by its magistrate.

### Q: What is Section 145 of the Code of Criminal Procedure and why did it govern the site?

Section 145 of the Code of Criminal Procedure is a preventive provision empowering a magistrate, where a dispute over land or a building is likely to cause a breach of the peace, to attach the property, appoint a receiver, and hold possession until a competent court decides the parties' rights. It decides nothing about ownership; it freezes a dangerous situation. It governed the Ayodhya site because Additional City Magistrate Markendey Singh invoked it on 29 December 1949, attaching the Babri Masjid structure and placing it with receiver Priya Dutt Ram pending the parties' claims. The provision's interim design is the point of the Ayodhya story: a bridge to adjudication became a residence, since the attachment and receivership it authorised in a week of emergency structured the site's custody for decades, renewed through the civil litigation that the attachment was meant merely to precede.

### Q: Who was Priya Dutt Ram?

Priya Dutt Ram was the chairman of the Municipal Board of Faizabad-cum-Ayodhya whom Additional City Magistrate Markendey Singh appointed receiver of the disputed Ayodhya property on 29 December 1949. As receiver he took charge of the attached building on the state's behalf, holding it for whichever party the courts might eventually find entitled, and he arranged its care, including the engagement of a priest to perform the daily service of the idols installed beneath the central dome. The choice of a municipal notable rather than a court official or a member of either congregation was deliberate in form: the receiver embodied the administration's claim that custody was neutral care of endangered property. His receivership began the line of official custody that, under successive arrangements and authorities, managed the site until the demolition of 1992 abolished the office along with the structure.

### Q: Who was Gopal Singh Visharad and what did his suit of January 1950 seek?

Gopal Singh Visharad was the Hindu devotee who filed the first of the Ayodhya title suits, in the Faizabad civil court on 16 January 1950, three weeks after the lock-up. His suit prayed for a declaration that he was entitled to worship the deity installed beneath the Babri Masjid's central dome and for a permanent injunction restraining the removal of the idols and any interference with that worship. The civil court granted an interim injunction in the suit's first days, restraining removal and obstruction until the case was decided. The suit's importance is architectural: the litigation that justified seventy years of official deferral began not with a state seeking judgment on the trespass, nor with the dispossessed congregation, but with a devotee asking a court to conserve the situation the trespass had created, and being granted conservation within the week.

### Q: Why did the Uttar Pradesh government appeal against the 1950 injunction?

The Government of Uttar Pradesh appealed in April 1950 against the interim injunction granted in Gopal Singh Visharad's suit, which restrained the removal of the idols and interference with worship at them. The government's stated ground was that a civil court's interim order ought not to fetter the executive in dealing with a property at the centre of a public-order emergency, and that the injunction froze an administrative question that the province remained responsible for managing. The appeal is the documentary proof that Lucknow's acceptance of the frozen settlement was not instantaneous: the Pant government first contested the legal form of the freeze even as its own custody sustained the freeze in fact. The appeal failed to displace the injunction, which remained in force, and the province settled into the custodial role it would keep for decades.

### Q: What was the Ram Chabutra?

The Ram Chabutra was a raised platform in the outer courtyard of the Babri Masjid complex at which Hindu worship had been conducted for generations before 1949. Tradition held it to mark the resting place associated with Ram's birth, and devotees offered prayer there while Muslim worship proceeded inside the domed mosque, the two usages separated after 1859 by a fence erected by the British administration of Faizabad. The platform was the object of the first Ayodhya litigation, Mahant Raghubar Das's suit of 1885 for permission to build a temple over it, dismissed on appeal in March 1886. Its existence explains the site's pre-1949 condition: not a mosque uncontested, nor a temple suppressed, but a managed partition of sacred space between two congregations, policed by the state, which December 1949 collapsed when the worship of the outer courtyard entered the inner building by night.

### Q: What did the Sunni Central Waqf Board's suit of 1961 claim?

The suit filed on 18 December 1961 by the Uttar Pradesh Sunni Central Board of Waqfs, with nine Muslim residents of Ayodhya as co-plaintiffs, claimed that the Babri Masjid and its adjoining land were waqf property dedicated to Muslim worship, that the mosque had been in Muslim possession and use for prayer until December 1949, and that the installation of idols in that month was an unlawful encroachment. It demanded the removal of the idols, the restoration of the mosque to Muslim possession, and the end of the arrangements under which Hindu worship continued inside the locked structure. Hashim Ansari, the Ayodhya tailor who became the litigation's most persistent Muslim plaintiff, was among the suit's plaintiffs. The filing came days inside the twelve-year limitation period, and the suit became the Muslim claim's principal vehicle through the title litigation decided in 2010 and 2019.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court's 2019 judgment say about the legality of the acts of 1949?

The Constitution Bench that decided the Ayodhya title appeals on 9 November 2019 held that the acts of December 1949 were unlawful. Reconstructing the week from the district administration's own files, the Bench recorded that idols were installed beneath the central dome by a group of fifty to sixty persons, that the mosque was thereby desecrated, and that Muslim worshippers were ousted from the mosque otherwise than by due process of law. The Bench treated the 1949 placement as a wrong that could not ground rights, just as it called the demolition of 1992 an egregious violation of the rule of law. Its award of the site to the Hindu claimants rested on the balance of the evidence of possession and worship across the preceding centuries, not on the legality of 1949, which the judgment condemned in terms its custodians of seventy years had avoided.

### Q: What happened to K.K.K. Nayar after 1949?

K. K. Nayar left the Indian Civil Service in the early 1950s, his Ayodhya posting having made him a figure of national controversy, defended by the ascetic orders and the Hindu Mahasabha's constituency as the officer who had preserved the deity's seat and attacked by secular critics as the magistrate who had licensed a seizure. He entered politics with the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the party whose Ayodhya sympathies matched the reputation his December 1949 decisions had earned him, and he was elected to the Lok Sabha in 1967. His later writings and statements maintained the defence he had offered in his official reports of December 1949: that the installation had been illegal, that its forcible reversal would have cost lives, and that custody pending the courts' decision was the only course open to a magistrate responsible for the peace of a town balanced on communal violence.

### Q: Why did the Ayodhya title suits take seven decades to reach a final judgment?

The Ayodhya title suits took seven decades because every institution that touched them found deferral cheaper than decision. The suits began in 1950 in a Faizabad civil court, multiplied to five by 1989, and moved through consolidation, the transfer to a special bench of the Allahabad High Court in 1989, long evidentiary proceedings involving archaeology and thousands of documents, a three-way division ordered in 2010, and appeals that the Supreme Court stayed in 2011 and heard at length only in 2019. The plaintiffs died and were substituted; Hashim Ansari, a plaintiff from 1961, died in 2016 with the appeals unheard. Governments of every party cited the pendency as their reason for inaction, and the courts, carrying a record of exceptional size and sensitivity, adjourned across generations. The delay was not one actor's design. It was the deferral mechanism of 1949 operating as designed incentives aligned.

### Q: What was the Akhil Bharatiya Ramayan Mahasabha?

The Akhil Bharatiya Ramayan Mahasabha was the Ayodhya-based organisation that campaigned through the late 1940s for the recovery of the Babri Masjid site as Ram's birthplace and that prepared the ground for the events of December 1949. In the days before the idols' placement it sponsored a nine-day continuous recitation of the Ramcharitmanas near the mosque, drawing the crowds of devotees and ascetics whose presence in the outer courtyard the Faizabad police recorded with apprehension. The men named in the first information report for the entry of 22 and 23 December, Abhiram Das among them, moved in the Mahasabha's ascetic circles. The organisation matters to the episode's documentary history because it connects the placement to a sustained local campaign rather than a spontaneous night: the recitation, the gathering and the entry formed a sequence that the district administration watched unfold and recorded in its own files.

### Q: What records survive from the Faizabad administration's December 1949 decisions?

The surviving record of the Faizabad administration's December 1949 decisions is unusually rich. It includes the first information report of 23 December 1949 from Ayodhya police station; the district magistrate's reports to the Government of Uttar Pradesh, in which the installation's illegality and the refusal of removal are both stated; the superintendent of police's written warnings that preceded the entry; and the attachment order of 29 December 1949 under section 145 of the criminal procedure code, whose text appoints the receiver and survives in full. The Nehru and Pant correspondence is preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, and further district papers rest at the Uttar Pradesh State Archives. The record's quality shaped the litigation's end: the Allahabad High Court called for the original 1949 district file in May 2009, and the Supreme Court's 2019 reconstruction of the week rests on the administration's own paper.
