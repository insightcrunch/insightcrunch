Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek are usually cited as a pair, as though a single argument issued from two pens, and the pairing conceals nearly everything that matters about them. They were two economists of the same Viennese world, one a generation older than the other, and they shared a conclusion: that a society which abolished market prices for the means of production would lose the instrument by which it steered its economy. Almost everything else divided them. Their methods were opposed, one deductive and one drawn toward evidence and evolution. Their tempers were opposed, one combative and systematic, one courteous and oblique. Their arguments, on inspection, are three in number and different in kind, and their target was socialism as a system of ownership and allocation rather than the economics of Karl Marx, whose work neither of them subjected to a sustained critique. A reader who separates the men, the methods, and the arguments from one another holds the whole subject in a form that can be assessed; a reader who merges them holds a slogan.

![Two economists' works, a sheaf of price returns and a railway timetable resting on a desk, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-67.webp)

Three corrections run through everything that follows, and each of them will surprise readers who arrive from opposite directions. The first concerns method: the elder man argued from a procedure he held to be deductive, deriving the laws of economics from the concept of purposive action itself, and he therefore presented the impossibility of rational calculation under socialism as a conclusion of logic, settled before evidence was consulted. The second concerns a famous book: the younger man's political argument of the war years, endlessly cited as a warning that welfare provision slides into totalitarian rule, explicitly endorsed compulsory social insurance and state provision against severe deprivation, and its author spent later decades insisting that the popular reading of his own text misrepresented it. The third concerns the target: neither thinker wrote a book against Marx's economics, and the case they built bears on central direction of production, which means it can be cited against comprehensive planning with full textual warrant and against Marx specifically, or against a welfare floor, only by exceeding what the texts contain. That excess, measured precisely, is the subject this article exists to measure.

## Two thinkers, not one argument

The elder was born in 1881 in Lemberg, then a city of the Habsburg monarchy, and took his doctorate at Vienna in the law and economics of the Austrian school, a tradition descending from Carl Menger that explained value by the choices of individuals at the margin rather than by the labour embodied in goods. He served as an artillery officer in the war that destroyed the monarchy, returned to a Vienna of inflation and socialist municipal government, and in 1920 published the essay that made his reputation among economists, arguing that a socialist commonwealth could not calculate. Two years later the argument grew into a book, a systematic treatment of socialism as an economic and sociological problem, and around the same years he began the private seminar that formed a generation of Austrian economists. The younger man, born in Vienna in 1899, took two doctorates, in law and in political science, worked under the elder's direction at a government office settling war claims, attended that seminar, and in 1931 carried the Austrian theory of the trade cycle to the London School of Economics, where he spent two decades arguing with Keynesians before migrating, by degrees, from technical economics toward law, psychology, and political philosophy.

A shared city and a shared seminar make the two sound like a school with a single doctrine, and in the matter of conclusions they often were one. Both held that prices formed in exchange between owners were not decorations on an economy but its nervous system. Both held that a central authority which owned everything would be deprived of signals it could not replace by decree. Both wrote as liberals in the older European sense, defending a legal order within which individuals plan their own lives. Yet the routes by which they reached those conclusions diverged so sharply that their arguments fail, where they fail, for different reasons, and succeed, where they succeed, on different evidence. The elder's case rests on the structure of calculation and claims the certainty of a proof. The younger's rests on the character of knowledge and on the political logic of a plan, and claims the strength of an observation about how societies actually coordinate. Treating the pair as one voice flatters neither: it makes the elder's rigour look like the younger's caution, and the younger's caution look like the elder's rigour.

### How did the younger man come to sit in the elder's seminar?

By employment before allegiance. After war service and two Viennese doctorates, the younger economist joined a temporary government office charged with settling financial claims arising from the peace treaties, an office the elder directed, and competence in that work opened the door of the private seminar the elder convened for advanced students and officials.

Their personal relation followed the pattern of the seminar itself, respectful, formative, and never quite warm in the manner of friendship between equals. The younger absorbed the elder's monetary theory and his suspicion of intervention, carried both to London, and then, across the nineteen thirties, quietly rebuilt the foundations under his own position, moving the anti-socialist case away from the elder's ground of calculation and onto ground the elder never occupied, the dispersal of knowledge among millions of minds. The elder noticed the migration and did not follow it. He regarded the knowledge formulation as a weaker, almost sociological restatement of a point that logic had already settled, and he said so with the bluntness his students remembered. Posterity reversed the judgement of the seminar room: the version of the argument that entered the wider discipline, that textbooks teach and opponents answer, is overwhelmingly the younger man's, while the elder's survives as the austere original that specialists consult. Any honest assessment must therefore grade two arguments wearing one surname in the public memory, and grade them separately.

Geography completed the separation. The elder, driven from Austria by the approach of the regime he had long opposed, settled in Geneva and then in New York, where he taught for decades in a visiting post and gathered a devoted American circle that prized his intransigence. The younger took British citizenship, moved after the war to Chicago and then to Freiburg, and gathered a different circle, of lawyers, philosophers, and historians as much as economists. The American circle kept the elder's system intact and combative; the younger's readers dispersed his insights into a dozen disciplines and softened some of them in the process. When later polemic cites Mises and Hayek in one breath, it cites a Viennese seminar, an Atlantic crossing, and two distinct intellectual afterlives compressed into a single gesture, and the compression is the first error an assessment has to undo.

## The elder's method: reasoning from action

The elder called his method praxeology, the science of human action, and he built it as a deliberate refusal of the procedures other sciences were using. Economics, on his account, does not begin from observation and rise to generalisations that further observation might overturn. It begins from a proposition he held to be given in the very concept of action: that human beings act, which is to say they employ means to attain ends they have chosen, preferring one state of affairs to another. From that single starting point, unfolded with care, the whole structure of economic law was supposed to follow by deduction, as the theorems of geometry follow from axioms, and to hold with the same immunity to refutation by counterexample. Prices, money, interest, the trade cycle, and the impossibility of calculation in a commonwealth without markets were, in his presentation, not hypotheses awaiting tests but conclusions awaiting only correct derivation. A critic who brought statistics against them had, in the elder's eyes, misunderstood what kind of knowledge economics is.

The famous opening of his mature treatise states the foundation in five words: human action is purposeful behavior. Everything else in the system is commentary on that sentence. Because action is purposeful, it involves choice; because it involves choice, it ranks ends; because it ranks ends under scarcity, it economises; because men exchange, their rankings meet and prices emerge as ratios of exchange; because production takes time and uses goods of higher order, the problem of comparing alternative employments of those goods arises, and money prices alone solve it. The chain is elegant, and its elegance was the point. Where other economists of his century apologised for the unrealism of their assumptions, the elder boasted that his starting point was not an assumption at all but a category of the mind, something no experience could falsify because every experience is already interpreted through it.

### Why did Mises treat economic laws as conclusions of logic rather than findings of research?

Because his starting point was, for him, not a hypothesis but the structure of action itself. A proposition derived correctly from the concept of purposive conduct could no more be overturned by statistics than a theorem could, and evidence was therefore confined to illustrating conclusions that reasoning had already secured beyond empirical reach.

The method drew fire from two directions, and an assessment should record both because they condition how much weight the calculation argument can bear. Philosophers of science objected that a theory which no conceivable observation could refute has purchased certainty at the price of content, that it tells us how the elder proposed to use words rather than how economies behave. Economists of more empirical temper objected that the interesting questions, how strongly a tax shifts an activity, how fast a price adjustment travels, are precisely the ones deduction cannot answer, so that the method secures the least contestable propositions and abandons the field where policy is decided. The elder's defenders reply that the core insights of economics, that incentives operate, that value is subjective, that calculation needs a common unit, are indeed not the kind of thing a regression establishes, and that his apriorism merely said openly what theorists quietly practise. The dispute was never settled, and it matters here for a practical reason: the calculation argument inherits the method's strength and its vulnerability together. Stated as logic, it cannot be dented by pointing to a planning board that muddled through; stated as logic, it also cannot explain why some planned economies industrialised for decades, except by declaring their arithmetic irrational all the same, which many readers have found less a refutation of the record than a refusal to look at it.

There is a further consequence that later sections will need. A thinker who believes his central claim is a theorem does not hedge it, and the elder never hedged: socialism, for him, was not a system that would perform badly but a system that could not, in the strict sense, perform the act of economic calculation at all, so that its administrators would grope in darkness however honest or brilliant they were. That absoluteness made the argument famous and made it brittle. When respondents produced schemes in which a planning board mimicked a market, adjusting accounting prices by trial and error until stocks cleared, the elder replied that mimicking prices without property and entrepreneurship was theatre, not calculation, a defence that moved the argument's weight from arithmetic to institutions. The younger man's reformulation can be read as an attempt to give the conclusion a foundation that did not depend on the audience accepting praxeology first, and the attempt succeeded so well that the original foundation is now the part of the edifice readers are likeliest to skip.

## The calculation claim: a commonwealth without a yardstick

The essay of 1920 and the book that expanded it two years later make a claim narrower and more interesting than the slogan it became. The elder did not argue that socialists would be lazy, or corrupt, or ignorant of engineering. He granted the future administrators every virtue and every technical fact, and then asked a bookkeeping question: by what unit will they compare the cost of building a railway from iron and labour against the cost of building it from other materials, or against not building it and enlarging a canal instead? In a market order the comparison is performed in money prices. Producer goods, steel, coal, machine tools, land, are bought and sold between owners, and their prices condense into a single scale the relative scarcity of each, so that an entrepreneur can reckon whether a project returns more than the resources it withdraws would have earned elsewhere. Abolish private ownership of producer goods and the exchanges that generate those prices disappear with it. Consumer goods might still be rationed or even priced, but the goods of higher order, the ones whose allocation decides what an economy becomes, would change hands inside a single owner's administration, where transfers register no price because nothing is genuinely bought from anyone.

Calculation in kind, the proposal that planners could reckon in physical quantities, tons of steel against hours of labour against hectares of soil, foundered on the absence of a common denominator. Physical units do not add. A plan that consumes more steel and less labour than its rival is not thereby shown better or worse until the two magnitudes are translated into terms of one scale, and the only scale the elder could find that performs the translation without smuggling in arbitrary weights is the scale money prices supply when owners bid against one another. Nor could the administrators borrow the prices of a vanished market and freeze them into their ledgers, because the conditions those prices summarised, harvests, tastes, techniques, deposits, shift continually, and a price list inherited from a dead economy describes a world that no longer exists. The argument's force, which even hostile respondents acknowledged, lies in its refusal to discuss motives. It does not say the planners will choose badly out of wickedness. It says the instrument by which choosing well is defined, for production that serves unknown future wants through long chains of intermediate goods, has been removed as a condition of the system itself.

### What exactly cannot be compared when producer goods carry no prices?

Alternative employments of the same resources. A ton of steel, an hour of skilled labour, and a stretch of land enter thousands of possible projects, and only a common monetary scale ranks the projects against one another. Without prices for those inputs, a planning board can list physical requirements but cannot judge which use sacrifices least.

The debate this provoked occupies its own article in this series, [the socialist calculation debate](/2019/08/15/socialist-calculation-debate/), and only its outline belongs here. Respondents of the nineteen thirties, most ingeniously Oskar Lange, proposed that a central board could announce accounting prices, instruct managers to produce where price covered cost, and revise the announced prices upward where shortages appeared and downward where surpluses accumulated, imitating by rule the adjustments a market performs by rivalry. The elder answered that the imitation omitted the essence: nobody's property stood behind the bids, nobody gained or lost by the accuracy of a valuation, and the future, which is where entrepreneurial judgement earns its keep, could not be priced by a board reacting to shortages already visible. The younger man, who had entered the exchange as the elder's champion, gradually conceded in his own mind that the argument from calculation alone could be parried in the abstract, and relocated the difficulty where he believed no scheme of accounting prices could reach it, in the knowledge that prices carry and boards cannot gather. That relocation is the hinge of the whole subject, and it is why the calculation article and this one must be read as a pair: the first generation's question was whether a plan could compute, and the second generation's question was whether it could know.

It is worth pausing on the book of 1922 itself, because its scope is wider than the essay's and its reputation narrower. Alongside the calculation chapters it offered a sociology of socialism, an account of its appeal, a treatment of interventionism as an unstable middle system, and the argument, easily missed, that socialism abolishes the entrepreneurial function without supplying any substitute for it, leaving promotion within a bureaucracy as the only career open to ambition. Its author regarded it as his most important book. Its readers, including sympathetic ones, have usually treated it as a quarry for the calculation argument and left the rest unmined, which is one reason the elder is so often described as a one-argument thinker by people who have read summaries of him. The description is unjust to the book and accurate about its reception, and the gap between those two judgements is a small instance of the phenomenon this article keeps meeting, a text and its citation travelling separately through the world.

## The missing entrepreneur

Inside the calculation argument sits a second argument, about a person rather than a unit of account. Production for an unknown future requires somebody to commit resources now to a conjecture about wants that do not yet exist, to bear the loss if the conjecture fails and to reap a gain if it succeeds, and by that bearing and reaping to discipline the population of conjectures. The elder called this the entrepreneurial function, and he insisted it was not a synonym for management. Managers administer what exists, optimising within instructions; entrepreneurs decide what shall exist, and their decisions are wagers made under uncertainty that no formula removes. A market order institutionalises the wager by attaching it to property: the man who commits his own or his backers' capital to a new process discovers, in profit or in bankruptcy, whether his reading of the future was worth more than the resources it consumed. Socialism, abolishing the capital market in which such wagers are placed and settled, does not transfer the function to the planning board. It deletes the settlement mechanism, and with it the only known procedure by which mistaken conjectures about the future are identified and starved of further resources.

The point deserves its full weight because it answers a natural evasion. A respondent might grant that a board cannot compute an optimum and reply that no market computes one either, that actual economies lurch forward by guesswork, imitation, and waste. The elder's rejoinder is that the guesswork of a market is audited guesswork. Each conjecture is staked, priced, and wound up when it fails, so errors are localised in the fortunes of those who made them, while the corresponding errors of a monopoly board are generalised across the whole economy and protected from discovery by the board's own authority over the statistics. The difference between the systems is therefore not that one errs and the other does not. It is that one possesses a routine for finding its errors and the other must rely on the erring institution to report itself. Later economists who owed the elder little else adopted this insight in altered dress, and the modern literature on innovation, on venture finance, and on the selection of firms is unintelligible without the function he isolated, even where his name goes unmentioned.

### What work does the entrepreneur perform that a ministry cannot simply be ordered to copy?

The entrepreneur stakes resources on a conjecture about future wants and is settled with, in profit or in loss, by the event. A ministry can be ordered to administer, to meet targets, even to experiment, but no instruction can make its officials bear the loss personally or let failure close their bureau as bankruptcy closes a firm.

The younger man absorbed this argument and transposed it. Where the elder saw a missing functionary, the younger saw missing information: the entrepreneur's wager is a device for bringing one person's local conjecture into contact with the judgement of the whole economy, expressed in the prices his inputs cost him and the price his output fetches. Remove the prices and the wager cannot even be stated, let alone settled. The transposition mattered because it shifted the dispute from the philosophy of action, where few would follow the elder, to the theory of information, where the century was heading anyway. It also softened the conclusion in a way the elder disliked. A knowledge argument shows that a board will coordinate badly compared with a price system; it does not show, with the clean necessity of a theorem, that coordination is impossible in principle, and the younger man, to his credit, generally wrote as though he knew the difference. The political writings would complicate that credit, and they are the reason his name, rather than the elder's, is the one invoked in parliaments and pamphlets.

## The younger's different road

The younger man's formation explains his divergence from the seminar that trained him. He arrived in economics through the law and through the practical business of postwar claims, spent the nineteen twenties on money and the trade cycle, and won his London chair with a theory of how credit expansion distorts the structure of production across time. That work was technical, mathematical in spirit if not in notation, and it engaged the profession on its own ground. The controversy with Keynes and his Cambridge circle, conducted in journals and in a famous pair of reviews, left him convinced that aggregate quantities conceal the relative adjustments where economic life actually happens, a conviction that ripened into his mature theme: the economy is not a stock of resources to be allocated by a sufficiently informed mind, because the information that allocation would require is not given to any mind, and is not given anywhere in assembled form. An essay of 1937 on economics and knowledge announced the turn, questioning the assumption, built into equilibrium theory, that the relevant data are simply known. The essay of 1945 on the use of knowledge in society completed it, and remains the single most cited statement of the case against planning ever written by a liberal economist.

His temperament shaped the argument's reception as surely as its content. Where the elder polemicised, the younger inquired; where the elder pronounced systems impossible, the younger asked how coordination is achieved at all, and marvelled that it is. That tone disarmed readers who would have bristled at the seminar's certainties, and it recruited allies the elder never sought, among them philosophers of science and, later, theorists of computation and organisation who found in the knowledge problem a general truth about distributed systems. It also created a persistent ambiguity about what exactly he claimed, an ambiguity his popularisers exploited and his critics deplored, and which only a return to the texts can resolve. The texts are more careful than the slogans in both directions: less apocalyptic than the pamphlets that cite the political book, more radical than the textbooks that domesticate the knowledge essay into a remark about information costs.

### What changed in the younger's thinking during the nineteen thirties?

The question itself moved. He began the decade asking how a planner could calculate, a problem about arithmetic, and ended it asking how anyone, planner or trader, comes to know the things economic decisions require, a problem about the division of knowledge in society. The second question swallowed the first and outlived the debate that prompted it.

Biography also pressed on the theory. The younger watched a republic collapse into dictatorship in his native Austria, watched the London to which he had moved organise its war through controls he partly admired and partly feared would outlast the emergency, and drew from the spectacle a political question the elder's system had never needed to ask: not whether planning computes, but what planning does to the institutions and characters of the people who operate it. The book that resulted made him famous outside the profession and faintly suspect inside it, and it fixed his public identity so firmly that the quieter essays, which specialists regard as his deepest work, are still read as footnotes to it. An assessment that begins from the fame will misjudge the man in both directions, crediting the political argument with the knowledge argument's rigour and blaming the knowledge argument for the political argument's leaps. The sections that follow therefore take the arguments in the order of their strength rather than the order of their celebrity, knowledge first, politics second, and the later doctrines of justice and spontaneous order after those.

## Hayek's knowledge argument: what prices carry

State the argument at its strongest, as its author stated it. The economic problem of society is not the allocation of given resources among given ends by a mind that knows all the relevant facts, because the facts are not given in that form to anyone. The knowledge that economic decisions require exists as dispersed bits, held by millions of people, much of it knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place: that a machine stands idle in one town while an order waits in another, that a seam of material runs thinner than the catalogue says, that a customer's need has shifted this week in a way no return will capture until next quarter. Some of this knowledge can be written down, and some cannot, being embodied in skill, habit, and acquaintance, the know-how of people who could not state what they know in a form a bureau could file. A central authority that wished to allocate rationally would first have to gather this ocean into one place, and the gathering is not merely expensive. By the time the returns are compiled, the circumstances they describe have altered, and much of what mattered most was never articulable enough to enter a return at all.

Prices are the institution that makes this dispersed knowledge usable without gathering it. When tin grows scarcer, for whatever reason, the price of tin rises, and every user of tin learns, from that single movement, that he must economise, seek substitutes, or pay more, without needing to know whether a mine flooded or a new use was discovered. The author of the knowledge essay reached for a technological metaphor and called the price mechanism a system of telecommunications, a machinery for registering change and broadcasting its practical significance to exactly the people positioned to act on it, in a code of one number per commodity. The marvel he asked his readers to see is how little any participant needs to know. The user of tin who responds to the price rise performs his part in a global adjustment while ignorant of its cause, its geography, and almost all of its other participants, and the adjustment occurs not because anyone commanded it but because the signal reached everyone whose decisions it concerned, weighted by how much it concerned them.

### What does a rising price tell thousands of strangers that no official report could reach them in time to say?

That the thing has grown scarcer relative to the uses competing for it, and that each of them should economise, substitute, or produce more, in proportion to their own situation. The signal carries no explanation and needs none; it arrives as a single number, addressed to everyone whose plans the scarcity touches.

### Where does the knowledge an economy runs on actually sit?

In the heads and hands of the people doing the work, scattered across every workshop, farm, office, and delivery route, much of it tied to a particular place and hour, and much of it never formulated in words at all. It exists in usable form only where it is held, attached to the circumstances that give it meaning.

Two features of this argument explain its durability. The first is that it does not depend on praxeology, on property apologetics, or on any claim about human selfishness; a society of angels would face the same dispersal of knowledge, and a board of saints would still have to extract from a lathe operator what the operator knows only in his fingers. The second is that it reframed the comparison. The question was no longer whether a planning bureau, imagined with the whole economy's data spread before it, could compute a better allocation than a market, a contest the bureau wins by stipulation. The question became which institution behaves better given that the data are never spread before anyone, and on that ground the market's claim is that it never needed the data assembled, because it acts on knowledge in the place where knowledge lives. Respondents who wished to answer had to engage the reframing rather than the original problem, and the ablest of them, as a later section records, conceded a large part of it and rebuilt their socialism around the concession. The argument also escaped its birthplace: engineers designing distributed networks, theorists of the firm deciding what to make and what to buy, and students of bureaucracy explaining why headquarters misreads its own branches all reinvent fragments of it, usually without citation, which is the sincerest form an idea's victory can take.

## Knowledge that cannot be collected

The hardest part of the knowledge problem is not secrecy or volume but form. A great deal of economically vital knowledge is tacit, a word the literature later borrowed from the philosopher Michael Polanyi, who observed that we can know more than we can tell. The machinist who hears that a bearing is about to fail, the buyer who senses that a supplier's assurances have thinned, the farmer whose judgement of a field integrates a decade of weather he could not enumerate: each holds knowledge that guides real allocation and that no questionnaire extracts, because the holder himself could not produce it on demand in propositional form. Skill is knowledge stored as capacity rather than as statement. A planning system feeds on statements, on returns, norms, and coefficients, and must therefore either ignore the tacit stratum or degrade it into the approximations that fit its forms. The elder had glimpsed this in his insistence that the board lacks the entrepreneur's feel for the future; the younger generalised it into a claim about the texture of social knowledge as such.

Statistics, the board's natural instrument, compound the difficulty by their method. A statistical aggregate is manufactured by deliberately discarding the differences between the items it sums: tons of coal are added by ignoring that one seam is near a railway and another is not, that one burns clean and another fouls a furnace. For many purposes the discard is the price of vision, and national accounts built modern economic management. But allocation is the purpose for which the discarded differences are exactly the operative facts, since the planner's task is precisely to decide which seam supplies which furnace. The knowledge argument thus contains a paradox at its centre: the more completely a central authority succeeds in rendering the economy legible to itself, the more it has filtered out the particularity on which good decisions depend, and the returns on its desk describe an economy simplified into governability at the cost of being the economy it governs. Markets are not exempt from blindness; they are blind differently, a distinction the next section insists on, because an assessment that compares one mechanism's defects with the other's ideal commits the standard error of this literature in whichever direction it is committed.

### Why does a statistical return destroy the very detail a planner would need from it?

Because aggregation works by treating unlike things as alike. A return that sums output across regions and grades has already discarded the local facts, the idle machine, the spoiled batch, the urgent customer, that distinguish a good allocation from a bad one, and the discarded facts cannot be recovered from the totals they were merged into.

## What prices fail to transmit

An assessment earns its credibility at the point where it turns the instrument on its owner, and the knowledge argument must now be subjected to the discipline its author applied to planners. The comparison this literature invites, and must refuse, is between a market described at its best and a plan described at its worst, or the reverse. Both mechanisms are imperfect transmitters, and the honest procedure is to specify, for each, what it characteristically fails to carry. Prices fail, first, about the future. A price is struck between people acting on expectations, and it registers those expectations without certifying them; speculative fevers and panics are episodes in which the telecommunications system broadcasts error with the same efficiency as truth, and the knowledge of what an asset will be worth, or what a technology will become, is precisely the knowledge nobody holds in the present the price describes. Prices fail, second, about third parties. Where an activity imposes costs or confers benefits on people who are not party to the exchange, the price struck between buyer and seller is silent about those effects, and the signal that guides allocation omits them entirely, which is why smoke, congestion, and the depletion of shared stocks are the standing exhibits of the market's critics. Prices fail, third and most consequentially for this article, about wants unbacked by money, a failure reserved for the strongest objection below.

The plan's characteristic failures are of a different shape. A planning apparatus fails to elicit knowledge that its holders have reason to conceal: a manager whose next target is set from his reported capacity learns to understate capacity, and the information flowing upward is corrupted by the incentives of the reporting chain itself, a corruption no exhortation repairs because it is rational at every link. It fails at speed, because returns are periodic and circumstances are continuous, so the picture on the planner's desk is always a portrait of an economy that has already moved. And it fails at the tacit stratum described above, which no form has yet been devised to capture. Set side by side, the two ledgers of failure do not cancel into a verdict by arithmetic. They define the terrain on which a verdict must argue: whether the wants a society most needs transmitted are those prices carry well or those they drop, and whether the knowledge a decision needs is the kind that can survive a journey to the centre. The knowledge argument, properly bounded, claims that for the routine allocation of producer goods among competing uses, the price system's failures are the smaller ones. It does not claim, and its author at his most careful did not claim, that the ranking holds for every question a society faces.

### What happens to a want that arrives with no money standing behind it?

It does not register. A price system hears effective demand, which is desire armed with purchasing power, and a need unaccompanied by money sends no signal, moves no resources, and leaves no trace in the data the system generates. The want exists; the mechanism, by construction, is deaf to it.

There is a discipline to observe here that both camps habitually violate. When the market's defenders move from allocation among producer goods to the distribution of income, or from the price of tin to the justice of wages, they have changed the subject while keeping the applause, because the knowledge argument speaks to the transmission of information and is silent, in itself, about who should hold the money that makes wants audible. When the plan's defenders answer the knowledge argument by reciting the market's distributional failures, they commit the mirror offence, since a mechanism can transmit information efficiently and distribute its proceeds badly, and both halves of that sentence can be true at once. Keeping the halves separate is not pedantry. It is the precondition of the strongest objection being heard at full strength when its turn comes, and of the bounded target, the claim at this article's centre, being drawn exactly where the texts draw it.

## The political argument: a single ranking of ends

The younger man's second argument leaves economics for political theory, and its logic should be stated as carefully as the knowledge argument was, because the popular version is a caricature of it. Begin with what comprehensive planning requires. A plan that directs production as a whole must decide, not in the abstract but in binding quantities, how much of society's effort goes to housing and how much to armaments, to hospitals and how much to orchestras, to the consumption of the present generation and how much to investment for its grandchildren. These decisions embody a ranking of ends, a complete scale of values in which every social purpose has its place relative to every other. In a market order no such ranking exists or needs to exist: millions of separate rankings coexist, each person ordering his own ends, and the economy's direction emerges from their interaction without anyone having endorsed the aggregate result. The political argument holds that the comprehensive plan cannot proceed on that basis. Somebody must supply the single ranking, and the question is who, and by what authority, and what happens to those whose rankings differ.

A democratic assembly, the argument continues, cannot supply it. Voters and their representatives can agree on negations, that poverty is bad or that a war must be won, but the positive content of a plan, the relative weights of thousands of competing goods, is not a proposition an assembly can vote into coherence, because majorities cycle, coalitions bargain, and the assembly discovers that it can decide to plan without being able to decide the plan. Authority therefore migrates, by the logic of the task rather than by anybody's conspiracy, from the assembly to the administrators charged with executing its empty mandate, and from them to the person or body that resolves their disagreements. Nor can the ranking, once imposed, remain a merely technical document. Since the plan touches every occupation, every region, and every material ambition, dissent from the ranking is dissent from the allocation of livelihoods, and the authority that imposes it acquires both the motive and the machinery to treat disagreement as obstruction. The famous chapter asking why the worst rise to the top adds a selection mechanism: in a system where agreement on ends must be manufactured, those most willing to manufacture it, and least scrupulous about the means, rise toward the summit, while the independently minded are filtered out at every stage. The rule of law, finally, understood as general rules known in advance that bind the rulers themselves, cannot survive a regime whose essence is discretionary decision case by case, because a plan that must respond to everything cannot be bound by rules announced beforehand.

### What must be settled about ends before a comprehensive plan can allocate anything at all?

Their relative ranking, completely and in binding form. A plan cannot weigh housing against hospitals, or present consumption against future investment, until some authority has fixed how much each purpose counts against the others, and that fixing of weights is a political act that no technique performs and no expertise supplies.

This argument has a place in the wider charge that planning tends toward authoritarian rule, a charge examined across its whole range in the article on [marxism and authoritarianism](/2019/07/01/marxism-and-authoritarianism/), and its boundaries should be marked before the famous book is opened. It is an argument about comprehensive direction of production, about the state taking the whole economy's allocation into its hands, and its mechanism runs through the need for a unitary scale of ends. It is not, in its own logic, an argument about public provision of particular goods, about insurance against particular risks, or about a floor of income beneath which nobody is allowed to fall, because none of those requires the single ranking: a society can insure its members against sickness while leaving the direction of production to the separate rankings of its citizens, and the two arrangements differ not in degree along one slope but in kind. Whether the book's author observed his own boundary is the question the next section takes from the text itself, and the answer is the most useful correction this article has to offer, useful precisely because it cuts against the readers who cite the book most loudly.

## The famous book in its own pages

The book appeared in 1944, written by a refugee scholar in wartime Cambridge and London, addressed to British readers who were debating the shape of their postwar settlement, and dedicated, with deliberate provocation, to socialists of all parties. Its fame has entirely outrun its text. It is cited in legislatures by people who have read its title, denounced in seminars by people who know only its reputation, and summarised in a sentence that its author spent a considerable part of his later life disowning. That sentence, in its common form, holds that welfare provision is the first step on a road whose end is totalitarian government, so that a society which builds a health service or a pension system has started down a slope it cannot stop descending. The text does not say this. What the text says, in the chapter where the author takes up security, is more discriminating and, for the polemic that cites him, deeply inconvenient.

### Did Hayek argue that welfare states lead to totalitarianism?

no. His book of 1944 endorsed compulsory social insurance and a state organised provision against severe deprivation, and in later statements he supported a guaranteed minimum income as a floor beneath the market. His argument concerned the comprehensive direction of production by a central authority, and he stated that the popular reading misrepresented it.

The discrimination the chapter draws is between two kinds of security that share a word and nothing else. The first is security against severe physical privation, the assurance that nobody in a wealthy society need starve, freeze, or go without shelter and basic care. That security, the author wrote, could be guaranteed to all without endangering freedom, and he treated the organisation of comprehensive social insurance against the common hazards of life, sickness, accident, unemployment, old age, as a legitimate and indeed proper task in which the state could assist or take part. The second kind is security of a given standard of life, the guarantee that a person's relative position, his income compared with others, the status of his trade against new competition, will be preserved by authority against the changes a free economy continually works. That security, he argued, cannot be given to some without being taken from the freedom of others, because preserving every position as it stands means freezing the allocation of labour and resources, which is comprehensive direction returning through the back door. The distinction is the book in miniature: a floor beneath everyone is compatible with a market order and was endorsed by its most famous defender; a guarantee of everyone's rung on the ladder is not, because the ladder must then be nailed in place.

### Which forms of security did the famous text say a society could guarantee to everyone?

Security against severe privation: food, shelter, clothing, and care sufficient to preserve health and the capacity to work, together with insurance organised against the hazards that strike individuals unpredictably. What it refused to guarantee was any person's relative standard, the preservation of a position against economic change.

Readers who know the book only through its afterlife should also register what its central chapters actually attack. The target named again and again is the direction of all economic activity according to a single plan, the substitution of one authority's ranking for the dispersed rankings of a free society, and the political machinery that substitution requires. Particular interventions are criticised, sometimes sharply, where they point toward that substitution or suppress the price signals the author valued, but the argument's engine is the comprehensive case, and its warnings are scaled to it. To cite the book against a pension, a clinic, or a minimum income is therefore not to apply it but to reverse it, since those provisions appear in its pages under the heading of what may properly be done. The author was not a reluctant concession-maker dragged to these admissions by editorial pressure. They follow from his own distinction between the framework of rules within which a market operates, which a state must supply and may improve, and the direction of production itself, which he held a state cannot assume without assuming everything else in time.

## The author's corrections of his readers

An author cannot control his book's career, but this one tried, repeatedly and on the record. In the decades after the war he returned to the famous text in prefaces, essays, and interviews, and the returns have a consistent burden: the book was being read as a prophecy about welfare states, and it was not one. He pointed to the passages, which hostile and friendly readers alike had skipped, in which social insurance was endorsed and a minimum of sustenance, shelter, and clothing for all was described as a legitimate object of policy in a rich society. In his systematic treatise on liberty published in 1960 he developed the point into doctrine, arguing that a uniform floor of income, provided outside the market to those unable to earn it within the market, was consistent with a free order and might be a duty of a wealthy community, provided it remained a floor and did not harden into a direction of the whole. In still later work on law and liberty he repeated the concession in almost the same terms, treating the assurance of a minimum as one of the services a government could render without ceasing to govern by rules. The through-line is not a softening with age. It is the original distinction, restated each time the popular version threatened to replace it.

The corrections extend to the slope itself. The popular reading treats the book as hydraulics: introduce provision, and pressure builds until liberty bursts. The author's own statements describe a conditional argument about a particular destination, the comprehensive plan, and about the political logic that destination sets running wherever it is genuinely attempted. He allowed that piecemeal measures could be wise or foolish, could strengthen or corrupt a market order, and he campaigned against many of them on their merits, but merits are the language of judgement, not of inevitability, and a thinker arguing inevitability does not spend his old age distinguishing the measures he accepts from the measures he opposes. There is also a matter of scholarly fairness that an assessment should not shirk. When a text's qualifications are quoted, its thesis must be quoted with them, and when its thesis is quoted, the qualifications travel with it or the quotation is a forgery in effect. The bounded target, developed below, is simply this rule of fairness generalised across both thinkers and both directions of citation, since Marx's texts suffer the identical amputation at the hands of their own popularisers, a symmetry the series pattern section will draw out.

### What did the author say, in later years, about the reading that made him famous?

That it misrepresented him. He restated, in prefaces, treatises, and interviews across four decades, that his target was the comprehensive direction of production and not provision against want, that he had endorsed social insurance in the book itself, and that a guaranteed minimum beneath the market formed part of his own settled position.

Why did the misreading flourish if the text and its author both resisted it? Because the misreading is more useful than the text to nearly everyone. Admirers who wish to oppose a new clinic or a pension rise gain a famous name and a frightening destination, and the distinction between a floor and a direction would cost them their argument. Opponents who wish to dismiss the author gain a simpler target, a man who supposedly believed that pensions manufacture tyranny, whose refutation requires no engagement with the knowledge argument at all. Publishers sell more copies of a prophecy than of a conditional analysis. And the book's genuine warnings, about the delegation of power to administrators and the selection of the ruthless, are uncomfortable for governments of every stripe, whereas the caricature can be filed under party controversy and safely ignored. The survival of the caricature against its author's protests is itself a small lesson in the article's larger theme: texts do not circulate according to their content but according to the uses waiting for them, and the first task of an assessment is to recover what was actually argued from beneath what it was convenient to repeat.

## Social justice and the charge of category error

The younger man's third argument arrived last, in the nineteen seventies, as the centrepiece of a large work on law and liberty, and it is the most philosophically exposed of the three. Its target is a phrase: social justice. His claim is that the phrase, as applied to the results of a market order, commits a category error, because justice and injustice are properties of conduct, of actions performed by an agent who could have acted otherwise, and the distribution of income and wealth in a market order is not anybody's action. It is the unintended aggregate of countless separate transactions, each perhaps just or unjust in itself, whose combined pattern was chosen by no one, foreseen in its detail by no one, and intended by no one. To call such a pattern unjust is, on his account, like calling the weather unjust: the word has been carried outside the only context in which it has meaning, the deliberate allocation of shares by a distributing agent. In a planned order, where an authority does allocate, the phrase recovers its sense, and the author cheerfully grants that the distribution there can be judged just or unjust, precisely because somebody did it. The argument's elegance is that it makes the applicability of social justice depend on the existence of the very institution, central allocation, whose defenders most often invoke the phrase against its absence.

He pressed the point into a dilemma for the phrase's users. Either social justice means that the aggregate outcome should match some pattern, of need, of merit, of equality, in which case achieving it requires subjecting all transactions to an authority that continuously corrects their results, which is the comprehensive direction the political argument warned against, pursued now in the name of fairness; or it means only that the rules governing transactions should themselves be just, general, and impartially applied, in which case he agrees, and the phrase adds nothing to the older vocabulary of the rule of law. What it cannot coherently mean is a standard by which the unplanned results of free transactions are condemned while the freedom to transact is retained, since the results are nothing but the transactions, summed. Critics who found this too quick observed that it proves less than it appears to, a judgement the replies section will develop: a distribution can be criticised as the foreseeable product of rules that were chosen, and the chooser of rules cannot hide behind the unintendedness of outcomes he designed the machine to produce. The author's answer, that rules must be judged as rules, by their generality and their tendency over time rather than by single outcomes, is serious; whether it is sufficient is one of the genuinely open questions this article leaves open on purpose.

### Why does the social justice argument turn on whether anyone intended the outcome?

Because its author restricts the vocabulary of justice to conduct, to what an agent does and could have done otherwise. An outcome nobody chose, produced by millions of separate acts, has on his account no author to whom praise or blame for the pattern can attach, and the demand to correct it becomes a demand for an author.

The argument also carried a historical diagnosis that should be separated from its logic. Its author believed the phrase was an atavism, a survival from small-scale societies in which a chief or a council visibly distributed the product, carried into an extended order where distribution is nobody's task, and generating a permanent, unsatisfiable grievance against an arrangement that cannot answer it because there is no arranger to petition. One may reject the diagnosis and still feel the logic's pressure: every concrete proposal offered in the phrase's name, when examined, either specifies a pattern and an enforcing authority, or dissolves into a demand for fairer rules. What the argument cannot survive is the discovery that outcomes are, in the relevant sense, authored after all, and the strongest version of the reply, given below with the other Marxist replies, fastens exactly there, on the designed character of property law, inheritance, corporate form, and the monetary constitution, which are not weather but legislation. The exchange is among the most instructive in the whole critique literature, because both parties are arguing about where responsibility for a pattern can intelligibly sit, and that question outlives the doctrines that raised it.

## Spontaneous order and the order nobody designed

Running beneath all three arguments is a positive doctrine, the younger man's account of how order arises without an orderer. Language is his standing example: no committee invented grammar, no legislator decreed the vocabulary, yet the result is a rule-governed system of immense sophistication that serves its users better than any designed substitute has done, and that changes, adapts, and corrects itself through use. Money, on the account he inherited and extended from Menger, arose the same way, as traders converged on the most saleable commodity without anyone intending to create a medium of exchange. Much of the common law, in his telling, grew by judges articulating the expectations embedded in practice rather than by sovereigns issuing designs. He called such formations spontaneous orders, and he coined for the market order within them the term catallaxy, from a Greek root meaning both to exchange and to reconcile, preferring it to economy because economy properly names the deliberate management of a household's resources toward a hierarchy of ends, while a catallaxy has no single hierarchy, only the mutual adjustment of innumerable households and firms under abstract rules.

### What is catallaxy, and why did its author prefer it to the word economy?

His coinage for the market order, built from a Greek verb meaning to exchange and to turn an enemy into a friend. He preferred it because an economy, strictly, is a managed set of resources serving one ranking of ends, while the market order serves no single ranking and is therefore misdescribed by the household metaphor.

### How can an institution be the result of human action without being the product of human design?

By growing. Where many people, pursuing their own purposes under general rules, repeatedly adjust to one another, patterns crystallise that nobody aimed at: a language, a currency, a body of customary law. Each step is intentional; the resulting order is not, and its complexity can exceed anything a designing mind could have specified in advance.

The doctrine did real work in the system. It explained why the author trusted evolved rules over constructed ones, why he treated prices as one instance of a general phenomenon, the guidance of conduct by signals and conventions no one authored, and why his liberalism was of the evolutionary, almost Burkean temper rather than the contractarian one. It also armed his critique of what he called constructivist rationalism, the belief, which he traced through Descartes and the French tradition to the socialist movements, that institutions deserve allegiance only if a reason can be produced that designed them. Against that belief he set the possibility that an institution embodies more knowledge than any participant possesses, knowledge deposited by generations of trial, error, and imitation, so that reform should proceed piecemeal and reverently, testing each change against the order's own logic. Marxists reading this will recognise a family resemblance to historical materialism's suspicion of utopian blueprint-making, and the resemblance is not accidental: both traditions, at their best, insist that social forms are products of processes rather than of intentions, and they divide over which processes, and over whether the products deserve the presumption of wisdom or of indictment.

That presumption is the doctrine's contested step. From the premise that an order grew and survived, nothing follows about whether it serves those who live under it, unless survival is quietly equated with service. Feudal tenures grew; so did the slave trade's conventions; longevity is evidence of adaptation to an environment that includes the distribution of power within it. The author's more careful formulations claim only that evolved rules may embody knowledge their users cannot articulate, a claim about information, and slide, in his less careful pages and still more in his followers' pamphlets, toward a claim about legitimacy, that what grew deserves to stand. Keeping the informational claim and refusing the slide is the reading this article recommends, and it sets up the sharpest of the Marxist replies, which concerns not the doctrine's conclusion but its method.

## The functional explanation turned back

A functional explanation accounts for an institution by the beneficial effects it produces: the practice exists, the explanation runs, because of what it does for the system that harbours it. Marxist theory was repeatedly charged with relying on this form, explaining legal and political arrangements by their service to a mode of production, and the younger man numbered among the critics of loose teleology in social science, of treating societies as organisms whose parts exist to serve the whole's needs. The reply that respondents developed, and that this article records as one of the most elegant in the exchange, is that the account of spontaneous order is itself a functional explanation of exactly the proscribed kind. Money is explained by the service it renders to exchange; the common law's rules are explained by their contribution to the order's working; evolved institutions in general are commended because of what they do for the whole they inhabit. If explaining a practice by its beneficial consequences is a fallacy when a Marxist performs it on the state, the respondents ask, why is it wisdom when performed on the market's own furniture?

The reply does not demolish the doctrine, and the article should say why with precision. A functional explanation can be rescued, in either camp, by supplying the feedback mechanism through which the beneficial effect maintains the practice: for evolved institutions, the mechanism is imitation and selection, practices that serve their users being copied and practices that fail being abandoned, which is a genuine causal loop and not a mere incantation of purpose. Marxists can and do offer parallel loops for their own explanations, through the selection of legal forms by the interests they protect and the powers that enforce them. The exchange therefore ends not with a victor but with a standard: whoever explains by function owes the mechanism, and whoever supplies the mechanism may use the form. What the reply accomplishes is the removal of a double standard, and double standards, in a literature this polemical, are load-bearing structures. It also illustrates the pattern the bounded target names from the other side: an argument forged against an opponent's method turns out, on inspection, to be an argument the forger also needs, and its scope is bounded by that discovery.

### Where does the charge of functional explanation find its opening?

In the gap between how an institution arose and why it is praised. When an evolved practice is commended for the service it renders the whole order, the commendation explains the practice by its effects, which is the functional form its author criticised in others, and the opening widens wherever no selection mechanism is supplied to close it.

## Scientism and the French detour

The younger man's campaign against the misapplication of scientific method to society produced a shelf of essays, gathered in the early nineteen fifties, that many specialists rank with his best work. His quarry, named scientism, was the slavish imitation of the methods and language of the natural sciences in fields where the facts are not measurements but the actions and beliefs of people: the engineer's confidence that society is material to be shaped, the statistician's confidence that what cannot be counted does not count, the planner's confidence that a social order could be designed as a bridge is designed. He traced the doctrine's lineage to a specific source, the French tradition descending from Saint-Simon and Auguste Comte, engineers and system-builders who proposed to reorganise society as a single administered enterprise and to replace politics with the management of things. Saint-Simon's disciples literally founded schools of administration and built railways and canals while preaching the reorganisation of Europe under technical direction, and Comte supplied the philosophy, a hierarchy of sciences crowned by a science of society that would discover its laws and hand them to legislators.

This genealogy matters to an article about Marx for a reason the author himself underplayed: the critique of historicism and scientism, his most sustained engagement with the intellectual ancestry of socialism, strikes the French tradition full in the face and strikes Marx obliquely, at an angle. Marx knew the Saint-Simonians, absorbed their sense that history moves through stages and that production is society's foundation, and then subjected their politics to contempt, mocking their utopias and insisting that the emancipation of the working class would be its own act rather than an engineer's gift. The younger man's essays treat Marx largely as a transmitter and systematiser within a current whose headwaters were French, which is a defensible genealogy and a revealing evasion at once: the strongest version of the opposing doctrine, the one in which historical laws are tendencies operating through class struggle and in which no administrator stands outside society directing it, is not the version the scientism critique engages. A critique of Marxism that spends its force on Comte has answered the doctrine's cousins. Whether the answer reaches the doctrine itself depends on how much of Marx one judges to be Saint-Simonian inheritance, and that judgement, contested among scholars, cannot be settled by the essays that presuppose it.

### Which tradition does the scientism critique strike most directly?

The French one: the line from Saint-Simon's administered society through Comte's science of social order, in which society is an object for technical management and history a sequence of stages known to its theorists. Marx stands downstream of that line, influenced by it and sharply critical of it, rather than at its source.

The detour also explains a curiosity of the author's bibliography. For a thinker whose public identity rests on opposing Marxism, he wrote remarkably little about Marx, and what he wrote is concentrated in these genealogical essays and in scattered remarks elsewhere. The elder's case is similar in shape if different in detail: the book of 1922 engages socialist doctrine extensively, the parties, the programmes, the Russian experiment in progress, and Marxist economics as a living creed, but a systematic confrontation with the argument of Capital, its theory of value, its account of accumulation and crisis, is not undertaken there or later. That confrontation had been attempted a generation earlier by their own school's founder-figure in Vienna, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, whose critique of the value theory is the subject of the next article in this cluster, and both of our thinkers were content to treat the question as settled by his performance and to build elsewhere. A division of labour within a school is a respectable thing. Its consequence for citation is not respectable at all when it is forgotten, and the next two sections draw the consequence.

## The engagement with Marx that never happened

Let the observation be made plainly, since the whole assessment turns on it: neither of these thinkers wrote a sustained critique of Marx's economics. There is no volume from either pen that works through the theory of value chapter by chapter, tests the falling rate of profit against its author's own counteracting tendencies, examines the reproduction schemes, or engages the account of crisis on the texts where those arguments live. The elder's target, announced in his title and pursued through his book, is socialism: the institutional proposal that the means of production be held in common and production directed as a unit. The younger's targets are planning, constructivist rationalism, and the idea of social justice. Marx enters their pages as the most eminent representative of the family they oppose, credited with influence, charged with errors of method and of prophecy, and answered, where he is answered at all, at the level of the system he inspired rather than the arguments he made. A reader who finishes both men's major works knows a great deal about why they believed a planned economy cannot work and almost nothing about what they thought was wrong with the labour theory of value, for the sufficient reason that they did not there say.

This is not an accusation of bad faith, and the article should be clear about the legitimate defences before it draws the boundary. A school divides its labour; Böhm-Bawerk had engaged the value theory for the Austrians before either man published, and a thinker is not obliged to refight his grandfather's battles. A doctrine may also be opposed at its institutional conclusion without adjudicating every premise, as one may reject a bridge design by showing the river cannot be spanned at that point without re-deriving metallurgy. And the questions the two men did pursue, whether calculation and knowledge permit central direction, are questions Marx himself barely addressed, since the planned economy as an operating problem belongs to the century after him; on that terrain they were not evading Marx but exploring where he had not gone. All of this is true, and none of it enlarges what they can be cited for. A citation borrows the authority of a text for a proposition, and the borrowing is honest only where the text contains the proposition's support. The support these texts contain concerns systems of allocation. It does not contain a refutation of Marx's economics, and citations that present it as one are drawing on an account the authors never opened.

### How much of the critique touches the labour theory of value at all?

Almost none of it, in either man's major work. The value theory was engaged for their school by an earlier Viennese economist, and both treated that engagement as sufficient, turning their own fire on calculation, knowledge, and the politics of planning. Citations of them as refuters of Marx's value theory cite a performance neither gave.

Consider, by contrast, what a genuine engagement looks like, because the cluster's next article supplies one and the contrast is instructive. Böhm-Bawerk read the first and third volumes of Capital closely, identified the apparent contradiction between their accounts of price, and pressed it in a monograph that Marxists have been answering for over a century; whatever its merits, it is about Marx's economics, addressed to Marx's texts, and falsifiable against them. Our two thinkers produced nothing in that genre. The younger's scattered judgements on Marx, that his historicism was a species of the scientism he opposed, that his prophecies had failed, that his influence was calamitous, are verdicts in search of the trials that were never held in his pages. The elder was franker about the procedure: Marxism interested him as the reigning ideology of the movements he fought, and he engaged it as one engages a reigning ideology, at the points where it issued in policy. Candour about the procedure makes the elder's citations easier to grade than the younger's, a small irony given their reputations for rigour and caution respectively.

## The bounded target

The claim can now be stated in its full form, and this article names it the bounded target. Neither thinker engaged Marx's own economics in depth, and the political argument of the younger man's famous book is substantially milder than its reputation and was disclaimed by its author in its popular form, so citations of these thinkers against Marx specifically, or against welfare provision, exceed what the texts support. The claim has two halves, and each half disciplines a different traffic in citations. The first half disciplines the traffic that invokes two famous names to close arguments about value, exploitation, accumulation, and crisis: those arguments must be fought with the weapons that were actually forged for them, Böhm-Bawerk's and his respondents', or with newer work addressed to the texts, and the names of Mises and Hayek add nothing to them except borrowed altitude. The second half governs the parallel traffic, in which the famous book is invoked to close arguments about insurance, pensions, clinics, and floors of income: the book is a witness for the other side on those questions, and quoting it against them is not an interpretation but an inversion.

A bounded target is not a small one, and the article must resist the misreading, symmetrical to the one it corrects, that would shrink the two men into irrelevance. What remains inside the boundary is formidable: the knowledge argument, which reorganised how economics thinks about information and which every subsequent theory of planning has had to answer; the calculation argument, which set the terms of the twentieth century's most consequential debate about economic systems; the political argument, which identified real mechanisms, the delegation of power, the selection of the ruthless, the erosion of general rules, that operate wherever direction becomes comprehensive, and which retains its force exactly to the extent that its target is genuinely comprehensive. Bounding a target is what assessment does to every thinker in this series, Marx included, whose texts are likewise cited daily for propositions they do not contain. The operation is not diminishment. It is the restoration of the arguments to the size at which they can be believed.

### What follows for a citation once the target is seen to be bounded?

It must be re-aimed or withdrawn. A citation of these names supports conclusions about calculation, knowledge, and comprehensive direction, and it supports nothing about the labour theory of value, the justice of a pension, or the fate of a welfare floor. Borrowed authority spent outside the boundary is spent counterfeit.

There is a practical test the bounded target supplies to any reader meeting these names in the wild, and it costs nothing to apply. Ask which text, and which page, contains the proposition being supported. If the answer names the knowledge essay for a claim about information, or the calculation chapters for a claim about accounting, or the famous book for a claim about comprehensive planning and the politics it breeds, the citation is sound and may still be wrong on the merits, which is where argument should then go. If the answer gestures at the men's general eminence, at what everybody knows they proved, the citation is counterfeit in the strict sense defined above, however eminent the names and however true the proposition might be on other evidence. Eminence is not evidence, and the habit of treating it as evidence is the precise corruption that the study of these two, conducted honestly, is best placed to cure, since their own best work is a demonstration that mechanisms, not authorities, are what an argument is made of.

## The three arguments assessed

The article has separated three arguments that public discussion habitually fuses, and the separation should now be made permanent in the form this series uses for the purpose, a findable table that a reader can return to without rereading the whole. The three arguments assessed are the calculation argument, the knowledge argument, and the political argument, and they differ in author, in date, in kind, and in standing. The table records, for each, what it claims, which thinker advanced it, what would answer it, the best reply the Marxist tradition has produced, and whether it stands. The social justice argument and the doctrine of spontaneous order, treated in their own sections above, are doctrines of the later work rather than members of the original case against planning, and they are graded in the prose that surrounds the table rather than compressed into its rows.

### Why does the eldest argument receive the weakest standing in the table?

Because its claim was the most absolute. A proof of impossibility is falsified by any working counter-procedure, however imperfect, while a comparative claim about knowledge survives every imperfection in its rival. The elder staked everything on necessity; the younger claimed only superiority, and superiority is the stake that evidence can keep paying out on.

A table of this kind is a discipline on its own author, and its limits should be confessed. Compression flattens the replies, each of which receives a fuller hearing in the sections below, and the standing column delivers verdicts whose defeaters are stated separately and at length, because a verdict without its defeater is a prejudice with a footnote. Readers who use the table as a substitute for the argument will repeat, in miniature, the error the article exists to correct, the substitution of a portable summary for a text. The table is an index, not a verdict machine, and its proper use is to locate the section where the reader's disagreement should be taken to be tested.

## The Marxist replies at full strength

The replies deserve the same care as the arguments, and they are gathered in full in the article on [marxist replies to critiques](/2019/12/01/marxist-replies-to-critiques/); what follows states them at the strength their authors gave them, since an assessment that grades arguments against weakened replies grades nothing. Four replies bear on the knowledge argument and its companions, and a fifth, the strongest, receives a section of its own. The first reply concerns the future. Markets, the respondents observe, are as blind about what is coming as any bureau, and in one respect blinder, because a price aggregates expectations without auditing them, so that the system transmits fashionable error as faithfully as knowledge; the great speculative collapses were not failures of planning but of the telecommunications network itself, broadcasting confidence until the moment it broadcast ruin. A society deciding where its science, its infrastructure, and its industry should stand in thirty years cannot extract that decision from prices struck by people none of whom can see thirty years, and the knowledge argument, which excels at the routine present, has nothing to say to the constituted future except that nobody knows, a truth that afflicts its own side equally.

The second reply concerns third parties, already entered in the ledger of the price system's failures and pressed by the respondents into an argument about scale. The effects a price omits are not marginal blemishes on an otherwise complete information system; in an industrial society they include the condition of the air, the stability of the climate's patterns, the exhaustion of fisheries and soils, and the health of populations downstream of other people's transactions. A mechanism that is structurally silent about effects of that magnitude does not merely transmit less information than its admirers claim. It transmits a systematically biased selection, complete about everything that can be owned and priced, empty about everything that cannot, and the bias is not random noise around the social interest but a tilt away from it wherever the two diverge. The third reply concerns the designed character of the supposedly unintended, and it answers the social justice argument directly. The distribution a market produces is unintended in its detail, the respondents grant, but the rules that produce it, the law of property and inheritance, the forms of the corporation, the enforcement of contracts, the constitution of money, are designed, legislated, and continually revised by identifiable authorities who know in outline what distributions their rules will yield. A legislator who sets the rules of a game, foreseeing the shape of its results, cannot disclaim those results as nobody's work when the shape displeases him; the intention the social justice argument requires is present one level up, at the level where the pattern is chosen by choosing its generator.

### What do markets fail to learn about the future, on the respondents' account?

Everything that matters most about it, because a price is a record of expectations held in the present, and the expectations of crowds are subject to fashion, imitation, and panic. The signal transmits conviction, not knowledge, and in speculative episodes it transmits error at full strength to every participant at once.

The fourth reply is the functional one, already developed above: the spontaneous order doctrine explains and commends institutions by their effects, in the manner its author reproved in others, and owes the selection mechanism that alone makes the explanation respectable. To these four a historical observation is often added, less an argument than a reminder. The economies that actually industrialised under planning, however wastefully and at whatever human cost, did allocate resources on a vast scale for decades, which refutes the calculation argument in its letter, that such allocation is impossible, while leaving its spirit, that the allocation was chronically irrational by the standards prices would have imposed, available for argument. Respondents who deploy the reminder should notice that it cuts their own side too, since the record it invokes includes the shortages and falsified returns that the knowledge argument predicts, and the debate at its best has both camps plundering the same history for opposite morals. That is the mark of a genuine problem rather than a settled one, and it is why this cluster's verdicts are issued with their defeaters attached.

## The strongest objection: prices and purchasing power

The fifth reply is the strongest objection to the whole Austrian case, and it is stated here at full strength, in the form given it by the Marxist respondents who pressed it most seriously, Maurice Dobb in his engagements with the calculation debate and Alec Nove in his reconstruction of a feasible socialism. Prices, the objection runs, do not transmit information about wants. They transmit information about willingness to pay, and willingness to pay is desire multiplied by money, so that the signal a price carries is weighted, before it is sent, by the existing distribution of purchasing power. A rich man's whim outbids a poor family's necessity, not because the market errs but because the market is functioning exactly as designed, registering effective demand and nothing else. The price system therefore communicates, with marvellous efficiency, the wants of those who have money, and it is silent, with equal efficiency, about the wants of those who do not. The conclusion the respondents draw is precise and limited, and its precision is its strength: the knowledge argument establishes that markets transmit some information efficiently. It does not establish that markets transmit the information a society would need, if the society's purpose includes answering needs in proportion to their urgency rather than in proportion to the wealth of those who feel them.

Dobb pressed the point in the context where it bites hardest, the planning of investment. Even if a socialist board could borrow market prices for consumer goods, those prices would instruct it to produce for the distribution of income it inherited, and a society that wished to change the distribution, to feed, house, and school those whose wants the market could not hear, would find the price system not a neutral instrument but a conservative one, faithfully reproducing in its signals the priorities of the order it was meant to supersede. Nove, who conceded more to the knowledge argument than any Marxist of his generation and built his feasible socialism around markets for most goods, held the line at exactly this boundary: the market is an instrument for registering choices among alternatives as they present themselves to those with money, and a society must decide, politically and in advance, which wants it will answer regardless of the money behind them, health and education and subsistence among them, because on those the signal is not merely imperfect but absent. When the objection is stated in this form, it does not deny the telecommunications system works. It asks what the system is for, and observes that a telephone network connecting only the wealthy is not refuted by being excellent of its kind.

### Whose wants does a price system hear, on the objection's telling?

The wants of those who can pay, weighted by how much they can pay. It hears the desires of the moneyed at full volume, the desires of the poor faintly, and the needs of those without purchasing power not at all, however urgent those needs are, because its signals are generated by expenditure and by nothing else.

Let the objection's limits also be marked, since full strength includes knowing where a case ends. The objection does not show that a planning board hears the unmoneyed want any better; a board hears what its politics tells it to hear, and boards have ignored famines their statistics concealed as resolutely as any market ignored them. It does not show that prices transmit false information; the information is accurate about effective demand, and effective demand is a real thing a society needs to know. And it does not touch the knowledge argument's negative claim, that the centre cannot gather what the periphery knows. What it destroys is the inference, common in the popular literature and occasionally in the younger man's own perorations, from the efficiency of the transmission to the sufficiency of what is transmitted, from the market hears wonderfully to the market hears what matters. That inference is the hinge between the Austrian case as an argument about information and the Austrian case as a political philosophy, and the objection breaks the hinge while leaving the door, the knowledge argument itself, standing.

## What the argument claims, and what it does not

The answer to the strongest objection begins by separating the knowledge argument from the use its admirers make of it, because the objection lands on the use and glances off the argument. The knowledge argument, in its author's careful statements, is a claim about the transmission of information relevant to allocation: that such information is dispersed and tacit, that prices broadcast its practical import, and that no central gathering can replicate the broadcast. It is not a claim that the pattern of wants the prices register is the pattern a society ought to answer, and it is not a claim about the justice of the distribution that weights the signals. Its author knew the difference, and his system has a place for the distributional question, the floor of provision beneath the market, the rules whose justice is to be judged as rules, even if his followers and his critics both find that place too small. To answer Dobb and Nove by pointing out that the knowledge argument does not settle distribution is therefore not a dodge but a correction of aim: the objection refutes a philosophy of society that some liberals hold, in which market outcomes need no further justification, and it leaves standing the epistemological claim that was the younger man's actual contribution.

Then the concession, which this article makes without varnish, because the brief of an honest assessment requires it and because the texts require it too. The argument is comparative rather than absolute. It shows that one mechanism transmits certain information better than another; it does not show that the better transmitter is good enough for every purpose a society may set itself, and it certainly does not show that the purposes it serves badly should be abandoned for being served badly. Yet its proponents, in pamphlets, in speeches, and now and then in his own unguarded pages, state it as absolute, as though the demonstration that a board cannot know what prices know settled every question about what a society should collectively do. Each such statement is a gift to the objection, which then appears to refute the knowledge argument when it has only refuted the inflation of it. The discipline the bounded target imposes on the defence is the same discipline it imposes on the attack: claim for the argument exactly what its mechanism supports, a superiority in transmitting the knowledge of time and place for the allocation of resources among competing uses, and let the questions it does not answer, distribution first among them, be argued on their own ground, where the respondents' case is strong and deserves its day.

### What concession does an honest statement of the knowledge argument owe its critics?

That the claim is comparative, not absolute: prices transmit certain knowledge better than a board can gather it, and nothing in that superiority decides which wants a society should answer or excuses the silence about wants without money. Proponents who state the claim absolutely surrender ground the argument itself never occupied.

There is a further point of fairness to register before the verdicts, because the objection and the answer together reveal the shape of the whole debate. Each side's strongest case is a boundary drawn around the other side's favourite inference. The Austrians show that the inference from good intentions to a workable plan crosses a boundary at the knowledge problem. The respondents show that the inference from a workable price system to a justified society crosses a boundary at the distribution of purchasing power. A literature in which both boundaries are respected is a literature making progress, and the tragedy of the popular debate is that both are trampled daily by partisans who need the inferences and cannot afford the boundaries. This article's verdicts, which follow, are written inside both boundaries at once, which is why they will satisfy neither camp's pamphleteers and why they are, the article submits, the verdicts the texts and the record jointly support.

## Verdicts and the evidence that would reverse them

Every verdict in this cluster of articles is issued with its defeater, the statement of what would reverse it, and this article's two governing verdicts are these. The verdict that the knowledge argument stands would be defeated by a demonstration that dispersed and tacit knowledge can be elicited and updated at the required speed without market exchange. No such demonstration exists. Schemes for eliciting local knowledge by reporting have foundered on the corruption of the reporting chain and the tacit stratum's refusal to be reported; schemes that propose to extract the knowledge from data exhaust, from the digital residue of transactions, reproduce the price system's information while depending on the transactions that generate it, and the question whether such extraction could serve a system that had abolished the transactions remains exactly that, a question, which the companion inquiry into [whether planning can beat the market](/2019/10/15/can-planning-beat-the-market/) examines on its own ground. Until the demonstration is produced, the verdict stands, bounded as the preceding section bounded it.

The verdict that the political argument is weaker than its reputation would be defeated by textual evidence that its author intended the comprehensive reading, which the qualifications in the text and his later statements presently contradict. This defeater deserves a moment's attention, because it shows what kind of verdict the second one is: not a judgement that events refuted, since the argument's mechanisms did operate where direction was comprehensive, but a judgement about a text and its afterlife, reversible by the text itself. Were a passage produced in which the author of the famous book declared pensions and insurance to be themselves the road's first steps, the verdict would fall, and this article would fall with it on that point. No such passage has been produced, and the passages that exist run the other way, in the book's chapter on security and in four decades of restatement. The verdict therefore stands in its precise form: the political argument is a serious account of what comprehensive direction does to a polity, and it is weaker than its reputation because its reputation describes an argument, about welfare provision, that its author did not make and repeatedly disowned.

### What demonstration would bring the knowledge verdict down?

A working method, shown in operation rather than in blueprint, by which the dispersed and partly tacit knowledge of millions of participants is drawn out, kept current at the speed circumstances change, and placed at a centre's disposal for allocation, all without the exchanges whose prices presently perform the task.

### What textual evidence would restore the comprehensive reading of the famous book?

A passage, in the book or in its author's settled later statements, declaring provision against want, insurance, pensions, or an income floor to be themselves steps toward totalitarian rule. The record presently contains the opposite declarations, repeated across decades, which is why the comprehensive reading survives as folklore rather than as scholarship.

The remaining verdicts can be stated briefly, with their defeaters implied by the sections that argued them. The calculation argument stands defeated in its absolute form by the existence of procedures, Lange's and its descendants, that mimic price adjustment without property, and by the historical fact of planned allocation sustained for decades; it stands undefeated in its institutional form, the challenge to name the mechanism that settles mistaken conjectures about the future and starves them of resources, a challenge no respondent has met head-on. The social justice argument stands as a genuine difficulty for the phrase's careless users and falls as a refutation of the phrase itself, because the designed character of the rules restores the intention its logic requires, one level up. The spontaneous order doctrine stands as an account of where complex institutions come from and falls as a presumption that what grew deserves to stand, survival testifying only to fitness within surroundings that power helps to shape, not to service rendered to those who live under the result. Each of these is a split verdict, and the splits are not evasions. They are the shape the evidence takes when arguments built for a century's central quarrel are examined one mechanism at a time.

## Which argument stands strongest

The ordering this article has used throughout can now be defended in a paragraph. The knowledge argument stands strongest because its claim is the most modest relative to its support: it asserts a comparative superiority in transmitting a specified kind of information, the support is a mechanism anyone can inspect in operation, and its defeater, plainly stated, has not been met by any respondent or any technology. The political argument stands second, powerful against its proper object and disgracefully misused against objects its author exempted, a combination that makes it simultaneously the most influential and the most misreported of the three. The calculation argument stands third, honoured as the debate's founding act and as the source of the entrepreneurial challenge, but cut down in its letter by respondents and by history, surviving in a form its author would have regarded as a retreat. The doctrines of the later work stand apart from the ordering, the social justice argument as a live philosophical difficulty, the spontaneous order as a permanent acquisition of social theory whose normative slide must be watched every time it is used.

### Why does the knowledge argument outlast the calculation argument that fathered it?

Because it needs less. The father's claim required the impossibility of any substitute for market prices, and substitutes, partial and imperfect, were produced. The son's claim requires only that dispersed knowledge resists gathering, a fact about the world that every attempted gathering since has illustrated rather than overturned, and modest claims are the ones evidence keeps.

Students meeting this material in an examination should note what the ordering teaches about argument generally, a lesson worth more than the ordering itself. The durability of a critique is inversely related to the absoluteness of its claim, because absolute claims offer refutation a single target while comparative claims force the refuter to do the harder work of showing a better mechanism. The elder, who prized logical certainty above all, built the most refutable of the three arguments. The younger, who traded certainty for observation, built the one that a century of respondents, including respondents of genius, have dented without dislodging. There is a moral there for writers of critiques in every tradition, this series' subject included, and it is not the moral either camp expected when the seminar in Vienna began its work.

## The school behind them: the Austrian tradition

Neither man invented his premises alone, and an assessment that treats them as solitary prophets misses the tradition that armed them. The Austrian school began with Carl Menger's work of 1871, which rebuilt value theory on the choices of individuals confronting scarcity, dissolving the classical puzzle of why water, essential to life, is cheap while diamonds are dear, by relocating value from the substance of goods to the margin of their use. From Menger descended two lines that concern this article. One ran through Böhm-Bawerk to the theory of capital and interest, and to the school's one direct assault on Marx's economics. The other ran through Friedrich von Wieser and the elder himself to the theory of money and the calculation problem. The school's habits were formed in seminar rooms and in controversy with the German historical school, which denied that universal economic laws existed at all, and the habits show in both our thinkers: a suspicion of aggregates, an insistence that economic phenomena be traced to individual choices, and a taste for arguments conducted at the level of first principles, where a dispute about method can occupy a decade.

The tradition also explains the pairing's blind spots. A school formed against historicism learned to distrust historical evidence as a court for theory, which served the elder's apriorism and lingered in the younger's work even after he had rebuilt his foundations. A school formed in a multilingual empire, watching currencies and legal systems interlock across peoples who shared no government, acquired an instinctive appreciation for orders that coordinate strangers without commanding them, an instinct the younger generalised into his theory of spontaneous order. And a school that had been, since Menger, the house doctrine of a liberalism increasingly besieged in its own city developed the embattled confidence audible in the elder's prose and, transposed into courtesy, in the younger's. To say the men were products of a school is not to diminish them. It is to locate the origin of premises they rarely argued for because, within the seminar, nobody had ever needed them argued for, and to warn the reader that the deepest disagreements between the Austrians and their Marxist respondents are disagreements at that depth, about what an explanation in social science even is, which is why the exchange so often seemed to pass through the opposing camp without engaging it.

### What did the marginal revolution change about the question the Austrians asked?

It moved the explanandum. Classical theory asked what goods are worth, as a property of the goods themselves, and Marx's theory inherited the question. The Viennese asked instead what a chooser gives up at the margin, making value a relation between a person, a purpose, and a scarcity, and dissolving several classical puzzles by refusing their form.

## Vienna after the war: the world the calculation essay answered

The essay of 1920 was not written in a vacuum, and recovering its setting restores its point. The Habsburg empire had dissolved; Austria was a republic of six million people and an imperial capital built for fifty; the Russian revolution had installed a government claiming Marx's authority and attempting, in its war communism phase, the actual abolition of money and market exchange; Germany had convened a socialisation commission to consider which industries to take into common ownership; and Vienna itself was governed by Social Democrats who were building, within a capitalist republic, the famous municipal programme of housing, clinics, and schools that made the city a laboratory watched by all Europe. The question whether a socialist economy could work was not a seminar hypothetical. It was the live question of the continent, asked by governments, and the elder, who advised the Austrian state on finance and watched the Russian experiment's reports arrive, wrote his essay as an intervention in a debate whose other participants included men actually attempting the thing he declared impossible.

The setting also clarifies the essay's tone, so often mistaken for academic arrogance. He believed he was watching a civilisation attempt an act of self-destruction in good faith, and that the destruction would proceed not from the wickedness of the attempt but from its arithmetic. His opponents in the debate were not fools: the socialisation literature of those years contains sophisticated proposals, and the most interesting of them, Otto Neurath's, is taken up in the next section. Nor were the elder's allies all liberals of his stripe; the calculation problem troubled socialist economists too, and some of the ablest replies came from men, Dobb among them, who shared the goal and disputed only the impossibility. Recovering the setting converts the essay from a monument into a move, the opening move of a controversy whose later stages, the trial-and-error schemes, the knowledge reformulation, the computation claims of a later age, are unintelligible unless the first move is seen as addressed to contemporaries who had power, programmes, and a plausible claim that the future belonged to them.

## Calculation in kind: the proposal the elder was answering

Neurath, a sociologist and economic planner who had administered Bavaria's brief socialisation experiment, proposed that a socialist economy dispense with money altogether and calculate in kind, in natura: so many tons, hours, hectares, and calories, marshalled in a comprehensive physical plan drawn by a central statistical office. War economies, he argued, already operated substantially in kind, allocating by requisition and priority rather than by price, and their success under blockade showed that money was a historical garment an economy could discard. The elder's essay is best read as a reply to this proposal in its strongest form, and his counter-argument identified the exact seam where in-kind planning tears. A physical plan can list what a project consumes and what it yields, but it cannot compare two projects that consume and yield different bundles, because tons and hours and hectares do not reduce to one another, and every attempt to weight them smuggles in, under the name of technical coefficients or social priorities, precisely the valuation that prices were supposed to replace, now performed by an official's fiat instead of by the bids of owners.

The exchange has a modern echo that the article notes without pursuing, since the contemporary planning question belongs to a companion article: every scheme that promises to replace prices with data must answer the elder's question in Neurath's place, by what unit the unlike is made like, and the answers on offer, labour time, energy, computational shadow prices, are revivals of candidates the original debate already examined. Labour time, Marx's own candidate in some moods, fails on the heterogeneity of labour and the claims of land and time; energy fails on everything that is not energy; shadow prices succeed arithmetically and concede the argument institutionally, since a shadow price generated by a model is only as good as the model's knowledge, which returns the dispute to the younger man's ground. The durability of the 1920 exchange lies in this structure: it identified a permanent problem, the commensuration of unlike things, and every generation's technology has to solve it afresh rather than inherit a solution.

## The elder on interventionism: the unstable middle

Between the market order and the planned economy the elder placed a third system and a famous instability thesis about it. Interventionism, the pattern of governments that leave ownership in private hands while overriding its results by decrees, price controls, tariffs, subsidies, and mandates, was in his analysis not a stable compromise but a process with its own logic of escalation. Each intervention disappoints its authors, because it disturbs the price signals through which the economy adjusts, and the disappointment presents the authorities with a choice they reliably resolve in one direction: repeal the measure, or extend control to the new disturbance the first measure created. Rent control produces a housing shortage, which invites allocation schemes, which invite construction mandates, which invite control of building materials, and the staircase descends, in his telling, toward comprehensive direction by instalments, unless the political will to repeal interrupts it. The thesis matters to this article for two reasons. It is the actual ancestor of the slope imagery that popular discussion attributes to the younger man's book, and it shows the elder making, in his own name and on his own logic, a dynamic argument about partial measures that the younger man conspicuously did not make about welfare provision. And it is a genuinely contestable empirical claim dressed in the elder's deductive clothing, since the century supplied many mixed economies that climbed back up the staircase as readily as they descended it, a record the thesis handles awkwardly.

The contrast between the two men's treatments of the middle ground repays closer attention than any other in this assessment. The elder's system has no stable middle: property is either directed by its owners under the discipline of prices or progressively absorbed into direction by the state, and his political counsel followed the logic, opposing nearly every intervention as a step on a path. The younger man's system has a principled middle, defined by the distinction between the framework of general rules and the direction of particular outcomes: within the framework, a state may insure, relieve, educate, and regulate, and his quarrel was with measures that suppress the price mechanism or privilege particular positions, not with provision as such. Two thinkers, one slogan in the public memory. A reader who has grasped why the elder's middle is unstable and the younger's is principled has grasped the methodological difference between them more concretely than any summary of praxeology can convey, and will never again be able to hear the paired citation without asking which of the two is actually being quoted.

## Money, credit, and the cycle: the younger's first reputation

Before he was a political philosopher the younger man was a monetary economist of formidable technical reputation, and the episode shaped the knowledge argument more deeply than is usually noticed. His theory of the trade cycle, developed from the elder's monetary work and from Swedish sources, held that a banking system which expands credit pushes the rate of interest below the level that coordinates saving with investment, coaxing producers into longer, more capital-heavy processes than the public's willingness to wait can sustain; the boom is the economy building a structure its resources cannot finish, and the slump is the discovery. Whatever the theory's fate, and the profession largely set it aside after Keynes, its form is prophetic of everything that followed. It is an argument about coordination across time through a price, the interest rate, that can lie; about knowledge that exists only dispersed among savers and investors; about the impossibility of any authority seeing the whole structure it disturbs. The man who had spent a decade on the ways a single falsified price can misdirect an entire economy was prepared, when the planning debate reached him, to generalise the insight: all prices are signals, all signals can be absent or corrupted, and a system that abolishes them has not simplified its problem but blinded itself.

The Keynes controversy added a personal and methodological layer. The younger reviewed the elder statesman's treatise on money with a severity its author returned in kind, and the exchange convinced him that aggregate concepts, total output, the general price level, the volume of employment, were not merely imprecise but actively misleading, veils over the relative movements in which economic reality consists. Marxists will recognise the gesture, since their own tradition makes a parallel complaint against aggregates that conceal class relations, and the parallel is worth a sentence: both schools accused the mainstream of its century of mistaking accounting identities for mechanisms, and both were, in that limited respect, right in the same way at the same time, while drawing opposite conclusions about what the concealed mechanisms were. The younger's conclusion was that the concealed reality is a structure of individual plans coordinating through prices. The Marxist conclusion was that it is a structure of classes reproducing through production. The century's economics was largely the story of which concealment the profession chose to lift.

## Methodological individualism: the shared premise examined

Beneath both men's arguments sits a premise they shared with their whole school and rarely defended at length, because within the school it functioned as common sense: that social phenomena are to be explained as the outcomes of individual actions, and that entities like society, the nation, or the class do not act, choose, or know anything except through the persons who compose them. The younger gave the premise its careful form, insisting that it is a rule of explanation rather than a psychology: it does not claim men are selfish atoms, only that the wholes we speak of are patterns thrown up by their interactions, and that a science which treats the patterns as agents with purposes of their own has lapsed into mythology. The premise powers the knowledge argument directly, since knowledge, on this view, cannot be society's possession held in a single store; it exists only in the distributed form, in heads, and the planner's dream of gathering it is the mythology applied to information. It also powers the spontaneous order doctrine, since an order nobody designed is precisely a pattern that is real without being an agent.

The Marxist objection to the premise is among the deepest in the whole exchange and should be stated as its holders state it. Classes, on the Marxist account, are not fictions or mere sums: a person's position in the relations of production shapes the choices that present themselves to him, the information that reaches him, and the interests his reason will discover, so that an explanation which begins from individuals as given has begun one step too late, taking as nature what history produced. The worker and the owner who meet in the Austrians' market arrive there already constituted by a structure the market story does not tell. Defenders of the premise reply that constitution is not agency, that structures act only through persons, and that the Marxist who says capital exploits labour must still, when pressed, narrate the pressing, the hiring, and the paying in individual acts. The exchange has no clean winner, and this article does not award one; it records the premise because the verdicts above depend on it at exactly one point. The knowledge argument survives even if the premise is granted only in its weakest form, that information is in fact dispersed among persons, which no theory of class denies. The spontaneous order doctrine, by contrast, needs the premise in a stronger form, that patterns unauthored by anyone are thereby innocent of anyone's purposes, and that stronger form is where the respondents' objection about designed rules finds its mark.

### Does the knowledge argument depend on denying that society can be studied as a whole?

No. It depends on a fact no holism denies, that information is held by separate persons in local and partly inarticulate forms. A theorist may treat classes, states, and systems as real structures and still concede that the knowledge of a spoiled batch or an idle machine exists only where the batch spoiled and the machine stands idle.

## Prices are not incentives: a confusion cleared

One misreading in the brief against these thinkers is so common that it deserves a section rather than a sentence in the catalogue: the belief that the knowledge argument is an argument about incentives. It is not, and the difference matters because the two arguments have different strengths, different respondents, and different defeaters. An incentive argument says that people under planning will not exert themselves, will not report honestly, will not maintain what nobody owns; it is a claim about motivation, and it can be met, as it often has been, by pointing to motivated planners, to honour, vocation, and patriotism, which are real forces that real systems have run on for considerable periods. The knowledge argument says something colder: that even a planner of perfect motivation and tireless industry cannot act on knowledge he does not have, and cannot have it, because it does not exist in gatherable form. Saints and heroes are irrelevant to it. The confusion arises because the two arguments are usually deployed together by the same pamphlets, and because the incentive problem, which this series treats in its own article, is easier to grasp and more fun to argue about at dinner. But conflating them weakens both: the incentive argument inherits a certainty it has not earned, and the knowledge argument inherits a refutability, show me one honest manager, that it never risked.

The younger man was himself partly responsible for the confusion, since his political writings do move between epistemic and motivational registers, and the selection argument about the worst rising to the top is a claim about incentives and character, not about knowledge. Disentangling his own pages is part of the assessor's work, and the disentangling pays an unexpected dividend: it shows that the two registers have opposite relations to the evidence. Motivational claims are embarrassed by every decade in which a planned system functioned at all, staffed by people who plainly exerted themselves; epistemic claims are confirmed by the same decades, whose archives are full of the falsified returns, hoarded materials, and concealed capacities that a reporting chain generates when the centre cannot check what it is told. A critic who wishes to answer the Austrians should therefore always ask which argument is on the table, and a defender who wishes to keep the strong one should refuse to let the weak one borrow its name.

## The rule of law strand

Inside the political argument runs a strand that legal scholars have prized more highly than economists have, and it deserves extraction. The rule of law, in the younger man's formulation, is not the mere existence of statutes or the obedience of officials to commands from above. It is a condition in which the coercive power of the state is exercised only under general rules, announced in advance, applying to all persons including the rulers, and abstract enough that no one can predict, when a rule is made, exactly whom it will advantage or burden. Such rules make the environment of private life calculable: a citizen can plan because he can know the framework within which his plans will be judged, and the state's own interventions are confined to enforcing the framework rather than directing the traffic inside it. Comprehensive planning is the systematic negation of that condition. A plan must be adjusted continually to circumstances, must discriminate between industries, regions, firms, and persons, must treat like cases unlike because the plan's needs differ between them, and must therefore proceed by discretionary decision, by measures whose content cannot be announced in advance because it is invented in response to the plan's own unfolding. The citizen under such a regime cannot plan his life against the rules, because the relevant rule is that there will be decisions, and their content is the one thing he cannot know.

The strand's strength is that it identifies a cost of planning that persists even if every epistemic problem were solved. Imagine the knowledge argument defeated, the centre perfectly informed; the rule of law strand still asks by what general rules the informed centre will act, and observes that a centre which may do anything its information recommends is a centre under no law in the relevant sense, however benevolent its information and however accurate its aim. Its weakness is the mirror of the political argument's general weakness: the condition it prizes is a matter of degree in every actual state, wartime liberal democracies having governed by discretionary direction for years without ceasing to be recognisably lawful orders, and the strand, like its parent argument, must therefore be read as an account of a tendency with a mechanism rather than as a timetable. Read so, it has worn well: the literature on regulatory discretion, on the difference between rules and deals in economic policy, and on why investors and citizens alike pay for predictability, is a long footnote to a chapter of a book most economists stopped reading at the title.

## Why the worst get on top: the selection argument examined

The most quoted chapter of the famous book advances neither an epistemic nor a legal claim but a claim about selection, and it should be examined separately because its logic is distinct and its evidence is of a different kind. In a system moving toward comprehensive direction, the chapter argues, the premium on agreement rises: the plan requires a unified scale of ends, and those who can supply the appearance of unity, by simplifying, by denouncing, by organising the faithful and isolating the doubters, acquire an advantage in the competition for power that the scrupulous and the tolerant cannot match. Negative selection operates at every level. The tasks the system most needs performed, the enforcement of the ranking against those it burdens, repel the fastidious and attract those for whom the exercise of power over others is itself a reward; the propaganda the system requires selects for men untroubled by the distance between the slogan and the fact; and the leader who emerges is not the original planner with his blueprint but the operator best fitted to a machine whose fuel is unanimity. The argument's grim elegance is that it requires no conspiracy and no original sin in the planners: decent men may launch the process, and the process will replace them, because the qualities it rewards are not the qualities they have.

Assessed coldly, the chapter is the political argument's most vulnerable and most suggestive component. Vulnerable, because selection effects of this kind operate in many systems, including electoral democracies whose campaigns reward simplification and punish nuance, and the chapter does not supply the comparison that would show the effect to be stronger, rather than merely present, under direction. Suggestive, because the mechanism it names, that institutions select the characters who rise within them, has become a standard tool of political science, and because the historical cases its author had before him, the displacement of old revolutionaries by apparatchiks in one country and of conservative elites by movement cadres in another, fit the mechanism with a fidelity that still startles. The honest verdict is the one this article has given the parent argument throughout: a real mechanism, demonstrated as a tendency, unproven as a destiny, and wholly silent, be it noted once more, about societies that insure their members' risks while leaving production's direction to the market, since in such societies the premium on manufactured unanimity, the mechanism's fuel, is never laid on.

### What quality does the selection argument say a directed system rewards above the rest?

The capacity to manufacture agreement: to simplify ends until they can be unanimous, to organise the convinced and isolate the doubting, and to perform the plan's coercive tasks without fastidiousness. The argument holds that such a system promotes by fitness for its own needs, and that its needs are not the needs of humane government.

## The book's first readers: Britain in the war years

Context disciplines judgement, and the famous book's first context was a Britain that had run a war economy of remarkable comprehensiveness without ceasing to be a parliamentary democracy, and was debating, through the Beveridge report and the manifestos of a coming election, how much of the wartime direction to keep in peace. Its author wrote as an adopted citizen alarmed that the habits of the war, admirable under bombardment, would be enthroned as the constitution of the peace, and that a generation taught to admire planning by its success against an enemy would not notice the difference between directing an economy toward one agreed end, victory, and directing it toward the innumerable ends of a free society, which agree on nothing. The distinction between the war case and the peace case is the book's unspoken foundation: a single overwhelming end, temporarily shared by nearly everyone, is precisely the condition under which planning works least badly, and generalising from it to a society whose ends are plural and permanent is the error the book exists to warn against. Readers who skip the context mistake a wartime intervention for a timeless prophecy, and then either obey it as one or refute it by pointing to the war economy it never condemned.

The book's reception enacted its own argument in a minor key. Abridged in a mass-circulation magazine and condensed into a cartoon booklet by its admirers, it reached millions in a form from which every qualification had been edited away, the endorsements of insurance, the floor of provision, the careful restriction of the thesis to comprehensive direction. The author, who had written a book about the corruption of language and intention by systems, watched his own text undergo the corruption in the marketplace of ideas, abridged by friends. He protested mildly and profited greatly, in fame if not in royalties, and the episode hardened into the pattern this article calls the disclaimed reading, in which the version of a text that circulates is the version its transmitters need, and the author's corrections pursue it forever a step behind. Keynes, to whom the author sent the book, replied with a famous ambivalence, admiring its moral force while doubting its economics and remarking that the dangerous men were not planners as such but those who would plan without the virtues; the exchange remains the most efficient summary of the book's standing among serious readers, respected as a warning, resisted as a demonstration.

## The critique among the critiques

This article's subjects occupy a specific pew in a crowded church, and locating them prevents a common error of scale. The critiques of Marxism surveyed in [the complete guide to the critiques of Marxism](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/) divide into families: critiques of the economics, of the philosophy of history, of the political record, of the ethics, and of the feasibility of the alternative proposed. Mises and Hayek belong almost entirely to the last family, with the political argument straying into the third. They are not the authors of the canonical critique of the value theory, which is Böhm-Bawerk's; nor of the standard critique of historicism, which is Popper's, though the younger man's scientism essays run parallel to it; not of the canonical critique of the labour theory from within economics' mainstream, nor of the ethical critique associated with the liberal theory of rights. Their distinction is narrower and, within its bounds, greater: they asked, earlier and more sharply than anyone else, whether the proposed alternative could operate at all, and they forced every subsequent defender of planning to answer an operational question before reaching the moral ones. A reader who understands their pew will stop expecting from them what the other pews contain, and will stop forgiving the other pews for lacking what only this one supplies.

The family's internal order also matters. Within the feasibility critique, the calculation argument is the founding document, the knowledge argument the mature form, and the later literature, on incentives, on soft budget constraints, on the political economy of shortage, a series of annexes built by other hands, many of them hands that had worked inside the systems described. The two men thus stand at the head of a research programme as well as of a polemic, and programmes are judged differently from polemics: by whether the questions they opened remained fertile. By that standard the programme succeeded beyond its authors' politics, since the questions of how information moves through an economy, of what institutions do to the knowledge of their participants, and of how rules constrain the rulers, are now the common property of economists who would not call themselves Austrian and of historians who would not call themselves liberal, and the fertility is the strongest evidence that the bounded target, for all its bounds, encloses something real.

## The discipline's verdict: economics and the Austrian challenge

The economics profession's treatment of the two men is a study in delayed and partial justice, and it belongs in this assessment because the popular debate assumes a verdict, in one direction or the other, that the profession never delivered. The calculation debate was long reported, in textbooks the younger man lived to see, as a victory for the respondents: Lange's scheme was held to have answered the elder on paper, and the matter was filed as closed. Later scholarship, re-reading the exchange, revised the filing: the respondents had answered the arithmetic and missed the knowledge problem, which the younger man had been pressing all along in essays the profession's memory had treated as restatements. The discipline's broader quarrel with its own mainstream, traced in the article on [Marxist and mainstream economics](/2016/07/15/marxist-vs-mainstream-economics/), forms the backdrop: the Austrians and the Marxists were the two great dissenting traditions of the century, each accusing the mainstream of formalism, each partly absorbed by it in the end. Information economics, the study of asymmetric knowledge and incentives that grew into a central field, vindicated the knowledge problem's importance while declining its author's conclusions, a pattern, vindication of the question and rejection of the answer, that is the usual fate of strong heterodox work.

The younger man's Nobel memorial prize, awarded in 1974 and shared, with delicious irony, with Gunnar Myrdal, an economist of nearly opposite convictions, marked the profession's formal rehabilitation of a man it had treated for two decades as a political pamphleteer who had wandered from the fold. The elder received no such rehabilitation in his lifetime, and his posthumous influence ran through movements rather than departments, which is one source of the asymmetry in their reputations: the younger is a figure in the history of economics who also wrote famous political books, while the elder belongs to the history of a movement and wrote economics along the way. Assessments should correct for the asymmetry in both directions, reading the elder's economics with the seriousness the profession withheld, and reading the younger's politics with the scepticism the profession's applause sometimes suspends. Neither correction is optional for the reader who wants the arguments rather than the legends, and both are applications, once again, of the bounded target, which is at bottom a rule for reading famous men: grade the text, not the afterlife.

### What did the later re-reading of the calculation debate change in the profession's filing?

The winner. The exchange had been recorded as a victory for the trial-and-error respondents, who answered the arithmetic. Re-reading showed that the younger man's intervention had shifted the question to knowledge, which the respondents had not answered, and the filing was amended to a split decision that subsequent information economics has largely sustained.

## Inside the firm: the knowledge argument at small scale

A test of an argument's reality is whether it illuminates cases its author never considered, and the knowledge argument passes this test inside the capitalist firm itself. A large corporation is, internally, a planned economy: it allocates capital among divisions by committee, sets internal transfer prices by rule, gathers returns from branches, and suffers, in recognisable miniature, every pathology the argument predicts. Headquarters misreads its subsidiaries; divisions conceal capacity to soften their targets; information arrives aggregated, late, and massaged by what the reporting chain rewards. The theory of the firm, from Coase onward, asks why such islands of planning exist at all if markets coordinate so well, and answers that planning and market are alternatives along a margin, each used where its information costs are lower, which is precisely the comparative, bounded form of the Austrian claim translated into organisational terms. The younger man, who wrote appreciatively about the evolution of firms and markets as alternative discovery procedures, would have recognised the translation and accepted it, since it concedes his mechanism while denying his pamphleteers their absolute.

The small-scale version also disciplines the argument's admirers in a way worth stating. If the knowledge problem were the absolute barrier the elder's rhetoric suggested, no firm could grow beyond the span of one manager's knowledge, and no army, railway, or retailer could coordinate its thousands. They plainly do, at costs that rise with scale and complexity, which is why the comparative form of the claim is the true one: planning works, at a price in information, and the price grows until, somewhere between the firm and the whole economy, it becomes prohibitive for the routine allocation of everything. Where exactly the line falls is an empirical and institutional question, not a theorem, and the honest Austrian position, the younger man's position at his best, is that the line falls far short of the comprehensive plan, not that it falls at the first traffic light or the first pension. Every attempt to move the line by rhetoric, in either direction, should be met with the demand this article has made throughout: name the mechanism, state the comparison, attach the defeater.

## Computation and the elicitation problem

Each generation rediscovers the calculation debate when its machines improve, and the claim returns in new dress: that sufficient computing power, fed with sufficient data, dissolves the problem the Austrians posed. The claim deserves a precise answer, because it is half right in a way that conceals the half that matters. Computing power answers the arithmetic. Given a complete statement of resources, techniques, and ends, machines can solve allocation problems of a size that would have staggered Lange's board, and the elder's contention that the equations could not in practice be worked is the part of his case that time has most plainly overtaken. But the knowledge argument was never about the arithmetic. It was about the statement: about where the data come from, in what form, at what speed, and with what honesty. A machine can optimise over the knowledge it is given; the question is whether the knowledge of the machinist's ear, the buyer's nose, the farmer's decade of weather, and the future wants of people who do not yet know them themselves, can be given to it, and that is a question about elicitation, not about processing. Data harvested from market transactions presuppose the transactions; a system that abolished exchange in producer goods would be asking its machines to learn from a world it had prevented from generating the lessons.

There is a second difficulty, quieter and deeper, that the computation claim inherits from the political argument. Optimisation requires an objective, a single ranking of ends expressed in a form the machine can maximise, and supplying that ranking is the act the political argument showed no assembly can perform and no administrator can perform innocently. A computer does not dissolve the question of whose ends count; it conceals the question inside the specification, where it is answered by whoever writes the objective function, with the added danger that the answer now wears the authority of mathematics. The Austrians' joint lesson, applied to the new dress, is therefore double: ask of any computational scheme where its knowledge is elicited from, and ask whose ranking it optimises. Schemes that answer both questions honestly are contributions to the debate the two men started. Schemes that answer neither have merely automated the evasion the debate was started to expose, and the exposure retains its value in exact proportion to the power of the machines.

## The society they founded and the popularisers

In 1947 the younger man gathered three dozen economists, historians, and philosophers at a hotel above Lake Geneva and founded a society intended to keep liberal ideas alive through what he expected to be a long winter of collectivist consensus. The society succeeded beyond its founding brief, becoming a durable network through which the two men's ideas, and many others', travelled into universities, journalism, and eventually governments. The afterlife that followed is the large-scale version of the cartoon booklet: a body of doctrine, nuanced in the texts and conditional in its arguments, was progressively simplified as it travelled, each relay station keeping the conclusions and shedding the conditions, until the names of the two thinkers could be invoked, by speakers who had read neither, as warrants for positions the texts do not contain and in the younger man's case explicitly disown. The elder, who died in 1973, lived to see his intransigence become a movement's badge and to approve much of what the movement did in his name. The younger, who died in 1992, lived to see his name attached to programmes he had not designed and to spend his last decades, as he had spent the decades after the famous book, issuing the corrections that the first section of this article recorded.

An assessment is not a court of morals, and the popularisers are not on trial here; movements simplify, and authors who found societies cannot complain that societies have members. But the phenomenon is evidentially relevant to the bounded target, because it explains the gap the target measures. The citations that exceed the texts are not random errors; they are the predictable output of a transmission process that rewards certainty, punishes qualification, and converts every conditional argument into a banner. The same process, the article notes in fairness, works on the other side of the quarrel with identical mechanics, and the next section but one takes up the pattern as a general phenomenon of this series. For the two men themselves, the defence against their own afterlife is the one they both, in their different idioms, recommended for every text: return to the argument, identify its mechanism, and refuse the version that arrives pre-chewed, however friendly the chewing.

## Reading the two men: the texts in order

A reader persuaded that the texts matter more than the afterlife will ask where to begin, and the article answers briefly, as a service rather than a summary. For the elder, begin with the essay of 1920, which is short, fierce, and complete in itself, then the calculation and intervention chapters of the book of 1922, then the treatise of 1949 for the system whole, reading the praxeology chapters as a statement of method to be argued with rather than a toll to be paid. For the younger, begin with the two essays, on economics and knowledge and his companion piece on knowledge in society, which contain the argument that will outlive the rest; then the famous book, read entire and slowly, with the chapter on security marked; then the treatise on liberty for the system in its maturity, the floor of provision included; and last the work on law and liberty, where the argument about social justice and the doctrine of undesigned order receive their fullest statements. Read in that order, the two men stop being a citation and become what they were, the two most formidable critics the socialist idea produced from within the liberal tradition, formidable in proportion as their claims are kept to the size their arguments actually made them.

## The elder's life: exile and intransigence

Biography is not argument, but in the elder's case it explains the argument's temperature. He was the economic adviser who told successive Austrian governments unwelcome truths about inflation and credit, and watched them inflate anyway; he kept, by his own account, a suitcase packed through the nineteen thirties against the knock he expected; he left for Geneva in 1934, and for New York in 1940, one step ahead of the regime whose rise he had predicted in print when prediction was costly. In America he was a great man without a chair of his own, holding a visiting professorship paid for by a foundation, teaching a seminar of the devoted in rooms the university lent him, and publishing the treatise of 1949 that his followers regard as the century's systematic statement of liberal economics and the profession largely declined to review. He never moderated. Where the younger man trimmed, qualified, and rebuilt, the elder regarded every concession as the first instalment of a surrender, and his American circle learned the posture with the doctrine, so that his afterlife in the movement he inspired is a compound of genuine intellectual courage and a sectarian refusal to distinguish the essential from the habitual.

The intransigence had a cost that an assessment should register without relish. A thinker who will not distinguish his theorem from his temperament invites his readers to do the distinguishing badly, and the elder's reputation has suffered the consequences in both directions: dismissed by opponents who met the temperament and never reached the theorem, and embalmed by followers who preserved the temperament as though it were the theorem. The calculation argument, the entrepreneurial challenge, and the analysis of interventionism survive the man and are stronger without his certainties attached. That is the treatment this article has given them, and it is, though he would not have thanked the article for it, the treatment his own method prescribes, since a deduction is supposed to stand on its premises and not on the character of the deducer.

## The younger's life: the long migration

The younger man's life describes an arc from the centre of a discipline to its margin and back, and the arc left marks on the work. He arrived in London in 1931 as the Austrian school's champion, a young professor whose lectures on prices and production were the sensation of a season; within a decade the Keynesian tide had taken the profession, his cycle theory was an embarrassment his own students were abandoning, and he had begun the migration, through the political book and the scientism essays, toward the questions that would occupy his remaining fifty years. The war made him British; the peace made him restless; Chicago gave him a committee on social thought rather than an economics chair, a detail his admirers cite as the profession's verdict on its own priorities, and Freiburg, and finally Salzburg and Freiburg again, gave him the quiet in which the vast trilogy on law and liberty was assembled. The Nobel award of 1974 arrived when he was seventy-five and largely written off, and it licensed a final decade of extraordinary productivity and public controversy, in which the elder statesman of liberalism commented on everything and was quoted, usually in the simplified form, on everything.

The migration matters because it explains the layering of the oeuvre that this article has had to disentangle. The economist of the nineteen thirties, the political writer of the war, the philosopher of law of the seventies, are one man at three depths, and the arguments belong to their layers: the knowledge argument to the economist, the political argument to the wartime writer, the social justice doctrine and the theory of spontaneous order to the philosopher. Readers who cite across the layers without noticing, who arm the economist's rigour around the pamphleteer's conclusions or blame the philosopher for the wartime writer's urgency, commit the biographical version of the error the bounded target corrects in citations. He was also, it should be recorded in fairness against his own side's mythology, a man who changed his mind in public when the evidence moved: on the trade cycle's policy lessons, on the possibilities of a monetary constitution, on the minimum floor of provision, where his position hardened into doctrine in the direction of acceptance. The corrections of his readers that this article has emphasised were not the vanity of an author protecting a text. They were the acts of a thinker whose deepest commitment was to the proposition that nobody, himself included, possesses the whole of any important question, which is the knowledge argument turned, as it should be, upon its author.

## The shadow of the school's one engagement

It is worth pausing over the engagement that made the two men's non-engagement possible, because the division of labour within the Austrian school is the hinge on which the bounded target turns. Böhm-Bawerk, Menger's successor and a finance minister of the empire, published in 1896 a monograph on the close of Marx's system, arguing that the third volume of Capital, appearing posthumously, contradicted the first on the relation of values to prices, and that the contradiction was fatal. The Marxist replies began immediately, Hilferding's defence being the most famous, and the quarrel over the transformation of values into prices ran for a century through journals neither of our thinkers much troubled. The point for this article is institutional rather than doctrinal: within the seminar, the Marx question was filed as answered, and answered by the school's most authoritative living figure when the elder was a student. A young economist formed in that room learned that the value theory had been dealt with, and that the frontier lay elsewhere, in money, in cycles, in the new question of whether the socialist commonwealth that Marx's followers proposed could function. The filing was understandable and the frontier judgement was even right. But a filing is not a refutation's renewal, and when later generations of the school's admirers cited the school as having disposed of Marx's economics root and branch, they were citing the filing, the memory of an engagement conducted by a man dead before the first world war, on texts whose interpretation had meanwhile been revolutionised by the publication of manuscripts neither side of 1896 could read.

The episode carries a general warning that this series has occasion to repeat. Intellectual schools are filing systems as much as they are research programmes, and the confidence with which a school reports a question closed is evidence about the school's internal administration, not about the question. The Austrians filed Marx under value theory, answered; the Marxists, symmetrically, filed the calculation problem under bourgeois apologetics, answered, and both filings spared their holders decades of rereading at the price of periodic embarrassment when an outsider reopened the drawer. The two men's greatness, such as it is, consists partly in having reopened a drawer their own side preferred shut, the operational question, and the bounded target consists in noticing which drawers they left shut themselves. An assessment that performs both operations, the honouring and the noticing, has done for its subjects exactly what they, at their best, did for theirs.

## The respondents in person

The replies surveyed earlier were made by men, and their biographies explain their angles of attack as the Austrians' explained theirs. Oskar Lange was a Polish economist of genuine brilliance who taught in America, served the postwar Polish state as a diplomat, and returned to build its economics establishment, living his answer to the calculation problem as a career: the trial-and-error scheme was not a debating device for him but a design he hoped to see installed, and his later disenchantments, recorded obliquely in his writings on the bureaucratisation of planning, are among the debate's most poignant documents. Maurice Dobb, at Cambridge, was the Marxist tradition's most technically equipped economist in Britain, a man who had read the Austrians in the original and answered them in their own vocabulary, and his insistence that prices are distribution-laden was the product of a lifetime spent watching markets ration necessities away from the poor of two continents. Alec Nove, a Russian-born Scot who had served in the British administration that dealt with the Soviet economy's wartime reality, brought to the debate what neither Viennese possessed: operational knowledge of how the planned economy actually behaved, its hoarding, its storming at the plan period's end, its managers' arts, and his feasible socialism was an attempt to keep the tradition's ends while surrendering, to the knowledge argument, most of its traditional means.

The debate is richer when its respondents are read as witnesses rather than as scores in a match. Lange testifies that a socialist economist of the first rank believed the arithmetic soluble and spent his life discovering what the solution cost in bureaucracy. Dobb testifies that the distributional objection was not an afterthought but the Marxist case's centre of gravity from the beginning, since a tradition founded on the critique of distribution was never going to accept prices as neutral instruments. Nove testifies that honesty about the record drove the tradition's best minds toward the market's rehabilitation within socialism itself, which is the strongest corroboration the knowledge argument has ever received, stronger than any liberal applause, because it was purchased at the price of a life's allegiances. An assessment that reports only the arguments and not the witnesses understates both sides, and the bounded target applies here too: the respondents refuted what they engaged, the arithmetic and the neutrality of prices, and their testimony does not extend, any more than the Austrians' does, to questions they never took up.

## The witness from inside: shadow prices in the planned economy

One witness deserves separate treatment because he testifies from inside the machine. Leonid Kantorovich, a Soviet mathematician, was asked in 1938 to solve a practical problem for a plywood trust, the optimal cutting of materials and assignment of machines, and produced, in solving it, the method of linear programming and with it the concept of objectively determined valuations, shadow prices that fall out of an optimal plan's mathematics and measure the scarcity of each resource within it. Here, apparently, was the elder's missing yardstick, generated by the plan itself without markets or property. The Soviet system received its own mathematician's discovery with decades of suspicion, since valuations that measure scarcity look uncomfortably like prices, and prices were what the system existed to abolish; Kantorovich shared a Nobel award in 1975, by which time his methods were used at the margins of the economy he had hoped to rationalise whole.

The episode is the calculation debate in parable, and both camps may draw their morals with equal legitimacy, which is why the article reports it without awarding it. The Austrian moral: the plan, to allocate rationally, must reinvent prices, and the shadow price is the market's ghost confessing that scarcity cannot be exorcised, only priced or ignored. The respondent's moral: the yardstick the elder declared impossible in principle was produced in practice by a mathematician working for a plywood trust, and its suppression was a political and ideological failure, not a logical one, which is precisely the distinction between the calculation argument's letter and its spirit that this article has maintained throughout. The deeper moral belongs to neither camp: the knowledge that would have made the valuations useful, the true capacities and costs of every enterprise, was exactly what the reporting chain corrupted, so that Kantorovich's mathematics waited on data the system could not honestly supply. The elicitation problem outlived the arithmetic problem inside the very economy that posed both, and no verdict on the two Viennese thinkers is complete that does not record how faithfully their questions organised even their opponents' experience.

## Competition as a discovery procedure

Late in his career the younger man gave his epistemology its most radical formulation, in a lecture that recast competition itself as a discovery procedure. The point is easily missed because it sounds like a slogan for rivalry, and it is instead a claim about ignorance. In many of the matters competition decides, he argued, we do not know in advance what the relevant facts are: which goods are scarce, which methods are cheapest, which talents are real, which wants will persist. A procedure that presupposes the facts, as a plan must, cannot discover them; a procedure that sets rivals to work under rules, and lets the results speak, discovers facts that no participant and no observer possessed beforehand, including facts about the procedure's own domain. The market's outcomes are therefore not the execution of a known optimum but the findings of an experiment permanently in progress, and the case for the market, in this final form, is not that it computes well but that it learns.

The formulation retroactively organises the whole assessment, and the article adopts it as the fairest statement of what survives. The calculation argument claimed the plan cannot compute; the discovery formulation claims, more modestly and more deeply, that the plan cannot learn what only the process it replaces would have found out. The respondents' counters also find their sharpest form against it: that discovery procedures have domains, that a market discovers wants backed by money and is blind to the rest, that societies may rationally prefer a duller instrument that looks where they point it. The exchange, at this altitude, is no longer about socialism and capitalism as the century staged them. It is about how any complex society finds out what it knows, and on that question the two Viennese thinkers, the elder with his entrepreneur staking capital on a conjecture and the younger with his prices broadcasting discoveries nobody commissioned, made a contribution that their opponents' best work confirms by the effort it spends answering them.

## The philosopher's counter-statement

The social justice argument met, in the same decade that produced it, a counter-statement from within liberal philosophy itself, and the encounter defines the argument's true standing better than any Marxist reply. John Rawls had rebuilt the theory of justice as a doctrine about the basic structure of society: the institutions, property, family, and market among them, whose combined operation distributes life chances, and which can therefore be judged just or unjust as a structure, without any single distributor intending the pattern. The move answers the younger man's category argument at its root, by relocating the subject of justice from outcomes and from acts to the rules and institutions that generate both, which is, in different vocabulary, exactly the relocation the Marxist respondents performed when they pointed to designed rules behind unintended distributions. That a Kantian liberal and the Marxist respondents converged on the same relocation, from opposite directions and with opposite destinations in view, is evidence that the relocation answers a real pressure in the original argument: the pressure of the citizen who asks why he should accept a pattern merely because nobody signed it.

The younger man replied, in effect, that a basic structure judged by its outcomes will be perpetually adjusted toward a pattern, and that the adjusting authority is the comprehensive direction returning in judicial dress; Rawls's readers replied that a structure can be judged by principles its citizens could accept without being managed toward outcomes case by case. The article does not settle this quarrel, which belongs to political philosophy's permanent agenda, but it draws the assessment's moral: the social justice argument survives the encounter as a demand for precision, a standing challenge to say whether one is judging acts, rules, structures, or outcomes, and it falls as a dissolution of the subject, since the subject demonstrably survives the challenge in the work of the century's most careful liberal philosopher and in the practice of every legal system that reforms its rules because of the patterns they produce. A claim that began as a refutation ends as a distinction, which is the commonest fate of strong philosophical arguments and no disgrace to its author.

## An audit of concessions

The debate's progress can be measured by what each side has quietly stopped denying, and an audit of concessions is a useful instrument for a reader who wants the state of play without the theatre. The liberal side, in the persons of its serious representatives, has conceded that prices are not neutral between distributions; that markets fail about the future and about third parties in ways that are structural, not incidental; that provision of a floor beneath the market is compatible with, and may be required by, a free order; and that the calculation argument's absolute form cannot be maintained against the trial-and-error procedures and the computing machinery that answers its arithmetic. The socialist side, speaking through the representatives who engaged the arguments most seriously, has conceded that the knowledge problem is real and that no reporting system has solved it; that incentives and honest information are corrupted by the very chains a plan must use; that markets are indispensable instruments for most consumer goods and much else; and that the political mechanisms the famous book described, delegation, discretion, and the selection of the ruthless, operated in the systems built in its tradition's name and require institutional answers, not circumstantial excuses. These concessions, assembled, describe a debate that has converged on a set of shared problems, the elicitation of knowledge, the design of rules, the protection of dissent, the weighting of wants, while continuing to divide, irreconcilably, on the answers.

The audit also locates the remaining disagreement with a precision the polemics never achieve. What still divides the traditions is not information and not, at bottom, efficiency. It is the question the strongest objection raised and the answer bounded: whether a society is entitled to treat the distribution of purchasing power as a political question, to be settled by collective decision, or as a framework condition, to be accepted as the price of the only signalling system that works. The Austrians, at their deepest, held that the entitlement dissolves the signalling system that any answer would need; the respondents, at theirs, held that a signalling system deaf to unmoneyed need has already answered the political question in favour of the moneyed. Both cannot be wholly right, and the article's verdicts have distributed the honours mechanism by mechanism, which is the only honest way they can be distributed. What neither side may do, after a century of this, is pretend the other's problem does not exist, and the pretence, in seminar rooms and in legislatures alike, is the one position this article condemns without a defeater.

## The arguments in the classroom

Because this series is read by students, a word on how the material behaves under examination conditions will not be wasted, and it anticipates the persona sections that follow. The two thinkers make an ideal examination subject precisely because their case divides into gradable parts: a candidate can state the calculation argument, distinguish it from the knowledge argument, state the political argument and its textual boundary, and deploy the bounded target as an evaluation, and each operation is separately visible to a marker. The errors that cost marks are equally definite. Conflating the two men into one voice; attributing the welfare slope to the famous book; presenting the knowledge argument as a claim about laziness; citing either thinker against the labour theory of value; and answering the distributional objection as though it had never been made, are the five reliable ways to lose credit, and they map exactly onto the misreadings catalogued in the next section. A candidate who has internalised the article's distinctions possesses, in effect, a marking scheme for the topic, and the persona section for students converts the distinctions into a study procedure.

## The seminar as an institution

The elder's private seminar, gathered in his office at the Vienna Chamber of Commerce, deserves a section of its own, because it was the machine that manufactured the Austrian school as a living tradition rather than a bibliography. Meeting every second Friday evening through the nineteen twenties and into the thirties, it gathered economists, mathematicians, philosophers, and historians, among them men who would carry its influence into exile across three continents, and its proceedings were famously demanding: a paper, then criticism without deference, conducted by a chairman who regarded politeness as a tax on truth. The younger man was formed in that room and never ceased to acknowledge the formation, even as his conclusions migrated from the chairman's. The seminar's sociology explains features of the work that purely intellectual history leaves mysterious: the school's coherence under dispersion, since its members had been tempered in one another's company before the diaspora scattered them; its polemical edge, trained in a room where an argument survived or died in an evening; and its blind spots, since a circle that agrees on the questions can mistake its agreement for the questions' obviousness.

The diaspora is the seminar's second act and the century's familiar story. As Austria slid toward authoritarianism and then annexation, the members left, for Geneva, London, Paris, and the American universities, carrying the tradition in their heads because its institutions, the chamber office, the journals, the university posts, were being extinguished behind them. Ideas that travel in heads travel differently from ideas that travel in libraries: they arrive simplified by memory, hardened by the circumstances of their rescue, and attached to persons rather than to texts. The bounded target has one of its roots here. The Austrian case that reached the English-speaking world after the war was in part a carried doctrine, recited by exiles and their students, and the recitation, like the cartoon booklet, shed qualifications as it went. That the two principals survived to correct the recitation in person is the accident that makes this article's corrections possible, and the corrections' failure to catch up with the recitation is the measure of how much stronger a carried doctrine is than a printed one.

## What the elder meant by socialism

Precision about definitions is pedantry only to those whose arguments do not depend on them, and the elder's argument depends entirely on his. By socialism he meant the socialisation of the means of production: the transfer of ownership of land, factories, mines, and instruments to the community, organised as a single directing authority, such that exchange between independent owners in producer goods ceases. He was explicit that lesser measures, taxation, regulation, municipal enterprise, even extensive welfare provision, were not socialism in this sense but interventionism, a different system with different pathologies, and his taxonomy was not a courtesy but the load-bearing wall of the calculation argument, which bites only where producer goods have ceased to be exchanged. The taxonomy cuts against his own popularisers as sharply as against his opponents: a citation of the calculation argument against a mixed economy, in which producer goods are overwhelmingly exchanged between owners and prices for them exist in profusion, is a citation outside the argument's jurisdiction, and the elder, whatever his private hopes for the argument's wider application, built the jurisdiction into its foundations.

The definition also locates the debate's genuine frontier with a clarity both sides have found inconvenient. Where an economy socialises producer goods, the calculation and knowledge arguments apply in full force, and the respondents' work must be done. Where it does not, the arguments apply, if at all, in the attenuated forms appropriate to interventions, and the work to be done is the different work of the interventionism analysis, with its contestable escalation thesis. Much of the popular quarrel consists of artillery from the first frontier fired across the second, by defenders of planning who answer the calculation argument by pointing to functioning mixed economies it never described, and by critics of planning who deploy it against provisions it never governed. The article's insistence on the definition is therefore not antiquarian. It is the simplest application of the bounded target available, requiring no judgement about the arguments' merits at all, only the observation that an argument, like a statute, has a scope, and that citing it outside the scope is a category error before it is anything else.

## The younger on democracy and the limits of majority will

The political argument's author was a democrat of a distinctive and often misunderstood kind, and his doctrine of democracy belongs in an assessment because it explains what the political argument was for. Democracy, in his mature statement, is not a value in itself or a procedure for discovering a collective will; it is a method, the least bad known, for the peaceful removal of governments and for confining all governments, including elected ones, within general rules. The majority's authority, on this account, is real but derivative: it rests on the majority's own acceptance of principles that limit what any majority may do, and a majority that votes away the framework, that empowers an authority to direct production comprehensively, has not expressed the popular will more authentically but has destroyed the conditions under which popular will can continue to be expressed at all. Hence the political argument's true centre, which is not hostility to democracy but a claim about its preconditions: the dispersal of economic decision among millions of independent centres is, among other things, the material basis on which political pluralism rests, since a citizen whose livelihood, housing, and newspaper all issue from one authority does not possess, in any usable form, the independence that dissent requires.

The doctrine drew fire from democrats who heard in it a brief for limiting the people's power in the people's name, and the fire was not misplaced: the author did propose constitutional devices to confine legislatures, some of them eccentric, and his suspicion of unlimited democracy was genuine and deepened with age. It also drew fire from his own side, from liberals who found the defence of democracy too instrumental and conservatives who found it too warm. What an assessment should preserve is the doctrine's diagnostic core, which survives its author's prescriptions: that the forms of political freedom depend on material dispersals that a comprehensive plan abolishes as a side effect of its economics, so that the plan's political cost is not an accidental accompaniment but a structural one. That core is the political argument with its mechanism exposed, and it is, once more, an argument about comprehensive direction and about nothing else, a boundary its author observed in his theory even when his admirers dissolved it in his name.

## The mixed economies as evidence

The decades after the war built, across western Europe, the mixed economies that both camps have since mined for evidence, and an assessment should state what the record can and cannot show. Societies combined market allocation of most producer goods with extensive public provision, social insurance of exactly the kind the famous book endorsed, nationalised industries at the commanding heights in some countries, and floors of income beneath which citizens were not allowed to fall. They did not slide, in the decades that followed, into comprehensive direction or totalitarian rule; several of them dismantled their nationalised sectors by ordinary legislation and continued as democracies throughout. That record is a decisive refutation of the popular reading of the political argument, the hydraulic slope from provision to tyranny, and the article has treated it as such. It is not a refutation of the political argument itself, whose condition, comprehensive direction, the mixed economies never met, having kept the price system for producer goods substantially intact; a conditional argument is not refuted by cases where its condition is absent, however often the refutation is announced.

The same record disciplines the other side with equal impartiality. The planned economies of the east did exhibit, in documented abundance, the phenomena the knowledge argument predicts: chronic shortage amid gluts of the unwanted, falsified reporting, hoarding of materials and labour by enterprises insuring themselves against the plan's demands, and a grey economy of favours that functioned as the system's unofficial price mechanism. They also exhibited, for decades, the capacity to mobilise resources toward priorities the centre actually cared about, armaments and heavy industry and space flight among them, which the calculation argument's absolute form said could not be rationally done at all and which the comparative form handles by noting the price paid in everything the centre did not care about. Evidence, in this debate, is not a trophy to be seized but a set of constraints on the size of claim either side may honestly make, and the mixed and planned records together enforce the article's central discipline from opposite directions: the Austrians' claims survive at comparative size, and the respondents' claims survive at distributional size, and neither survives at the size its pamphleteers prefer.

## Money: the first spontaneous order

The doctrine of spontaneous order had a birthplace inside economics, in Menger's account of the origin of money, and returning to it shows the doctrine's original modesty. No legislator invented money, Menger argued, and no social contract established it. Traders holding goods that others did not want learned, each for himself, to accept in exchange a good he did not want either, provided it was more saleable than his own, more readily accepted by the next trader; and as this practice spread, the most saleable goods converged, by a process nobody directed, into media of exchange, until cattle, salt, shells, and finally the precious metals occupied a role that looks, in retrospect, like a designed institution and was in fact a crystallised habit. The younger man took the account as the paradigm of his social theory: an institution of the highest utility and complexity, arising from individual adjustments under no one's plan, embodying in its conventions the experience of uncounted transactions, and irreducible, without loss, to the intentions of any participant. Prices, law, language, and money form, in his system, a family of such crystallisations, and the planner's error is the same toward each: to mistake a grown order for a made one, and to suppose that what was never designed can be redesigned at will without destroying the knowledge the growth deposited.

The paradigm also displays, with unusual clarity, where the respondents' functional objection and the designed-rules objection meet the doctrine. Money's evolution was not a process in a state of nature: it proceeded within legal orders that defined property, punished fraud, and eventually monopolised coinage, debased it, and managed it, so that the purest case of spontaneous order in the whole literature proves, when inspected closely, to be a hybrid, grown within a framework of made rules and repeatedly rescued, corrupted, and re-founded by authority. The younger man's own monetary writings concede as much in practice, proposing constitutions for money because the grown order had proved corruptible by its custodians. The concession is the doctrine's maturity: grown and made are not two kinds of institution but two moments in every institution's life, and the interesting questions are about their proportions and their custody. Stated at that maturity, the doctrine of spontaneous order is a permanent contribution to social theory. Stated as a slogan, grown good, made bad, it is the kind of thing its author's own monetary work refutes, and the distance between the two statements is, once again, the distance this article exists to measure.

## The ethical strand: responsibility in an impersonal order

One strand of the younger man's case is neither epistemic nor institutional but moral, and it is often missed because it sits awkwardly with his reputation. A market order, he argued, has a moral property that its critics count as its chief defect: it is impersonal, and its results are nobody's doing, so that no one can be blamed for a pattern and no one can claim credit for one. He counted the impersonality a virtue on two grounds. It confines coercion, since a process without a director cannot be petitioned, bribed, or terrorised into changing a result, and the disappointed must improve their offer rather than capture the director. And it preserves the sphere in which moral responsibility is intelligible, the sphere of conduct under rules, by refusing to extend blame and praise to aggregates where they can only curdle into resentment against persons for outcomes those persons did not choose. The demand for social justice, on this ethical reading, is not merely a category error but a moral temptation: the temptation to hold someone, anyone, answerable for the pattern of the whole, which in practice means holding the successful answerable for existing and the state answerable for not abolishing them.

The respondents' ethical counter-case deserves the same unhurried statement. A doctrine that declares the pattern nobody's doing, they observe, performs a service for those the pattern favours, who are relieved of the question whether their advantages are deserved, and it performs it precisely where the question is most worth asking, since the rules that generate the pattern are, as established above, somebody's doing at the level of legislation. Impersonality, on this reading, is not a property of the order but an alibi constructed for it, and the moral emotions the younger man diagnosed as atavistic, the sense that a distribution in which some lack necessities while others hold superfluities stands in need of justification, are not survivals of tribal distribution but perceptions of a genuine fact: that the pattern is alterable by the rules, and that what is alterable and unaltered is, in a morally serious vocabulary, chosen. The article awards this exchange to neither party and commends it to the reader as the point where the debate stops being about mechanisms and becomes what, at its root, it always was: a disagreement about what human beings owe one another in a society rich enough that the owing is a choice.

## What each side should read in the other

An assessment may properly end its analytical work with an exercise in directed reading, since the two literatures are most valuable where they are most uncomfortable to their own partisans. The Marxist reader should take from the Austrians three things that his own tradition needs and has repeatedly had to relearn at great cost. The knowledge problem, as a permanent constraint on any project of conscious social direction, to be designed around rather than denounced. The entrepreneurial challenge, the demand to name the mechanism that discovers and disciplines error in a system without bankruptcy. And the political mechanism, the account of how direction selects its personnel and manufactures its unanimity, which the tradition's own internal critics, from the open letter writers of the European east to the feasible socialists, arrived at independently and at ruinous personal prices. A Marxism that has absorbed those three lessons is a different and stronger doctrine from the one that dismisses their authors unread, and the tradition's best minds, Dobb conceding the problem of calculation's terms, Nove rebuilding around the market's information, knew it.

The liberal reader should take from the respondents a matching triad. The distributional objection, as the permanent boundary of the price system's authority: the signal is weighted by wealth before it is sent, and a philosophy that forgets the weighting will mistake the map of effective demand for the map of human need. The designed-rules objection, as the correction of the spontaneous order doctrine's normative slide: rules are chosen, and their foreseeable patterns are chosen with them. And the reminder concerning what lies ahead and what falls on third parties, that the telecommunications system is silent about the unborn and the unpriced, whose interests a society must represent by deliberate institutions because no signal represents them. A liberalism schooled by those three lessons can hold the knowledge argument at its full strength without inflating it into a philosophy of indifference, which is, the article submits, exactly the position the younger man's own texts, read whole and against their afterlife, were written to defend. The two triads together describe the ground on which an honest continuation of the debate would stand, and the article leaves its readers there, with the misreadings and the pattern that explains them still to be catalogued.

## The formal possibility: equations before the quarrel

The calculation debate had a prehistory that both camps later found convenient to forget, because it shows the question changing its meaning under their hands. In 1908 the Italian economist Enrico Barone demonstrated, with the mathematics of general equilibrium, that a collectivist state could in principle determine the same allocation as a competitive market: the conditions for an optimum can be written as a system of equations, and the equations do not care whether the unknowns are solved by a ministry or by the higgling of traders. The demonstration was directed against the socialists of Barone's own day, to show them the magnitude of what they proposed, the ministry would have to replicate, coefficient by coefficient, the entire information structure of the market, and Barone himself doubted the replication could be performed in practice. But the formal point stood, and it framed everything after: the question was never whether a planned optimum exists on paper, but whether any institution can find it, keep finding it as conditions change, and induce men to act on the finding. The elder's essay, arriving twelve years later, can be read as the claim that the finding requires prices that only exchange generates; the trial-and-error proposals of the nineteen thirties, anticipated by the American economist Fred Taylor in a presidential address of 1929, were attempts to make the ministry behave as though it were the market's higgling, compressed into a rule.

Seeing the prehistory clarifies the verdicts this article has issued. Against Barone's formal possibility, the elder's impossibility claim was always vulnerable, since a system of equations does not become unsolvable because the solver wears a uniform; the respondents who rested on the formal point were answering a claim the elder had overstated, and their victory in the textbooks was the reward of his overstatement. Against the practical question Barone himself had raised, how the ministry is to know the coefficients and keep them current, the younger man's reformulation was the true successor argument, and it is no accident that the subsequent scholarly re-reading, which amended the filing in the Austrians' favour, did so by relocating the whole exchange onto the ground Barone had marked out and the elder had overshot. Intellectual debates are often decided by which participant first states the question at its proper size. In this one the honour is divided three ways, and the division is a better monument to all concerned than the victory either camp claimed.

## The abuse of reason: the younger's unfinished project

Across the middle decades of his life the younger man worked intermittently on a large historical project, which he called the abuse and decline of reason, of which the scientism essays and the famous book were fragments. Its thesis was genealogical: that a particular deformation of rationality, born in the French engineering schools and the philosophy of Saint-Simon and Comte, had taught educated Europe to regard society as an object for conscious construction, to distrust every institution that could not produce its designer's credentials, and to treat the recalcitrance of grown orders as a scandal to be engineered away. Socialism, in this genealogy, is not primarily an economics but an epistemology, the engineering temper applied to the whole of social life, and Marxism is its most consequential carrier because it fused the engineering temper with a movement of the exploited, giving the constructivist idea an army. The project was never finished, and its fragments show both the power and the danger of genealogy as a method. The power: it explains the family resemblances among doctrines that share no premises, the engineer's plan, the technocrat's ministry, the revolutionary's vanguard, by tracing them to a common deformation of reason. The danger: genealogy convicts by ancestry, and a doctrine's grandfather is not its refutation, a principle the author applied rigorously to others' genealogies of liberalism and loosely, his critics charged, to his own.

For this article the project's importance is that it locates the younger man's deepest disagreement with Marx, deeper than calculation and prior to knowledge: a disagreement about whether reason's proper posture toward social order is construction or cultivation. Marx, on the genealogy's telling, stands in the constructivist line despite his contempt for utopias, because his science of history promised its adepts a knowledge of the whole sufficient to justify remaking it. The telling is contestable, and Marxists have contested it, pointing to the texts in which emancipation is the act of a class rather than the application of a blueprint, and to the author's own savage reviews of the engineers and system-builders of his century. The contest matters because it decides whether the scientism critique, the younger man's most scholarly campaign, strikes Marx or only his cousins, the question this article raised in the scientism section and here leaves, as it must, in the state the evidence leaves it: the genealogy is a genuine illumination of a tradition Marx drank from, and an unproven indictment of the use he made of the draught.

## Direction under an agreed end: the wartime test

Every argument in this article can be tested against the century's largest experiment in temporary planning, the war economies of the democracies, and the test is worth conducting explicitly because both camps cite the wars and both cite them badly. Between 1939 and 1945 the British and American states directed production on a scale approaching the comprehensive: they allocated steel, rubber, shipping, and labour by administrative decision, fixed prices, rationed consumption, and conscripted the workforce's direction, and the machine worked, producing armaments in quantities that decided the war. The planning advocate cites the episode as proof that direction succeeds when it is tried seriously. The Austrian answer, which the younger man gave in substance during the war itself, distinguishes the conditions: the end was single, supreme, and shared, victory, so the ranking of ends that peacetime planning cannot obtain was supplied by the emergency; the price system was suppressed but its memory and its black markets persisted as correctives; the effort was avowedly temporary, with the framework of a market order waiting to be restored; and even under those ideal conditions the direction exhibited the predicted pathologies, hoarding, falsified returns, and allocation by influence, on a scale the official histories politely understate. The episode therefore tests nothing about comprehensive direction in a plural society at peace, because its defining feature, the agreed end, is the one thing such a society does not have.

The test cuts the other way as well, and the article records the cut. The war economies refute any version of the Austrian case strong enough to predict that administrative allocation simply cannot produce, since it produced, under pressure, at a tempo no market transition could have matched in the same years. What survives the war test is the comparative and conditional core this article has defended throughout: direction works by consuming a stock of agreement, prices, and institutional patience that it does not replenish, and its successes are real, dated, and purchased. A critic who needs more than that from the Austrians is asking the texts for what the texts, read carefully, never promised; a defender who settles for less has not read the respondents. The war, in short, is the debate's best laboratory precisely because it satisfies neither camp's pamphlet, and a reader who can state what it shows for each side, in one paragraph each, has understood the calculation and knowledge arguments better than most of the literature that cites them.

## The division of knowledge and the division of labour

The knowledge argument has an ancestor in the founding text of economics itself, and the descent is worth tracing because it shows the argument to be a development within the mainstream's own tradition rather than an eccentricity at its edge. Adam Smith founded the science on the division of labour, the observation that production advances by splitting work into specialised fragments, each worker's skill and attention narrowed to a point. The younger man's contribution was to see the corollary Smith left unstated: a society that divides labour divides knowledge with it, necessarily and irreversibly, so that the total knowledge embodied in the economy's operations exists nowhere except in fragments, each fragment adapted to its station, and the problem of economic order is transformed from the allocation of things into the coordination of knowers who cannot report what they know to any centre because their knowledge is their specialisation itself. Specialisation is not an accident that better communication could heal; it is the source of the wealth the coordination is meant to serve, so that a planner who could gather all knowledge into one place would first have to abolish the division of labour that generates it, impoverishing the society in the act of rendering it legible.

This is the deepest form of the knowledge argument, and it is the form least vulnerable to the respondents' counters, because it does not depend on the imperfections of reporting chains or the tacitness of skill, real as those are, but on the structure of a developed economy as such. It also marks the exact point where the argument stops being a weapon in a party quarrel and becomes a permanent acquisition of social thought, available to any tradition willing to use it. Marxist political economy, with its own account of the division of labour and its costs to the labourer, has the conceptual furniture to absorb the corollary and turn it: the fragmentation of knowledge is, on its telling, not a natural fact but a feature of a mode of production that splits conception from execution and lodges the plan of the work in the capitalist's office rather than the worker's head, so that the knowledge problem the Austrians treat as eternal is, in part, the signature of the system they defend. The rejoinder is serious and the article records it as the debate's proper frontier: whether the division of knowledge is a condition of all complex production or a deformity of one kind of it is a question that neither the seminar in Vienna nor its respondents settled, and it is the question a reader should carry out of this article into the rest of the cluster.

## The elder on money: the root of his politics

The elder's economics began with money, and his politics never left the subject. His first book, published in 1912, performed the feat his school most needed: it explained the value of money itself by the marginal principles the school applied to everything else, closing the gap between the theory of goods and the theory of the medium in which goods are priced, and it laid down the analysis of credit expansion, fiduciary media, and the trade cycle that his seminar inherited. The experience of the postwar inflations, which he watched from inside the Austrian administration as paper crowns dissolved the savings of the middle class that employed him, fixed his conviction that inflation is not a technical accident but a political instrument, the one tax no legislature has to vote for, and that a state armed with the printing press holds a power over every citizen's substance that no constitutional parchment restrains. His counsel to the Austrian government, that it stop the presses and accept the consequences, was followed late and partially, and the episode became his parable of the economist's duty: to say the arithmetically necessary thing in the season when saying it is most unwelcome.

Money matters to this article because the calculation argument is, at root, a monetary argument. The yardstick whose absence the essay of 1920 laments is money prices, and money, in the elder's theory, is not a veil over real exchanges or a token the state can define at pleasure, but the economy's common denominator precisely because it emerged from exchange and continues to be revalued in every transaction. A socialism that retained money for consumer goods while abolishing it for producer goods would, on his analysis, keep the denomination and lose the denominator, since the prices that do the economy's comparing are struck where the means of production change hands. The point also explains his lifelong ferocity about monetary constitutions, the gold standard first and any binding rule thereafter: a society that lets its rulers manufacture the unit of account has handed them the instrument by which every calculation in the society can be silently falsified, and falsified calculation, the reader will notice, is the original sin of his whole system, the thing the socialist commonwealth commits by design and the inflationary state commits by stealth.

## The constitution of liberty: the system in maturity

The younger man's treatise of 1960 is the book in which the wartime argument grew into a system, and it is the text a reader should consult when the famous book's slogans are quoted, because it contains the doctrine in its considered form, qualifications included. Its definition of freedom is exact: the absence of coercion by other men, the condition in which a person can act on his own knowledge and purposes within a protected sphere that general rules delimit. Its account of law refines the rule of law strand into a jurisprudence. And its third part, on the welfare state, is the section that the bounded target most needs the world to read, since there the author goes through the agenda of the postwar settlement item by item, accepting social insurance and a minimum income and public provision of certain services, rejecting the monopolisation of those services by the state and the use of provision to equalise positions, and conducting the whole review in the register of judgement about particular measures rather than prophecy about destinations. Whatever one thinks of the judgements, their form is the refutation of the caricature: a thinker who believed that provision as such starts a slide does not write three hundred pages distinguishing the provisions he accepts from those he refuses.

The treatise also displays the system's genuine difficulties without resolving them, and an assessment should note two. The definition of coercion, confined to the deliberate pressure of one will on another, struggles with the pressures that structures exert without any will directing them, the very pressures the Marxist respondents put at the centre of their objection, so that the treatise's protected sphere can look, to a reader formed by the respondents, like a legal description of a cage whose bars are nobody's coercion. And the criterion by which state action is sorted into framework and direction, fruitful as it is, blurs exactly where modern policy lives, in measures that are general in form and directional in intent. These difficulties are not gotchas; they are the points where the system meets its limits and where its author's own later work worried at the seams. They are recorded here because the bounded target is a two-edged instrument: it protects the texts from their popularisers, and it forbids their defenders to claim for the system a completeness its own author, worrying at those seams into his ninth decade, never claimed.

## Two tempers of liberalism

Step back from the arguments and the pairing itself becomes the lesson. The two men represent the two tempers in which a doctrine can be held. The elder's temper is systematic and embattled: truth is a structure, error is a breach in it, and the holder's duty is to keep the walls unbreached and the gate shut against compromise, because compromise with a false principle is the principle's victory by instalments. The younger's temper is evolutionary and concessive: truth about society is a deposit accumulated by processes nobody commands, the holder's own grasp of it is partial, and his duty is to tend the processes, correct his positions as the deposit shifts, and distrust above all the man, including himself, who believes he holds the whole. Each temper has its characteristic achievement and its characteristic failure. The systematic temper produced the calculation argument's clarity and the entrepreneurial challenge's edge, and it produced a movement that embalms. The evolutionary temper produced the knowledge argument's depth and the political argument's self-correcting author, and it produced a doctrine so qualified that its followers must simplify it to use it, which they promptly did.

The Marxist tradition, the reader will not need telling, houses the same two tempers under other names, the orthodoxy that keeps the walls and the revisionism that tends the processes, and the symmetry is more than a pleasantry. It suggests that the fate of these two men's texts, the disclaimed readings, the bounded targets, the filings and the embalming, is not a peculiarity of liberalism but the general fate of doctrine in transmission, which this series encounters in every cluster and which the next sections take up directly. To assess Mises and Hayek is therefore to practise, on two unusually well-documented cases, the skill the whole series exists to teach: the recovery of arguments from the movements that carry them, and the grading of citations by the texts they cite. The men would have approved the skill's application to their opponents. The article has applied it to them, and submits that they survive the application better than their reputations do, which is the most that honest assessment ever says for anyone.

## What this article has deliberately not done

The boundaries of the treatment should be stated before the closing sections, in the manner of this series. The calculation debate as a historical episode, its proposals, counter-proposals, and dramatis personae in full, belongs to the companion article on that debate and has been sketched here only where the assessment required it. The question whether a plan can outperform the market under the conditions of a later age belongs to the companion inquiry on that question and has been touched only at the point where computation claims required an answer about elicitation. The authoritarianism charge in its full range, beyond the younger man's version of it, belongs to the article on that charge. Incentives, the problem of motivation under socialism, belong to their own article and have been separated from the knowledge argument here precisely so that the separation can be honoured there. Within its own ground the article has attempted completeness: the three arguments stated from the texts, the replies at full strength, the strongest objection answered and conceded to, the verdicts issued with their defeaters, and the target bounded. What remains is the catalogue of misreadings, the pattern that explains the catalogue, and the guidance owed to the article's several kinds of reader.

## A glossary in prose: the terms that carry the case

Six terms carry the whole subject, and a reader who can define each in a sentence owns the vocabulary of the debate. Praxeology is the elder's name for the science of human action, the deductive study of choice that begins from the fact that men employ means for ends and derives the laws of economics as theorems rather than as generalisations from data. Calculation is the act the first argument concerns, the comparison of alternative employments of resources in a common unit, which money prices supply and which no physical accounting replaces. The knowledge problem is the second argument's core, the dispersal of economically relevant information among millions of holders in local and partly tacit forms that no centre can gather. Spontaneous order is the younger's name for a pattern that arises from human action without human design, language and money and the market order itself being his standing examples, and catallaxy is the word he coined for the market order specifically, the mutual adjustment of innumerable plans under general rules. Scientism is his name for the deformation of reason that imitates the natural sciences in the study of society and issues in the ambition to construct social order as an engineer constructs a bridge. A candidate who meets any of these terms in a question should define it in its author's sense before evaluating it, since half the errors in the literature are definitions quietly swapped between the statement of an argument and its refutation.

## Five misreadings and their corrections

The misreadings this article exists to correct can now be catalogued in prose, each with the correction the texts supply. The first misreading holds that the famous book of the war years opposes welfare provision and prophesies that pensions and clinics end in tyranny; the correction is the book's own chapter on security, its endorsement of insurance and of a floor against privation, and its author's four decades of restating the distinction his readers had dropped. The second misreading holds that the two thinkers made one argument; the correction is the seminar's own history, the elder's theorem about calculation and the younger's observation about knowledge, different in method, in evidence, and in fate, the first cut down by its respondents and the second largely sustained by them. The third misreading holds that one or both of them refuted Marx's economics; the correction is bibliographical, since neither wrote the refutation, their school having filed that task under a predecessor's name, and their fire was directed at socialism as a system of allocation rather than at the theory of value, of accumulation, or of crisis. The fourth misreading holds that the knowledge argument is about incentives, about the laziness or dishonesty of men under planning; the correction is that the argument is about the location and form of knowledge, and would hold in a society of saints, which is exactly why it is stronger than the motivational argument it is confused with. The fifth misreading holds that the social justice argument denies that distributions can be criticised at all; the correction is that it denies one specific thing, that an unintended pattern can be just or unjust as a pattern, while leaving open, and itself practising, the criticism of rules, of structures, and of the foreseeable consequences of choosing them, which is where its respondents met it and where the live argument remains.

Each misreading, notice, is useful to somebody, and the catalogue's lesson is that usefulness, not carelessness, is what keeps a misreading alive against its author's protests and its text's plain sense. The slope version of the famous book serves those who oppose provision and those who oppose the author with equal convenience. The fused version of the two thinkers serves everyone who needs a citation more than an argument. The refutation version serves a school that prefers its filings closed. An assessment cannot make misreadings useless, but it can raise their price, by putting the corrections in a form as portable as the errors, and that, in the end, is what the bounded target is for: a correction small enough to carry and exact enough to spend.

## The disclaimed reading: a pattern in this series

This article's subjects instantiate a pattern the series has met before, and naming the pattern is the last analytical task. A text becomes famous in a form its author did not write and would not sign: a qualification is stripped in transmission, the stripped version does the travelling, and the author's protests, prefaces, and restatements pursue the traveller without overtaking it. The pattern governed the reception of the pamphlet of 1902 whose author spent later prefaces explaining what its bent stick had meant, and of the manifesto of 1848 whose authors' own later prefaces revised its programme in particulars its quoters omit. It governs the famous book of 1944 with unusual completeness, because there the stripping was performed by the author's friends, in an abridgement prepared for a mass magazine, and because the qualification stripped, his acceptance of insurance and of an income floor, was the one on which the book's whole distinction between kinds of security depended. The series draws from the pattern a rule of reading that this article has applied throughout: when a famous text is cited for a famous proposition, read the chapter, not the citation, and treat every author's own later qualification as part of the text, not as a retraction to be discounted. Applied to Marx, the rule recovers the prefaces and the qualifications that his popularisers drop. Applied to his critics, it recovers the floors and the endorsements that theirs drop. The rule is impartial because the pattern is universal, and a series about a doctrine and its critics could have no more fitting discipline.

## For students and exam candidates

Build your preparation around the distinctions, not the survey. Make a card for each of the three arguments with its author, its mechanism, its best reply, and its defeater, and drill the cards until you can assign any question to its argument within a minute, because most lost marks on this topic are assignment errors, answers about incentives where knowledge was asked, or about welfare where direction was asked. Learn the bounded target as your evaluation paragraph: it converts a summary answer into an assessed one in four sentences, and markers reward it because it shows the texts were read rather than the slogans memorised. Keep your revision notes, quotations, and timed practice scripts in one organised place, and use the study tools at [the Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) to hold the three arguments and their replies in a single view you can rehearse from. When a question touches examinations you are preparing for in the Indian civil services pattern or in university papers that reuse previous years' questions, work through the archive at [the UPSC previous year question explorer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) to see how often this material is asked as a comparison and how the command words shift what a good answer must do.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The misreading correction is your most reliable instrument in the classroom, because it surprises both halves of a divided room in the same hour: set the chapter on security beside the caricature of the book that contains it, and let students discover, from the page, that the century's most famous warning against the welfare state endorses social insurance and a floor of provision. From that discovery the whole assessment unfolds naturally, since a class that has watched a citation collapse will attend to mechanisms, to the difference between the two thinkers, and to the distributional objection, with an appetite that no lecture on method creates. Structure the seminar as the debate ran: the elder's essay, a trial-and-error reply, the younger's knowledge essay, then Dobb and Nove, and require each student to state the defeater of the position they defend, a requirement that converts advocates into assessors for the length of an afternoon and teaches the series' central skill better than any summary of it.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Your professional hazard on this topic is the carried doctrine, the version of the two men that arrives through movements, abridgements, and other people's citations, and your defence is the archive: the seminar's proceedings and correspondence for the school's internal history, the successive prefaces and interviews for the famous book's disclaimed reading, and the respondents' papers for the debate's other side, which the victors' filing long obscured. Treat every quotation attributed to either thinker as unverified until you have seen its page and its surrounding qualification, and treat every claim that one of them refuted Marx as a filing to be reopened rather than a finding to be repeated. The most promising unworked ground lies in the transmission itself, in how the abridgements were made and by whom, in which qualifications were dropped at which relay, and in the parallel transmissions on the Marxist side, a history of the debate's afterlife that would explain more about the century's polemics than another rehearsal of the arguments. The arguments themselves, bounded and graded, are in this article; the afterlife is in your archives, and it is still largely unwritten.

## The market socialists and the concession that shaped a generation

Between the elder's challenge and the younger's reformulation stands a generation of socialist economists who took the challenge seriously enough to rebuild their doctrine around it, and their work is the debate's most honourable chapter. H. D. Dickinson, Evan Durbin, and their colleagues in Britain, like Lange in Poland and America, accepted that a socialist economy needed prices, not as a concession to capitalism but as an instrument any rational allocation requires, and they designed institutions, a supreme economic council announcing prices, managers instructed to follow rules mimicking competition, consumer goods markets left free, to supply what the elder had declared unsuppliable. The designs were ingenious and their authors were candid about their difficulties, the honesty of managers, the centre's information, the capital market's absence, in a way that polemics on both sides rarely matched. The younger man's response, delivered across the nineteen thirties and gathered in a volume he edited on collectivist planning, was to grant the designs their arithmetic and press the two points this article has made central: that announced prices without rivalrous bidding are instructions wearing the costume of information, and that the knowledge the prices would need to summarise is not sitting in returns awaiting collection.

The episode deserves its place in an assessment because it falsifies the lazy version of both camps' histories. The socialists of the nineteen thirties were not dogmatists refusing to hear the argument; the ablest of them heard it, rebuilt, and in rebuilding conceded its premise, that allocation needs something functionally equivalent to prices, which the elder could fairly claim as the debate's first decision. The Austrians were not obscurantists refusing to hear the reply; the younger man read the designs closely, praised their ingenuity, and relocated his objection to ground the designs could not reach, which is how a research programme behaves when it is alive. What died in the exchange was the innocence of both original positions: the socialism that had expected to abolish prices learned to counterfeit them, and the liberalism that had expected arithmetic to settle the matter learned that its real argument was about knowledge and power. The counterfeit, like most counterfeits, testifies to the value of the original, and the argument about whether the counterfeit can pass is, in modern dress, the argument the computation section conducted above.

## The intellectuals and the climate of opinion

The younger man's most sociological essay, published in 1949, asked why the intellectual class, the professional dealers in ideas at second hand, journalists, teachers, broadcasters, civil servants of the pen, leaned toward socialism in the democracies, and his answer was characteristic: not conspiracy and not corruption, but a selection mechanism. Ideas reach the public filtered through people whose trade is the transmission of ideas, and that trade rewards the general, the novel, and the morally dramatic, while penalising the conditional, the technical, and the defence of arrangements nobody designed. A doctrine that promises conscious mastery of society flatters the transmitting class twice over, once by exalting ideas as the motors of history and once by promising its members employment as the motors' engineers. The essay is the political argument's companion piece, an account of how a society talks itself toward the single ranking of ends, and it remains uncomfortably applicable to climates of opinion far from its author's target, including climates that invoke his own name, since the mechanism he described, the filtration of doctrine through professional transmitters who simplify as they carry, is the very mechanism that produced the disclaimed reading of his famous book.

Marxist readers will recognise the essay's form, because it is a sociology of knowledge of the kind their own tradition practised on its opponents for a century: an explanation of a belief by the position of the believers, offered in place of a refutation of the belief. The younger man seems, to his credit, to have known the resemblance and accepted its discipline, presenting the essay as an account of transmission rather than of truth, and its lesson cuts his own side as readily as its target, since a liberalism that explains socialism by the intellectuals' incentives has licensed its opponents to explain liberalism by the incentives of those who fund its institutes. The article records the essay for two uses. It completes the portrait of a thinker whose subject was always, underneath the economics, the movement of ideas through societies. And it supplies the missing mechanism in the disclaimed reading pattern: texts are not merely misread, they are misread systematically, by a class whose professional situation selects the versions that travel, and any author, of any doctrine, who does not reckon with that class is writing for a world that does not exist.

## Theory and history: the elder's epistemology of the past

Late in his career the elder wrote a book on the philosophy of history that his followers cite and his critics ignore, and it belongs in this assessment because it completes the account of his method and explains, from inside his system, why his engagement with Marx took the shape it did. History, he argued, is not a laboratory in which economic laws are tested, because historical episodes are unique conjunctions of innumerable causes, and no controlled repetition can isolate the contribution of any one of them. The historian's proper instrument is understanding, the reconstruction of the meanings and valuations that moved the actors, an instrument the elder, borrowing a term, called thymology. Economic theory stands apart from this enterprise, prior to it and immune from its findings, supplying the categories with which the historian's narrative is organised but never itself at risk from the narrative. A Marxist will hear in this architecture an inverted image of his own tradition's claims for historical science, and the inversion is instructive: where Marxism made history the tribunal before which doctrines are judged, the elder made history a theatre in which doctrines, already judged by reason, are merely illustrated.

The architecture has a consequence for the debate that this article has repeatedly encountered without naming. If history cannot test theory, then the record of the planned economies, their shortages and their mobilisations, cannot count against the elder's theorem in his own court, and his followers' habit of citing that record triumphantly is, by the master's own epistemology, an illegitimate move, an appeal to a tribunal he abolished. The younger man, whose method never abolished the tribunal, could and did argue from the record, and the difference between the two men's relations to evidence, visible in miniature in their treatments of the same history, is the methodological difference with which this article began, now seen from its far side. An assessment that has followed the difference through method, argument, and afterlife may fairly close the analytical work here: the elder built a system that evidence cannot touch and paid for the immunity with relevance whenever the questions turned empirical; the younger built an argument that evidence can touch and has been touched by it, dented at the distributional boundary, sustained at the epistemic core, and the dents and the sustainment together are what a century of scrutiny actually found.

## Prices and the poor: the counter-reply examined

The strongest objection was answered above by bounding the knowledge argument, and fairness requires that the Austrians' counter-reply to the objection also be heard at its strength, since it is the reply their serious representatives actually give. It runs as follows. The weighting of signals by purchasing power is a defect of a distribution, not of a signalling system, and the remedy for a defective distribution is to redistribute the purchasing power, by transfers, by the income floor the younger man himself endorsed, by public provision of the services whose absence from the market's hearing matters most, after which the price system will register the wants of the poor in proportion as policy has armed those wants with money. To abolish the signalling system because the signals are weighted is to destroy the instrument by which any distribution, including a juster one, can be provisioned, since the planner who equalises incomes on Monday still faces, on Tuesday, the question of what to produce for the equalised citizens, and faces it without the only instrument that elicits their wants at scale. The objection, on this reply, proves that distribution must be corrected politically, a conclusion the younger man's system accommodates, and it does not prove that allocation can dispense with prices, a conclusion no respondent has established.

The exchange at this depth has the structure of a genuine philosophical standoff, and the article reports it as one. The respondents answer the counter-reply by questioning whether the correction can remain external to the system: purchasing power redistributed is purchasing power that re-accumulates unequally through the very transactions the system celebrates, so the weighting reasserts itself and the correction must be perpetual, a standing political interference with outcomes that the Austrian framework elsewhere condemns. The Austrians answer that perpetual correction under general rules is exactly what their framework licenses, a floor maintained as a fence is maintained, without directing what grows inside it. Each answer is respectable; each side's next move is predictable; and the predictability is the sign that the debate has reached the bedrock where political philosophy begins, the question of what a society owes its members and what it may not do in the paying. The article's task was to bring the reader to that bedrock with the arguments intact and the misreadings cleared, and at the bedrock it stops, as an assessment should, leaving the choice where the two traditions have always left it, with the citizen.

## The Russian experiment as evidence

Both men wrote in the shadow of an experiment neither could ignore, and their uses of it repay a final examination, because the experiment is where the debate's abstractions met their evidence. The elder treated the Russian case from the beginning as an illustration rather than a test: the abolition of money under war communism and its hurried restoration, the chaos of in-kind allocation, confirmed for him what deduction had already established, and the later decades of Soviet planning, which allocated on a vast scale for two generations, he absorbed into the theorem by insisting that the allocation was irrational in his technical sense however massive in extent, a defence that preserved the system at the price the epistemology section recorded. The younger man treated the case as evidence of a more ordinary kind: the falsified returns, the hoarding, the grey markets, the eventual stagnation of an economy that had mobilised brilliantly toward a few priorities and could not learn its way toward the innumerable small adjustments a consumer economy requires, all entered his later work as corroboration of the knowledge argument's mechanisms, offered with the caveat, which his method required and his rhetoric sometimes dropped, that corroboration is not proof.

The respondents used the same record in mirror image, and the article's even-handedness requires the mirror to be shown. The experiment demonstrated, they argued, that a society could industrialise without a capitalist class, could defeat the century's most powerful invasion, could put the first satellite over the earth, and that these were not the achievements of an arithmetic impossibility; the costs, which the honest respondents did not hide, they charged to backwardness, encirclement, and the political deformation the authoritarianism article examines, rather than to the absence of prices. The record, in short, is rich enough to furnish every camp's museum, which is why this article has rested its verdicts on mechanisms rather than on exhibits: mechanisms can be inspected in any economy, including the reader's own, while exhibits can be selected. A reader who finishes the article able to watch a reporting chain corrupt its information, a price broadcast a scarcity, an administrator inherit an empty mandate, and a floor of provision coexist with a free economy, in whatever country the reader inhabits, has acquired what the two thinkers at their best were selling, which is not a team to join but a set of processes to see.

## The sensory order: the mind as the first dispersed system

The younger man's least read book may be the foundation of his most read argument. Published in 1952 after two decades of intermittent labour, his study of theoretical psychology proposed that the mind is itself a spontaneous order: a network of classifications built by experience, in which no central inspector surveys the whole, and in which much of what the mind knows, it knows as dispositions to respond rather than as items it can list. The parallel with the social theory is exact and was intended. A mind that cannot fully report its own operations is the original of the economy that cannot report its knowledge to a bureau, and the author drew the moral explicitly in his later work: the limits of central direction in society are of a piece with the limits of introspection in the person, since in both cases the knowledge that guides successful action is embodied in a structure whose workings outrun any participant's survey of them. Readers who find the knowledge argument persuasive only as economics may test it here, in the one laboratory every reader carries, by attempting to state, completely and in words, how they recognise a face, balance on a bicycle, or hear that a bearing is about to fail.

The psychology also armours the argument against a rejoinder that economists sometimes accept too quickly, that tacit knowledge is merely knowledge not yet written down, awaiting better instruments of capture. On the theory of the sensory order, some knowledge is tacit in principle, because the classifying network that holds it operates by connections whose content is the pattern of their firing, not a store of propositions that a sufficiently patient interrogation could transcribe. A skill is not a manual its owner has mislaid. This is the deepest stratum of the case against gathering, and it explains why the argument has survived every improvement in the instruments of gathering since it was made: faster returns, finer statistics, and larger machines all address the transcription of what can be stated, and the stratum that cannot be stated is untouched by them. Whether the stratum is as large as the argument needs is a fair question, and the honest answer is that its size is an empirical matter on which the record of reporting systems, in firms and in planned economies alike, has so far testified in the argument's favour.

## The chapter on the enemy: a claim examined and discounted

One chapter of the famous book requires separate handling because it is the place where its author's argument most plainly overreaches, and an assessment that has defended the book against its caricaturists owes its readers the same candour about the book itself. The chapter argues that National Socialism grew from socialist roots: that the movement which destroyed German democracy inherited its collectivism, its contempt for liberal individualism, and many of its personnel and techniques from the socialist movements it fought. There is a documented stratum of truth in the genealogy, in the wartime planning apparatus both movements admired, in the anti-liberal vocabulary they shared, and in individual careers that crossed from one camp to the other. But the chapter's rhetoric invites a stronger reading, that the two movements were species of one genus and their war a family quarrel, and that reading flattens what most needs explaining: the racial doctrine that stood at the second movement's core and had no counterpart in the first, the annihilationist ends that distinguished its rule, and the fact that its first prisoners, in the camps it built within weeks of taking power, were socialists and trade unionists. A genealogy that cannot register the difference between a movement and its first victims has mistaken a shared vocabulary for a shared nature.

The article records the chapter as the political argument's clearest internal caution. Its author, writing in wartime against an enemy his adopted country was fighting, reached for the strongest available connection between that enemy and the doctrine he opposed at home, and the reach exceeded the evidence in the manner of wartime. The mechanisms the rest of the book identifies, delegation, discretion, selection, operate on the German case as on others, and they explain features of the regime's operation without explaining its essence, which lay in doctrines the book's framework, built to analyse planning, was not designed to hold. Readers should therefore take the chapter as its author's weakest performance and the book's standing warning against its own method: an argument about mechanisms becomes a libel when it is stretched over a case whose centre is elsewhere. The bounded target, once more, is the corrective, and it applies to the defence of these thinkers exactly as it applies to their attack. Nothing in the chapter's failure touches the knowledge argument, the calculation argument, or the bounded political argument, which is precisely why the failure is worth isolating: a case is strengthened, not weakened, by surrendering the ground it cannot hold.

## The diaspora in the universities

Where a doctrine lands shapes what it becomes, and the Austrian diaspora landed unevenly in ways that explain the two men's divergent afterlives. In Britain the younger man's two decades at the London School of Economics placed him inside the institution where the calculation debate's English round was fought, among colleagues who included his respondents, and the proximity kept his work in conversation with the profession even as the profession moved away from him. In America the elder's seminar at New York University, sustained by private money and attended by the devoted, produced a school in the strong sense, with a canon, a method, and a boundary, and its graduates carried the system into the movement's institutes and eventually into university posts of their own. A third landing, at the University of Chicago, gave the younger man's later work an institutional home among social theorists rather than economists, which is one reason his influence seeped into law and political science while the elder's remained concentrated in economics and in the movement press. The pattern generalises a lesson the series meets repeatedly: doctrines do not diffuse through a culture like gases but travel along institutional plumbing, and the shape of the plumbing, a state university here, a foundation's cheque there, a journal's editorship elsewhere, is recoverable in the shape of the doctrine that arrives.

The plumbing also explains the simplifications. A doctrine carried by a movement's institutes must be teachable in a weekend and printable on a pamphlet, and the qualifications that complicate it are selected against at every relay, not by malice but by the physics of transmission, exactly as the younger man's own essay on the intellectuals would have predicted had he applied it to his friends. A doctrine carried by universities is selected for different virtues, for publishable refinements and teachable distinctions, and it sheds, instead, its urgency and its political edge. The two men thus survive in two different elements, the elder in the movement's, simplified and hardened, the younger in the academy's, qualified and dispersed, and the reader who meets either survival should perform the same operation this article has performed throughout: ask which element the version arrived through, and discount accordingly, before returning to the texts where both men, at their incompatible best, are still waiting to be read whole.

## The century's end as evidence

The collapse of the European planned economies at the century's end was received, in the first flush of commentary, as the debate's final verdict, and an assessment written with the record complete should state carefully what that event did and did not settle. It settled, as a matter of historical fact, that the systems built on comprehensive direction could not reform themselves into competitiveness with the market economies they faced, and that their own elites, in the end, largely ceased to believe the claims made for the model: the elicitation failures, the falsified information, the innovation famine outside the priority sectors, are documented in the archives those systems left behind, in their own officials' handwriting. It did not settle the distributional objection, which concerns what market signals hear, and which the successor societies' own experience of inequality, arriving with the markets that replaced the plans, kept alive in a new register. It did not settle the calculation argument's letter, since the systems had allocated for two generations before they failed, nor the political argument's popular form, since the lesson of the collapse was about comprehensive direction and not about the welfare provisions that the victorious societies conspicuously retained.

What the event most resembled, in the debate's long perspective, was a very large corroboration of the mechanisms this article has graded, delivered at a scale no seminar could have commissioned. Corroboration is the right word and its modesty is deliberate. A mechanism corroborated is not a philosophy vindicated, and the decades since the collapse have been as unkind to the pamphlet versions of the Austrian case, which predicted that provision and direction were one road, as they were to the pamphlet versions of the respondents' case, which had predicted that the planned economies would outgrow their rivals. The texts survive the event better than the pamphlets do, which is the fate this article has documented at every stage, and a reader who wants the debate's actual yield should take it in the form the yield actually takes: a set of mechanisms, each with its domain and its defeater, a boundary around two famous names, and a standing instruction, valid for every doctrine in this series, to grade citations by pages rather than by reputations.

## Monopoly and the concentration the framework must answer

No assessment of the two men is complete that spares them the question their framework finds hardest after distribution: the concentration of private economic power. If the case against planning rests on the dangers of concentrating decisions in one authority, what does the framework say when exchange itself concentrates decisions, when a handful of firms command the prices, the employment, and the information of whole industries, and when the spontaneous order's own processes, mergers, network effects, the economies of scale, deliver the many into the hands of the few? The younger man's considered answer was more discriminating than his pamphlets suggest. Monopoly, he held, is frequently the creature of the state, of patents stretched beyond their purpose, of licences, tariffs, and regulations that incumbents learn to farm, and the remedy lies in removing the privileges and in general rules against coercive practices rather than in discretionary direction of the industries concerned. He conceded, in the treatise on liberty, that some concentrations arise without privilege and that the case for public action there is real though difficult, since the acting authority must proceed by rules and not by the discretionary management his whole system exists to prevent.

The respondents' rejoinder has force and should be recorded at its strength. A framework that traces every concentration to a privilege will misread concentrations that arise from the technology of an industry itself, from the mathematics of networks, or from the simple compounding of advantage, and a doctrine whose remedy is confined to general rules may find itself armed with a hose against a fire that rules, by their generality, cannot reach in time. There is also an asymmetry the article has met before, in the functional objection and in the designed-rules objection, and it recurs here in its most practical form: the framework scrutinises the state's concentrations with a microscope and private concentrations with a telescope, though the citizen whose livelihood depends on a single employer's decisions experiences the concentration identically whichever legal form it wears. The honest summary is the one this article has given at each such point. The two men identified a real pathology of concentrated decision and a real discipline, generality of rules, for containing it; their framework applies the discipline asymmetrically, trusting processes it elsewhere teaches its readers to interrogate; and the interrogation, conducted with their own instruments, is the respondents' most productive use of them, since the knowledge argument itself, turned on a concentrated industry, asks whose local knowledge the concentration suppresses and whose signals it falsifies, questions the industry's defenders answer no better than the planning boards did.

## The Polish coda: the respondent who lived his answer

The calculation debate has a coda that belongs in any full assessment, because it was written by a life rather than by a pamphlet. Oskar Lange, whose trial-and-error design had been the respondents' most elegant construction, returned after the war to the Poland whose government he served, and spent his remaining two decades inside the attempt to operate a planned economy with the instruments he had designed in exile. The record of those decades, in his official writings and in the recollections of those who worked with him, is not a recantation, and the article does not manufacture one. It is something more valuable to the historian: a progressive, documented disenchantment with the bureaucracy that any operating plan engenders, expressed in his late work on the political economy of socialism, where the apparatus he had imagined as a neutral computing service appears as an interest with its own momentum, defending its targets, its information, and its personnel against the centre that nominally commanded it. The man who had answered the elder on paper spent his maturity discovering, from the ministerial side of the desk, the phenomena the younger man's side of the debate had predicted about reporting chains and administrative selection.

Set beside it the elder's coda, which was a refusal of codas: no disenchantment, no revision, the system defended entire into his tenth decade, the treatise's propositions reaffirmed against a century that had declined to arrange itself as they required. And set beside both the younger man's, which was a migration rather than a settlement, the economist of information becoming the philosopher of law, conceding a floor here, a framework there, worrying at his own seams in public. The three codas are the three tempers of the whole debate in biographical form: the respondent who let experience argue with his design, the systematist who let nothing argue with his, and the evolutionist who made a method of letting things argue. An assessment is not required to choose among tempers, but a reader choosing reading time might notice which temper produced, in the end, the work that opponents still have to answer, and which produced the work that only followers still read. The debate's honours, distributed across a century, fall where the mechanisms were stated most inspectably, and the mechanisms are in the texts this article has spent its length recovering, bounded, graded, and left, with their defeaters attached, in the reader's hands.

## The revival of the nineteen seventies

Ideas have their business cycles, and the Austrian case, dormant in the profession for a generation, revived in the nineteen seventies under the pressure of events its authors had not foreseen in their particulars but whose shape their framework claimed to expect. The postwar settlement's economics entered a crisis it could not narrate: inflation and unemployment rising together, contrary to the trade-off the reigning doctrine had taught governments to exploit; nationalised industries exhibiting, in the democracies' own house, the softness of budget constraint and the politicisation of prices that the calculation debate's literature had catalogued; and a general suspicion, voiced well beyond the right, that the administrative state had accumulated commitments its information and its incentives could not discharge. Into that climate the younger man's Nobel award of 1974 arrived like a reprieve from a court of appeal, and the elder's movement, organised in institutes and journals through the lean decades, found its pamphlets suddenly describing the weather. The revival was real, and an assessment should record both what it vindicated and what it distorted, since revivals are transmissions like any other and obey the physics this article has described.

What it vindicated was the questions. The profession's own research programme shifted, in those years and after, toward information, incentives, expectations, and the institutional rules of the game, toward the very terrain the two men had occupied when the terrain was unfashionable, and the younger man lived to see graduate students assigned problems he had posed in essays their grandfathers' generation had filed as eccentric. What it distorted was the answers, in the familiar direction: a revival needs banners, and the banners carried the conclusions without the conditions, the famous book's slope without its chapter on security, the elder's certainty without his definitions, so that the decade which restored the two men's questions also multiplied the citations the bounded target exists to correct. The respondents, meanwhile, underwent their own nineteen seventies, in which the feasible socialists absorbed the knowledge argument into their designs and the distributional objection migrated from the Marxist journals into the mainstream of welfare economics, so that by the century's end the debate's living content had largely detached from the banners of both camps and taken refuge in the literatures where mechanisms are graded one at a time. That detachment is the revival's final lesson and this article's warrant: the arguments were always better than the movements carrying them, and the reader who has followed them here, in their bounded and graded form, possesses what the revival's participants, for all their energy, so often lacked, the texts themselves.

## A note on the texts and their transmission

Scholarly conscience requires a closing note on the physical texts themselves, since an article about the gap between works and their reputations should not exempt the works' material history from scrutiny. The elder wrote in German for the first half of his career and in English for the second, and the translations of the early work, made by other hands under the pressures of exile publishing, occasionally blunt the edges of arguments whose force depends on terminological precision: the book of 1922 bears in German a title naming the socialised economy itself, where the English title names the doctrine, a small displacement that nudges the reader toward ideology and away from the institutional analysis the chapters perform. The treatise of 1949 was itself an expansion and reworking of a German manuscript composed in Geneva, so that the system's canonical statement exists in two linguistic strata whose differences specialists still mine. The younger man wrote in English from his London years onward, which spared him the translation problem and exposed him to the opposite one: a prose so courteously qualified, so fond of the subordinate clause that carries the condition, that abridgers found his conditions conveniently detachable, and the history of his famous book's magazine condensation is, among other things, a history of subordinate clauses deleted by editors who needed the main clauses to march faster.

The note generalises into the article's final methodological bequest. Every text in this long quarrel reached its readers through material channels, translation, abridgement, anthology, syllabus, movement pamphlet, and each channel has its own selection pressure, its own way of deciding which parts of an argument are portable. The scholar's defence is bibliographical before it is philosophical: know which edition, whose translation, and what was cut, before grading what a thinker held. The citizen's defence is the same habit at lower magnification, the refusal to meet a famous name in a controversy without asking for its page. Both defences are applications of the single rule this article has enforced from its first section to its last, and with the rule restated the assessment is complete: the two men made three arguments and several doctrines, the arguments have the standings the table records and the verdicts defend, the doctrines have the boundaries the bounded target draws, and everything beyond those standings and those boundaries that is said in their names is said, however confidently, without their authority.

## The two men on each other

It remains to record what the principals thought of one another, since their mutual judgements, delivered across fifty years, anticipate this article's verdicts with an accuracy that suggests the assessment has been fair. The elder's judgement of the younger was double from the beginning: pride in the seminar's most brilliant product, whose monetary work he praised and whose appointment in London he counted a victory for their school, joined to a reservation that deepened as the younger's foundations migrated, a suspicion that the knowledge formulation conceded to the opposition what the calculation theorem had denied it, and that courtesy toward respondents was the first shape of surrender. He never withdrew the reservation and never let it become a breach; the younger remained, in his references, the student who had carried the flag furthest into the enemy's country, with the elder reserving the right to think the flag had been carried at a slight discount. The younger's judgement of the elder was equally double and more publicly expressed: gratitude without limit for the formation, for the seminar's rigour and the master's example of intellectual courage under unpopularity, joined to a clear-eyed inventory of the master's limits, the apriorism he could not follow, the intransigence he believed had cost the doctrine its hearing, and the political judgements, delivered with a certainty the younger's whole epistemology forbade, from which he quietly distanced the school's better name.

Posterity has ratified the double judgements in both directions, which is the strongest external check this article's grading can offer. The profession kept the younger's questions and the elder's challenge, discarded the younger's wartime overreach and the elder's immunity to evidence, and filed the two men, not as a pair, but as successive approximations to a problem neither solved: how a society of strangers coordinates what none of them can survey, and what that impossibility licenses, and forbids, a government to do. The respondents' tradition, at its best, filed them the same way, keeping the distributional boundary the younger's argument cannot cross and the elicitation problem the elder's cannot be answered without. Two men, three arguments, one bounded target, and a century of readers who needed them simpler than they were: the article closes where the seminar began, with the texts on the table and the instruction, which the elder enforced on his students and the younger on himself, to read what is written and grade nothing else.

A last word on proportion is owed to the reader who has travelled this far. Forty thousand words on two critics of Marxism, in a series devoted to Marxism, is not an imbalance but a recognition: a doctrine is known by the quality of the opposition it provokes, and this opposition, at its best, was very good indeed, good enough to force the doctrine's ablest defenders to rebuild their economics around its objections and to concede, in the rebuilding, that the objections had located real joints in the world. The series will return, in the articles that follow, to the critics who engaged Marx's texts directly and to the replies that the tradition assembled against the whole battery of them. What this article has established, and what those articles will presuppose, is the standing of the operational critique and the boundary of its authors' names. The knowledge argument stands, bounded by distribution. The political argument stands, bounded by its author's own exemptions. The calculation argument stands as a challenge about entrepreneurship after falling as a theorem about arithmetic. And the names of Mises and Hayek stand, in any honest citation, for exactly that much and not one proposition more.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Austrian critique of socialism?

It is the case, built by two Viennese economists and their school, that a society which socialises the means of production deprives itself of the instruments by which an economy steers. In its elder form it holds that without exchanges between owners there are no prices for producer goods, and without such prices no common measure exists for comparing what rival projects cost. In its younger form it holds that the information allocation needs is scattered among millions of people in local and partly inarticulate shapes, and cannot be gathered to a centre, while prices broadcast what each decision-maker needs. A third, political strand adds that directing everything requires one agreed scale of social purposes, which free societies do not possess and administrators must therefore impose.

### Q: Did Hayek argue that welfare states lead to totalitarianism?

He did not, and his most famous book is the evidence. Writing in the middle of a world war for readers planning a peace settlement, he separated two ideas that share the word security: protection against utter destitution, which he said a rich society could and should organise for everyone, and the guarantee of each person's relative standing, which he opposed because preserving every position means freezing the economy that produces positions. Social insurance against sickness, accident, unemployment, and old age appears in his pages as a proper field for state action. His warning was aimed at the wholesale direction of production by a single authority, an arrangement he believed must manufacture agreement on ends and punish disagreement. Later restatements, across several decades, repeat the distinction and complain that readers had collapsed it.

### Q: What is praxeology?

Praxeology is the name Ludwig von Mises gave to his science of human action, and the name announces a method as much as a subject. Its starting point is the proposition that people act, choosing means for ends they prefer, and its procedure is deduction: unfold what is contained in that starting point with the same rigour a geometer applies to axioms, and treat the results as laws that no accumulation of statistics can overturn. Prices, money, interest, and the impossibility of calculation without markets all appear in the system as conclusions derived rather than hypotheses tested. Critics objected that a doctrine immune to evidence has secured certainty by emptying itself of content. Defenders answer that the core of economics really is of that character, and that the method merely refuses to pretend otherwise.

### Q: What is spontaneous order?

The phrase names a kind of order that arises from many people following general rules and adjusting to one another, without anyone having planned the resulting pattern. Language is the standard illustration: grammar is intricate, serviceable, and constantly adapting, yet no academy invented it and no legislature decreed its vocabulary. Money, in the account this school inherited from Carl Menger, grew by a comparable route, as traders converged on whichever good was easiest to pass on. The younger of the two thinkers generalised such cases into a social theory and coined a separate word, catallaxy, for the market order among them. The doctrine carries a warning for reformers: an institution may embody the experience of generations in a form its users cannot articulate, so demolishing it for lacking a designer can destroy knowledge nobody knows they are destroying.

### Q: Why does Hayek reject the idea of social justice?

His objection is grammatical before it is political. Justice and injustice, he argues, describe conduct: an act by someone who could have acted otherwise. The overall distribution of income in a market society is not an act. Nobody distributed it; it accumulated out of innumerable separate dealings, each perhaps fair or unfair on its own, whose combined shape nobody selected or foresaw in detail. Calling that shape unjust therefore borrows a word from the only setting where it has a clear sense, deliberate allocation, and spends it where no allocator exists. He grants that where an authority does hand out shares, as a planning office would, the phrase regains its meaning and the shares can be judged. His critics answer that the rules producing the pattern were themselves chosen, and that chosen rules make their foreseeable patterns somebody's responsibility after all.

### Q: Did Mises and Hayek engage Marx's economics directly?

Scarcely at all, which is the honest observation at the centre of this article. Neither produced a sustained examination of how Marx theorised value, accumulation, and crisis in his own texts. The senior man's quarry was socialism as an institutional scheme, common ownership with central direction, and he pursued it through the programmes and parties of his own century. The junior man's quarry was planning, constructivist rationalism, and later the idea of social justice, with Marx appearing mainly as the most influential carrier of a way of thinking whose sources he traced to French system-builders. Their own school had engaged the value theory a generation earlier, through Böhm-Bawerk, and both were content to leave that engagement where it stood. What they built instead was an operational critique: not whether Marx analysed capitalism correctly, but whether the alternative could run.

### Q: What are the Marxist replies to the knowledge argument?

Four replies do most of the work, and a fifth outweighs them. Markets, respondents note, are blind about what lies ahead, since a price records expectations and crowds can be fashionably wrong together, as every speculative collapse shows. Prices are silent about effects on bystanders, the smoke and congestion and depleted commons that no bargain registers. The order prices reveal is said to be unintended only at the surface, because the property law, inheritance rules, and corporate forms generating it were legislated by identifiable hands. The doctrine of undesigned order is charged with explaining institutions by their benefits, a style of explanation its author reproved in others. Above all, prices register willingness to pay, which wealth weights in advance, so the information transmitted is complete about the demands of the moneyed and empty about needs that arrive without money behind them.

### Q: Which of their arguments is strongest?

The knowledge argument, by a margin this article has argued throughout. Its claim is modest beside the others: not that central direction is impossible by logic, but that one mechanism moves a particular kind of information better than another, because the information lives dispersed among the people doing the work and much of it cannot be put into words for a form. Modesty is its armour. The calculation argument insisted that no substitute for market prices could exist, and partial substitutes were duly produced, from accounting prices adjusted by trial and error to the shadow prices a Soviet mathematician derived inside the planning system itself. The political argument identifies real mechanisms but was aimed at wholesale direction, and collapses when aimed at insurance or pensions. The knowledge claim survives because every attempt to gather what it says cannot be gathered has illustrated the difficulty instead of removing it.

### Q: Do prices transmit neutral information?

No, and the concession belongs inside the argument rather than against it. A price faithfully reports the balance of effective demand and supply at a moment, but effective demand is desire backed by money, and money is unequally held, so the report reaches its hearers already weighted: a wealthy household's preference registers at full strength, a poor household's faintly, and the need of someone with nothing does not register at all. Prices are also silent about consequences for third parties and about everything beyond the horizon of present expectations. None of this makes the signals false. It makes them partial, excellent about the scarcity of things that can be owned and traded, mute about much that a society might care to know. The dispute between the traditions is largely a dispute about how much of what matters falls inside the audible range.

### Q: Is the political argument well supported by the text?

Supported in its bounded form, contradicted in its popular one, and the text is the judge of both. The famous book does argue that wholesale direction of production requires a single scale of social ends, that representative assemblies cannot manufacture such a scale, and that power consequently migrates toward administrators and toward the least scrupulous among them. Those mechanisms are in the chapters, argued at length. What is also in the chapters, and in the author's treatises and interviews across the following decades, is an endorsement of social insurance, of organised relief against severe want, and of a minimum income beneath the market, together with repeated complaints that readers had mistaken his target. A citation that keeps the mechanisms and drops the endorsements is not an interpretation of the book but an abridgement of it, and the abridgement, not the book, is what entered political folklore.

### Q: How does the bounded target grade citations of these thinkers against Marx?

It asks what the cited text actually contains and confines the citation to that. Their pages support conclusions about calculation without prices, about knowledge that cannot be centralised, and about the politics of wholesale direction, and a citation drawing on those conclusions is sound whatever one thinks of them. Their pages contain no worked refutation of the theory of value, of exploitation, or of crisis, so a citation presenting them as having demolished Marx's economics borrows authority that was never earned in print. The same rule grades citations aimed at domestic policy: the famous book's author endorsed insurance and an income floor, so invoking him against those provisions reverses his position. Grading by the text rather than by the name is the whole of the method, and it protects Marx's texts from their own popularisers by the identical standard.

### Q: What should a student separate between an argument against comprehensive planning and an argument against welfare provision?

The object. Comprehensive planning means a single authority directing production as a whole, settling what shall be made, in what quantities, by whom, and it is against that object that the calculation, knowledge, and political arguments were built, since each depends on the totality: no prices anywhere for producer goods, all knowledge needing one centre, one ranking of ends imposed on everyone. Welfare provision means specified transfers and services inside an economy that still allocates most resources through exchange, and none of the three arguments engages it, because prices still exist, knowledge still moves through them, and no unitary ranking is required to run a pension scheme. A student who keeps the objects separate can state each argument at full strength without embarrassment. A student who merges them will attribute to the texts a slope argument their more careful author refused to make, and will lose marks to any examiner who has read the chapter on security.

### Q: Why is the political argument of 1944 milder than its reputation?

Because the reputation was manufactured by abridgement. The book that reached millions in magazine condensations and cartoon form had shed, in transit, the passages where its author approved compulsory insurance, state help against life's common hazards, and a guaranteed minimum of food, shelter, and clothing for all. What remained was the warning about wholesale direction, detached from its object and reattached, by readers with uses for it, to every act of public provision. The author then spent decades, in prefaces, in a systematic treatise that reviews welfare measures one by one, and in interviews, restoring the distinction and protesting the misuse, which is why the milder reading is not a charitable gloss but the documented position. Reputation measures what a culture needed a book to say. The text, with its author's corrections counted as part of it, says less, and means it more precisely.

### Q: How does the three arguments assessed table order the claims?

It arranges them so that a reader can find, for each argument, the same five pieces of information in the same places: what the argument asserts, which of the two thinkers put it forward, what sort of showing would dispose of it, the most formidable answer the Marxist tradition has given, and a judgement on whether it survives. The arrangement matters because the three are habitually quoted as one blended case, and blending hides the fact that they differ in kind. One is about a unit of account, one about the geography of information, one about the politics of agreement, and they have fared differently under attack. A table cannot argue, and this one is offered as an index to the sections that do, but an index that keeps unlike things visibly unlike prevents the commonest error in the whole literature, which is grading a strong argument and a weak one at the average of the two.

### Q: What separates what a text argues from what later readers cite it for?

Transmission. A text argues what its pages contain, qualifications included, in the order and proportion its author gave them. What readers cite it for is selected by the uses waiting for it: movements need banners, lecturers need villains and heroes, abridgers need copy that fits a magazine's pages, and each need trims the text toward itself. The famous book was trimmed by its admirers into a prophecy against provision its author had endorsed; the pamphlets and manifestos on the other side of this series were trimmed by theirs with the same shears. The separation is therefore not repaired by goodwill but by a habit: returning to the page, counting the author's own corrections as part of the work, and refusing any citation whose proposition cannot be found where the citation points. That habit, applied impartially, is the closest thing this long quarrel has produced to common ground.
