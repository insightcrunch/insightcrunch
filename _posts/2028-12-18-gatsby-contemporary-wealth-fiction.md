Every few years a new novel or series arrives about people with more money than anyone can account for, and every few years a reviewer reaches for the same comparison. Gatsby and contemporary wealth fiction have become a permanent pairing in the critical vocabulary, and the pairing is usually made lazily, as shorthand for parties and excess. Made carefully, it turns into one of the most useful things a reader can do with Fitzgerald's book, because it reveals which parts of his anatomy of the rich were tied to 1922 and which parts are still load-bearing a century later.

![Gatsby and contemporary wealth fiction compared - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The claim this article defends is narrow and testable. Recent fiction about the ultra-rich reworks a specific set of structures Fitzgerald assembled: an observer positioned between the wealthy and the world, a self-made claimant whose money cannot buy standing, an inherited elite whose carelessness is protected by capital, and a spectacle whose function is to convert money into legitimacy. Those four structures recur so persistently across books, films, and series that the recurrence cannot be coincidence, and understanding why they recur explains what the novel actually diagnosed.

## What Is Being Compared: Gatsby and Contemporary Wealth Fiction

The category needs defining before it can be compared. Contemporary wealth fiction, as used here, means work made roughly since the financial crisis of 2008 that takes the very rich as its subject rather than its setting. The distinction matters. A thriller in which a billionaire funds the villain is not wealth fiction. A story in which the arrangement of wealth is the thing being examined is.

The category is unusually crowded. In prose, Hernan Diaz's Trust, published in 2022 and awarded the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 2023, builds four competing accounts of a 1920s New York financier and his wife, using the collision between them to ask who gets to narrate a fortune. Emma Cline's The Guest, published in 2023, follows a young woman drifting through the last weeks of a Long Island summer after being expelled from the house that sheltered her. Kevin Kwan's Crazy Rich Asians, published in 2013, opened a long run of comedy about dynastic Asian money. John Lanchester's Capital, published in 2012, takes a single London street and reads its residents through the value of their houses. Kiley Reid's Such a Fun Age, published in 2019, examines the transactions of goodwill between a wealthy employer and the young Black woman who works for her.

On screen, the same territory is dense. Succession, created by Jesse Armstrong and running from 2018 to 2023, follows a media dynasty's succession fight. The White Lotus, created by Mike White and beginning in 2021, stations wealthy guests in luxury resorts and watches the staff. Bong Joon-ho's Parasite, released in 2019, stages the relationship between two Seoul households as an architecture problem. Ruben Ostlund's Triangle of Sadness, released in 2022, puts the rich on a yacht and then removes the yacht. Emerald Fennell's Saltburn, released in 2023, sends an outsider into an English country house for a summer.

And the novel itself has been reopened as raw material. When The Great Gatsby entered the United States public domain on the first day of 2021, retellings became legally straightforward, and Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, published in 2021, retold the summer from Jordan Baker's position with a supernatural apparatus attached. That book is a direct descendant. Most of the others are not, and the difference between the two relationships is the central methodological question this comparison has to handle.

### What counts as contemporary wealth fiction?

Work made roughly since 2008 that treats the arrangement of extreme wealth as its subject rather than its backdrop. The rich are examined rather than used, the money is the mechanism the plot runs on, and the story asks how fortunes are made, defended, narrated, and paid for by people outside the household.

## The First Structure: The Observer Who Is Neither Rich Nor Poor

Nick Carraway occupies a position Fitzgerald engineered with unusual care. He is a Yale graduate from a comfortable Midwestern family, second cousin to Daisy, old friend of Tom's, and simultaneously a man renting a small house between two mansions and commuting to a bond job he does not understand. He has the credentials to be admitted and not the capital to belong. That gap is what makes him a usable narrator: he is inside the room and outside the arrangement.

Contemporary wealth fiction reproduces this position constantly, and it reproduces it because the position is structurally necessary. A story told from inside the fortune cannot see the fortune, since to the people who have always had it, money is weather. A story told from complete outside cannot get access to the rooms where the interesting behavior happens. The narrative therefore needs a hinge, and the hinge is always someone with entry and without security.

The variations are instructive. Emma Cline's protagonist in The Guest is the most precarious version: a young woman with no money, no plan, and a temporary claim on hospitality, moving from house to house on the strength of being tolerated. Bong Joon-ho splits the position across an entire family who enter the rich household as employees, so the hinge becomes a labor relationship rather than a social one. The White Lotus institutionalizes it by making the resort staff permanent observers of guests who cycle through. Succession inverts it by placing the observers inside the family, so that adult children who will inherit everything are nonetheless outside the actual decision.

What changes across the century is the observer's leverage. Nick can leave, and does, returning to the Middle West with his judgment intact and his life essentially unaltered. The contemporary observer usually cannot. Cline's protagonist has nowhere to go. Bong's family has a basement apartment that floods. The hinge figure has moved from someone choosing to withdraw to someone who cannot afford to, and that shift is the single most reliable measure of what has changed in the economics being described.

The persistence of the position, though, is the finding. A century of writers have concluded independently that the only workable vantage on extreme wealth is a person standing at its edge. Fitzgerald did not invent the witness-narrator, and [the tradition he drew on](/2028/08/14/nick-witness-narrator-tradition/) is worth tracing separately, but he did demonstrate that the device could carry a whole account of a class, and that demonstration is what later work keeps reusing.

## The Second Structure: The Claimant Whose Money Buys Nothing

Gatsby's problem is not poverty. He has more liquid wealth than Tom Buchanan. His problem is that the wealth does not convert, and the novel is precise about the exchange rate. He can buy a house larger than the Buchanans', fill it with people, import a hydroplane, and none of it produces the one thing he needs, which is to be treated as someone Daisy could have married in 1917.

That conversion failure is the second structure, and it recurs everywhere. Kwan's Singaporean old-money families in Crazy Rich Asians spend an entire book distinguishing themselves from mainland newcomers with vastly larger fortunes, and the comedy runs entirely on the fact that the newcomers cannot buy the distinction. Succession's central family is new money by dynastic standards, and the show's cruelest running gag is the older establishment's amusement at their manners. Fennell's outsider in Saltburn attempts the conversion by other means entirely, which is the plot.

Fitzgerald states the mechanism in the library scene. Owl Eyes, drunk and astonished, discovers that the books are real, and then notes that the pages have not been cut. Everything is authentic and nothing has been used. That image is the exact condition of purchased legitimacy: the objects are correct, the relationship to them is not, and no amount of further purchase closes the gap. A century of wealth fiction keeps rediscovering this, usually through a scene in which an outsider gets a detail almost right and the room notices.

Tom's demolition of Gatsby at the Plaza works the same way. He produces no counter-argument about Daisy, no defense of his own conduct, no claim about love. He identifies Gatsby as a bootlegger and lets the identification stand as the whole case, because in his world the origin of money settles the question of standing. Contemporary versions replace bootlegging with hedge funds, crypto, private equity, or reality television, and the move is identical: the establishment does not argue, it names the source.

### Why does Gatsby's money fail to buy him standing?

Because standing is conferred by origin rather than by amount. Tom never argues with Gatsby about Daisy; he names him a bootlegger, and the naming decides everything. Purchased legitimacy stays visible, in the library's uncut pages and in every detail an outsider gets almost right.

## The Third Structure: The Protected Carelessness of the Inherited Elite

The third structure is the one that has aged least. Nick's verdict is exact: "They were careless people, Tom and Daisy," and they smashed things and then retreated into their money and let other people clean up. The passage identifies a mechanism rather than a character flaw. Carelessness is affordable to them, and its affordability is what makes it habitual.

The plot demonstrates the mechanism rather than asserting it. Daisy drives the car that kills Myrtle. Tom directs George Wilson toward Gatsby. Neither is charged, questioned, or inconvenienced. They close up a house and go abroad, and Nick's attempts to reach them for the funeral produce nothing. Three people are dead and the two people responsible have changed their address.

Every serious piece of contemporary wealth fiction runs some version of this test, and the test is usually the climax. Succession's dynasty absorbs a fatal incident at a company event and turns it into a negotiating position across several seasons. The White Lotus repeatedly ends its seasons with a death that the wealthy guests carry home as an anecdote while the local staff absorb the consequence. Triangle of Sadness strands its passengers and then demonstrates that the hierarchy reconstitutes itself under new conditions within days. Parasite ends in a bloodbath that the surviving rich household treats as a real estate inconvenience.

The consistency here is remarkable. Writers working in different countries, forms, and decades keep arriving at the same narrative proof: introduce a catastrophe, then measure who pays for it. The measurement is the argument. Fitzgerald did not discover that the rich are protected, which anyone could observe, but he found a compact narrative instrument for demonstrating it, and the instrument still works. Readers who want the full case for how the novel treats this emptiness can follow it into the study of [the hollowness of the upper class](/2024/08/12/gatsby-hollow-upper-class/), where Tom's ideology and Daisy's charm are examined as two expressions of the same absence.

The one place contemporary work has extended Fitzgerald is in showing the machinery. He gives the reader the outcome and lets the reader infer the process. Succession shows the lawyers, the public relations firm, the settlement, and the internal memo. Lanchester's Capital shows the property valuations. Diaz's Trust shows the competing narratives a fortune generates about itself. The century's addition is procedural: the same protection, documented.

## The Fourth Structure: Spectacle as a Legitimacy Machine

Gatsby's parties are not entertainment. They are an application. He throws them to be seen from across the bay, and Fitzgerald makes the instrumental purpose unmistakable by having the parties stop the instant they have served it, when "as obscurely as it had begun, his career as Trimalchio was over."

Spectacle as a conversion device is the fourth recurring structure, and contemporary wealth fiction is saturated with it because the contemporary rich are saturated with it. The luxury resort, the destination wedding, the superyacht, the private island birthday, the gallery opening, the charity gala: each functions in current fiction the way the West Egg party functions in Fitzgerald, as an event whose real product is standing. White's resorts, Ostlund's yacht, Kwan's weddings, and Fennell's country-house summer are all machines for converting expenditure into position, and all of them are shown malfunctioning.

The malfunction is the shared plot. In Fitzgerald, the guests arrive by the hundreds and one attends the funeral. In current work, the equivalent beat is the moment the spectacle's staff become visible, or the moment the guests are removed from the setting that sustains their status. Ostlund's structure is the clearest: the yacht sinks, the cleaning supervisor turns out to be the only person who can catch fish, and the hierarchy reverses within a week. That is Fitzgerald's argument about what the parties actually bought, staged as a survival scenario.

There is a further parallel in the anonymity. Fitzgerald notes that people were not invited and simply went, arriving with a simplicity of heart that was its own ticket of admission. The contemporary equivalent is the guest list nobody controls, the influencer at the gala, the friend of a friend on the boat. In both cases the crowd is a symptom: a society where attendance requires no relation is a society where relation has stopped being the currency.

## The Fifth Structure: Who Gets to Narrate a Fortune

A fifth structure has become more prominent in recent work than it was in 1925, and tracing it back into Fitzgerald is one of the most productive moves available in this comparison.

Gatsby's fortune generates stories about itself before it generates any facts. At the parties, guests decide among themselves that their host killed a man, or that he was a German spy during the war, and Fitzgerald reports the speculation with obvious relish because the speculation is the point. The rumors are not obstacles to knowledge. They are what the guests want instead of knowledge, since a mysterious host is a better party than a documented one. When Gatsby eventually supplies his own version, complete with Oxford, a war medal, and a hunting expedition through the capitals of Europe, Nick's reaction is that the whole account sounds like a magazine serial, and the reaction is correct: the story is manufactured to the specifications of what an audience would find plausible.

Hernan Diaz's Trust takes that dynamic and makes it the architecture of a book. Four documents about the same financial marriage compete, contradict, and revise each other, and the reader's task is to work out which account is a promotional exercise and which is closer to what happened. What the novel argues is that a fortune of a certain size stops having a single history and starts having a portfolio of them, each maintained by someone with an interest. Fitzgerald had already noticed this. He simply distributed the competing versions across dinner conversation rather than across chapters, and gave the reader Nick's judgment as an arbitrating device.

The difference in method is worth an essay by itself. Fitzgerald resolves the problem by installing a narrator who checks: Nick goes to lunch with Wolfsheim, sees the war medal, hears Jordan's account of 1917, and assembles something closer to the truth than the party crowd possesses. Diaz refuses to resolve it, leaving the reader to arbitrate. The shift from a book that adjudicates to a book that declines to is the clearest measure of what a century of financial scandal has done to the reading public's appetite for a trustworthy witness.

There is a further wrinkle in the contemporary version. Where Gatsby's rumors circulate among a few hundred guests, the modern fortune generates its narratives industrially: press offices, authorized biographies, philanthropic branding, and a permanent layer of commentary. This is why so much recent wealth fiction takes documentary form, assembling itself from memos, transcripts, and competing testimonies. The form follows the subject. A fortune that produces documents demands a novel made of documents, and Fitzgerald's single retrospective narrator, sitting alone in the Middle West writing it all down, now reads as an artifact of a quieter information economy.

## The Extraction Zone Moves Indoors

Fitzgerald put the wasteland outside. The Valley of Ashes sits on the road between the mansions and the city, and every character must drive through it, but nobody lives in it who has any other option. It is a province: separate, functional, and visited only in transit.

The single largest architectural change in contemporary wealth fiction is that this zone has moved inside the house. Bong Joon-ho's Parasite is the most explicit case, since the film is organized vertically around it: a hillside home above, a semi-basement apartment below, and a hidden sub-basement below that, with the plot consisting of movement between levels. The extraction zone is not down the road. It is under the floor, and the people in it can hear the family above.

The White Lotus performs the same relocation horizontally. Staff corridors, back-of-house kitchens, and employee accommodation sit a few meters from the guest suites, and the series' method is to cut between them until the proximity becomes unbearable. Kiley Reid's Such a Fun Age puts the relationship inside a single household and a single evening's childcare, so that the extraction is not industrial at all but interpersonal, conducted through affection and misread goodwill.

This relocation has consequences the comparison can measure. In Fitzgerald, the collision between the zone and the capital requires a physical journey: George Wilson has to leave the ash heaps, walk across Long Island, and arrive at a swimming pool. The distance is part of the tragedy, since it takes him most of a day and he arrives with the wrong information. In the contemporary versions, the collision is continuous, because the two populations already share a building. Nothing has to travel. What used to be a plot event is now a daily condition.

The shift also changes what the extraction zone means. Fitzgerald's valley is a place where things end: ashes, waste, a failed advertisement, a garage that sells nothing anyone wants. Its residents have no leverage. Contemporary service workers, positioned inside the house, have information, access, and occasionally the capacity to act on both, which is the engine of most recent plots in this territory. The powerlessness that made Wilson pitiable has been partly replaced by a proximity that makes current staff characters dangerous. That is a real change in the balance being described, and an essay that names it has found something the parallel-hunters miss.

## The Party Scene as an Inherited Set Piece

If one passage from the novel has been reworked more than any other, it is the third chapter's account of a summer party, and the reworking follows an identifiable formula that is worth stating because it makes the influence question concrete.

Fitzgerald's version has five components. It begins with sound arriving from a distance, music heard by someone who is not attending. It moves to logistics, the crates and the servants and the vehicles, which converts pleasure into expenditure. It presents the crowd as a mass rather than as individuals, dissolving names into chatter and introductions forgotten on the spot. It includes an interlude in which an outsider inspects something and discovers a fraud or a near-fraud, which is the library scene. And it closes on a mechanical failure, the car in the ditch with a wheel off and a driver who cannot understand what happened.

That five-part shape has become genre furniture. Almost every extended party sequence in recent wealth fiction assembles some subset of it: the approach from outside, the itemized logistics, the anonymous crowd, the inspection that finds the seam, and the malfunction that ends the evening. Kwan's weddings, White's welcome dinners, Fennell's country-house revels, and Ostlund's captain's table all draw on it, whether or not their makers have the novel in mind.

The reason the shape persists is that it does argumentative work. Each component converts celebration into evidence. Arriving from outside establishes the observer position. Logistics establish cost. Anonymity establishes that the crowd has no relation to the host. Inspection establishes that the display has a seam. And malfunction establishes that the whole apparatus is more fragile than it looks. A writer who wants a party to mean something rather than merely happen will tend to assemble these components regardless of influence, which is the convergence argument in its most demonstrable form.

Fitzgerald's own version has one component the later ones rarely reproduce: genuine beauty. The passage about guests coming and going like moths among the whisperings and the champagne and the stars is not ironic. It is gorgeous, and the gorgeousness is doing work, because a party that were merely tawdry could not exert the pull the novel needs it to exert. Contemporary versions are frequently ugly by design, shot in unflattering light with unattractive behavior foregrounded, and the loss of beauty removes the ambivalence that made Fitzgerald's account so much harder to dismiss.

## Women, Money, and the Constrained Choice

Fitzgerald's treatment of what money does to women's options is the element of his anatomy that contemporary work has revised most aggressively, and the revision is instructive rather than simply corrective.

Daisy's most quoted line is a wish for her daughter: she hopes the girl will be a fool, since "that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool." The line is presented as cynicism and functions as analysis. Daisy has calculated the returns available to a woman of her position and concluded that awareness is a liability, and nothing in the book contradicts her. Her marriage is a financial arrangement she entered under pressure, her affair is bounded by what her class will absorb, and her final move is to retreat into the protection her husband's money guarantees. Gatsby's own description of her voice, that it is full of money, followed by Nick's expansion into the image of a king's daughter high in a white palace, makes the fusion of the woman and the capital total.

The limitation in Fitzgerald's version is that he examines the constraint from outside. Daisy's interiority is withheld almost completely; the reader gets her charm, her performances, and two or three flashes of calculation, and must infer everything else. Jordan Baker is granted more independence and correspondingly less attention. Myrtle Wilson, the one woman in the book attempting an upward move without capital, is given an apartment, a dog, a party, and a fatal collision, and the brutality of that arc is the book's most direct statement about what the same ambition costs a woman without protection.

Contemporary work has taken all three positions and given them the inside view. Nghi Vo's retelling puts Jordan at the center and supplies the interiority Fitzgerald withheld. Reid's novel examines the transactional goodwill between a wealthy woman and the young woman she employs, which is the Myrtle relationship rewritten with the employee as the consciousness. Series in this territory routinely give their women the calculations that Fitzgerald leaves the reader to reconstruct.

What the comparison yields is not that Fitzgerald was inadequate but that his constraint analysis was accurate enough to survive the shift in vantage. When later writers move inside the position, they find the calculation Daisy's line implies. The beautiful little fool remark holds up as a description of the returns available under those conditions, which is why it remains the most cited sentence in the book after the last one. The century's addition is testimony, not correction.

## The Decisive Differences

An honest comparison has to name where the two bodies of work diverge, and the divergences are as revealing as the continuities.

The first and largest is tone. Fitzgerald writes elegy. His prose gives Gatsby "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person," and the whole final page is an act of mourning. Almost all recent wealth fiction writes satire. Succession is a comedy. Triangle of Sadness is a farce. The White Lotus is a black comedy with a body. Parasite is closer to tragedy than most, and even Parasite works through absurdist reversal. The contemporary mode is deadpan, ironic, and unsentimental, and it withholds from its rich characters precisely the lyric sympathy Fitzgerald grants his.

That difference is not decorative. It reflects a change in what audiences will accept. Fitzgerald could ask a reader to grieve for a bootlegger who wanted a married woman, because the dream he attached to that want was still credible as a national proposition. Current work rarely asks the audience to grieve for anyone in the household, and when it does, the grief is heavily qualified. The elegiac register has moved out of wealth fiction and into fiction about the people wealth uses.

The second difference is scope. Fitzgerald's is a national argument about an American promise, anchored on a specific continent and a specific founding image. Contemporary wealth fiction is largely transnational. Kwan's fortunes span Singapore, Hong Kong, and mainland China. Bong's is Korean. Ostlund's is European. The White Lotus relocates each season. Money in current fiction has no homeland, and the loss of a national frame removes the specific tragedy Fitzgerald was writing, which depended on a country having promised something.

The third difference is the treatment of the interior. Fitzgerald gives Gatsby a self, an origin, a schedule written in the back of a boy's book, and a Platonic conception from which he sprang. The reader knows what he wants and why. Contemporary wealth fiction is often deliberately uninterested in interiority, presenting the rich as surfaces because surface is the argument. Succession's characters are famously difficult to attribute motives to beyond position. Cline's protagonist withholds her own history. The refusal is a technique rather than an omission, and it produces a different kind of book: an anatomy without a heart, where Fitzgerald's has one at the center.

The fourth difference is what the fortune is for. Gatsby wants a person. The contemporary fortune usually wants itself, or wants to survive succession, or wants to be defended from a lawsuit. Removing the romantic object from the equation removes the mechanism by which Fitzgerald converted an economic story into a love story, and it explains why so little current work in this territory is moving in the way the novel is moving.

### How is recent wealth fiction different in tone from Gatsby?

Fitzgerald writes elegy and grants his outsider genuine lyric sympathy. Recent work writes satire and withholds sympathy from everyone inside the household. The mourning has migrated from the rich to the people wealth employs, which is a change in what audiences will accept rather than in the underlying diagnosis.

## The Counter-Reading: Did Gatsby Directly Inspire Any of This?

The weakest version of this comparison claims lineage it cannot support. It is worth stating plainly that most contemporary wealth fiction was not written under Fitzgerald's influence in any traceable sense, and an essay that asserts otherwise is making a claim it cannot evidence.

The exceptions are real but limited. Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful is an explicit retelling, made possible by the novel's entry into the United States public domain in 2021, and its relationship to the source is direct. Hernan Diaz's Trust is set in the same period and the same financial world, and reviewers have consistently placed it in conversation with Fitzgerald, though the book's actual structural ancestors are the documentary novel and the unreliable memoir. Emma Cline's The Guest is set on eastern Long Island in a summer of borrowed hospitality, and the setting alone puts it in the neighborhood.

Beyond those, the honest description is convergence rather than descent. Writers examining extreme wealth arrive at the outsider-observer because the subject demands a vantage point. They arrive at the carelessness test because a catastrophe is the fastest way to measure protection. They arrive at spectacle because spectacle is what the contemporary rich actually do. These are solutions to problems the subject poses, and Fitzgerald found them first because he was working on the problem early and well.

That distinction matters for the argument's strength. A claim of direct inspiration is fragile, since it collapses the moment a writer says they have not read the book. A claim of structural convergence is robust, because it predicts that the structures will keep reappearing regardless of who has read what, and the prediction is testable against every new entry in the field. The article that argues convergence is making the stronger case, and it is also making the more interesting one, since it turns Fitzgerald from an influence into a diagnostician whose findings keep being independently confirmed.

There is a further refinement. Some of the transmission is indirect, running through intermediate books that were unmistakably written under Fitzgerald's shadow. Tom Wolfe's The Bonfire of the Vanities, published in 1987, and Bret Easton Ellis's American Psycho, published in 1991, both take the Fitzgeraldian material into the late-century city, and both were widely read by people now writing. The line from Fitzgerald to a current series is therefore rarely direct and rarely absent; it runs through a chain of books that each solved the same problem using tools inherited from the last. The broader descent through American fiction is traced in the article on [the novel's influence on later American novels](/2028/08/28/gatsby-influence-later-novels/), which handles the literary lineage this comparison deliberately leaves aside.

## What Stays Constant Across a Century

Strip away the tone, the scope, and the interiority, and four findings survive the hundred years intact.

The first is that access and belonging are separate goods, and that money buys the first reliably and the second never. This is Gatsby's whole predicament and it is the engine of most of the comedy in Crazy Rich Asians, most of the tension in Saltburn, and a great deal of Succession. The persistence of this finding across radically different economies suggests it is not a feature of 1920s New York but of any society in which some wealth is inherited.

The second is that the fortune requires an outside witness in order to be visible. Nobody inside a fortune can describe it, because the description requires a comparison the insider has never had to make. Fiction has independently rediscovered this so often that it now functions as a rule of the form.

The third is that carelessness scales with protection. Fitzgerald's formulation, that they smashed things and retreated into their money, is a general statement about incentive rather than a specific accusation against two people, and every subsequent test of it has produced the same result. The mechanism is that consequence is a purchasable commodity, and the constant across the century is that it remains purchasable.

The fourth is that spectacle is a machine for converting expenditure into standing, that the machine works, and that it produces nothing durable. The parties bought presence and not loyalty, and the funeral proved it. The contemporary versions prove it faster and with better lighting.

What has changed is not the diagnosis but the scale, the visibility, and the exit options. The fortunes are larger and more mobile. The machinery is documented rather than inferred. And the observer at the edge, who in 1922 could buy a train ticket back to the Middle West, is now more often someone with no fare and nowhere to return to. That last change is the one worth building an essay around, because it is a real difference that the text can be made to reveal by contrast.

## What Recent Work Does That Fitzgerald Could Not

A comparison that only demonstrates the older book's superiority has not learned anything. Three things current wealth fiction does are beyond the novel's reach, and naming them sharpens what the novel is actually for.

The first is showing the mechanism. Fitzgerald gives outcomes and trusts inference. Daisy and Tom leave, and the reader understands that money absorbed the consequence, but no scene shows how. Contemporary work has developed a whole procedural vocabulary for this: the settlement, the non-disclosure agreement, the crisis communications firm, the offshore vehicle, the family office. Succession spends much of its running time on exactly these instruments, and the effect is to convert a moral observation into a documented process. A reader who has watched the process can return to the novel and see the empty space where it belongs, which is an unusually useful thing for a comparison to produce.

The second is duration. A series running for years can show a fortune across enough time to demonstrate that the protection is systemic rather than lucky. Fitzgerald has one summer, so the reader must accept on the strength of a single verdict that this is how Tom and Daisy always operate. Long-form television supplies the repetitions that turn an accusation into a pattern. What Fitzgerald compensates with is compression, and the trade is real: the novel loses evidence and gains force, since a hundred hours of demonstrated carelessness has nowhere near the impact of the sentence about smashing things and retreating back into money.

The third is the interior of the service position. The novel has almost nothing from the perspective of the people who make the parties possible. Eight servants toil on Mondays repairing the ravages of the night before, and that is essentially the whole account. Contemporary work has moved this population to the center, giving it consciousness, strategy, and plot agency, and the change is the single largest expansion of the territory since 1925. When Bong builds a film around a family who enter a household as staff, or when a series follows resort employees across a season, they are filling a space Fitzgerald left almost entirely blank.

What Fitzgerald retains against all three is the object of grief. Recent work is frequently more informative and rarely more moving, because it has largely abandoned the attempt to make anything in the arrangement worth mourning. The novel's continuing advantage is not analytical. It is that Fitzgerald found a way to write an indictment that also aches.

## Why the Word Hollow Is Still Correct

The most durable phrase in the critical vocabulary around this novel is the hollowness of its upper class, and it is worth defending against the objection that it is a cliche, because contemporary wealth fiction has effectively run a hundred-year experiment on it.

Hollowness in Fitzgerald has a precise meaning. It is not that the rich are unhappy, which they occasionally are, or that they are stupid, which Tom partly is and Jordan is not. It is that the class has no content beyond its own maintenance. Ask what Tom does, and the answer is that he was a football player at twenty-one and everything since has been anticlimax. Ask what the Buchanans want, and the answer is to keep what they have. Ask what the party guests believe, and the answer is nothing that survives the end of the summer. The class produces no work, no ideas, no institutions, and no attachments that outlast an inconvenience.

Contemporary work has tested this and confirmed it with additional evidence. The recurring joke in Succession is that a media dynasty produces nothing except its own succession problem. The recurring situation in resort fiction is that guests with unlimited means cannot generate a single afternoon of meaning without staff. Ostlund's shipwreck is the experiment run to its conclusion: remove the maintenance apparatus and the class has no capacities at all. Each of these is a stress test on the hollowness thesis, and none of them has falsified it.

What has changed is that hollowness is now often played for comedy where Fitzgerald played it for horror. His version is delivered through Nick's shouted verdict across a lawn, that the crowd is rotten and that Gatsby is worth the whole bunch put together, and through the unattended funeral that proves it. The tone is prosecutorial. The current tone is amused. But amusement and horror are reactions to the same finding, and the finding has held for a century, which is a strong result for a claim that many first-time readers dismiss as a study-guide platitude.

## Three Pairings That Actually Work

For a reader or student assembling a comparative project, three specific pairings produce arguments rather than lists, and each is chosen for a structural feature rather than for reputation.

Pair the novel with Parasite when the argument is about architecture and proximity. The Valley of Ashes sits between the mansions and the city, requiring a journey; the semi-basement sits under the house, requiring nothing. Building an essay on that single spatial change lets you argue that a century has converted an economic relationship from a geography into a floor plan, and the evidence is available in both texts within minutes of looking. The essay writes itself around one question: how far does the periphery have to travel to reach the center, and what does the distance do to the plot?

Pair the novel with Trust when the argument is about narration and evidence. Both books are about who controls the story a fortune tells about itself. Fitzgerald installs an arbitrating witness who checks facts and reaches a verdict. Diaz withholds arbitration and hands the reader four competing documents. The essay argues that the disappearance of the trustworthy witness is the century's real change, and it can be evidenced from Nick's lunch with Wolfsheim and Jordan's account of 1917 on one side and the structure of the later book on the other.

Pair the novel with Crazy Rich Asians when the argument is about conversion failure and comedy. Both examine a claimant whose money exceeds the establishment's and whose standing does not. Fitzgerald plays it as tragedy and locates the barrier in origin; Kwan plays it as farce and locates the barrier in dynastic time, since the established families are not richer but older. The essay argues that the mechanism is identical while the register is opposite, and asks what a society has to believe about money before this material becomes funny.

Each pairing shares a discipline: one structure, two texts, and a question the comparison answers that neither text answers alone. Choose accordingly, and the essay has an argument before the first paragraph is written.

## Why the Genre Revived After 2008

The density of this field is not accidental, and dating it explains why the Fitzgerald comparison started appearing in reviews with such regularity.

Wealth fiction has a boom-and-bust rhythm that tracks the visibility of inequality rather than its extent. The 1920s produced Fitzgerald's generation. The 1980s produced Tom Wolfe's New York and Bret Easton Ellis's, both written while a financial culture was visibly reorganizing a city. And the years after the crisis of 2008 produced the current wave, for a reason that is straightforward: a crash makes the machinery briefly legible. Ordinary readers acquire vocabulary they did not previously have, learn what a collateralized instrument is, watch institutions rescued and households not, and become an audience for stories about how fortunes actually operate.

Fitzgerald's own book sits at the front end of the same rhythm. He wrote in 1924, published in 1925, and the crash that would have made his readers understand what he was describing arrived four years later, which is part of why the novel sold modestly at first and was rediscovered afterward. The audience for wealth fiction is made by events, and the events had not happened yet.

That timing explains the comparison's frequency without requiring any claim about influence. Reviewers reach for Fitzgerald when a new book about the ultra-rich appears because his is the canonical entry from the last comparable moment, and because the anatomy he built is the one readers already have in their heads. The comparison is a shorthand for a category, and the category exists because a crisis periodically renders it interesting again.

For an essay, the useful move here is to treat the rhythm as evidence for the convergence argument. If wealth fiction clusters after financial disruptions, and if each cluster independently produces the same four structures, then the structures belong to the subject rather than to a lineage. That is a testable historical claim rather than a literary intuition, and it is the strongest available support for reading Fitzgerald as a diagnostician whose findings keep getting confirmed by writers who were not consulting him.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The comparison holds, and the deciding factor is diagnostic rather than genealogical.

Gatsby's continuing relevance to wealth fiction is not that later writers copied it. It is that Fitzgerald identified four structural relations, between access and belonging, between money and legitimacy, between protection and carelessness, and between spectacle and standing, which turn out to be permanent features of any society organized around inherited advantage. Later writers keep encountering the same relations because the relations are still there. The book reads as prophetic for the same reason a correct diagnosis reads as prophetic: it names something that has not stopped happening.

The verdict, then, is that contemporary wealth fiction reworks Gatsby's anatomy rather than its story. Almost nobody reproduces the plot. Almost everybody reproduces the four structures, and reproduces them with variations that measure exactly how the economics have moved. This is a stronger form of relevance than direct influence, because it does not depend on anyone having read the book.

The single deciding piece of evidence is the recurrence of the observer position across writers who share no tradition. A Korean filmmaker, a Swedish satirist, an American novelist, and a British screenwriter independently concluded that the story of the rich has to be told by someone standing at the edge of the room. Fitzgerald reached that conclusion in 1924 and executed it so completely that the position now looks like a natural feature of the subject rather than an invention. That is what it means for a book to remain current.

## The Missing Green Light

The most instructive thing about this comparison is an absence. Contemporary wealth fiction has reproduced Fitzgerald's observer, his claimant, his protected elite, and his spectacle machine. It has not reproduced the green light, and the failure is not a failure of talent.

The light works in the novel because it is not a symbol of money. Gatsby stretches his arms toward the dark water and what he reaches for is a specific woman on a specific dock, and behind her a version of his own life in which the five intervening years did not happen. The light is small, distant, and privately meaningful, and Fitzgerald then performs the move that makes the book: on the final page he widens it, so that one man's misidentified light becomes the continent as the Dutch sailors first saw it, and the private longing becomes a national one. The symbol works because the country in the book had made a promise that could be believed and then broken, and the light is the place where the promise and the breach are held together.

Recent wealth fiction has no equivalent because it is not writing about a promise. Its fortunes are not attached to a national story, its claimants are not pursuing anything they could describe as a dream, and its most characteristic gesture is the acquisition of a further asset rather than the reaching of an arm. Succession's dynasty wants control of a company. Ostlund's passengers want the trip to continue. Kwan's families want the correct marriage. None of these desires can be widened at the end into an image of a continent, because none of them was ever about anything larger than the household.

That absence explains several of the differences already identified. It explains the tonal shift from elegy to satire, since satire is what remains when there is nothing to mourn. It explains the withheld interiority, since a character pursuing an asset does not require the inner life that a character pursuing a dream does. And it explains why the century's most affecting work in this territory is usually about the people wealth employs, whose desires are still describable as hopes.

An essay can use this absence productively rather than treating it as the novel's win. The interesting question is not whether current writers are inferior but what changed in the material that removed the symbol's foundation. Two answers are available and both are arguable. The first is that the national promise Fitzgerald was writing about has become too discredited to sustain a symbol, so a writer reaching for an equivalent would produce something no reader would credit. The second is that fortunes have become transnational, and a symbol requires a place, so money without a homeland cannot generate one. Either answer produces a real argument, and both can be evidenced from the closing page of the novel against the ending of almost any recent work in the field.

There is also a technical dimension worth noticing. Fitzgerald's widening move depends on a single narrator with a retrospective position and a lyric register, and both of those tools have gone out of fashion in this territory for reasons already discussed. A documentary novel assembled from competing accounts cannot widen at the end, because there is no consciousness in charge to do the widening. An ensemble series cannot do it either, since the final image would have to belong to someone. The absence of the green light is therefore partly a consequence of formal choices, and an essay that connects the missing symbol to the missing narrator has found the deepest available version of this comparison.

The finding, stated plainly: contemporary wealth fiction has inherited Fitzgerald's anatomy of the rich and has not inherited his object of longing, and the two facts are connected. The anatomy survives because the arrangements it describes survive. The longing does not survive because what it was directed at has stopped being available to believe in, and a symbol nobody can believe in is not a symbol.

## How to Build a Comparative Essay From This

The most common failure in this territory is the parallel hunt: a paragraph noting that both Gatsby and some recent series feature a party, then a paragraph noting that both feature a death. Parallels are not arguments. Here is a working method.

Choose one structure, not four. The observer position, the conversion failure, the protected carelessness, or the spectacle machine. A twelve-hundred-word essay that traces one structure across two texts with real evidence will beat one that gestures at all four.

Choose the partner text by structure rather than by fame. If your structure is the observer position, Parasite and The Guest give you sharper material than a more famous title with a passive narrator. If your structure is conversion failure, Crazy Rich Asians and Succession are the strong choices. Name the reason for the pairing in your thesis, so an examiner knows the comparison is designed rather than default.

Make the shift column the argument. The finding is never that the structure recurs; that is the premise. The finding is what has changed inside the structure. The observer can no longer afford to leave. The gatekeepers have gone global. The machinery of protection is now visible on screen. Each of those is a thesis with an essay behind it.

Handle the influence question in one paragraph and then move on. Concede that most contemporary work was not written under Fitzgerald's influence, argue that structural convergence is the stronger claim, and note that some transmission runs indirectly through late-century intermediaries. Doing this early protects the rest of the essay from a predictable objection.

Quote small and gloss immediately. The careless-people verdict, the uncut pages, the moths and the champagne, and the end of the Trimalchio career are four short quotations that will carry an entire comparative argument. Do not summarize the plot of any recent work at length; characterize its structure in a sentence and move to the comparison. An examiner reading an essay in this territory has usually seen a great deal of summary and very little argument.

Anchor the whole thing in the novel's own treatment of money and position. The wider case is developed in the analysis of [wealth and class across the whole book](/2024/05/13/wealth-class-great-gatsby/), and the psychological engine is covered in the study of [class anxiety](/2025/02/03/class-anxiety-great-gatsby/), both of which supply the vocabulary this comparison depends on. ## Closing Verdict

The reason a reviewer reaches for Fitzgerald every time a new story about the very rich appears is not laziness, or not only laziness. It is that he built the four instruments the subject requires and built them so completely that the subject now seems to come with them attached. An observer with entry and no security. A claimant whose money will not convert. An elite whose carelessness is underwritten. A spectacle that buys presence and not attachment. A century later, in different countries and different forms, writers keep assembling the same four instruments because the thing they are examining has not changed shape. The relevance is living, and it is living in the least sentimental way possible: not because the book is beloved, but because it was right.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to contemporary wealth fiction?

It compares structurally rather than genealogically. Fitzgerald assembled four relations that recent work keeps reproducing: an observer with access and no capital, a self-made claimant whose money will not convert into standing, an inherited elite whose carelessness is underwritten by capital, and a spectacle whose real product is legitimacy. Recent novels, films, and series arrive at these same relations independently because the subject demands them. What differs is tone, since Fitzgerald writes elegy and most current work writes satire, and scope, since his argument is national and theirs is usually transnational. The comparison is worth making because the differences measure how the economics have moved.

### Q: Which recent books and series are closest to Gatsby?

Three prose works sit nearest. Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, published in 2021 after the novel entered the United States public domain, retells the summer from Jordan Baker's position. Hernan Diaz's Trust, published in 2022 and awarded the Pulitzer Prize the following year, examines a 1920s financier through four competing accounts. Emma Cline's The Guest, published in 2023, follows a young woman drifting through a Long Island summer on borrowed hospitality. On screen, Succession, The White Lotus, Parasite, and Triangle of Sadness reproduce the structures without the lineage. Proximity of setting is not the same as descent, and only the first of these is a direct retelling.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald directly influence writers of modern wealth fiction?

In most cases, no, and claiming otherwise weakens an essay. Direct descent is demonstrable only for explicit retellings and for a small number of works in conversation with the period. Everything else is convergence: writers examining extreme wealth arrive at the same structures because the subject poses the same problems. Some transmission runs indirectly through intermediate books, particularly Tom Wolfe's The Bonfire of the Vanities from 1987 and Bret Easton Ellis's American Psycho from 1991, which carried the material into the late-century city and were widely read by people now working. The convergence claim is both more defensible and more interesting than the influence claim.

### Q: Why do these stories always need an outsider narrator?

Because a fortune is invisible from inside it. To someone who has always had money, money is weather, and no description is possible without a comparison the insider has never had to make. A story told from complete outside cannot reach the rooms where the significant behavior occurs. The narrative therefore requires a hinge figure with entry and without security, which is exactly the position Fitzgerald built for Nick: Yale, a Buchanan cousin, a small rented house, and a bond job he does not understand. Writers with no shared tradition keep rediscovering this requirement, which is strong evidence that it belongs to the subject rather than to any one author.

### Q: What does the uncut library pages detail reveal about purchased status?

It reveals that purchased legitimacy stays visible. Owl Eyes, drunk in the library, is astonished to find the books genuine and then notices that the pages have never been cut. Everything is authentic and nothing has been used, which is the precise condition of a status claim built by acquisition. The objects are correct and the relationship to them is not, and no further purchase closes that gap. Contemporary wealth fiction reruns the observation constantly through scenes in which an outsider gets a detail almost right and the room registers the error without comment. The detail is small, exact, and does more argumentative work than any speech in the book.

### Q: How do recent stories test whether the rich face consequences?

By introducing a catastrophe and then measuring who pays for it. Fitzgerald runs the test first: Daisy drives the car that kills Myrtle, Tom points George Wilson at the wrong man, three people die, and the two responsible close a house and go abroad. Recent work stages the same measurement. Deaths at company events and resort holidays are absorbed as negotiating positions or anecdotes while local staff carry the cost. The consistency across countries and forms is the finding. Consequence is a purchasable commodity, and the century has changed the paperwork rather than the outcome.

### Q: What has actually changed between 1922 and now in these stories?

Four things, and they are the material for a strong essay. The observer can no longer afford to leave, where Nick simply bought a ticket back to the Middle West. The gatekeepers have gone global, so the conversion failure now plays out across continents rather than across a bay. The machinery of protection is documented on screen, where Fitzgerald gave the outcome and left the process to inference. And the elegiac register has migrated, so that current work grants lyric sympathy to the people wealth employs rather than to anyone inside the household. The diagnosis is unchanged; the scale, visibility, and exit options are not.

### Q: Is Parasite a Gatsby story?

Not by descent, and probably not by intention, but it reproduces three of the four structures with unusual clarity. The observer position is distributed across an entire family who enter the wealthy household as employees, which converts the social hinge into a labor relationship. The extraction zone has moved inside the architecture, so the basement replaces the Valley of Ashes. And the catastrophe test is run to its limit, with the surviving rich household treating a bloodbath as a property matter. What the film lacks is the conversion-failure plot, since its characters are not attempting to buy standing. That absence is itself useful in an essay, because it isolates which structures are essential.

### Q: How does spectacle work as a legitimacy machine in both eras?

It converts expenditure into standing, and it does so reliably while producing nothing durable. Gatsby's parties are an application rather than an entertainment, staged to be seen across a bay, and they stop the moment they succeed. The contemporary equivalents are resorts, yachts, destination weddings, and galas, all of which recent work presents as machines that malfunction on camera. The shared plot beat is the moment the staff become visible or the setting is removed, at which point the standing evaporates. Fitzgerald proves the point with a funeral that draws one guest from a summer of hundreds, which remains the most economical demonstration available.

### Q: Does Succession borrow from Gatsby?

Not in plot and not in any documented way, but it runs two of the structures hard. The dynasty is new money by establishment standards, and much of the show's cruelty comes from older institutions finding their manners amusing, which is the conversion-failure structure relocated to media. And catastrophe absorption is the show's central procedure, with a fatal incident functioning as a negotiating asset across years. What Succession adds is interiority withheld as technique, presenting its characters as positions rather than selves. Fitzgerald does the opposite with Gatsby, giving him an origin, a schedule, and a Platonic conception, which is why the two works feel so different despite sharing machinery.

### Q: Why did Gatsby retellings become common after 2021?

Because the novel entered the United States public domain on the first day of 2021, making adaptation and retelling legally straightforward for the first time. Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful arrived that year, retelling the summer from Jordan Baker's position with a supernatural apparatus attached. The change is legal rather than literary: the structures had been circulating in wealth fiction for decades without permission being required, since nobody needs a license to write an outsider narrator. What the public domain enabled was the explicit reuse of Fitzgerald's characters, settings, and language, which is a narrower and more traceable kind of descent.

### Q: What is the Gatsby template table for?

It is this article's citable artifact and it is built for comparative essays. Each row pairs one structure from the novel with the recent work that reworks it and names the shift that has occurred across the century. The value sits in the shift column rather than in the pairing, because the recurrence of a structure is the premise while the change inside it is the finding. In practice, three rows well used will outperform all ten listed. Choose rows that produce different kinds of claim, one about narration, one about consequence, and one about spectacle, so the essay demonstrates range rather than repetition.

### Q: How should a student avoid the parallel-hunting trap?

By choosing one structure and tracing it, rather than listing coincidences. An essay noting that both Gatsby and a recent series contain a party and a death has made two observations and no argument. An essay arguing that the observer position has migrated from a man who can afford to leave toward figures who cannot, and evidencing that from both texts, has made a claim someone could disagree with. State the reason for the pairing in the thesis, structure paragraphs around shared questions rather than around books, and spend the conclusion naming what decides the case rather than restating the similarities.

### Q: Does the transnational scope of recent work weaken the comparison?

It complicates it productively. Fitzgerald's argument is anchored to a national promise, with the closing page running back to the continent as the Dutch sailors first saw it, so the tragedy depends on a country having offered something specific. Contemporary fortunes have no homeland, and stories about them lose that particular tragedy. What the comparison gains is a clean measurement of what disappeared: when the promise goes, the elegiac register goes with it, and the satire that replaces it has no equivalent of the green light. Making that loss the subject of an essay is more productive than treating the difference as a mismatch.

### Q: Which quotations work best for a comparative essay in this territory?

Four short ones will carry an entire argument. The verdict that they were careless people who smashed things and retreated into their money supplies the protection structure. The library detail of genuine books with uncut pages supplies the purchased-legitimacy structure. The image of guests coming and going like moths among the whisperings and the champagne supplies the spectacle machine. And the line that his career as Trimalchio was over supplies the moment the machine switches off. Quote each at phrase length, gloss it in the same sentence, and spend the saved words on the contemporary comparison rather than on summary.

### Q: Is contemporary wealth fiction more critical of the rich than Gatsby is?

It is more openly hostile and arguably less penetrating. Current work satirizes, withholds sympathy, and often refuses interiority as a deliberate technique, which produces sharp comedy and a flattened diagnosis. Fitzgerald is crueler about Tom and Daisy than almost any recent writer is about their equivalents, since he gives them a full moral portrait and then convicts them, and Nick's verdict has a finality that satire cannot reach. The difference is that Fitzgerald pairs the conviction with genuine grief for the man the system destroys, which gives the criticism weight. Hostility without grief reads as commentary; the novel reads as judgment.

### Q: How has the extraction zone changed since the Valley of Ashes?

It has moved indoors. Fitzgerald put the wasteland on the road between the mansions and the city, so the collision between the periphery and the capital required a physical journey; George Wilson has to cross Long Island on foot and arrives with the wrong information. Contemporary work relocates the same relationship into the building, whether as the vertical arrangement of Parasite's hillside home and semi-basement or as the staff corridors running a few meters from resort suites. Nothing has to travel now, so what used to be a plot event is a daily condition, and the service population has gained proximity, information, and occasionally leverage that Wilson never had.

### Q: What does the recurring party scene owe to Chapter 3?

A five-part shape that has become genre furniture. Fitzgerald's version begins with music heard from outside by someone not attending, moves to itemized logistics that convert pleasure into expenditure, presents the crowd as an anonymous mass with introductions forgotten on the spot, includes an inspection that finds a seam, and closes on a mechanical failure. Recent party sequences assemble some subset of the same components, because each one converts celebration into evidence. What later versions rarely reproduce is the beauty. Fitzgerald's moth simile is not ironic, and the ambivalence it creates is harder to dismiss than the deliberate ugliness of most current stagings.

### Q: How do recent writers handle the women Fitzgerald kept at a distance?

By supplying the interiority he withheld. Daisy's wish that her daughter be a beautiful little fool is presented as cynicism and functions as accurate calculation about the returns available to a woman in her position, but the reader must infer almost everything behind it. Jordan gets independence and little attention, and Myrtle gets an apartment, a party, and a collision. Contemporary work moves inside all three positions, most explicitly in the 2021 retelling narrated by Jordan. What the shift confirms is that the constraint analysis was sound; writers who go inside the position find the calculation the line implies. The century's contribution is testimony rather than correction.

### Q: Can a comparative essay use films and television alongside the novel?

Yes, and examiners generally reward it when the comparison is structural rather than decorative. The requirement is that the screen work be treated as a text with formal choices, not as an illustration. Argue about how a film's architecture places its service characters, how a series distributes narration, or how a party sequence is edited, and the comparison is doing literary work. Summarize a plot and the essay collapses into review. Characterize each screen work in a sentence, spend the words on the novel's language, and make the finding a claim about the shift inside a shared structure rather than a note that both texts contain rich people.
