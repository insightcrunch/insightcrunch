## The Second English Romeo and Juliet: Why Painter Matters

Every account of the play's sources begins with Arthur Brooke, and the beginning is just. Brooke's long poem of 1562 is the text Shakespeare worked from, the narrative whose sequence the play follows and whose phrases keep surfacing inside the verse. Yet Brooke's poem was not the only English Romeo and Juliet on the shelf when the young playwright went looking for the story. Five years after the poem, in the second tome of The Palace of Pleasure, William Painter published the tale again, this time in prose, under a heading that promised "the true, and constant Love between Rhomeo and Iulietta". Two English versions of the Verona story, one in verse and one in prose, both drawn from the same French telling, circulated side by side for three decades before the play was staged. The cliché of source study remembers one of them. Restoring the second changes the picture of what Shakespeare had at hand, and it sharpens the most delicate question in the whole source dossier: not whether the play descends from Brooke, which is settled, but whether a second book also lay open, or at least lay remembered, beside the first.

![William Painter's Palace of Pleasure, the prose Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict argued through this study can be stated at the outset, because the evidence sorts itself into two kinds that must not be confused. As a working text, the poem stands alone: the play's verbal echoes of Brooke run into the hundreds, its sequence of scenes tracks Brooke's narrative order, and no comparable density of borrowing from Painter has ever been shown. As a book its author had read, Painter is another matter. G. Blakemore Evans, editing the play for Cambridge, judged that Shakespeare worked directly with Brooke's poem, "that he knew Painter's prose version is highly probable", and that a small residue of "four or five suggestive and scattered details" points toward the prose tale rather than the poem. Two of those details are strong enough to name here and weigh in full below: the length of Juliet's potion sleep, which Painter gives as forty hours at the least, where Brooke gives three days and the play gives two and forty hours; and the hero's very name, Romeo in the play and Rhomeo in Painter, where Brooke calls him Romeus throughout. Knowledge and desk work are different claims. The first, on the evidence, is highly probable. The second remains unproven, and the distinction between them is the honest settlement of a question that looser accounts blur in both directions, either inflating Painter into a co-source or erasing him from the record altogether.

What Painter indisputably added needs no detective work, because it sits on the surface of the book trade. His prose made the Verona story into an English novella, readable in the way a long poem in poulter's measure is not, and it lodged the tale inside the most successful English story collection of its generation, a book reprinted again and again and mined by playwrights for forty years. Whatever Shakespeare took from its pages, the culture took the story from them: Painter is the tale's second English carrier, and the last full telling of it in English before the play absorbed both its predecessors.

## Who Was William Painter?

William Painter, born around 1540 and dead by 1595, was not a poet by trade. He was a civil servant, a clerk of the ordnance in the Tower of London, an officer concerned with the Crown's artillery, stores and accounts, and his great book was the work of a translator with a bureaucrat's patience for large undertakings. The contrast with Brooke is instructive and was drawn neatly by Jill L. Levenson, who observed that Brooke the gentleman poet rationalizes the story, making social disorder more controllable, while Painter the civil servant brings out the elements of romance and adventure. The two English versions bear the marks of their makers' stations. Brooke preaches at his readers from a preface; Painter, the administrator, assembles, arranges and files the tale among dozens of others, and lets the filing itself do the persuading.

### What office did Painter hold, and how did it end?

Painter served as clerk of the ordnance in the Tower of London, the office responsible for the Crown's guns, powder and stores. It ended in disgrace: charged with peculation, with diverting Crown funds passing through his hands, he closed his career under an embezzlement scandal rather than in honourable retirement.

The scandal belongs in a study of the book because it fixes the translator's world. Painter moved among soldiers, officials and courtiers; his dedicatees were men of war and state; his collection is full of sieges, stratagems and sudden deaths handled with an administrator's eye for how enterprises fail. The Romeo and Juliet he Englished is correspondingly practical in its furniture: letters carried and miscarried, watchmen posted, a friar's plan drawn up like a memorandum and ruined by a quarantine. Brooke's poem keeps asking what the lovers' deaths mean. Painter's prose keeps showing how the machinery around them worked, and where it jammed.

His learning was the working equipment of the Elizabethan translator class. He read French fluently, Italian at least through French spectacles, and Latin for the classical historians who fill his first tome. He was not an original author manqué; nothing suggests he wanted to be. The Palace of Pleasure announces itself as a storehouse gathered from other men's gardens, and Painter's talent was the gatherer's: selection, arrangement, and a plain, serviceable English style that could carry a Roman siege and a Veronese love affair in the same unhurried gait. That gait is the first thing a reader notices against Brooke. Where the poem hurries its couplets toward a moral, the prose version lingers, expands speeches, and lets scenes breathe at the length narrative prose allows.

His death in the mid-1590s fell close to the play's first performances, a coincidence the record leaves unglossed. Whether the ageing translator ever heard that the tale he had Englished a generation earlier had become a stage sensation, no document says. The book, at any rate, survived him in the bookstalls, and the play's first audiences included, beyond argument, men and women who had met Romeo and Julietta between Painter's covers before they ever saw them on a scaffold stage.

## What Is The Palace of Pleasure?

The Palace of Pleasure appeared in two tomes. The first, in 1566, gathered some sixty tales, drawn from classical historians such as Livy and Herodotus, from Boccaccio, from Bandello, from the Heptaméron of Marguerite de Navarre and from other Italian and French storehouses, and it was dedicated to Ambrose Dudley, Earl of Warwick, a soldier-statesman whose service Painter's own office touched. The book succeeded at once. The second tome followed in 1567, longer than the first and weighted toward the tragic Italian novella: more than half of its tales come from Bandello, among them, as its twenty-fifth tale, the story of Romeo and Julietta. Three editions of the first tome and two of the second had appeared before the century turned, a publication history that measures how thoroughly the collection saturated the reading public whose sons and apprentices filled the playhouses.

### Why does the collection call itself a palace of pleasure?

The title promises architecture and delight: a palace, because the book is a great house of many chambers, each tale a room furnished from a different master's stores; pleasure, because the rooms are offered for recreation before instruction. Painter keeps Boccaccio's promise of variety while dropping the garden party of narrators.

The Romeo and Juliet tale sits in the second tome among Bandello's darker merchandise: murders, adulteries, revenges and poisonings, the histoires tragiques that Boaistuau had already quarried from the Italian and that Belleforest continued in French. Painter's immediate source for the tale, as for the tome's Bandello block, was not the Italian text but the French: Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques of 1559, where the Verona story stands as the third of the tragical histories. Painter's novella runs to some thirty printed pages, a substantial short novel by the standards of the collection, and it is Englished with a fidelity that later collators have repeatedly confirmed. Evans's summary of the relation is exact: Painter's translation of Boaistuau's version is close and generally accurate, while Brooke's much longer verse narrative, 3,020 lines in poulter's measure, gives essentially everything in Boaistuau, and hence in Painter, with expansions of its own.

Two consequences follow for everything argued later in this study. First, because both Englishmen translate the same French text, agreement between Painter and the play is ambiguous evidence: the detail may have reached Shakespeare through Brooke, who usually carries the same matter. Second, because Brooke expands, compresses and omits as a poet does, while Painter hews close to the French, there exist details that Painter preserves in a form nearer the play than Brooke's rendering of the same passage. The desk question lives entirely inside that narrow gap between the two consequences.

The collection's reach extended well beyond one tale. More than forty Elizabethan and Jacobean plays have been traced to plots in The Palace of Pleasure, which makes Painter, by that measure, one of the most consequential prose writers of the English sixteenth century, a supplier of raw narrative to the entire dramatic generation. Romeo and Juliet is simply the most famous instance of a general phenomenon: the stage feeding on the novella, and on Painter's Englished novelle above all. When the playwright wanted the Verona story, he reached into a book trade in which Painter's volumes were furniture, present in stationers' shops and gentlemen's studies alike, reprinted within living memory, and excerpted by the culture until their contents passed for English common stock.

## Painter's French Source: Boaistuau Behind the Prose

Boaistuau's handling of Bandello is the hinge of the whole transmission, and it is treated at full length in the study of [Pierre Boaistuau and the French tradition](/2025/10/24/pierre-boaistuau-french-tradition/); here the French book matters only as Painter's exemplar, the text on his desk. What Boaistuau did to Bandello's novella of 1554 was threefold. He tightened the plot's joints, working out business the Italian left loose, such as the Capulet restraint when Romeo is discovered at the feast and the dilemma of Juliet on first hearing of Tybalt's death. He developed the apothecary from a function into a character with a poverty to plead. And he altered the ending's mechanics: where da Porto and Bandello let Juliet revive while Romeo still lives, so that the lovers speak before his death and she then simply ceases to breathe, Boaistuau's Juliet wakes after Romeo is dead and kills herself with his dagger. Brooke, whatever his title page claims about translating Bandell from the Italian, followed Boaistuau's amended ending, and Painter, translating the French openly, followed it too. The family resemblance of the two English versions, mapped whole in [the sources of Romeo and Juliet: a family tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), is therefore a French resemblance before it is anything else.

### What did Painter take from Boaistuau beyond the plot?

Painter took the Frenchman's architecture entire: the order of episodes, the worked-out feast business, the developed apothecary, and the dagger ending in place of the Italian held breath. He took Boaistuau's moral weather too, though he blows it thinner than Brooke. The sentences, pacing and warmth are his own English workmanship.

For the source hunter, this shared French parentage sets the rules of evidence, and they are strict. A detail in which the play agrees with Painter against Brooke can arise three ways: Shakespeare read Painter; Shakespeare read Boaistuau or Bandello, for which no good evidence exists; or Brooke, in compressing, dropped something that stood in the French and that Shakespeare somehow recovered, which without a second text is impossible. The middle path fails for want of evidence, so any play detail that matches Painter where Brooke diverges is, on its face, a fingerprint of the prose book. The catch is scarcity. Because Brooke is so full, giving essentially everything in Boaistuau, such divergences are rare, and each candidate must be checked against the possibility that Brooke carries the same matter in different words. Evans's "four or five" is the count of candidates that survive that checking, and the two strongest are weighed in the evidence sections below.

Painter himself seems to have felt no rivalry with the poem he followed into print by five years. The Palace nowhere glances at Brooke. The prose version simply occupies the tale afresh, in the medium its maker commanded, for the audience his collection had built: readers of novelle, a public Brooke's verse sermon never addressed. That division of the audience is part of the story's English fortune. Between them, the preacher and the clerk stocked both the study and the parlour with the same Verona.

## The Tale as Painter Tells It

Painter's heading spreads the whole story in one breathless title: "The goodly hystory of the true, and constant Love between Rhomeo and Iulietta". Goodly promises edification, true promises fact, and constant promises the virtue the tale will test to destruction. His Verona is ruled by Duke Escalus of the Scaliger line, and the feud between the Montegues and the Capellets burns at the pitch the Italian tradition fixed: armed servants, street brawls, a city tired of its two great houses. Rhomeo, a young gentleman of the Montegues, loves first an unnamed hard-hearted lady of Verona, the figure the play will name Rosaline, and goes masked to the Capellets' feast in hope of seeing her. There he sees Iulietta, and the first love evaporates in the standard physiology of the tradition: eyes strike, hearts exchange, and the dance leaves both of them prisoners.

The courtship proceeds by the machinery every version shares, because Painter is translating Boaistuau closely and Boaistuau had already perfected the sequence. There is the night talk at the window and the garden below it, Romeo climbing to her chamber window as the French text arranged; there is the go-between nurse, garrulous and purchasable; there is Friar Laurence, the Franciscan whose learning spans herbs, potions and consciences, and who agrees to marry the pair in hope that the alliance may turn the houses' rancour to peace. The marriage is solemnized in his cell. The street fight follows in its fixed shape: Theobaldo, the Capellet kinsman, kills Romeo's friend Mercutio in the quarrel Romeo tries to part, and Romeo, his forbearance spent, kills Theobaldo and is banished to Mantua. The Capellets press a marriage with the County Paris. The Friar supplies the sleeping potion and the letter that must carry the plot to Mantua, and the letter miscarries, because its bearer is shut out of the plague-suspect city: the mechanism da Porto invented, preserved entire through every translation, and the single accident on which the whole catastrophe pivots.

What then distinguishes Painter's telling, read whole, is not incident but texture. His lovers speak at a length Brooke's couplets rarely allow, in set orations that the prose dilates with rhetorical patience: Iulietta's laments, Romeo's complaints in exile, the Friar's counsels all unroll in periodic sentences that imitate, in English prose, the formal speeches of the Italian novella tradition. The tomb sequence, some thirty pages into the tale, gathers the full inheritance. Romeo buys poison from the Mantuan apothecary, whose poverty Boaistuau had already developed and Painter renders with particular relish: the starved shop, the starved man, the bargain struck between two desperations. He breaks into the Capellet monument, finds Iulietta on her bier in her counterfeit death, speaks over her at length, drinks, and dies. She wakes, in the French manner, too late for speech with him, finds the empty cup and the body, and takes his dagger to herself. The watch and the Prince close the account: the Friar's confession unravels the plot, the apothecary is hanged for his sale, the nurse is banished for her brokerage, the Friar retires to a hermitage to end his days, and the two houses are reconciled over the bodies, with golden statues promised to the dead.

Against that fullness, the play's compressions and inventions stand out in the relief that the companion study of [what Shakespeare changed from Brooke](/2025/09/26/what-shakespeare-changed-from-brooke/) maps for the poem. Nearly every change Shakespeare made from the tradition, the killing of Paris at the tomb, the five-day haste, Juliet's fourteen years, the Chorus itself, can be measured against Painter as readily as against Brooke, because Painter carries the tradition complete. That is the prose version's quiet evidentiary value even where its influence cannot be proved: it is the control text, the fullest English statement of what the story was before the play remade it, told without a poet's expansions or a preacher's interruptions.

## Painter's Prose Beside Brooke's Verse

Set the two English books side by side and the differences are differences of kind, not merely of dress. Brooke's poem runs to 3,020 lines of poulter's measure, the jog-trot of twelve-syllable and fourteen-syllable couplets that even sympathetic readers have found tedious, and it is prefaced by an address to the reader that condemns the tale's own matter in advance: the lovers are disobedient, the friar is a superstitious hypocrite, the whole is a warning against lust and popery. The poem then proceeds, in defiance of its preface, to tell the story with warmth and pity, and that doubleness, sermon outside, sympathy inside, is Brooke's signature. Painter's signature is the opposite arrangement: no thundering preface, a prose narrative morally upholstered but fundamentally hospitable to its lovers, and a narrator who behaves less like a preacher than like a host, showing the reader into the next chamber of the palace.

### Where does Painter's prose slow down inside a scene?

The prose slows wherever feeling can be formalized into speech. Laments, counsels and tomb farewells expand into set orations in which one emotion is turned over clause by clause. Brooke's couplets hurry past speeches toward narrative and moral. Painter's dilation is the novella's rhetoric: grief and love made eloquent as proof of weight.

The table makes the methodological point visible at a glance. Row by row, the two English books agree, because one French book stands behind both. The rows where they disagree are few, and they are exactly the rows where the source hunter must work: the hero's name, the sleep's duration, the shape of the speeches. In the first two of those rows, the play stands with Painter. In the third, it stands with neither, having converted narrative oration into dramatic dialogue by a process no source can explain.

Brooke's study in this series, [Arthur Brooke's 'Romeus and Juliet' (1562)](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/), reads the poem whole as a work; the point here is narrower. As carriers of Boaistuau, the two Englishmen differ chiefly in temperature. Brooke moralizes the tale hotter and expands it longer; Painter cools the moral and keeps the tale nearer its French dimensions, while warming the rhetoric inside the speeches. A playwright ransacking both would find in Brooke the fuller quarry and in Painter the cleaner line, and the play's actual borrowings, overwhelmingly Brooke's in mass, show which quarry he worked. The cleaner line survives in the play only as traces, but traces, in source study, are data, and the next sections weigh the two that matter.

## The Desk Question: What Would Count as Proof?

Source study has a hierarchy of evidence, and the Painter question is the cleanest classroom case of it in the whole Shakespeare canon. At the bottom stands agreement in plot: worthless here, because both English books translate one French book, so a shared incident proves a shared grandfather, not a shared desk. Above plot stands agreement in arrangement, the order and proportion of episodes: nearly as weak, for the same reason, since Brooke's order is Boaistuau's order and Painter's is too. Higher stands agreement in detail where the two English versions diverge: this is the first tier that can discriminate, because a detail Painter keeps and Brooke drops cannot have reached the playwright through the poem. Highest of all stands verbal echo, a phrase or figure in the play that matches one Englishman's wording against the other's: the fingerprint tier, decisive when found, because wording is the one thing translators do not share by inheritance.

### What trace would a borrowed phrase leave that a borrowed plot cannot?

A borrowed plot leaves only resemblance, which common descent explains away. A borrowed phrase leaves wording: a distinctive figure, an unusual number, a name's spelling, preserved across the change from prose to verse. Translators inherit incidents but manufacture phrases, so a phrase matching one translator against the other marks the page in the playwright's head.

Measured against that hierarchy, the play's debt to Brooke passes at the highest tier hundreds of times over: verbal echoes of the poem resound through the verse, as Evans puts it, and the sequence of the narrative is followed scene by scene. The debt to Painter can be tested only at the detail tier, and there the candidates reduce, after a century of collation, to Evans's handful. That so few survive is itself information about the size of any Painter influence; that any survive at all, given how completely Brooke covers the French original, is the puzzle the next section opens.

One more instrument belongs in the kit before the evidence is weighed: availability. A source cannot be used by a writer who cannot get it, and a source reprinted three times in one tome and twice in another, present in the shops for thirty years, excerpted by rivals and mined by fellow playwrights, clears the availability bar without argument. Painter's book was not a rarity the playwright might have glimpsed once. It was part of the period's narrative furniture, and the prior probability that a professional story-hunter of the 1590s had read its most famous tale is high before a single parallel is produced. Availability cannot prove use; it fixes the odds against which the details are read.

## The Evidence for Painter: Forty Hours and a Name

The strongest single detail in the dossier is a number. When the Friar gives Juliet the vial in the play, he specifies the term of her counterfeit death with an odd precision: "Thou shalt continue two and forty hours, / And then awake as from a pleasant sleep" (4.1). Where does the figure come from? Not from Brooke, whose sleeping powder keeps Juliet in her deathlike trance for three days. Not from the Italian versions, whose draught likewise runs to three days. Painter's prose gives the sleep as forty hours at the least. The Clark Library's description of its copy of the tale states the inference flatly: there are details in the play that can only derive from Painter, and the most notable is the time for Juliet's drug to wear off, forty hours at least in Painter, two and forty hours in Shakespeare. Numbers are the least poetic and therefore the most probative of borrowings, because no law of genre forces a dramatist compressing three days of story time to land on forty-two hours by chance when a book he plausibly owned prints forty.

The figure also makes dramaturgical sense as a borrowing rather than a coincidence. Shakespeare's whole design compresses Brooke's nine months into less than a week; the potion's term had to shrink with everything else. A dramatist shrinking three days, with Painter's page somewhere in his memory, had a ready-made shorter term, long enough for a funeral and a burial, short enough for the Friday deadline of the plot. The borrowing, if it is one, is exactly of the kind a working playwright makes: not a scene, not a speech, but a serviceable number picked up from a book read earlier and retained because it was useful.

The second detail is smaller and stranger: the hero's name. Brooke's title page and text give Romeus, the Latinized form the French tradition also used. Painter gives Rhomeo, restoring the Italian Romeo under English spelling. The play's title, from its earliest printings, is Romeo and Juliet, not Romeus and Juliet. Evans instances the point among his scattered details, and it deserves its place there. A playwright versifying Brooke for three thousand lines of echo might still be expected to keep the poem's name for its hero; that he did not, and that the name he used is the prose book's name, suggests the prose book had furnished his imagination's first or firmest impression of the man. Names, like numbers, are sticky details: they lodge in memory independently of the narratives that carry them, and they betray reading that plot cannot.

Below these two, the remaining candidates in Evans's handful are matters of colour rather than proof: turns in the tomb sequence and in the apothecary's presentation where the play's wording has been thought to stand nearer Painter's prose than to Brooke's couplets covering the same ground. Each, taken alone, can be explained by the dramatist's own invention working on Brooke's fuller account. Taken with the number and the name, they form the penumbra of a reading experience: the sense, hard to argue away entirely, that the prose tale had passed through the playwright's mind and left the kind of residue that reading leaves, scattered, verbal, half-conscious, quite unlike the systematic quarrying that Brooke's poem underwent when the play was actually composed.

## The Evidence Against: One French Book Behind Both

The case against a working use of Painter is formidable, and honesty requires it at full strength. It begins with coverage. Brooke's poem gives essentially everything in Boaistuau, and hence in Painter, as Evans concedes in the same breath as his verdict. A playwright equipped with Brooke lacked nothing that Painter could supply in the way of incident, character or sequence; the prose book is, for narrative purposes, a subset of the poem's contents in a different dress. Borrowing presupposes need, and need is what the record does not show. Where the play follows the tradition, Brooke suffices to explain every step, and parsimony, the source hunter's first law, assigns the debt to the sufficient book.

The second argument is the density and kind of the Brooke echoes. They are not the scattered residue of reading but the sediment of work: phrases lifted, images versified, whole speeches in the poem unfolded into scenes. Against that sediment, the Painter candidates are two hard details and a penumbra. If Shakespeare had composed with both books open, the prose would be expected to leave more than a number and a name in a text this saturated with its sources; open books leak. The pattern of leakage instead matches a single open book and a well-stocked memory: Brooke on the desk, Painter in the head, the second book's contribution confined to the small, durable items, a figure, a spelling, that memory retains when narrative has been forgotten.

The third argument concerns the penumbra details themselves. The tomb and the apothecary are the two places where the play most plainly expands its sources by invention: the apothecary's scene in Mantua is a small masterpiece of improvised bargaining, and the tomb is the play's own construction out of the tradition's materials. Resemblances to Painter in precisely these passages may register not borrowing but convergence, two imaginations heating the same French metal to the same colour. Boaistuau had already developed the apothecary for both Englishmen; Painter Englished him closely and Shakespeare dramatized boldly, and parallel heat is not a documentary relation.

The hard Brooke-only line has been drawn in so many words by the older editorial tradition: the tale's line of transmission runs from Masuccio through da Porto, Bandello and Boaistuau to Brooke, with Painter standing unconsulted to one side. That sentence overstates what its own evidence can bear, since unconsulted is a claim about a dead man's reading that no collation can prove, but it registers the genuine burden of proof. The documentary record establishes one desk book. Everything beyond it is inference, and inference must be graded as such: which is what the critics, at their careful best, have done.

## How Painter Translates: The Clerk's Method

Painter's title pages advertise translation, and for once the advertisement understates the literal truth. Collation of his tale against Boaistuau's French shows a translator keeping close company with his original: episode by episode, speech by speech, the English follows the French with the steadiness Evans summarized as close and generally accurate. But closeness is a method, not an absence of one, and Painter's method has a recognizable signature wherever the collation is pressed. He expands small courtesies into full exchanges. He turns the Frenchman's narrative summaries into dramatized speech at the story's pressure points, above all in the lovers' laments and the tomb. He Englished the Italian names through their French spellings, Rhomeo, Iulietta, Theobaldo, Montegues and Capellets, giving the tale a Gallic polish that Brooke's more Latinized Romeus and Juliet partly refused. And he moralizes with a lighter hand than either of his fellow translators of the story: where Boaistuau frets and Brooke thunders, Painter upholsters, inserting the brief sententious address, the nod to example, and moving on with the story he is plainly enjoying.

### Is Painter's version a translation or a retelling?

It is a translation that behaves, at the story's hot points, like a retelling. The skeleton is Boaistuau's, kept with a clerk's fidelity; the flesh at laments, counsels and the tomb is dilated in the period's rhetorical manner. Tudor translation licensed such dilation as faithfulness to effect, not to the letter.

The contrast with Brooke's method completes the triangle. Brooke versified the same French text but treated it as a quarry for a poem with designs of its own: he added addresses to Fortune, fattened some episodes, starved others, and wrapped the whole in a preface at war with his own practice. Painter, with no poem to build and no sermon to preach, had only the tale to serve, and served it with a translator's self-effacement that paradoxically preserves more of the common original than the poet's freedom does. For the modern collator this makes the prose version doubly useful: it is both a candidate source and a witness, the control against which Brooke's departures from Boaistuau, and then the play's departures from Brooke, can be triangulated.

One more feature of the method deserves record because it bears on the desk question. Painter's dilation runs toward speeches: he is at his most expansive exactly where a dramatist's interest also concentrates, in what people say at the crises. Nobody argues that the play's dialogue descends from Painter's orations; the media are too different and the play's language is its own. But a young playwright who had read the prose tale before he read, or while he read, the poem would have found the story already half-dramatized in Painter, its people already talking at length in their extremity. Influence of that atmospheric kind leaves no parallel passages for collators to find, which is one more reason the surviving hard details, the number and the name, carry more weight than their size suggests: they are the visible fraction of a familiarity the rest of which was bound to evaporate.

## Bullough, Muir and Evans: The Critics Weighed

The modern debate was set in its frame by Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, first published in 1957, prints Brooke's poem and Painter's tale in its opening volume and argues the relation in its introduction. Bullough's settlement became the discipline's default: Brooke is the play's direct and principal source; Painter is a possible subsidiary, a book the playwright may have consulted, whose agreements with the play are mostly inherited from the French original both Englishmen share. The architecture of Bullough's volume performs the verdict before the argument begins: the poem is given entire and first, the tale entire and second, and the reader who collates them watches the play gather its substance overwhelmingly from the first while glancing, at most, toward the second.

Kenneth Muir, surveying the same ground in Shakespeare's Sources, allowed the glances slightly more substance. Muir noted slight indications that Shakespeare may have read, besides Brooke, other versions of the story, Painter among them, and he treated the possibility as live rather than decorative. The difference between Bullough and Muir is one of temperature rather than of map: both put Brooke at the desk; Muir leaves the study door open wider to the corridor where the prose book stood.

G. Blakemore Evans, in the Cambridge edition, pushed the temperature higher than either. His formulation deserves quoting because it is the strongest careful statement the pro-Painter case has received: Shakespeare worked directly with Brooke's poem, for verbal echoes resound throughout the play; that he knew Painter's prose version is highly probable; Painter's influence, beyond four or five suggestive and scattered details, remains slight, and one of the suggestive details he instances hangs on Painter's very title, the Rhomeo of the heading against Brooke's Romeus. Evans thus separates the two questions this study has kept apart: knowledge, highly probable; influence, marginal in bulk. His edition's authority has made that separation the position to beat.

Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition and her earlier study of the story before Shakespeare supply the fourth voice, and hers is the contribution that survives any verdict on the desk. Levenson redirected attention from the detective question to the cultural one: each retelling stamps its teller's world on the tale, Brooke the gentleman poet rationalizing disorder, Painter the civil servant drawing out romance and adventure, Boaistuau writing amid French civil unrest, Bandello amid Italian courts. On her showing, the prose version matters not because a playwright may have opened it but because it demonstrates what the 1560s wanted from Verona: not Brooke's warning but a romance of adventure, stocked in a best-selling palace of tales. The desk question asks what Shakespeare read. Levenson's question, what England read, has the larger answer, and Painter wins it outright.

René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, represents the modern editorial centre: Brooke as the narrative bedrock, the Italian line behind him through Boaistuau, Painter acknowledged in the ancestry without promotion to the desk. Weis's conservatism is the discipline's resting point after Evans's warmth: the prose book is named, its handful of details is noted, and the composition is explained by the poem.

### Where do the critics actually disagree?

They disagree on the grading of an inference, not on any fact. All accept Brooke as principal source, both English versions as translations of Boaistuau, and a handful of details aligning the play with Painter. The dispute is whether those details license saying Shakespeare had read Painter. Evans grades the licence highly probable; Bullough and Weis leave it possible.

Adjudicated on the evidence set out in this study, the verdict goes to Evans, with his own qualification enforced. The forty hours is a detail of the wrong kind to be coincidence: a precise, arbitrary, non-narrative figure that Brooke contradicts with three days and that no compression of the plot required. Combined with the hero's name, which the play takes in Painter's form against the poem it otherwise plundered, it establishes reading beyond reasonable scholarly doubt. What it does not establish is composition from the prose book: the mass of verbal and structural debt runs to Brooke alone, and a desk copy of Painter would have leaked more than traces into so absorbent a text. The older line's word unconsulted is therefore half right and wholly misleading: unproven as a workbook, indefensible as a claim that the book stood unread at the playwright's elbow.

## The Feast, the Window and the Tomb: Three Scenes in Painter's Prose

The fairest test of a narrative source is to watch it handle the scenes the play made immortal, and Painter's handling of the three great stations shows both his fidelity and the distance a dramatist still had to travel. At the feast, his Rhomeo enters masked among the torchbearers, hoping for a sight of the hard-hearted lady, and is arrested instead by Iulietta. The prose gives the Capellet restraint its full French working: the host perceives the intruder, is moved toward expulsion, and is talked down by his kinsman's counsel to patience, so that courtesy, not ignorance, shelters the enemy at the board. The play keeps that machinery almost intact in Capulet's checked rage at Tybalt, "He bears him like a portly gentleman" (1.5), while compressing the lovers' meeting into the shared sonnet, "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much" (1.5), a formal invention no source contains. Painter's lovers, by contrast, exchange the period's full ceremonial of glances, a dance, and reported speech across the following days: the novella's love begins as a social fact before it becomes a conspiracy, and the prose lingers over the decorum of its kindling exactly where the play detonates it into fourteen lines.

At the window, the difference of media declares itself most plainly. Boaistuau had already supplied the garden, the window and the corded ladder by which Romeo climbs to her chamber after the marriage, and Painter translates the arrangement entire: his lovers' night talks are conducted at the window of her chamber, in speeches whose length the prose can afford and the poem's couplets keep trimming. The play dissolves the ladder and the chamber into the orchard's darkness and Juliet above at a window the text never builds into architecture, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2), converting prose logistics into pure overhearing: Romeo below, unheard, translating sight into the sun conceit. What Painter gives the scene that Brooke also gives, and the play transfigures, is duration: in the prose the courtship occupies its nights at leisure, so that the marriage, when it comes, crowns an acquaintance. The play's lovers marry on a day's knowledge, and the haste, Shakespeare's own contribution, reads as haste precisely against the prose's unhurried calendar.

The tomb has been treated already as evidence; as writing it deserves its paragraph. Painter's Romeo buys the poison in a Mantua rendered with the novella's eye for trade: the apothecary's shop hangs its tortoise, its stuffed alligator and its beggarly accounts, in the prose Boaistuau had already furnished, and the bargain is struck in the coin of two poverties, the seller's and the buyer's. The play's apothecary scene, "I see that thou art poor" and "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1), reads like that prose melted down and recast in dialogue, which is why source hunters have always lingered there. In the monument, Painter's Romeo speaks over the body at the length the French allowed, takes the poison, and dies into a silence Iulietta wakes to break alone. No farewell passes between them. The play preserves the cruelty and sharpens it by minutes, letting Juliet wake to a cup "closed in my true love's hand" (5.3) and a dagger she can name happy, "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath" (5.3). Everything characteristic is present in that sharpening: the prose's pathos is real, and the play's superiority is a superiority of timing, not of feeling invented from nothing.

## The Palace in the Playhouses: Availability and the Common Stock

Arguments about what a playwright read are usually conducted as if books were rare objects glimpsed in libraries. The Palace of Pleasure was not. Between its first appearance and the play's composition, the two tomes went through edition after edition; the tales were excerpted, imitated and rifled; and the dramatic profession treated the collection as a licensed quarry. More than forty plays of the period have been traced to its plots, a statistic that converts Painter from a private reading experience into a public utility. A tale standing twenty-fifth in the second tome of the age's most successful story collection did not need to be sought out by the young Shakespeare. It needed only not to be avoided, and the play's circle, actors and sharers whose trade was story, lived amid the avoiding's opposite: the tale was in the air of the profession, retold in the tiring-house whether or not it was read in the study.

That saturation cuts both ways in the desk argument, and the honest weighing registers both cuts. It raises the prior probability of reading to near certainty, so that Evans's "highly probable" asks the evidence to clear only a low bar, which the forty hours and the name clear easily. It also lowers the diagnostic value of ambience: a detail might reach a playwright through conversation, through a rival's lost play on the same story, through the general stock of the tale as London repeated it, without any book changing hands. The lost-stage-version possibility, raised by Brooke's own preface mention of having seen the story acted, hangs over all source work in this dossier as a standing reminder that print is the surviving fraction of a culture that also transmitted by performance. The verdict of this study is therefore framed in terms of reading rather than of desk use, because reading is what the evidence, at its strongest, can reach: the prose tale was known to the playwright, most plausibly by reading, possibly refreshed by the common stock, and its two hardest traces survive in the play because memory keeps numbers and names when it drops narratives.

Painter's own profession adds a last shade to the picture. He was, as Levenson noted, the civil servant among the tale's tellers, and his book is a civil servant's monument: comprehensive, ordered, durable, built to be used by others. That is precisely the afterlife it had. Brooke's poem, for all its priority and its desk-side honours, was not reprinted after the sixteenth century's early decades with anything like the Palace's persistence in the trade; the prose collection remained the tale's standing English address. When later centuries went looking for the story Shakespeare found, they found Brooke because the scholars printed him; the Elizabethans had found Painter because the stationers did.

## The Verdict Stated Plainly

The claims can now be sorted by the strength of what supports them. That Shakespeare composed Romeo and Juliet with Brooke's poem as his working source is certain: the verbal echoes, the sequence, the retained inventions of the poem prove a desk relation beyond argument. That he had also read Painter's prose tale is highly probable: Evans's grading is the right one, carried by the potion's forty hours at the least against Brooke's three days and the play's two and forty, and by the hero's name in Painter's form against the Romeus of the poem he plundered. That he composed with Painter open beside Brooke is unproven and, on the leakage pattern, unlikely: an open second book would have left more than traces in a text that absorbed its first book so greedily. That Painter mattered to the story's English life independently of the playwright's desk is beyond dispute: the prose version was the tale's popular English form, the one the culture stocked, reprinted and remembered.

This sorting, and not any single slogan about two sources or one, is what the evidence buys. It also explains why the argument has lasted a century without resolution: the two camps have been answering different questions with the same documents. Asked what lay open during composition, the record answers Brooke. Asked what had been read and retained, it answers Brooke certainly and Painter with high probability. Critics who demand desk evidence for every claim find Painter a ghost; critics who grade reading by its traces find him a presence. The traces are real, the ghost is not, and the presence is a reader's, not a collaborator's. Painter stands to the play as the second book in a well-read man's memory of a story he loved before he remade it. Graded claims are less quotable than slogans, and a source study that ends in a grading rather than a banner may look like indecision; here the grading is the finding, the precise shape the surviving evidence takes when every tier of it is weighed and none is asked to bear more than it can carry.

## What Painter's Prose Added to the Story's English Life

Strip the desk question away and the brief's last demand remains: what did Painter's prose add to the English availability of the story? Four additions can be entered in the ledger with confidence.

The first is readability. Brooke's 3,020 lines of poulter's measure are a commitment; poulter's measure was already creaking in 1562 and has deterred readers in every century since. Painter's thirty pages of prose are an evening. The tale's passage from the poem's specialist audience to the general reader of novelle, the audience the playhouses were built from, ran through the prose. A story cannot become common stock while it is locked in a difficult metre, and the unlocking of Romeo and Juliet for the English imagination is Painter's doing at least as much as Brooke's.

The second addition is respectability of a new kind. Brooke's preface had presented the tale as a pill to be sugared, its lovers a warning, its friar a scandal. Painter's palace presented it as furniture for a gentleman's leisure, one chamber among Roman histories and Boccaccian comedies, dedicated into the households of the great. The tale under his roof could be read as the heading directs, as a history of true and constant love, edifying without being a sermon. That reframing mattered to the story's temperature in England: the prose naturalized sympathy for the lovers a generation before the play made sympathy irresistible, and Levenson's romance-and-adventure Painter is the agent of the change.

The third addition is preservation. Because Painter translated Boaistuau closely while Brooke expanded freely, the prose keeps the French original's proportions and some of its matter in a form collators can trust, making the English tradition legible to itself. When Evans remarks that Brooke gives essentially everything in Boaistuau, and hence in Painter, the hence is Painter's monument: the tale exists in English twice, and the second existence is the check on the first. Source study of this play would be poorer, and the desk question unaskable, without the prose control text.

The fourth addition is the profession's quarry. The forty-odd plays drawn from The Palace of Pleasure testify that Painter taught the English stage to feed on the novella, and Romeo and Juliet, the supreme product of that feeding, is unthinkable in a culture where the tale existed only as a moralized poem. Even if every Painter parallel in the play dissolved under scrutiny, the enabling condition would stand: Painter's book made stories like this one into the period's circulating capital, convertible at sight into drama. The play is the conversion at its richest.

Against these additions, the debits are small and should be recorded for honesty. Painter's prose is not beautiful; its sentences serve, they do not sing, and no one reads the tale now for its style. His fidelity is also a ceiling: he adds nothing to the story's meaning, where Brooke's expansions and Shakespeare's inventions added worlds. The prose version is the tradition's faithful clerk, and clerks are not remembered as authors. But faithfulness, availability and timing were what the story needed in 1567, and the clerk supplied all three. The verdict on Painter's addition is therefore not a consolation prize. Brooke gave the playwright his text; Painter gave England its tale.

## The Long Shelf: Painter After the Play

The Palace of Pleasure continued to be printed into the 1580s in its second tome's line, and the tale with it, so that the prose Romeo and Juliet and the stage Romeo and Juliet coexisted in the same bookstalls for the play's first audiences. Thereafter the collection's fortunes followed the novella's: as the seventeenth century's taste turned, the great storehouse came to seem old-fashioned, and Painter's name sank into the footnotes of the dramatists he had fed. The tale survived by changing carriers, through the play's own texts first, and then through the prose chapbooks and summaries that descend, by a long irony, from the novella tradition Painter represented: the story England would not stop retelling in prose had learned its prose habits in part from him.

Scholarship recovered him slowly. The nineteenth-century reprints of the Palace, and Joseph Jacobs's edition at the century's end, put the tales back into circulation for scholars; Bullough's 1957 volume gave the Romeo and Juliet novella its modern scholarly form, printed entire beside Brooke's poem, and set the terms on which the two English versions are still compared. Every subsequent editor, Evans and Levenson and Weis among them, has worked in Bullough's architecture even when adjusting its verdicts. The clerk of the ordnance, charged in his lifetime with making Crown money vanish, has had the last institutional laugh: his book is the one piece of the ordnance office's paperwork that the world still files under literature.

This study is the two hundredth in its series, and it is fitting that the landmark falls on Painter. The first hundred and ninety-nine have circled a story that reached its playwright down a chain of tellers, Italian, French and English, and the chain's most undervalued link is the prose translator who asked for no credit beyond his office and his industry. To restore him is not to demote Brooke, whose poem earned the desk. It is to complete the record of how a story travels: by genius at the end of the line, certainly, but before that by clerks, translators and gatherers, keeping the tale in stock, in print and in prose, against the day the genius should come into the shop.

## Painter's English: The Sentence, the Heading and the Address

A translator's fingerprints are in his small furniture, and Painter's furniture is distinctive. Each tale in the Palace arrives under a heading that is half title, half advertisement: the goodly history of the true and constant love between Rhomeo and Iulietta advertises virtue, fact and pathos before the first sentence is read. Many tales also carry a brief argument or address in which Painter steps forward, commends the example, warns where warning is due, and retires. These addresses are the prose book's equivalent of Brooke's preface, with the volume turned down: Brooke's address is a sermon that tries to convict the tale before it is told; Painter's are ushers' remarks, seating the reader comfortably and trusting the story to do its own work. The difference is generic as much as personal. A poem claiming descent from Chaucerian tragedy needed a moral passport in 1562; a palace of pleasure in 1567 sold delight first and let instruction ride as the passenger it claimed, in the period's formula, to be: profit folded inside pleasure.

His sentences have the clerk's virtues. They are long by modern measure, built on balanced clauses and semicoloned inventories, and they keep their subjects in view through thickets of subordination that would wreck a lesser stylist. In narrative they move at a walk that can break, at the tomb or the duel, into a genuine trot of short clauses. In speech they dilate, as noted earlier, into set orations. What they never do is glitter. Painter has no equivalent of Brooke's occasional startling image or the poem's addresses to Fortune, those apostrophes in which the verse narrator wheels on the goddess and arraigns her. The prose narrator does not arraign; he records, and his record's calm is precisely what makes his tale the collator's control text and the general reader's easy chair.

The handling of names shows the same serviceable intelligence. Through Boaistuau, Painter inherits the French spellings, and he naturalizes them just enough for English mouths: Rhomeo and Iulietta, the Montegues and the Capellets, Theobaldo the hot kinsman, Count Paris the suitor, Friar Laurence the Franciscan. Brooke's Romeus is a schoolboy's Latinizing; Painter's Rhomeo is a traveller's Italian by way of Paris. The play's Romeo completes the journey Painter's spelling began, shedding the French h and the Italian ending together. It is a small thing, a name, but source study is the discipline of small things weighed exactly, and this one sits in the scales on Painter's side.

His omissions are as telling as his inclusions. Painter does not preach against friars, though his Friar Laurence is as meddlesome as Brooke's; the anti-mendicant thunder of the poem's preface, a piece of 1562's confessional weather, finds no echo in the prose of 1567, whose maker served a regime that had finished its quarrel with the old religion and preferred its Italian tales without a Protestant gloss. Nor does Painter linger, as Brooke does, over the moral arithmetic of the ending, the exact apportioning of blame. His judgments on the survivors are reported as facts of the story, sentences passed by Duke Escalus in the fiction, rather than verdicts thundered by the narrator over it. The tale's meaning is left to accumulate in the reader, a trust the play would repay by abolishing the narrator altogether.

## Objections Heard: The Strongest Counter-Cases

A verdict that cannot state the other side's best arguments has not earned itself, and the Painter case has three objections worth their full weight.

The first concerns the forty hours. A dramatist compressing a three-day sleep into a plot that runs from Sunday to Thursday does not need a book to tell him that the sleep must shrink; the architecture of his own five days dictates a term nearer two days than three, and forty-two is the kind of figure a writer lands on by counting backward from a Friday funeral. The objection is real but weaker than it looks. Compression explains a shorter sleep; it does not explain this sleep's number, and the number in the prose book is not two days but forty hours at the least, an unround figure with no architectural necessity behind it, sitting within two hours of the play's. Coincidence must posit that Shakespeare independently generated a near-identical unround number for the same narrative slot that Painter, translating the shared original, had already filled with it. Possible; not parsimonious.

The second objection concerns the name. Romeo might have reached the playwright through the culture's Italianate chatter, through a lost stage version of the tale, through the general currency of the Italian story in a London full of Italian teachers and travellers, without Painter's page intervening. This is true and cuts the name's value as proof of reading Painter specifically. But it cuts equally against every channel's rivals and leaves the field where it was: the form Romeus stood in the book Shakespeare demonstrably used, and the form Romeo stood in the other English book, the famous one, the reprinted one. Of the attested printed channels, Painter's is the only English one carrying the play's spelling.

The third objection is the deepest, because it concerns method. Source study's history is littered with parallelomania, with source claims built on resemblances that dissolve into the period's common stock, and the discipline's severity toward Painter is partly a scar from those fevers. Evans's handful survives severity because its two hard items are the right kind of evidence, a number and a name, arbitrary and checkable, rather than the wrong kind, a mood, a theme, a situation. The counter-case, at its strongest, thus ends up policing the verdict's size rather than its existence: it forbids the promotion of Painter to co-source, which this study has not proposed, and it leaves standing the modest, graded claim that the evidence supports, a book read, remembered, and twice betrayed by the play's smallest details.

## The Company the Tale Keeps: The Second Tome's Neighbours

Context is a form of evidence about how a book was read, and Painter's tale was read in company. The second tome is the Palace's tragic wing: tale after tale Englished from the French tragical histories, themselves drawn from Bandello's Novelle, stories of poisoned wives, revenged seductions, tyrants, duels and unfortunate loves, each closing its account with death and a moral shrug. Romeo and Julietta stands twenty-fifth in that company, and the company fixes its first readers' expectations before the heading is finished: this is a histoire tragique, a machine for exemplary catastrophe, not a romance idyll. Painter's own addresses in the tome play the same note, commending the tales as mirrors of fortune's mutability. The lovers, in their first English prose home, were shelved among the cautionary dead.

That shelving explains a feature of the tale's English reception that Brooke's preface had already announced from the other side. Both English versions present the story under moral guard, the poem by sermon, the prose by neighbourhood, and the play's abolition of the guard is its founding act of interpretation: Shakespeare's Chorus promises "the fearful passage of their death-marked love" in the Prologue and then lets the lovers charm their way out from under every warning the tradition posted over them. Knowing the tale's shelf in Painter, among the poisoners and the revenged, sharpens the sense of what the play risked. It staged a cautionary tale as a love story and made the audience complicit in the lovers' disobedience, a transformation prepared, ironically, by the prose version's own hospitality: Painter had already let sympathy in, by the side door of pleasure, while the guards were watching the gate.

The first tome's company completes the picture of the translator's range. Livy and Herodotus stand there beside Boccaccio and the Queen of Navarre; Roman sieges neighbour French gallantries; the palace is a museum of the whole novella and history trade of the century. The man who Englished Romeus's story for the second tome had spent the first tome proving he could English anything, and the collection's unity is the unity of his desk. When the play later drew its Italian plots from the same palace for other tragedies and comedies of love and intrigue, it was paying Painter's compilation the compliment his century had designed it to earn: the book had become infrastructure, and infrastructure is invisible precisely when it works.

## A Page of Painter: The Opening Movements

Open the prose tale at its first page and the method is on display before the lovers appear. Painter begins where the tradition begins, with the city and its sickness: Verona under its duke, the two great houses named, the feud stated as a condition of public life rather than a backdrop. The exposition is a clerk's minute of the case, who these families are, how their hatred stands, what the city has suffered from it, and it performs in a page what the play's Prologue performs in fourteen lines, "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue). Where the Chorus compresses the feud into a sonnet's abstractions, ancient grudge and civil blood, Painter particularizes: brawls, partisans, a citizenry armed by allegiance. His Rhomeo enters the narrative already placed inside that machinery, a young gentleman of the Montegues whose first love, the hard-hearted lady he serves hopelessly, is given the full Petrarchan weather of sighs and disdain that the play will compress into Romeo's borrowed idiom in its opening scenes.

The feast then arrives with the deliberateness of prose that knows it has thirty pages and means to spend them. The Capellets' house is prepared, the guests assemble, the torchbearers and maskers enter, and Rhomeo's eye makes its famous exchange of objects. Painter dwells on the physiology of the moment with the novella's patience: the first love is not argued away but extinguished, as a candle is, by the arrival of a greater light in the same room, and the narrative pauses to register the physiology in the period's terms, the spirits, the eyes as messengers, the heart changing tenants. Brooke's couplets hurry the same exchange toward the dance; the play abolishes the interval altogether, letting Romeo see Juliet and ask her name of a servingman in the same breath, "What lady is that, which doth enrich the hand / Of yonder knight?" (1.5). Three media, three tempi, one event: the comparison is the whole source question in miniature, because everything the play does to the tradition is already visible in the way it refuses Painter's leisure.

The early pages also establish the prose tale's characteristic moral temperature. Painter does not excuse the lovers and does not arraign them; he exhibits them, and his narrator's rare interventions have the tone of a man who has read many such histories and knows how they end. That tone, adult, unshocked, quietly providential, is neither Bandello's courtly sympathy nor Brooke's Protestant alarm, and it is the tone in which the English reading public actually consumed the story for thirty years. When scholars ask what the tale meant before Shakespeare, the honest answer is not the poem's meaning or the play's but this prose meaning: a goodly history of constancy destroyed by fortune and family, to be read with pity and filed under warning, in a palace built for the purpose.

## Did Painter Read Italian? The French Fingerprint

Whether Painter ever worked directly from Bandello's Italian for other tales in the Palace is a question his whole bibliography raises; for the Romeo and Juliet novella the answer is written in the text, and it is no. The fingerprints of the French intermediary are on every page. His spellings are French spellings, Rhomeo and Iulietta filtered through Boaistuau's Roméo and Juliette rather than taken from Bandello's Romeo and Giulietta at first hand. His plot includes the business Boaistuau invented or developed: the worked-out restraint of the Capellets when the intruder is discovered at the feast, the heroine's dilemma elaborated on the news of the duel, the apothecary grown from a function into a character with a poverty to plead. Above all, his ending is the French ending. Bandello's heroine dies by ceasing to breathe after a spoken farewell; Boaistuau replaced that ending with the waking after death and the dagger, and Painter, like Brooke, reproduces the replacement. A translator working from the Italian would have had the held breath in front of him. Painter has the dagger, because his page was French.

The point is not pedantry. The French fingerprint is what makes the two English versions siblings rather than cousins, and siblings are what the desk question requires them to be: only because Brooke and Painter share one parent can their divergences be used as a discriminating test, and only because they diverge so seldom is the test so nearly empty of results. It also fixes Painter's place in the stemma with an exactness his modesty would have approved. He is not an independent witness to the Italian tradition; he is the French tradition's English voice for this tale, as Brooke is its English voice in verse, and the play is the child of the household the two of them kept.

There is a corollary for the lost Italian question that editors sometimes raise about Shakespeare himself. If the playwright had read Bandello in Italian, the held-breath ending and the spoken farewell were there to tempt a dramatist who never elsewhere refused a strong theatrical effect; the play instead keeps the French machinery entire, the miscarried letter, the dagger, the waking too late. The transmission that reached the stage was the French transmission, in its English dress, and of its two English dresses the prose was the one cut for the general reader.

## Why the Cliché Forgot Him: Provability and the Brooke Monopoly

If the evidence for Painter's presence in the play's ancestry is as real as this study has argued, why does the cliché remember Brooke alone? The answer lies in an asymmetry built into the discipline itself. Source study canonizes what it can prove, and proof, in its strictest form, is verbal echo. Brooke can be proved on every page; Painter can be proved, at most, in two details. Survey chapters, examination syllabi and edition introductions therefore converge on the provable book, and the unprovable one dwindles into a subordinate clause: Painter is the "also available" of a thousand footnotes, named for completeness and dismissed in the same breath. The cliché is not an error. It is a selection effect, the fossil of a method.

Bullough's great volume both corrected and confirmed the monopoly. By printing Painter entire, Bullough made the prose tale permanently available to scholarship, an act of restoration for which this study, like all others, is in his debt. By ranking him second and doubtful, the same volume fixed the terms on which he would be available: as the control, the alternative, the might-have-been. Editors after Bullough inherited the architecture. Evans warmed the verdict to highly probable; Weis held the centre; the classroom kept the poem. Each generation's Painter is exactly as large as its tolerance for unprovable knowledge, and the tolerance, in a discipline burned by parallelomania, runs low.

There is also a matter of literary prestige working quietly against the clerk. Brooke, however tedious his metre, is a poet, and poems attract the kind of attention literary history is organized to give; Painter is a translator of other men's tales, a compiler, a civil servant with a sideline, and translation has always been the invisible trade of English letters. The play's own mythology prefers a single poetic source, a young dramatist transmuting a dull poem by genius, to the truer and less romantic picture of a professional reader working a stocked shelf. Restoring Painter is thus a small act of justice against two prejudices at once: the prejudice for the provable and the prejudice for the poet. The record, read without either, shows a story carried into English twice in five years, and a playwright who, in all probability, read both carriers.

## The Translator's Century: Painter Among the Englishers

Painter belonged to the great age of Englishing, the decades when the Tudor translation enterprise carried the whole of European and classical literature into the vernacular at industrial pace: Golding's Ovid, North's Plutarch, Hoby's Courtier, Phaer's Virgil, and dozens of lesser hands filing the classics and the novelle into English dress. The Palace of Pleasure is that enterprise's story department, and Painter's prefaces breathe its ideology entire: foreign books are treasure, translation is enrichment of the realm's stock, and the translator is a merchant adventurer of letters, importing wares the home market cannot grow. The Romeo and Juliet novella arrived in England as part of that import trade, one bale among hundreds, and its unremarkable arrival is part of its significance. By 1567 the tale did not need a patron's miracle to cross the Channel; it needed only a clerk with French, a printer with capital, and a public the trade itself had created.

The comparison with Brooke's position in the same enterprise is telling. Brooke was a gentleman amateur of the older kind, translating for honour and warning; Painter was the new professional of the book trade, translating for a market. The play's generation inherited the market's world: stories as stock, plots as currency, the novella as the small change of the drama. When the young Shakespeare went shopping for a tragedy of love, he shopped in a market Painter had helped to build, whether or not he opened the particular volume at the particular moment. That is the broadest and safest form of the prose book's influence, and it needs no parallels at all: Painter's Palace is one of the machines that made a play like Romeo and Juliet commercially thinkable.

## The Penumbra Weighed: The Apothecary's Shop and Other Near-Misses

Between the two hard details and the mass of inherited agreement lies the penumbra, the set of passages where the play has been felt to stand nearer Painter than Brooke without quite standing on him. The apothecary's shop is the type specimen. When Romeo describes the Mantuan shop in the play, he inventories it like a prose narrator: "In his needy shop a tortoise hung, / An alligator stuff'd, and other skins / Of ill-shaped fishes; and about his shelves / A beggarly account of empty boxes" (5.1). Boaistuau had developed the apothecary for the tradition, and both Englishmen inherit the development, Brooke in verse and Painter in prose whose cadence the passage has been thought to recall. But inheritance explains the shop's existence, and the play's inventory is its own dramatization of poverty into scenery; the resemblance grades as atmosphere, not evidence, and this study files it accordingly.

The tomb speeches make a second penumbra case. Romeo's address over the body in the play expands the tradition's lament into the great valediction of the fifth act; Painter's Romeo likewise speaks at a length Brooke's couplets curtail, and readers collating the three texts have repeatedly felt the prose's proportions nearer the play's amplitude. Proportions, however, are the weakest currency in the source market: a dramatist expanding a lament needs no prose model to expand it, and the play's words are nowhere Painter's words. The penumbra's honest use is corroborative only. It cannot found the claim that Shakespeare read the prose tale; held in the hand with the forty hours and the name, it thickens the sense of a familiarity that the hard details have already established. Source study goes wrong when the penumbra is asked to bear weight; it goes right when the penumbra is allowed to do what it can, which is to make a proved reading feel like the reading experience it was.

## The Second Tome's Architecture: Addresses Between the Tales

A story collection is an argument made by arrangement, and Painter's second tome argues by juxtaposition and by voice. Between the tales stand his own addresses, brief prose thresholds in which the gatherer commends the coming example, draws its moral in advance, or promises the reader a change of key. These thresholds are easy to skip and fatal to skip, because they are where the collection thinks. In them Painter appears as the period's ideal reader of his own book: grave over the tragedies, delighted by the comedies of the first tome, always returning to the twin formula of profit and pleasure, always insisting that the foreign dead have something to say to the English living. The Romeo and Juliet novella is prefaced and framed in that voice, and its first audience therefore met the lovers already interpreted, as an example in a gallery of examples, before a word of the story proper was read.

The tome's order makes a second argument. Bandello's matter dominates, and within it the tragical histories cluster, so that the book darkens as it proceeds from its classical and comic holdings toward the Italian catastrophes. The lovers' tale, standing twenty-fifth, is read by its first audience in a context of accumulated disaster: the palace's later chambers are hung with corpses, and the goodly history takes its place in a gallery where true and constant love is one exhibit among the era's many machines for dying young. Shakespeare's play, read against that shelf, performs a rescue the prose never attempted: it carries the tale out of the gallery of examples and into the open air of the stage, where the lovers stop being a warning with names and become the standard by which warnings are judged.

## What a Modern Reader Finds in the Prose Tale

Read now, for its own sake and not for the play's, Painter's novella rewards the visit in ways the source dossier never mentions. Its Julietta is a fuller speaking presence than the tradition usually allows: the prose gives her laments, deliberations and farewells at an oratorical length that makes her the tale's rhetorical centre, and a reader coming from the play will recognize the lineaments of Shakespeare's heroine more clearly here than in Brooke's busier, more mediated heroine. Its Romeo is correspondingly the Petrarchan apprentice of the Italian tradition, sighing over the first hard-hearted lady in set speeches whose very conventionally is the point: the prose shows the mould the play would break. And its Friar, prudent, learned and fatally competent, is drawn with an administrator's respect for expertise that neither Brooke's Protestant suspicion nor the play's dramatic irony quite shares.

The tale also preserves, as no summary can, the felt duration of the story as the sixteenth century experienced it: months of courtship at the window, a marriage with a season to breathe in, a catastrophe assembled slowly out of quarantines and miscarried paper. The play's five days are a violence done to that duration, and the violence is only measurable against the prose's patience. For the student of the play, that may be Painter's most practical gift in the end: not a source to be cited but a tempo to be felt, the long Italian afternoon of the story against which Shakespeare's English week burns like a fuse. The two hundredth study of the series can leave him with that: the clerk who gave the tale its English prose, its English patience, and, in two small hard details, its English playwright's memory.

## The Counterfactual: The Story Without Painter

Source study rarely permits itself the counterfactual, but the Painter question earns one, because it separates the kinds of influence that the debate habitually fuses. Suppose the second tome had never appeared: Brooke's poem still stands in 1562, the French and Italian versions still circulate among the bilingual, and the young Shakespeare still finds his tragedy. The play that results is, in its skeleton, recognizably the play that exists, since the skeleton is Brooke's gift entire. The differences gather at the extremities. The hero is likelier Romeus in the culture's mouth, however the dramatist spelled him. The potion's sleep is likelier three days awkwardly compressed, or some figure of the dramatist's own arithmetic rather than the prose book's forty hours. The tale's English readership is smaller, more Latinate, more poetic; the story is a poem's property rather than a parlour's, and the playhouse audience meets a less familiar Verona, its recognition thinner, its patience for the story's machinery less practised by prose acquaintance.

None of those differences is a scene, a character or a speech, and the counterfactual therefore confirms the verdict from the other side. Painter's contribution to the play is real and located entirely in the category of the enabling and the retained: availability, familiarity, a name, a number, a tempo in the culture's ear. Contributions of that category are exactly the ones source study is worst at seeing, because its instruments are built to detect the transfer of matter, and enabling conditions transfer no matter at all. A criticism that can only count what crossed the desk will always undercount the books that built the room the desk stood in. The Palace of Pleasure built a large part of that room for the English drama of love and intrigue, and its twenty-fifth tale furnished the corner where the greatest of those dramas was composed.

The counterfactual also disciplines the enthusiasm the prose book can excite in its restorers. To say that England's tale was Painter's is not to say that the play was, and the distinction protects both books. Brooke's poem remains what the echoes prove it to be, the quarry of the composition; the play remains what its language proves it to be, an original work that no source accounts for. Painter stands between them in the humbler and more durable office his whole career rehearsed: the officer who kept the stores, issued the materiel, and left no signature on the victory. Ordnance clerks are not generals. Armies, however, do not march without them, and stories do not conquer stages until clerks have carried them across the water and entered them, tale by tale, in the national stock.

## The Name on the Title Page

The smallest exhibit in the dossier deserves its own frame, because title pages are where books announce their lineage to the trade. Brooke's volume of 1562 announces "The tragicall historye of Romeus and Iuliet, written first in Italian by Bandell, and nowe in Englishe" by its translator: Romeus, the Latinized hero, heads the English tradition's first title. Painter's tale, having no title page of its own, announces itself in its heading inside the second tome: the true and constant love between Rhomeo and Iulietta, the Italian name restored through French spelling. When the play reached print in the quarto of 1597, its title page read "An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Iuliet": Romeo, not Romeus. The spelling of a hero's name is a triviality until it is a tracer, and as a tracer it behaves exactly as the forty hours does. The book the playwright demonstrably worked from called the man one thing; the play calls him what the other book called him.

Scepticism has its answer ready, and the answer should be recorded: titles are publishers' furniture as much as authors', quartos are shop products, and the Italian name was in the London air through channels no bibliography can now enumerate. All true, and all consistent with the graded form of the claim. The name does not prove that Painter's volume lay open at any scene's composition. It indicates that the prose book's version of the man, the Rhomeo of the goodly history, had reached the imagination that named the play, by the page or by the culture the page had saturated. For a study whose verdict distinguishes reading from desk work, an indication aimed at reading is evidence of precisely the right weight.

## The Duke, the Watch and the Golden Statues: Painter's Civic Ending

The prose tale ends, as the tradition taught it to end, in the forum rather than the tomb. Painter's closing movement is civic bookkeeping conducted at full length: the watch's discovery, the Duke's examination, the Friar's confession unfolding the whole machinery before the assembled houses, the sentences on the apothecary, the Nurse and the Friar, and then the reconciliation, in which the bereaved fathers compete in commemoration and promise the golden statues that will keep the lovers' fame in Verona while the city keeps its peace. The prose lingers over the settlement with an administrator's satisfaction in a file closed and a city rebalanced: the tale's final interest is not the lovers, who are beyond interest, but the polity that survives them and must now be governed.

The play keeps the forum and changes its physics. Shakespeare's Prince closes the action with the tradition's materials, the examination, the confession, the promised statues, Montague vowing Juliet's image in pure gold and Capulet answering with Romeo's (5.3), but the civic satisfaction is gone: the closing couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3), weighs the settlement and finds it worthless beside the loss. Reading Painter's ending after the play's is the sharpest way to feel what dramatization did to narration in this dossier. The prose believes in the settlement and reports it faithfully; the play reports the same settlement and disbelieves it in the same breath. Between those two relations to identical facts lies the whole distance from source to work of art, and it is the distance Painter's fidelity, paradoxically, makes measurable: because his account is complete and calm, the play's disturbance of the same account can be charted to the line.

## Shared Errors and Shared Inventions: The Stemmatic Test

Textual criticism possesses a sterner instrument than resemblance, and the Painter dossier is a clean place to watch it work. In the stemmatic method, descent is proved not by agreements any copyist might produce but by shared innovations, the distinctive departures, even errors, that one witness introduces and another reproduces. Applied to sources, the test asks whether the play ever reproduces a departure that is Painter's own, an invention of the English prose with no counterpart in Boaistuau or in Brooke. Such a find would settle the desk question outright, because a Painter-only invention in the play could have arrived by no other road than Painter's page.

The test has been run, implicitly, by a century of collators, and its result explains the debate's shape. No Painter-only invention of substance has been produced. His amplifications of the laments and the tomb, the places where his hand is freest, leave no verbal deposit in the play; his fidelity elsewhere means his text seldom departs from the French in ways Brooke does not match. The forty hours survives the test only in a weakened form, since the figure stands in the line of the French tradition's own numbers rather than as a demonstrable Painterism, and the name Rhomeo is a spelling, not an invention. The stemmatic verdict is therefore negative, and the negative must be given its full force: by the strictest standard the discipline owns, Painter's desk use is not merely unproven but unprovable from the surviving texts, because the prose book interposes no unique, checkable matter between the French original and the play.

Yet the same test, honestly read, also shows why the negative settles less than it appears to. The stemmatic instrument detects the copying of texts, and reading is not copying. A book may shape a writer's sense of a story, supply the two details memory keeps, and still contribute no unique invention for the instrument to catch, because what it contributed was emphasis, tempo and familiarity rather than matter. The history of the question is the history of critics mistaking the instrument's silence for the fact of absence, from the older line's unconsulted to every textbook that names Brooke alone. The instrument is silent about reading. The reading, on the graded evidence, happened anyway.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Painter comparison, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch Painter comparison sets Painter's prose novella of 1567 beside Brooke's poem of 1562, feature by feature: form, source, the hero's name, the narrator's stance, the feast, the potion's term, the failed letter, the tomb, the heroine's death, the apothecary and the sentences on the survivors. For each feature it adds a third column recording where the play stands. Its purpose is diagnostic. Because both English books translate Boaistuau, their agreements are inherited and prove nothing; their disagreements are the only places where the play's allegiance can be tested, and the comparison isolates exactly those rows.

### Q: Who was William Painter, and what office did he hold in the Tower of London?

William Painter, who lived from about 1540 to 1595, was a civil servant and translator, clerk of the ordnance in the Tower of London, the office charged with the Crown's artillery, powder and military stores. The post gave him standing, a salary and the ear of the soldiers and statesmen to whom he dedicated his books. It also ended his career in disgrace, when he was charged with peculation, the embezzlement of funds passing through his hands. His fame rests entirely on his translation work, above all on The Palace of Pleasure, the great story collection he assembled from classical, Italian and French sources.

### Q: When did the two tomes of The Palace of Pleasure appear, and which one holds the lovers' story?

The first tome appeared in 1566 and the second in 1567. The first, dedicated to Ambrose Dudley, Earl of Warwick, gathered some sixty tales from historians, Boccaccio, Bandello, Marguerite de Navarre and others. The second tome is longer and darker, weighted toward the tragical histories Englished from the French, with more than half its tales from Bandello. The story of Romeo and Julietta stands in the second tome, as its twenty-fifth tale, in the form of a prose novella of some thirty pages. Shakespeare's generation thus met the tale in a current, reprinted collection, not in an antiquarian survival.

### Q: What heading does Painter give the tale, and what does its wording promise?

Painter heads the tale "The goodly hystory of the true, and constant Love between Rhomeo and Iulietta". Each adjective makes a promise. Goodly pledges edification, that the story will do the reader moral good. True pledges fact, presenting the tale as history rather than invention, in the manner of the French tragical histories. Constant names the virtue the narrative will exhibit and destroy, the lovers' steadfastness under fortune and family. The spelling is also evidence: Rhomeo, not Brooke's Romeus, stands at the head of the English prose tradition, and the play's Romeo completes the journey that spelling began.

### Q: Why did Painter translate from French when the story was Italian?

Painter worked from the text that the English trade had made current: Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques of 1559, the French rendering of Bandello's novella. His competence and his market were both French-facing; the first tome had already drawn heavily on French intermediaries, and Boaistuau's version had effectively become the story's European standard text, tighter and more dramatic than Bandello's. Brooke had done the same thing five years earlier, despite a title page claiming the Italian. Translation in the period followed the available exemplar, not the ultimate original, and for the Verona story the available exemplar was French.

### Q: How close is Painter's translation to Boaistuau's French?

Close and generally accurate, in the Cambridge editor G. Blakemore Evans's summary. Episode follows episode and speech follows speech, and collators checking the English against the French find a translator keeping faith with his original's sequence and substance. The closeness is not mechanical. Within the licence Tudor translation allowed, Painter dilates at the pressure points, expanding laments, counsels and the tomb farewell into fuller set speeches than the French strictly contains. The skeleton is Boaistuau's entire; the rhetorical flesh at the crises is Painter's amplification. That combination, fidelity in structure and freedom in proportion, defines his method throughout the Palace.

### Q: How long does Juliet's sleep last in Painter, and how does the figure compare with Brooke and with the play?

Painter gives the sleeping draught a term of forty hours at the least. Brooke's poem gives three days, following the longer span of the Italian tradition. The play's Friar specifies two and forty hours before Juliet shall awake as from a pleasant sleep (4.1). The comparison makes the play's figure the most discussed number in the source dossier: it is far from Brooke's three days and within two hours of Painter's unround figure. Compression alone would explain a shorter sleep; it does not explain why the shortened term should land so near the prose book's number, and that nearness is the strongest single detail suggesting Shakespeare had read Painter.

### Q: Do Romeo and Julietta speak together in Painter's tomb?

No. Painter follows Boaistuau's amended ending, in which Julietta wakes after Romeo is already dead, so no farewell conversation passes between the lovers in the monument. She finds the body and the empty cup, understands the whole catastrophe in silence and a brief lament, and kills herself with his dagger. The speaking farewell belongs to the Italian versions: in da Porto and Bandello she revives while Romeo still lives, they exchange last words, and she dies by simply ceasing to breathe. The French line, and both English versions after it, traded that conversation for the dagger and the crueller timing Shakespeare inherited and sharpened.

### Q: How does Julietta die in Painter's version?

She dies by Romeo's dagger, in the manner Boaistuau had substituted for the Italian ending. Waking in the monument to find Romeo dead beside her, she takes his weapon and stabs herself, and is found so by the watch. The detail matters because it aligns both English versions against their Italian ancestors: da Porto's and Bandello's heroine dies by holding her breath, an act of will without a weapon, while the French, and Painter and Brooke after the French, give her the dagger. Shakespeare's "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath" (5.3) therefore descends from the French amendment, whichever English book carried it to him.

### Q: What place does Bullough's edition assign to Painter among the play's sources?

Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, first published in 1957, prints Brooke's poem and Painter's tale entire in its opening volume and assigns them unequal ranks. Brooke is the play's direct and principal source, the working text behind its sequence and its phrases. Painter is a possible subsidiary: a version Shakespeare may have consulted, whose agreements with the play are mostly explicable as inheritances from the French original both Englishmen translated. Bullough's architecture performs his verdict, the poem first and full, the tale second and slighter, and that settlement became the discipline's default frame for the next half-century of argument.

### Q: What did Kenneth Muir add to Bullough's settlement of the question?

Kenneth Muir, in Shakespeare's Sources, kept Bullough's map and loosened its border. Surveying the versions of the tale, he recorded slight indications that Shakespeare may have read, besides Brooke, other tellings as well, Painter's among them, and he treated that possibility as a live one rather than a courtesy. The difference from Bullough is of temperature, not geography: both critics place Brooke open on the desk during composition. Muir simply leaves the study door wider open to the corridor, allowing that a playwright of Shakespeare's appetite read around his main source, and that reading of that kind can leave traces without leaving a pattern.

### Q: Who states the case for Shakespeare knowing Painter most strongly?

G. Blakemore Evans, in his Cambridge edition of the play, states it most strongly among careful editors. His formulation separates the two questions the debate often confuses: Shakespeare worked directly with Brooke's poem, for verbal echoes resound throughout the play; that he knew Painter's prose version is highly probable; and Painter's influence, beyond four or five suggestive and scattered details, remains slight. One of the details Evans instances hangs on the prose book's very title, Rhomeo against Brooke's Romeus. His verdict, knowledge affirmed and influence minimized in the same breath, is the strongest form of the Painter case precisely because it concedes so much to Brooke.

### Q: What did Jill Levenson notice about the tempers of the two English retellings?

Jill L. Levenson noticed that each English teller stamps his station on the tale. Brooke, the gentleman poet, rationalizes the story in the direction of control, making its social disorder more governable, preaching at the lovers from his preface even while his verse pities them. Painter, the civil servant, brings out instead the elements of romance and adventure, filing the tale in a best-selling palace of stories and letting sympathy accumulate without a sermon. The observation relocates the debate: whatever Shakespeare's desk held, England's two versions had already split the story into a warning and a romance, and the play inherits the tension between them.

### Q: Why does a detail the play shares with Brooke as well as Painter prove nothing about Painter?

Because both English versions translate the same French book. Any incident, speech or arrangement that stands in Boaistuau will normally stand in Brooke and in Painter too, so its presence in the play is fully explained by the known desk source, Brooke. Proof of Painter requires discrimination: a place where the two Englishmen diverge, where Painter keeps something Brooke drops or renders differently, and where the play sides with Painter. Such places are rare, because Brooke's poem is so full that it gives essentially everything in the French. The rarity is why the surviving candidates, a number and a name, are argued over so fiercely.

### Q: How many editions of The Palace of Pleasure appeared before 1600, and why does the count matter?

Three editions of the first tome and two of the second had appeared before the century turned. The count matters because availability is the silent premise of every source claim: a playwright cannot use a book he cannot obtain, and a book reprinted five times across thirty years, stocked by stationers and excerpted by rivals, was obtainable everywhere. Painter's publication history converts the question from whether Shakespeare could have read the tale to whether anything could have prevented a professional story-hunter of the 1590s from reading it. Availability cannot prove use, but it sets the odds against which the two hard details, the forty hours and the hero's name, are weighed.

### Q: How many later plays drew their plots from The Palace of Pleasure?

More than forty Elizabethan and Jacobean plays have been traced to plots in Painter's collection. The figure measures his real historical function: not an author competing for laurels but a supplier of narrative capital to an entire dramatic generation. The Palace taught the English stage to feed on the novella, Italian stories Englished in prose and converted at sight into drama. Romeo and Juliet is the supreme instance of that conversion, and its supremacy has obscured the general phenomenon. Painter's book stands behind the period's theatre the way infrastructure stands behind a city, everywhere assumed and rarely named.

### Q: Why do collators use Painter as a control text for measuring Brooke's departures from Boaistuau?

Because Painter translated Boaistuau closely while Brooke expanded, compressed and rearranged freely. Where a passage in the poem puzzles collators, the prose version, keeping the Frenchman's sequence and proportions, shows what stood in the shared original before the poet's hand went to work. The control runs in both directions: Painter's fidelity exposes Brooke's inventions, and Brooke's fullness exposes the few places where the prose compresses. Without the second English version, Brooke's departures from the French would be far harder to isolate, and the play's own departures from Brooke harder still to grade.

### Q: What happens to the Friar, the Nurse and the apothecary at the end of Painter's version?

Painter, following the French tradition, closes the tale with formal sentences on the surviving instruments of the plot. The apothecary who sold the poison is hanged for the sale. The Nurse, whose brokerage made the marriage and the intrigue possible, is banished from Verona. Friar Laurence, his part confessed, retires to a hermitage to end his days in penitence. The distribution is judicial and complete, an account settled item by item. Shakespeare dissolves all three sentences into the Prince's general reckoning over the bodies, where punishment is replaced by shared loss and the famous closing couplet.

### Q: How should an essay use the Painter comparison to argue about the play's two English sources?

An essay should begin from the comparison's third column and work only with the discriminating rows. It should concede at once that plot agreement proves nothing, since both English books translate Boaistuau, and that Brooke's priority as working source is settled by hundreds of verbal echoes. It should then build on the details where the English versions diverge and the play sides with Painter: the potion's forty hours at the least against Brooke's three days and the play's two and forty, and the hero's name in Painter's form against the poem's Romeus. The conclusion those rows support is graded, not absolute: Shakespeare worked from Brooke and had very probably read Painter.

### Q: What did Painter's prose give English readers that Brooke's poem could not?

It gave them the story in an evening rather than a siege. Brooke's 3,020 lines of poulter's measure, already a creaking metre in 1562, confined the tale to readers with a taste for long moralized verse. Painter's thirty pages of serviceable prose, shelved in the century's most successful story collection and reprinted for thirty years, made Romeo and Julietta common property: a novella for the parlour as well as the study, read by the very public that filled the playhouses. Brooke gave the playwright his working text. Painter gave England the tale itself, in the form in which a whole culture could keep it.
