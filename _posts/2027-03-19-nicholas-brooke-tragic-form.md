## Nicholas Brooke and the Tragic Form: The Verdict First

Nicholas Brooke's Shakespeare's Early Tragedies, published by Methuen in 1968, contains the most consequential single claim ever made about the form of Romeo and Juliet: the play's mixture of lyric, comic and tragic modes is a deliberate experiment, designed and patterned, and the long critical habit of treating that mixture as the unevenness of an apprentice is a mistake about what kind of object the play is. Brooke placed the play in a group of six early tragedies, Titus Andronicus, Richard III, Romeo and Juliet, Richard II, Julius Caesar and Hamlet, and argued that the group's real interest lies in its variety. Each play makes a different kind of tragic experience. The belief in a single tragic experience, one pattern against which every tragedy is measured and found complete or wanting, had obscured that variety for decades, and Romeo and Juliet had paid the highest price for the obscurity, because no play in the group departs further from the pattern that A. C. Bradley had canonized in Shakespearean Tragedy. Brooke's verdict reverses the charge. The departure is the design.

![The Capulet orchard and tomb as emblematic stage pictures in Brooke's reading of the tragic form - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict deserves to stand, and this article defends it against the two serious objections. The first objection holds that the mixture is unevenness: a young playwright of 1595, drunk on lyric poetry, letting a comedy run away with him for two acts and then scrambling to make a tragedy out of the wreckage. The second objection, Susan Snyder's, is subtler and comes from inside the formalist camp: the play is not a mixture at all but a transformation, a comedy that becomes a tragedy at a datable hinge, so that the two modes succeed one another in time rather than coexisting in a single design. Brooke's account survives both objections, with one concession registered in full below. It survives the unevenness charge because the mixture is patterned in ways that accident cannot explain: announced in a sonnet before the action starts, mirrored in scene after scene, and still operating, inverted, in the last act. It survives Snyder because the tragic pattern is present from the Prologue onward and the comic machinery never leaves the play, which a sequence cannot accommodate. The concession is that Brooke's pluralism, his refusal of any single tragic pattern, can degenerate into a licence to baptize any inconsistency as another mode. The test that keeps the method honest, and that this play passes, is recurrence: a mode is designed when its devices return, transformed, and collect a debt. In Romeo and Juliet they do exactly that.

The claim matters beyond the history of taste. If Brooke is right, the play's famous oddities change status. The sonnet the lovers share at their first meeting stops being a decorative set piece and becomes a formal announcement. The wedding at the play's centre stops being a structural embarrassment, a comedy ending stranded in the middle of a tragedy, and becomes the hinge of the design, the point at which comedy's goal is reached and its machinery is handed over to tragedy with the safety catches removed. The feigned death, the tokens, the undelivered letter, the near discovery, every device that romantic comedy uses to produce a wedding, returns in the second half to produce a tomb. Brooke's reading explains why the ending feels both shocking and prepared, which is the central formal fact about this play that any account of its form has to explain.

## The Downgrading Brooke Inherited

To see what Brooke accomplished, the standing of the play in 1968 has to be reconstructed, and the reconstruction begins with an absence. Bradley's Shakespearean Tragedy of 1904, the most influential account of Shakespearean tragedy ever written in English, built its theory on four plays, Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth, and passed Romeo and Juliet by. The grounds of the exclusion, examined in detail in the account of [why A. C. Bradley left out Romeo and Juliet](/2026/11/20/ac-bradley-excluded-romeo-and-juliet/), were structural in Bradley's own terms. His tragedy requires a catastrophe that issues from the hero's character and action, a conflict substantially inward, a hero whose deeds are the main cause of the disaster. This play fails every term of the definition. Its lovers are children rather than princes. Its conflict is outward, a feud between houses. Its catastrophe turns on a letter that never arrives. Bradley's scheme therefore classified the play as a lesser and earlier thing, a tragedy manqué whose beauties are lyrical and whose construction is a caution.

The classification stuck because it answered to something real in the play's surface. The first two acts behave like a comedy. There is a witty friend, a garrulous nurse, a pair of blocking fathers, a secret courtship, a helpful friar and a wedding. Then the wedding is scarcely cold before the play kills its comedian, banishes its hero and races through a potion plot to a double suicide in a tomb. A criticism armed with Bradley's criteria could only read that shape as two half plays stitched together, the second murdering the first. The lyric splendour was conceded on all sides, and the concession did the damage: the poetry was praised precisely as the thing that excused the form, as if the play were a garland accidentally threaded on a broken string. Generations of introductions repeated the formula, an immature tragedy, a young man's play, beautiful and flawed.

Brooke's book attacked the formula at its root, which was not Bradley's treatment of this play but Bradley's premise about all plays. If there is no single tragic experience, then failing to match the pattern of Hamlet is not a defect but a datum: the play is doing something else, and the critic's task is to find the pattern it actually follows. That move, simple to state, reorganized the study of the early tragedies. It also explains the company Brooke chose for the play. Titus Andronicus had suffered the same dismissal as a crude apprentice bloodbath, Richard III as a prolonged villain's romp, Richard II as a pageant of beautiful speeches. Grouping six such plays and insisting on the variety of their tragic kinds turned a shelf of supposed failures into a laboratory: a decade in which Shakespeare, working before the conventions of Elizabethan tragedy had hardened, tried out the form's possibilities one by one. Within that laboratory, Romeo and Juliet is the experiment in which tragedy is built out of the materials of lyric and romantic comedy, and its mixture is not the noise in the experiment. It is the experiment.

The downgrading, it is worth adding, was not the play's original condition. The earliest texts classify it without apology: the first quarto of 1597 announces "An Excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet" on its title page, the second quarto of 1599 raises the claim to "The Most Excellent and Lamentable Tragedie," and the First Folio of 1623 shelves the play among the tragedies as a matter of course. Nobody in the play's first century appears to have thought its kind in doubt. The doubt arrives with the nineteenth century's hierarchy of kinds and reaches its verdict in Bradley, which means the apprentice theory Brooke dismantled was a comparatively brief interlude in the play's reception, an era in which one definition of tragedy, drawn from four plays, was allowed to sit in judgment on a fifth that the definition's own century had shelved without hesitation. Brooke's pluralism is therefore also an act of historical recovery: it restores the conditions under which the play's first publishers, who called it excellent and lamentable and a tragedy in one breath, were simply right.

## Six Plays Against a Single Tragic Experience

Shakespeare's Early Tragedies is a short book, a little over two hundred pages, and its economy is part of its polemic. Brooke surveys a decade of work, from Titus Andronicus, which he dates to about 1593, to Hamlet at the century's turn, and refuses at the outset the assumption that gives most surveys their plot: that the early plays matter as steps toward the great tragedies, approximations graded by their distance from King Lear. In place of the ladder he puts a set of parallel trials. The emphasis, as his publisher's summary of the book states plainly, falls on the variety of the plays and of the experiences they create, a variety obscured by the belief in a single tragic experience. The kind of experience each play creates, and its quality as a work for the stage, become the questions. Development there is, but it is the development of a craftsman widening his range of modes, not of an apprentice closing on a single mastery.

The point is sharpest in the Julius Caesar chapter, where Brooke describes the play as evidence of, in his own words, "the developing sensitivity to varying modes of utterance with which Shakespeare experimented in his earliest work." The sentence is the book's thesis in miniature. Experiment is Brooke's governing word, and mode of utterance his governing unit: not character, not theme, not moral, but the register in which a play speaks, Senecan rhetoric in one, chronicle ceremony in another, lyric intimacy in the play at hand. A mode can be sustained, mixed, broken or set against its opposite, and the pattern of those dealings is the play's form. Judged by mode, the early plays stop looking like rehearsals for a form not yet invented and start looking like a survey of the forms available, each tested to destruction or to triumph.

For Romeo and Juliet the consequences are immediate. The Bradleyan question, why does the catastrophe not grow out of the hero's character, dissolves, because the question assumes that character causation is tragedy's one legitimate engine. Brooke substitutes a formal question: what happens when the utterances of lyric love poetry, the plot machinery of romantic comedy and the closing pattern of tragedy are made to occupy a single action? The play's answer occupies him for a chapter, and the chapter's argument, paraphrased in the sections that follow, is that the occupation is engineered. The lyric mode supplies the lovers' private world and its intensity. The comic mode supplies the social machinery and its momentum. The tragic mode, announced in the first fourteen lines and never withdrawn, supplies the end toward which both are bent. None of the three is a lapse into the others. Each is present from the start, and the form is the pattern of their interference.

### What exactly did Brooke claim in Shakespeare's Early Tragedies?

Brooke claimed that the six plays of the 1590s share no single tragic pattern and should not be graded against one: each invents its own tragic experience through its mixture of modes of utterance, and Romeo and Juliet builds its tragedy from lyric and comic materials, an experiment to be analysed rather than an immaturity to be excused.

The claim's negative half matters as much as its positive half, because the negative half disarms the opposing evidence in advance. Consider what the unevenness theory rests on: the play's swerve of tone at the midpoint, its reliance on accident at the close, its heroes' want of the stature and the self knowledge that the great tragic figures possess. Under a single pattern theory, each item is a count against the play. Under Brooke's pluralism, each item is a description of the mode the play has chosen. The swerve of tone is the experiment's central event, not its accident. The reliance on chance is the mechanism by which the comic machinery, deprived of comic providence, is made to misfire. The lovers' youth and lyric inwardness are the subject the mode was built to carry, a tragedy of private experience in a public world, for which a prince's fall would be the wrong instrument. Nothing in the play changes. Everything in its evaluation reverses, because the standard has been moved from what the play fails to repeat to what it succeeds in inventing.

## The Method: Stage Pictures and Emblematic Form

Brooke's method for reading form follows from his unit. If a play's form is the pattern of its modes, the pattern has to be perceivable, and Brooke reads it in two registers at once: the verbal, in the distribution of verse kinds, set speeches and prose; and the visual, in the disposition of bodies on the stage. The essays, as the book's own description puts it, develop an understanding of Shakespeare's use of the stage picture in relation to the emblematic imagery of Elizabethan poetry. The stage picture is the frozen composition an audience carries away: a girl at a window above a man in darkness, two bodies on a bier, statues promised in gold. The emblem is the Elizabethan device, picture plus motto, that fixes a meaning in an image. Brooke's insight is that the early plays, written while emblematic habits of seeing were still native to their audiences, compose their meanings in such pictures, and that a formal reading which listens only to the verse will miss half the design.

Applied to Romeo and Juliet, the method yields the play's most legible structures. The orchard scene is an emblem before it is a conversation: Juliet elevated and lit in the window, Romeo below in the Capulet darkness, the vertical distance between them measuring exactly the social distance the plot must cross, and the whole picture held while the verse crosses it. "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3). The tomb scene answers the orchard with the same grammar inverted. Romeo descends to where Juliet lies elevated on her bier, calls the vault a "feasting presence full of light" (5.3.86), and the picture of the lover below and the beloved above completes itself in death. Between the two pictures the play hangs its central emblem, the shared sonnet at the feast, two speakers composing a single fourteen line form between them at first touch: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5.92-93). A form built by two voices out of one pattern is the play's whole design in small, and Brooke's method is what makes the smallness visible as design rather than ornament.

### What is an emblematic stage picture, in Brooke's method?

An emblematic stage picture is a disposition of actors, a girl at a window above a man in darkness, two bodies on a bier, that carries meaning as an Elizabethan emblem does, by fixing an idea in a visible arrangement; Brooke reads the early tragedies through these pictures, treating their pattern as form, not decoration.

The method also disciplines the lyric question. Critics who conceded the play's poetry while denying its form were treating the lyric passages as detachable beauties, flowers stuck into a botched plot. On Brooke's account the lyric mode is load bearing. The lovers speak in completed forms, sonnets, aubades, formal laments, because their love is the one finished and patterned thing in a Verona of fragments, brawls and interrupted sentences. The city's speech breaks off, in challenges, in the Prince's edicts, in Mercutio's unfinished jokes. The lovers' speech completes its patterns even when its content is despair: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1) opens a speech whose rhymes and periods close with formal exactness while the speaker waits for a husband who will arrive as an exile. Formality under pressure is the play's signature, and it belongs to the larger argument Harry Levin made from the verse itself, examined in the reading of [Harry Levin's 'Form and Formality' (1960)](/2026/12/04/harry-levin-form-and-formality/): Levin shows the play's verbal figures enacting its meanings from inside the line, where Brooke shows the same patterning at the scale of scenes and modes. The two accounts are complementary, and their agreement from different scales is part of the evidence that the patterning is in the play and not in the critics.

## The Recurrence Test: The Design Checked Scene by Scene

Brooke's claim can be audited, and the audit is the strongest part of his case, because the play's second half is a systematic revisiting of its first. The feast of 1.5, masked, musical, governed by Capulet's genial tyranny, is answered by the funeral preparations of 4.5, the same household machinery running in reverse, music demanded and then silenced, the bride carried out to a different music. The orchard of 2.2, with its wall leapt, its servant calling from within, its lovers negotiating across a vertical distance, is answered by the tomb of 5.3, wall breached by mattock, servant dismissed, the distance between the lovers closed at last by the one means the feud left open. The friar's cell of 2.6, where a marriage is made in hope, is answered by the cell of 4.1, where a death is counterfeited in desperation, the same counsellor, the same secrecy, the same confidence that a rite privately performed can outwit a public world. The street of 1.1, where the feud performs itself for an audience of citizens and a prince, is answered by the street of 3.1 where the performance turns mortal, and by the tomb's threshold in 5.3 where the Prince must perform the feud's meaning to its survivors. Even the verbal surface keeps the ledger: the light imagery of the first meeting, Juliet as sun, as bright torch, as the wings of angels, returns in the tomb speeches transposed into the key of relics and shrines.

A botched or merely uneven play does not keep accounts at this density. Accident produces resemblances, since a short play reuses its locations and its personnel by necessity, but accident does not produce inversion with this regularity, the same device returning with its polarity reversed at the corresponding point of the design. The wedding stands at the structural centre and the returns radiate from it in both directions, forward into the catastrophe as reversal and backward into the courtship as promise. Brooke's chapter gave criticism the vocabulary for what the audit finds: the returns are the form, the pattern by which lyric, comic and tragic modes are made to interfere, and their systematic character is the empirical core of the experiment thesis. Any rival account, Snyder's included, must either explain the inversions or explain them away, and the history of the play's criticism since 1968 is largely the history of that obligation being met, evaded or transformed into new readings of the same pattern.

## The Two Choruses and the Missing Third

The play's frame tells the same story in miniature, and Brooke's attention to frames is among his chapter's quiet strengths. Two choruses appear, both sonnets, one before the first act and one before the second, and then the device stops. No chorus announces the third act, the fourth or the fifth. A dramatist who had lost interest in his machinery might simply have dropped it, and the unevenness theory has sometimes said exactly that. The formal reading says the opposite: the frame withdraws at the moment its work is done. The choruses exist to state the pattern, love announced under sentence of death, desire substituted within an endangered structure, while the pattern is still a diagram. Once the wedding has made the diagram flesh, statements of pattern are superfluous, and their function migrates into the action itself, into the Friar's sententious warnings, the Prince's edicts and finally the Friar's great narration in 5.3, which is a chorus speech delivered by a participant, the whole plot retold in order to the assembled city. The pattern no longer needs a spokesman outside the play because the play has become its own spokesman.

The withdrawal has a second effect that Brooke's mixture explains and a sequence cannot. In the first two acts the audience watches the comedy from outside its danger, briefed by the choruses, superior to the lovers in knowledge as an audience of comedy expects to be. When the frame falls silent at the third act, the briefing stops, and the audience is demoted to the characters' level, learning of the stopped letter only when the Friar does, arriving at the tomb when Romeo does. The loss of the chorus is the loss of the comic vantage, timed to the hour when the vantage would have become a lie. Form and experience coincide: the design withdraws its announcements exactly as the world withdraws its guarantees. A reading that treats the vanished choruses as carelessness has to regard that coincidence as luck. Brooke's reading, which finds the same timing in the wedding's placement and the devices' inversion, is entitled to call it by its proper name.

## The Prologue Sonnet: The Mixture Announced

Every account of the play's form must start where the play starts, with a sonnet that tells the audience the ending. "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 5-6). The Chorus calls the coming action "the fearful passage of their death-mark'd love" (Prologue, 9) and asks patience for the two hours' traffic of the stage. Brooke's reading gives this prologue a weight that the unevenness theory cannot. If the mixture of comedy and tragedy were an accident of composition, the tragic frame would be the last thing the play would announce in advance, in the most formal verse kind the period possessed. Instead the design declares itself: a sonnet, the lyric form of love, loaded with the feud, the deaths and the reconciliation, so that lyric and tragic modes are fused in fourteen lines before a single character appears. The audience is not promised a comedy that will go wrong. It is promised a tragedy whose substance will be love, and every comic delight of the first two acts is watched under that sentence.

The second Chorus extends the announcement into a theory of the mixture. "Now old desire doth in his death-bed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.Chorus, 1-2). The Chorus speaks of Rosaline discarded and Juliet enthroned in the language of inheritance and death, casual in its metaphors exactly where the play will be literal: desire on a death bed, an heir waiting, a beloved who can be reached only by "extreme" means because the feud has made ordinary courtship impossible. Brooke's modal reading notices what sequential readings glide past. The Chorus does not say that the play has stopped being one thing and started being another. It describes substitution inside a continuing structure, one desire replacing another within the same endangered form, which is the play's method at every level: the same machinery, running again, with the stakes raised.

The feud scenes of the first act supply the third mode in its rawest state. The brawl of 1.1 is choreographed as farce, servants inventing insults, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1), citizens with clubs, two patriarchs in their gowns calling for swords their wives mock them for wanting. Played alone, it is the opening of a city comedy. But the Prince's sentence closes the scene in the register of the scaffold: lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace if the brawls recur. Death is legislated over the farce within two hundred lines of the rise of the curtain, and the legislation is not decoration. It is the tragic mode holding the comic mode's licence, revocable at the third offence, and when the third brawl comes in 3.1 the licence is revoked in terms. Brooke's mixture is not a critic's metaphor imposed on the play. It is the play's own jurisprudence, mercy and death sentence sharing a scene from the first morning.

## The Comic Structure Driven Past Its Close

The core of Brooke's chapter is a piece of structural engineering analysis, and it can be stated as a measurement. A romantic comedy of the 1590s, the kind Shakespeare had just mastered in plays of disguise and multiple wooing, runs on a known engine: young lovers, a blocking father or guardian, ingenious servants and go-betweens, a benevolent fixer, obstacles of identity and misunderstanding, and a close in marriage that reconciles the generations. Romeo and Juliet assembles that engine part by part in its first two acts and completes it. The lovers meet under the enemy's roof. The go-betweens run their errands. The fixer in the friar's cell performs the marriage. By the standards of the form, the play is over at 2.6, with the Friar's warning as its only discordant note: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder" (2.6.9-10). What Brooke sees, and what the unevenness theory cannot see because it is looking for a join, is that the play then continues the engine past its terminus. The machinery does not stop. It is driven on, into a world from which comedy's providence has been withdrawn, and each device begins to do the opposite of its office.

The inventory of the second half is the comedy inventory read backwards. The feast of recognition becomes the funeral feast, the wedding torches inverted, in Capulet's own words, to funeral purposes: our celebration instruments, he laments over the supposedly dead Juliet, are turned to melancholy. The tokens that in comedy circulate to prove identity and unite the separated, rings, letters, messages carried by servants, become the instruments of separation: a ring sent to a banished man, a letter stopped in a plague house, a message carried by a servant who sees a funeral and reports a death. The feigned death, comedy's most reliable solvent, the device by which heroines escape forced marriages and wake to chosen ones, is deployed in perfect working order and produces a real corpse. The near discovery, comedy's heartbeat, the watcher almost seen, the door almost opened in time, resolves itself into arrivals minutes late. Brooke's term for the whole arrangement is experiment, and the experiment has a precise object: to discover what the comic form is worth when the world stops cooperating with it. The answer the play gives is that the form's momentum survives its providence, and momentum without providence is exactly what tragedy had always meant by Fortune.

The placement of the wedding is the boldest stroke in the design, and Brooke's reading is the first to give it full formal credit. Comedy ends at the altar because the altar is its social proof, the generations reconciled in public. This wedding is private, hurried, witnessed by one friar and no family, and it reconciles nothing: within hours the bridegroom has killed the bride's cousin in the street and the marriage exists only as a secret whose disclosure would be its destruction. A form's goal has been reached in a manner that voids the goal's function. The experiment is therefore not a comedy with a tragic sequel bolted on. The tragic condition is inside the comic success from the moment of its achievement, the way the tragic sentence was inside the sonnet of the Prologue. Brooke's structural claim, reduced to one line, is that the play does not change its form at the midpoint. It reveals what its form was always carrying.

### When does the comedy in the play actually stop?

On Brooke's account it never stops: the comic machinery of go-betweens, tokens, feigned deaths and near discoveries runs through the last act with comedy's providence withdrawn, each device performing the opposite of its office, until the wedding engine delivers a tomb; what ends at Mercutio's death is the guarantee that the devices will turn out well.

Mercutio's death is where every reading of the form must pay its toll, and Brooke's handling of it distinguishes his mixture from Snyder's sequence before the two are set side by side below. Mercutio is the comic mode's presiding spirit, the Queen Mab speech its set piece, a fantasia on dreams that mocks every desire in the play including the hero's: "I talk of dreams, / Which are the children of an idle brain" (1.4.96-97). When Mercutio dies in 3.1, cursing both houses in a line that yokes the modes in a single breath, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), the play loses its comedian. It does not lose its comedy. The Nurse's tangled errands continue, Peter and the musicians haggle over grief in 4.5 like a clown scene left running in a house of mourning, Capulet bustles about the wedding preparations with a comic father's energy on the morning of the funeral. The modes are layered, not sequenced: the tragic ascends while the comic persists underneath, degraded and unlicensed, and the persistence is what makes the second half grotesque rather than merely sad. Brooke's form has room for that grotesque. A form defined as comedy first and tragedy after has to treat the musicians as an embarrassment, a leftover. The mixture treats them as evidence.

## The Hinge Examined: Act 3 Scene 1 as the Experiment's Proof

Every formal theory of this play is finally a theory of one scene, and Brooke's mixture should be put to the scene directly. The street scene of 3.1 is built as a comic routine that keeps failing to stay one. It opens in prose banter, Mercutio teasing Benvolio with the catalogue of his quarrels, the byword for the comic mode's favourite sport, wit about violence at a safe distance from violence. Tybalt enters with the tragedy's errand, and the scene's registers begin to collide line by line: courtesy formulas used as challenges, Mercutio's punning defiance, Romeo's arrival speaking a language nobody on stage can parse, the language of secret kinship, "I do protest I never injured thee, / But love thee better than thou canst devise" (3.1). The speech is the mixture at its most concentrated, a lyric utterance of love delivered in the middle of a duel challenge, unintelligible to its hearers precisely because it belongs to the play's other mode, the private world of the marriage, breaking surface in the public world of the feud. Mercutio reads it as dishonour because the feud's world contains no other reading. His intervention, and his death under Romeo's arm, follow from a failure of translation between modes, which is to say from the form itself.

The scene then performs the experiment's central demonstration in miniature. Romeo, converted by grief and shame into the feud's own idiom, kills Tybalt in a dozen lines and understands what has happened to him in the vocabulary the Prologue supplied: he is fortune's fool, a man whose story has been seized by the machinery he had opted out of. Nothing in his character has ripened into the killing; the peacemaker of five minutes earlier performs it, because the scene's logic is modal, not psychological. The lover who speaks lyric in the street is answered by the street, and the answer is a corpse. A Bradleyan reading must treat the scene as the hero's flaw declaring itself, and then cannot explain why the flaw is an excess of the play's most praised quality, its hero's capacity for love. Brooke's reading treats the scene as the design's proof: the modes, held in interference for two acts, discharge. The rest of the play is the management of that discharge, edict by edict, message by message, rite by rite, until the tomb gives the interference its final picture. Critics who prefer Snyder's hinge can locate the discharge at the same minute; the difference is that Brooke's account explains why the discharge was loaded from the first scene, and a hinge that has been loaded for two acts is a mechanism, not a surprise.

## The Comic Personnel: Mercutio, the Nurse and the Prose World

Brooke's modes have bodies. The comic strand of the mixture is carried by two great prose intelligences, Mercutio and the Nurse, and the play's distribution of prose and verse is the modal map drawn in the text's own hand. Mercutio speaks prose when he is playing and verse when the play needs his wit to fly, and his imagination is the comic mode's philosophy: everything is convertible, every solemnity can be punned into its opposite, dreams are the children of an idle brain and love a thing to be pricked and beaten down. The Nurse speaks the prose of the body's calendar, of weaning and teething and fourteen years counted on fingers, a time scheme of seasons and growths against the plot's time scheme of hours. Between them they staff the world the lovers must escape, a world that is not wicked but unconverted, fluent in everything except the lovers' dialect of completed forms. Brooke's formalism notices what character criticism flattens: the comedy does not reside in these figures as their personality. It resides in their modes of speech, which is why their fates are formal events. Mercutio's death is the comic mode losing its best speaker at the moment of its boldest performance, a joke about a grave man that completes itself as fact. The Nurse's failure in 3.5, when she counsels the bigamous marriage to Paris, is the comic mode confessing its limit: her prose wisdom, so capacious about bodies, has no category for a vow.

Their afterlives in the design confirm the reading. Mercutio dead becomes a standard the survivors invoke, his name the measure of the world's narrowing; the Nurse alive becomes a relic, trailing through the wedding preparations of Act 4 with her jokes falling into a house that has stopped laughing, until the discovery scene reduces her to the lament's least formal voice, grief without pattern. Peter and the musicians then inherit the comic mode at its lowest wattage in 4.5, haggling over the price of music at a funeral feast, and the scene's notorious tonal discomfort is the mixture's signature at minimum scale: comedy's personnel still on the payroll of a household whose story has changed kind around them. Brooke's account dignifies even this discomfort as information. The modes do not vacate the premises when tragedy takes the lease. They linger, diminished, and their lingering is what an audience registers as the play's refusal to let its catastrophe be clean.

## Fortune, Stars and the Play's Causal System

No word in the play carries more formal weight in Brooke's reading than fortune, and the distribution of the word is itself evidence. The Prologue assigns the lovers to crossed stars before they exist as characters. Romeo enters his tragedy under a premonition, his mind misgiving some consequence yet hanging in the stars (1.4), and exits his freedom with the cry that he is fortune's fool (3.1). Juliet, alone of the principals, argues with the system: fortune is fickle, all men call it so, and she begs its fickleness to return her husband (3.5), a prayer whose logic is flawless inside a universe run by chance, since a fickle power can be petitioned for a change of mood. The Friar moralizes fortune to Romeo's face in the cell scene, and the Prince closes the action by distributing its blame among heaven, the houses and himself. The play, in short, talks incessantly about its own causal arrangements, and a criticism that treats those arrangements as a defect owes an explanation of why the defect is so elaborately theorized by the text that suffers from it.

Brooke's answer reframes the question in the terms of his experiment. Comic providence and tragic fortune are the same machinery viewed from opposite ends of the outcome: in comedy, coincidence is benevolent because the form guarantees the wedding, and letters arrive, disguises hold and fathers relent on schedule. Withdraw the guarantee, and the identical machinery, letters, potions, disguises of death, errands and timings, becomes fortune, malign not by intention but by indifference. The experiment of Romeo and Juliet is to run comedy's causal system with the guarantee removed and to observe that nothing else needs to change for tragedy to result. That observation is why Brooke's reading cannot be refuted by pointing at the stopped letter, since the stopped letter is the experiment's crucial datum, the delivered letters of a hundred comedies arriving, for once, at the wrong address. Bradley measured the play against a causation of character and found machinery. Brooke measures the machinery against the comic system it was borrowed from and finds a thesis: that the difference between the genres, at the level of causation, is a promise the world makes in one and breaks in the other. The lovers are not failed tragic heroes. They are successful comic heroes in a play that has withdrawn the promise, and the withdrawal, announced by the stars of the Prologue, is the form.

## Brooke Against Snyder: Mixture or Transformation?

Eleven years after Brooke's book, Susan Snyder published the reading with which it must now share the field, first as an essay, "Romeo and Juliet: Comedy into Tragedy," in Essays in Criticism in 1970, and then as the opening study of The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies in 1979. Snyder's thesis, in her own words, is that the play is different from the other tragedies "in that it becomes, rather than is, tragic": the action and the characters begin in familiar comic patterns and are then transformed, or discarded, to compose the pattern of tragedy. The full case for that reading, scene by scene, is made in the account of [Susan Snyder: comedy turning to tragedy](/2026/12/11/susan-snyder-comedy-to-tragedy/), and its power is not in dispute here. Its evidence is the audience's own experience. The play does feel like a comedy for two acts. Mercutio's death does feel like a door closing. Snyder locates the birth of the tragedy at that death with exactness: in Mercutio's sudden violent end, she writes, Shakespeare makes the birth of a tragedy coincide with the symbolic death of comedy. Any formal account that cannot explain the force of that moment is worthless, and Brooke's must be tested against it.

The difference between the two accounts is precise enough to be adjudicated, and it is this. For Snyder the modes are successive states of one action, comedy first, tragedy after, joined at a hinge. For Brooke the modes are concurrent strands of one design, lyric, comic and tragic present from the Prologue, their proportions shifting as the action proceeds. The test cases divide cleanly. On the Prologue, Brooke wins outright. A play that becomes tragic cannot announce its tragedy in a sonnet before the first scene and legislate death over its opening farce within the first act; the tragic strand is woven in from the start, and Snyder's model has to demote the Prologue to an ironic frame, foreknowledge without presence, which the verse will not support. On the felt hinge at 3.1, Snyder wins outright. Audiences do experience a generic bereavement at Mercutio's death, and Brooke's shifting proportions describe that experience less vividly than Snyder's closed door. On the second half's machinery, Brooke wins again, and decisively. The potion plot, the stopped letter, the feigned death are comic devices still functioning after the supposed death of comedy, and Snyder's own formula concedes the difficulty: patterns are "transformed, or discarded," and a device that has been discarded cannot kill the heroine. The devices were not discarded. They were retained and reversed, which is mixture, not sequence.

The adjudication therefore runs: on construction, Brooke; on reception, Snyder; and since the question Brooke set out to answer is a question about construction, about what the play is and not only about what it feels like minute by minute, Brooke's account governs the form. Snyder's hinge survives inside Brooke's mixture as the moment the proportions tip past recovery, the moment the tragic strand, present since the Prologue, becomes the strand the audience can no longer hope against. That absorption is the strongest evidence for Brooke's framework, since a good theory of form should be able to house its rival's best observation as a special case. Snyder's framework cannot reciprocate: within a sequence, the Prologue's tragedy and the fifth act's comic devices are anomalies on both sides of the hinge. Within a mixture, the hinge is an event the design prepares and the audience feels. The disagreement, staged at full strength, ends in Brooke's favour on the question his book actually asks.

### Where do Brooke and Snyder give different accounts of the same scenes?

They divide over three bodies of evidence: the Prologue, where Snyder treats the announced tragedy as an ironic frame and Brooke reads the tragic strand as present from the start; Mercutio's death, where Snyder sees comedy die and Brooke sees a mixture's proportions tip; and the potion plot, whose still functioning comic devices fit Brooke's mixture and strain Snyder's sequence.

## The Practitioner's Eye: Granville-Barker and the Editors

Brooke wrote as a scholar of the study, but his formalism had a theatrical conscience, and the affinity with the century's great practitioner critic is instructive. Harley Granville-Barker's preface to the play, the subject of the companion account of [Granville-Barker's preface to the play](/2026/11/27/granville-barker-preface-analysis/), reads the same mixed form from the stage outward: a swift, continuous action of a few days, lyric intensity sustained by pace, a tragedy whose effect depends on the audience never being allowed to cool. Where Brooke diagrams the mixture, Granville-Barker feels its temperature, and the two accounts corroborate each other in the way that matters most. Both refuse the apprentice theory. Both locate the play's unity in its handling of a single rapid action rather than in the moral growth of a hero. A form that a director and a formalist independently describe as one engine running at speed is a form that exists.

Brooke himself crossed the line between critic and maker of texts. Four decades of readers have met his formalism in its editorial embodiment in his Oxford Shakespeare edition of Macbeth, published in 1990, where the introduction reads that play through ritual, pattern and the disposition of its scenes with the same refusal to reduce tragedy to a case study of one man's character. The edition matters for the present argument because it shows the method of 1968 still at work at the end of Brooke's career, and because editing is where formal theories are audited. An editor must decide, scene by scene, what the text is doing, and a critic whose account of form were mere licence could not sustain the discipline of an edition. Brooke sustained it.

The editors of Romeo and Juliet itself have, in their introductions, gradually ratified the climate Brooke created. Brian Gibbons, in the introduction to his Arden edition of 1980, the edition whose lineation is followed for the quotations in this article, treats the play's generic mixture as its defining structural fact, following the comic conventions of the first half into the tragic design they serve. The editorial tradition Gibbons represents is instructive precisely because an edition's introduction cannot afford a critic's evasions: it must tell a first reader what kind of play this is, in a few pages, and be right. That the standard editions now describe a designed mixture of comedy and tragedy, where the editions of Bradley's century described a flawed tragedy with beautiful passages, is the rehabilitation Brooke argued for, completed in the place where students actually meet the play.

## The Unevenness Objection: Where Brooke's Case Strains

A verdict for Brooke that suppressed the costs of his method would repeat the fault he corrected in others, so the objections belong in the open. The gravest is methodological. A criticism that refuses all single patterns and reads every play as its own kind of tragedy risks explaining everything and therefore nothing: whatever a play does becomes, by definition, its experiment, and the word experiment loses its cutting edge, since an experiment that cannot fail is not an experiment. The objection has force against lazy Brookeanism, and the discipline that answers it has to be supplied from within the method. An experiment is identifiable by its controls. In this play the controls are visible: the same devices recur in both halves with their outcomes reversed, the same forms, sonnet, chorus, lament, recur with their contents darkened, and the source poem by Arthur Brooke stands behind the play as a record of the same story handled without the mixture, three leisurely months of wooing moralized into a warning. Against those controls, the claim of design is checkable, and it checks out. Where a play offered no such recurrences, Brooke's method, honestly applied, would have to return a verdict of unevenness. The method can fail a play. It simply does not fail this one.

The second objection is historical, and it was put with courtesy and precision by G. K. Hunter, whose essay "Shakespeare's Earliest Tragedies: Titus Andronicus and Romeo and Juliet" appeared in Shakespeare Survey 27 in 1974. Hunter singled Brooke out as the obvious exception to the critical habit of refusing to consider the earliest tragedies together, and then entered his reservation: Brooke's brief was much larger, covering six plays, and Brooke's sense of the mode of tragedy was rather different from the one Hunter meant to pursue. Hunter's own pursuit led toward convention and ritual, toward the Senecan and moral structures the young Shakespeare inherited, and away from Brooke's modal pluralism. The disagreement is real but narrower than it looks. Hunter's genealogy explains where the young playwright found his materials; Brooke's pluralism explains what he did with materials that no single genealogy covers. Titus Andronicus, saturated in Seneca, is Hunter's best case. Romeo and Juliet, whose Senecan content is negligible and whose materials are the sonnet, the novella and the romantic comedy, is Brooke's. On the play at hand the adjudication is not close, and Hunter's reservation survives as a warning against extending Brooke's key to doors it was not cut for.

The third objection concerns the ending's machinery, and it is Bradley's objection returning in modern dress: whatever the design, the catastrophe still depends on a friar detained by plague officers and a message miscarried, and a tragedy of accident is a lesser thing than a tragedy of character. Brooke's framework answers by relocation. Accident in this play is not a substitute for causation but the form's chosen instrument, the precise point at which comic providence, the luck that in comedy always arrives in time, is withdrawn. The stopped letter is the twin of the delivered letter in a dozen comedies, and its stoppage is prepared by the play's whole economy of messages, go-betweens and near meetings, an economy running from the Nurse's delayed errands to Romeo's purchase of poison from a man Mantua's law has already half destroyed. Chance that is systematically prepared is a theme, not a lapse. Romeo names himself fortune's fool at the moment of his banishment (3.1), Juliet apostrophizes fortune as fickle and begs its fickleness to work in her favour (3.5), and the Prologue had already assigned the lovers to crossed stars. A play that talks about its own causal system at that density is conducting an argument about it, and Brooke's reading is the one that hears the argument.

### Why did Bradley leave the play out of Shakespearean Tragedy?

Bradley's theory required a catastrophe issuing from the hero's character in an inward conflict, and the play fails each term: its heroes are children of no stature, its conflict is the feud of two houses, and its ending turns on a letter that never arrives, so Shakespearean Tragedy built its account on four other plays and passed it by.

## What Brooke Changed: The Rehabilitation of the Early Tragedies

The measure of a critical book is the shelf it rearranges, and Brooke rearranged two. The first shelf is the play's own. Before 1968 the standard description of Romeo and Juliet was a lyric masterpiece with a defective tragic structure, praised in the currency of poetry and apologized for in the currency of form. After Brooke, the description available to every subsequent critic was the reverse: a formal experiment of the first interest whose poetry is one of its modes. The revaluation did not make the older complaints disappear, since the complaints answer to real features of the text, but it changed their sign. Haste became pace, mixture became design, accident became the withdrawal of providence, and the burden of proof moved to the other side. A critic who now wishes to call the play uneven must explain away the patterned recurrences, the announced ending, the inverted devices, and that is a harder brief than repeating the formula Brooke inherited.

The second shelf is the decade's. By treating six plays as parallel experiments, Brooke gave the study of early Shakespearean tragedy its modern charter. Hunter's 1974 essay, dissenting in method, accepts the charter's premise, that the earliest tragedies repay being thought about together as tragedies, and disputes only the sense of mode. The subsequent half century of work on Titus Andronicus, long the most abused play in the canon, proceeds on ground Brooke cleared: a play's refusal to resemble Hamlet is the beginning of its analysis, not the end. For Romeo and Juliet the rehabilitation had a further consequence in the classroom and the edition trade, where the play is most readers' first tragedy. Taught as a failed version of a form it never attempted, it instructs students to apologize for the text. Taught as Brooke teaches it, as an engineered collision of the period's most finished lyric form, its most reliable comic engine and its starkest tragic pattern, it instructs them to read form itself, which is the skill the rest of the canon demands.

## Elizabethan Tragedy in the 1590s: The Conventions the Experiment Works Against

Brooke's word experiment carries a date, and the date does argumentative work. The Romeo and Juliet chapter reads the play against the developing conventions of Elizabethan tragedy, and the conventions of the mid 1590s were developing in the plain sense that they did not yet constitute a settled form. Seneca stood behind the revenge play, with its ghosts, its choruses and its rhetoric of atrocity, and Kyd's Spanish Tragedy had shown how those materials could fill a theatre. Marlowe had supplied the mighty line and the overreacher. The chronicle stage offered falls of princes on the de casibus pattern, greatness undone by Fortune's wheel in procession. A dramatist writing a tragedy in 1595 faced not a form but a market of forms, each with its own machinery, and Brooke's survey of the decade is precisely a survey of Shakespeare's dealings in that market: Senecan ritual in Titus Andronicus, the fall pattern ironized into a villain's progress in Richard III, ceremonial lyricism in Richard II, public rhetoric in Julius Caesar. Romeo and Juliet takes its materials from a different stall altogether, the Italian novella, the sonnet sequence and the romantic comedy, and builds a tragedy out of stock that tragedy had not previously used.

That generic provenance explains features that the Bradleyan tradition could only register as defects, and Brooke's historical framing converts them one by one. The heroes are private persons, a merchant family's son and a daughter not yet fourteen, because the novella and the comedy deal in private persons; the play imports their scale into a kind that had dealt in kings, and the friction between the lovers' private magnitude of feeling and their public insignificance is the tragedy's subject. There is no ghost, no soliloquizing villain, no madness scene, because the imported stock contains none; in their place stand the forms the stock does contain, the sonnet of meeting, the aubade of parting, the lament over the seeming corpse. The antagonist is not a person but a situation, an inherited feud whose origin nobody remembers, because comedy's blocking figures, promoted beyond their competence, make exactly such an antagonist: the senex who will not yield, armed here with swords and a city. Brooke's historical point completes his formal one. In a decade when tragedy had not hardened, mixture was not a young man's carelessness. It was the period's method, and this play is the method's most daring instance, the one that wagers a whole tragedy on materials from the wrong shelf.

The wager's risk should not be understated, since Brooke never understates it. A tragedy built from lyric and comic stock forfeits the kinds of causation its audience had been trained to expect: no flaw ripening, no villain plotting, no ghost demanding. What it gains is a causation of its own kind, in which the pressure is supplied by time and machinery rather than by will. Shakespeare had spent the early 1590s learning, in the comedies, how much plot pressure a misunderstanding, a token and a deadline can generate; the experiment of Romeo and Juliet is to ask whether that pressure, unrelieved by the comic ending that normally discharges it, is sufficient to kill. Brooke's chapter answers that it is, and that the sufficiency is the discovery. Later tragedy would find other engines, ambition, jealousy, ingratitude, and drive them from inside a single breast. The early experiment drives its engine from outside, from the feud, the calendar and the post, and the lovers' destruction by machinery they did not build and cannot persuade remains a kind of tragedy Shakespeare never repeated, because the experiment, once performed, had proved its point.

## Modes of Utterance: The Play's Registers Mapped

Brooke's phrase modes of utterance deserves the slow reading his chapter gives it, because in this play the map of utterances is the map of the form. At the top of the register stand the completed lyric kinds, sonnet, aubade, epithalamium and lament, assigned almost exclusively to the lovers and to the choruses who speak for the play itself. Below them runs the public verse of authority, the Prince's edicts, Capulet's commands, the Friar's sententious couplets, verse doing the work of law and custom. Below that, the wit combat of the young men, verse and prose alternating as fencing partners change weapons, and at the base the prose of the servants, the Nurse and the clown figures, the register of appetite, errand and anecdote. A conventional tragedy of the period polices these borders by rank. This play's experiment is to let its two lowest status principals, a boy and a girl of no public account, monopolize its highest register, so that the form's hierarchy of speech inverts the city's hierarchy of power. The lovers are the play's only finished speakers. Everyone who outranks them speaks in fragments, orders, jokes or laments by rote.

Juliet's progress through the registers is the experiment's human graph, and it can be plotted scene by scene. In 1.3 she speaks in obedient tags, her lines fitted inside her mother's periods like a child's verses in a copybook. At the feast she holds her half of a sonnet against a stranger, matching figure for figure. In the orchard she conducts the play's most exacting negotiation, inventing contract language as she goes, swearing, unsaying, and swearing again on better terms. By 3.2 she commands the epithalamium, by 4.3 the solitary aria of the potion speech, a form with no partner and no witness, and in the tomb she speaks the play's last private lines over a body. No other figure in the play travels so far through its modes, and the travel is the education that character criticism, looking for a psychology, persistently mistakes for one. What grows in Juliet, on Brooke's showing, is not a self in Bradley's sense but a command of utterance, and the tragedy completes itself when the most accomplished speaker in Verona can find no form left that the world will honour except the dagger's punctuation. Romeo's graph runs the other way, from the borrowed Petrarchism of the Rosaline speeches, forms worn like another man's coat, through the achieved reciprocity of the sonnet and the orchard, to the broken exclamations of the Mantua scenes and the final set speech in the tomb, where his utterance recovers its formality at the moment of its extinction. Two curves, crossing at the wedding, plotted in modes: that is the play Brooke's chapter teaches its readers to see, and it is invisible to any method that begins by asking after flaws.

## The Lyric Mode at Full Length: Sonnets, Aubades and Laments

Brooke's modal analysis earns its keep in the scenes the older criticism praised and skipped, the lyric set pieces, because it explains their placement as structure rather than as interlude. The play's lyric forms arrive in a deliberate sequence, and each arrives at the point where the comic structure tightens. The shared sonnet of 1.5 is courtship as a single completed artefact, two voices alternating quatrains and sealing the form with a kiss that the form itself has negotiated: the pilgrims and saints conceit does the persuading, and Juliet's verdict, "You kiss by th' book" (1.5), applauds the bookishness. The balcony scene of 2.2 begins inside the sonnet habit, Romeo apostrophizing in set comparisons, and then breaks the habit open: Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33) is a lyric question that demands a prose answer, an argument about names, and the scene's long negotiation, vows offered, withdrawn as too rash, offered again under oath by the moon and then forbidden that oath, is the sonnet impulse being educated into dialogue. Form is not decorating the courtship. Form is the courtship's medium and its problem, since everything the lovers say to each other must first pass through the inherited shapes of saying.

The later acts run the sequence in reverse, lyric forms under progressive damage. The epithalamium Juliet sings to the coming night in 3.2 is the sonneteer's art at full stretch, invocation, catalogue, paradox, and it is addressed to an event the audience knows has already been poisoned by the deaths in the street. The aubade of 3.5, the dawn parting debated through the lark and the nightingale, is the balcony scene's lyricism with the machinery of the plot audible inside it: the Nurse's warning, the ladder descending, the day that is also a deadline. Juliet's potion soliloquy in 4.3 is a lyric aria built from terrors itemized and mastered, and the formal lament of 4.5, in which the household discovers the seeming corpse, is the play's most artificial passage, grief arranged in parallel phrases like a madrigal, so stylized that production after production has stumbled on it. Brooke's reading makes the stylization the meaning. Verona grieves in forms because Verona only has forms, public rituals into which private feeling is poured and standardized; the lovers' tragedy is that even their mourning is conducted by the city's liturgy. Against that quartet of conventional lament stands the last lyric unit, Romeo's speech over the body in the tomb, where the light imagery of the first sonnet returns completed: the vault transfigured, in his words, into a feasting presence full of light. The lyric mode, which began as the lovers' alternative world, ends as the only world they can still share, a pattern of images closing over them like the form of the Prologue sonnet closing over the plot.

Two times run through the play, and Brooke's modal reading is finally an account of their collision. Lyric time is dilated and circular: the sonnet lingers, the aubade debates a moment, the lament repeats. Dramatic time in this play is the most accelerated in Shakespeare, a Sunday to a Thursday of deadlines, appointments and messages in transit, every scene stamped with an hour. The lovers live in lyric time while the plot processes them in dramatic time, and the catastrophe is the moment the two times are forcibly synchronized, in a tomb, at an hour appointed by a letter that travelled at the speed of plague. No account of the form that ignores the lyric set pieces can see the collision, because the set pieces are one of its terms. That is Brooke's permanent bequest to the reading of the play: the poetry is not the reward for enduring the plot. The poetry is half the plot.

## The Source as Control: Arthur Brooke's Poem and the Nine Months

An experiment claim needs a control, and literary history supplies one ready made. Arthur Brooke's Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet, the narrative poem of 1562 from which Shakespeare took the story, tells the same events without the mixture, and the comparison isolates exactly what the young Shakespeare's formal choices added. The poem spreads its action across some nine months, allows its lovers a season of married happiness, and surrounds the tale with a moralizing apparatus: a preface that condemns the pair as thralls to unhonest desire who neglected the authority and advice of parents and friends, and an ending that sentences the survivors, the Nurse banished, the apothecary hanged, the Friar retired to a hermitage. Whatever moves in the poem, and much does, its form is that of an exemplum, a story told to point a warning. Shakespeare compressed the nine months into a handful of days, deleted the moral apparatus, promoted Mercutio from a name at the feast to the presiding wit of the first half, and rebuilt the whole as the collision of modes Brooke describes. The control could hardly be cleaner. Same story, same deaths, different form: the poem instructs, the play devastates, and the difference is the mixture.

The compression is itself a formal argument for Brooke's side. Nine months of story permit causation of the ordinary kind, habits forming, positions hardening, a friar's scheme ripening at leisure. Four days permit none of it. At the play's tempo, no character has time to become the kind of self-knowing agent Bradley's theory requires, and the lovers' consistency, their refusal to deliberate their way out of the story, reads not as shallowness but as the tempo's product. Shakespeare chose the tempo. The choice is documented in the comparison with his source, where every leisurely interval has been cut: the weeks of window courtship reduced to one night, the months of marriage to one dawn. A playwright content to dramatize his poem would have kept its calendar. A playwright conducting Brooke's experiment, testing what comic machinery does when its time runs out, would cut exactly as this one cut. The source evidence cannot prove intention, and Brooke's case does not need it to. Design is visible in the pattern of choices, and the choices, measured against the poem, all cut the same way.

The poem also explains the play's strangest structural feature, the Prologue, by way of its own Argument. Arthur Brooke prefixed his tale with a summary sonnet of the whole story, love inflaming the pair, the secret wedding, the killing, the banishment, the potion, the double death, in fourteen lines. Shakespeare took over the device and changed its function: where the poem's sonnet merely tables the contents, the play's Prologues enter the action as one of its modes, lyric frames that keep restating the tragic pattern while the comic engine runs inside it. A borrowed table of contents became the experiment's control panel, announcing at the rise of the curtain and again at the second act the terms on which everything seen will be judged. Brooke the critic, alert to stage pictures and formal frames, was the first reader of the play to give those frames their full structural weight, and the source comparison shows the weight is earned: the device is inherited, the use is invented, and invention in the use of inherited devices is what experiment means in a working theatre.

## The Book's Architecture: Where the Chapter Stands Among the Six

Brooke's chapter on the play gains force from its neighbours, because the book is built as a demonstration and the demonstration depends on contrast. The Titus Andronicus chapter confronts the most Senecan of the six, a tragedy of ritual atrocity and revenge patterning, and finds its form in patterned spectacle. The Richard III chapter, as its surviving discussions show, reads that play's structure as symmetry and irony expounded in sequence, a pattern of little tragedies leading from Clarence through the murdered princes' uncles to Richard himself, the villain composing his own fall as a series of cameos. The Richard II chapter treats a tragedy of ceremony, where the mode is liturgical and the catastrophe a deposition conducted as a rite. The Julius Caesar chapter finds the Senecan shape of revenge tragedy inhabited by public rhetoric, and closes on Brooke's sentence about the developing sensitivity to varying modes of utterance. The Hamlet chapter ends the survey at the point where the modes begin to fuse into the compound manner of the great tragedies. Set in that company, the Romeo and Juliet chapter is the demonstration's extreme case: the play whose materials are furthest from tragic convention and whose achieved form is therefore the strongest proof that the period's tragedy is a field of experiments rather than a single road.

The architecture also clarifies what Brooke means by calling the plays early. Early, in his usage, is not a synonym for immature; it names the decade before the modes fused, when each attempt had to solve the problem of tragedy separately and the solutions could be compared. Hamlet closes the group because in Hamlet the comparison starts to collapse into synthesis, the lyric, the comic and the ceremonial all present but no longer separable into strands an audience can watch interfering. Romeo and Juliet stands near the group's centre because its strands are still gloriously visible: a spectator can point to the sonnet, the nurse's prose, the Prince's edict and say which mode is speaking. Brooke's form criticism is at bottom a defence of that visibility as an artistic good. The play does not hide its joins. It gilds them, frames them in choruses, rhymes them into couplets, and the candour of the construction is among the sources of its peculiar brightness, a tragedy that shows its working and breaks the heart anyway.

## The Ending's Formal Debts: Statues, Gold and the Last Picture

The last scene is where Brooke's reading either collects its debts or defaults, and it collects. Consider the materials the ending is built from. A tomb opened at night by lantern light; a count's corpse and a friar's tools; a suicide by poison at a bride's bier; a waking, a dagger, and the line in which Juliet envies the weapon its rest; a watch, a prince, two fathers and a pageant of explanation in which the Friar narrates the plot to the city like a chorus made flesh. Every element is a comic device at the end of its inversion. The tomb is the assignation place of the orchard scenes turned inside out, darkness now permanent, the beloved above now the beloved below ground. The Friar's narration is the go-between's errand completed at last, the message delivered to everyone, too late for its purpose and exactly in time for its moral. The reconciliation of the houses, comedy's closing chord, is struck in full and in gold: Montague promises a statue of Juliet in pure gold, Capulet answers with Romeo's, and the fathers compete in grief with the same reflex that made them compete in enmity, the feud's energy surviving its object into the currency of memorial. Brooke's stage picture method finds its final exhibit here, two golden children promised above a pair of real ones on biers, the emblem completed and glossed by the Prince's closing couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310).

The statues deserve a sentence of their own in the adjudication between experiment and unevenness, because they are the detail an unevenness reading cannot use and an experiment reading cannot do without. A botched apprentice ending would close on the reconciliation sentimentally, the houses united, the lesson learned. This ending closes on an image of the lovers converted into treasure by the world that destroyed them, their value finally legible to Verona in the only denomination Verona trusts. The lyric children become civic monuments; the private mode is absorbed by the public mode at the price of death; the mixture resolves into an emblem that shows the resolution's cost. That is not the ending of a play that lost control of its materials in the third act. It is the ending of a play whose design has been, from the Prologue's first couplet, an argument about what the city does to the forms of love, and Brooke's chapter remains the account that saw the argument whole.

## The Book's Afterlife: Five Decades in Print

The standing of Shakespeare's Early Tragedies can be read off its publishing history, which the bibliographical record documents plainly. First published by Methuen in 1968, the book returned in a Routledge and Kegan Paul paperback in 1973, and Routledge kept it in print through successive reissues, including the hardcover and paperback editions of the 1990s, the 2005 reissue in the Routledge Shakespeare collections and the 2013 edition that carries the book into the digital catalogue. Survey chapters on the early tragedies cite it as the founding exception, as Hunter did within six years of publication; editions of the individual plays list it in their bibliographies as a matter of course; and its governing thought, that the earliest tragedies are a set of experiments in kind rather than a staircase of failures, has passed into the common sense of the field so completely that its origin is often forgotten. Brooke himself spent his career at the University of East Anglia, where he became professor of English literature and in time emeritus, and the Macbeth edition of 1990 stands as the book's method applied by its author to the task of constituting a text. Critics are not always granted a second instrument for testing their first. Brooke built his own, and the edition's authority in the Oxford series is the profession's certificate that the formalism of 1968 was a discipline and not a mood.

For the play at the centre of this article, the afterlife has a particular shape. The experiment thesis migrated from the monograph into the introductions of the standard editions, from the editions into the textbooks, and from the textbooks into the expectations audiences bring to the theatre, where productions now routinely stage the first half at full comic stretch, trusting the design Brooke described to make the second half land as reversal rather than rupture. A critical idea that changes how a play is physically performed, four centuries after its first performance, has passed the hardest test criticism offers. Brooke's chapter asked readers to see a form where they had been taught to see a flaw. The stage, the edition trade and the survey literature have spent fifty years agreeing.

## Brooke's Criteria in Practice: Telling an Experiment from a Mess

The standing worry about Brooke's method, raised in the objections above, is that experiment becomes an honorific any play can claim. The worry can be retired by stating the criteria the chapter actually applies, since they are strict enough to fail plays, and several of the period's tragedies would fail them. Five tests emerge from Brooke's practice. Announcement: does the play declare its design early, in a frame, a chorus, a patterned opening, so that the audience watches the design and not merely the story? Recurrence: do its devices, images and stage pictures return, so that the second half can be audited against the first? Inversion: do the returns reverse an outcome or a polarity, proving that the recurrence is argumentative rather than merely economical? Register discipline: is the distribution of modes, of verse kinds and prose, systematic enough to map? Divergence: measured against its source or its genre's norm, do the play's departures all cut the same way, toward the mixture claimed? A play that passes all five is an experiment by evidence, not by courtesy. Romeo and Juliet passes all five at maximum strength, which is why Brooke chose it as his demonstration's centre and why the chapter still reads as a proof rather than as a plea.

The same tests show the method's honesty by the company they exclude. A loosely built tragedy of the period, assembled from a chronicle by joinery, fails announcement and inversion: its episodes recur only as chronology requires, and nothing in its second half answers its first. Even within Brooke's own six, the tests discriminate. Richard II passes recurrence and register discipline triumphantly, its ceremonies answering one another across the deposition, and its experiment is correspondingly secure. Julius Caesar passes announcement less cleanly, and Brooke's chapter on it is correspondingly the book's most hesitant, its verdict an appreciation of rhetorical experiment rather than of achieved design. The gradient matters. A method that finds degrees of success inside its own chosen canon is measuring something real, and the measurement of Romeo and Juliet stands at the gradient's top not because Brooke favoured the play but because the play's paperwork, sonnet frames, inverted devices, register map and source divergences, is the most complete in the group. Readers who doubt the verdict are invited to run the five tests on the rival hypothesis and count the coincidences it must postulate. Unevenness, as a theory, has to explain away the announcement in the Prologue, the symmetry of the returns, the timing of the choruses' withdrawal, the inversion of the wedding machinery and the single direction of the source cuts, five separate accidents conspiring to look like a design. Experiment explains the same five facts with one postulate. Brooke's chapter is, among its other virtues, an exercise in critical economy, and economy of that order is what a verdict looks like when it has been earned.

The five tests also give teachers and students a way to use Brooke without merely citing him, since each test assigns a task: find the announcement, audit the returns, mark the inversions, map the registers, collate the source. A seminar that works through the play with those five assignments discovers the design in the order Brooke discovered it, and the discovery, being procedural, survives disagreement about every other question the play raises, about fate, about the feud, about the lovers' wisdom. Method that can be handed on as procedure rather than as doctrine is the rarest kind of critical legacy, and it is the form Brooke's influence has in fact taken wherever his book has been taught.

## The Play Among the Six: A Comparative Glance

The comparative frame is not background to Brooke's chapter; it is part of the chapter's proof, because the experiment claim for Romeo and Juliet is strengthened by the behaviour of the plays around it. Consider Richard III, the nearest neighbour in the survey. Brooke reads its structure as a pattern expounded in sequence, symmetry and irony fulfilled across a series of little tragedies, Clarence, then Rivers, Vaughan and Grey, then Hastings, Buckingham and at last Richard himself, each fall a cameo in a design the villain composes and the design then consumes. The unit there is the episode, patterned by repetition, and the mode is a grotesque chronicle energy, inclined toward comedy in Richard's own commentary even as the bodies accumulate. Set beside it, Romeo and Juliet shows the same young dramatist patterning by recurrence with entirely different units: not episodes of a progress but devices of a machine, not falls in sequence but returns in inversion. The family resemblance is the patterning instinct. The difference is the material patterned, and the difference is the book's thesis in action, variety within a single developing practice.

Titus Andronicus supplies the opposite pole. Its materials are tragedy's own, Senecan revenge, ritual mutilation, the banquet of horrors, and its experiment, on Brooke's showing, concerns how much patterned spectacle a stage can bear. Romeo and Juliet borrows nothing from that shelf; its horrors arrive by post and potion rather than by the knife, and its single scene of public killing, the duel of 3.1, is over in a flurry of interventions and misunderstandings rather than staged as ritual. Richard II, the survey's other lyric tragedy, offers the instructive middle case: a play whose ceremonial mode is sustained almost unbroken, deposition as liturgy, where Romeo and Juliet subjects its lyric mode to constant interference from prose, edict and brawl. Hamlet, closing the group, begins the fusion of the strands that the earlier plays hold apart. Read in Brooke's sequence, the play of the lovers is the one in which the interference itself is the subject, the only member of the six whose form dramatizes the collision of its own components. That distinctness is not a ranking, since Brooke's whole point is that the kinds are incommensurable, but it explains the chapter's gravitational pull on later criticism. The play became the test case for formal pluralism because it is the member of the group where pluralism has the most to explain, a tragedy announcing itself in a sonnet, marrying its principals at its centre, killing them with comedy's tools and closing on an emblem of gold. Any theory of Shakespearean tragedy that cannot house it is too small, and the theories that preceded Brooke's were exactly that size.

## The Verdict on Brooke

Brooke's contribution, weighed whole, is the recovery of the play's form as an object of admiration equal to its poetry. He did not discover the mixture, which every reader feels, and he did not invent the comedy in the first acts, which every audience enjoys. He did the harder thing: he supplied the framework in which the mixture stops being a blemish to be excused and becomes a design to be traced, and he did it by breaking the monopoly of the single tragic pattern that had made the mixture illegible. Against Snyder, his mixture contains her hinge. Against Hunter, his pluralism covers the play Hunter's genealogy cannot reach. Against the unevenness charge, his recurrences are checkable and check out. The one lasting caution is the method's temptation toward licence, and it is a caution Brooke's own practice, in the chapter's controls and in the discipline of his later editing, shows how to observe. The play that Bradley could not use became, in Brooke's hands, the proof that Shakespearean tragedy was never one thing. That is the verdict, and the form of Romeo and Juliet is its evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Brooke summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Brooke summary is the table in this article that sets out the elements of Nicholas Brooke's reading of the play's tragic form: the refusal of a single tragic pattern, the variety of the six early tragedies, the mixture of lyric, comic and tragic modes, the wedding at the design's centre, the comic devices returning inverted, the emblematic stage picture, and the verdict that the mixed form is experiment rather than flaw. The pattern it exposes is recurrence with reversal: every device the first half assembles comes back in the second half with its outcome turned, and the table arranges Brooke's claims in the order his book builds them so that each can be checked against the scenes it names.

### Q: When was Shakespeare's Early Tragedies published, and which six plays does it study?

Shakespeare's Early Tragedies was first published by Methuen in London in 1968, and it studies Titus Andronicus, Richard III, Romeo and Juliet, Richard II, Julius Caesar and Hamlet, the catastrophic plays of Shakespeare's first decade in the theatre. Brooke dates the sequence from about 1593 to the turn of the century and treats the six not as steps toward the great tragedies but as parallel trials of what tragedy could be. The book runs to a little over two hundred pages, was reissued in paperback by Routledge and Kegan Paul in 1973, and has been kept in print by Routledge through successive editions, a publishing life that reflects its standing as the founding modern account of the early tragedies as a group.

### Q: Who was Nicholas Brooke, and where did he teach?

Nicholas Brooke was a British Shakespeare scholar who spent his career at the University of East Anglia, where he became professor of English literature and in time emeritus professor. His reputation rests on two connected achievements. The first is Shakespeare's Early Tragedies of 1968, which replaced the idea of a single tragic pattern with a survey of experiments in tragic kind across the plays of the 1590s. The second is his work as an editor, above all the Oxford Shakespeare edition of Macbeth published in 1990, in which the formalism of the early book is applied to the constituting of a text. The combination matters for judging his reading of Romeo and Juliet: his account of form was tested across a career, and in the discipline of an edition, rather than asserted once and abandoned.

### Q: What does Brooke mean by calling the play's form an experiment?

Brooke means that Shakespeare, working in a decade when the conventions of Elizabethan tragedy had not yet hardened into a single form, deliberately tried out an untested combination: a tragedy constructed from the materials of lyric poetry and romantic comedy. An experiment, in his usage, is not a guess or a jeu d'esprit but a controlled trial, identifiable by its design features, an announced pattern, devices that recur and invert, registers of speech distributed systematically, and departures from the source that all cut the same way. His sentence about the early work in general, a developing sensitivity to varying modes of utterance with which Shakespeare experimented, states the claim at its most compact. Romeo and Juliet is the extreme case in his survey because its materials are the furthest from tragic convention, which is what makes its success the experiment's strongest result.

### Q: How does the Prologue sonnet support Brooke's claim that the form is a mixture rather than a sequence?

The Prologue sonnet supports the mixture because it fuses the lyric and tragic modes before the action begins: a sonnet, the period's finished form for love, is made to carry the feud, the two deaths and the reconciliation in fourteen lines, so that tragedy is present as a strand of the design from the first minute rather than arriving later as a replacement for comedy. A sequential account, in which the play begins as comedy and becomes tragedy, has to treat the Prologue as an ironic frame standing outside the action it announces. Brooke's reading takes the verse at its word. The second Chorus performs the same fusion in miniature, describing one desire substituted for another within a continuing endangered structure, which is the play's method at every level of its construction.

### Q: Why does Brooke place the wedding at the centre of the play's design?

Brooke places the wedding at the centre because it is the point where the comic structure completes itself and changes hands. Romantic comedy ends at the altar, its machinery of lovers, go-betweens and a helpful fixer having delivered the generations to reconciliation. This wedding is reached on schedule, in Friar Laurence's cell in 2.6, but it is private, unwitnessed by the families and incapable of reconciling anyone, and within hours the bridegroom has killed the bride's cousin. The goal of the form has been achieved in a manner that voids the goal's function, and the machinery, its work nominally done, runs on into the second half with comedy's providence withdrawn. On Brooke's reading that placement is the design's boldest stroke: the tragedy is not bolted onto a finished comedy but generated from inside the comedy's success.

### Q: What happens to the Chorus after Act 2, and what does Brooke's reading make of its disappearance?

After the second Chorus, no chorus appears again: the sonnet frames that open Acts 1 and 2 fall silent for the remaining three acts. Brooke's reading treats the withdrawal as part of the design rather than as carelessness. The choruses exist to state the pattern while the pattern is still a diagram, briefing the audience and holding it in the superior knowledge that comedy grants its watchers. Once the wedding has made the diagram flesh, the briefing stops, the audience is demoted to the characters' level of ignorance about stopped letters and mistimed arrivals, and the chorus function migrates into the action itself, surfacing finally in the Friar's narration of the whole plot to the assembled city in 5.3. The frame withdraws at exactly the moment its guarantees would become a lie.

### Q: Why does the stopped letter count as evidence for Brooke's design rather than against the play?

The stopped letter counts for the design because it belongs to a system. Comedy's machinery runs on messages, tokens, errands and timings that arrive in time by the form's guarantee; this play builds the same economy, the Nurse's delayed errands, the ring sent to Mantua, the Friar's letter, and then withdraws the guarantee, so that the machinery's most routine failure becomes fatal. Brooke's point is that chance in the play is systematically prepared rather than casually invoked: the action talks about fortune at every joint, from the Prologue's crossed stars to Romeo's cry that he is fortune's fool. A catastrophe produced by a device the whole design has been exhibiting is a thesis about causation, not an evasion of it, and it answers Bradley's objection by relocating accident inside the experiment.

### Q: What is a mode of utterance, in Brooke's usage?

A mode of utterance is the register in which a play or a scene speaks: Senecan rhetoric, chronicle ceremony, lyric intimacy, prose wit. Brooke uses the phrase as his unit of formal analysis in place of character or theme, arguing that Shakespeare's early work shows a developing sensitivity to varying modes of utterance, experimenting with what each can carry. In Romeo and Juliet the modes can be mapped across the text: completed lyric forms assigned to the lovers and the choruses, public verse to authority, wit combat to the young men, prose to the servants and the Nurse. The play's form, on this account, is the pattern of interference among those modes, and its tragedy is what happens when the lovers' finished lyric speech meets a city that speaks in fragments, edicts and jokes.

### Q: How does G. K. Hunter's account of the earliest tragedies differ from Brooke's?

Hunter's account differs in its sense of what the mode of early tragedy is. In his essay "Shakespeare's Earliest Tragedies: Titus Andronicus and Romeo and Juliet," published in Shakespeare Survey 27 in 1974, Hunter singled Brooke out as the obvious exception to the critical refusal to consider the earliest tragedies together, and then registered his reservation: Brooke's brief was much larger, covering six plays, and his sense of the mode of tragedy was rather different from Hunter's own. Hunter pursued the mode through inherited convention, Senecan ritual and moral structure, where Brooke pursued it through the variety of kinds each play invents. The adjudication favours Brooke for this play, whose Senecan content is negligible and whose materials are the sonnet, the novella and the romantic comedy, while Hunter's genealogy explains Titus Andronicus more fully.

### Q: What does the musicians' scene in Act 4 show about Brooke's mixture?

The musicians' scene shows the comic mode persisting after its licence has been revoked, which is the mixture's signature in miniature. In 4.5, with the supposedly dead Juliet discovered and the household lamenting in formal parallel phrases, Peter and the musicians haggle over music, payment and the meaning of a song like a clown routine left running in a house of mourning. A sequential account of the form, comedy first and tragedy after, must treat the scene as a leftover embarrassment. Brooke's account treats it as evidence: the modes are layered rather than successive, the tragic action ascends while comedy continues underneath at diminished wattage, and the audience's tonal discomfort is the design registering itself, the grotesque pressure of a world whose personnel have not been told that the story changed kind around them.

### Q: What does Shakespeare's compression of Arthur Brooke's nine months into four days show about the play's design?

The compression shows that the play's tempo is a chosen instrument of its form. Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562 spreads the story across some nine months, allowing a season of married happiness and the slow ripening of consequences; Shakespeare cuts the courtship to a night, the marriage to a dawn and the whole action to a span of days, deleting in the process the poem's moralizing frame. At that tempo no character can deliberate a way out of the story, and accidents of timing, a delayed errand, a stopped letter, become the causal system itself. Measured against the source, every cut moves the same direction, toward machinery under time pressure, which is why the comparison functions as the control in Brooke's experiment: the same story without the mixture instructs, and with it devastates.

### Q: How did Brooke's book change the reputation of Titus Andronicus and the other early plays?

Brooke's book changed their reputation by changing the question asked of them. Plays that had been graded as failures against the pattern of the great tragedies, Titus Andronicus as a crude bloodbath, Richard III as a villain's romp, Richard II as a pageant of speeches, became legible as experiments in distinct tragic kinds once the single pattern was withdrawn, each to be analysed by the experience it creates and the modes it commands. Hunter's 1974 essay, dissenting in method, accepts the new premise by treating the earliest tragedies together as tragedies, and the subsequent rehabilitation of Titus in study and performance proceeds on ground the book cleared. The general consequence reaches beyond the six plays: a work's refusal to resemble Hamlet became the beginning of its analysis rather than the end of it.

### Q: What did Brooke bring to his edition of Macbeth from the argument of his 1968 book?

Brooke brought the method itself: the refusal to reduce a tragedy to a case study of its hero's character, and the habit of reading form through pattern, ritual and the disposition of scenes. His Oxford Shakespeare edition of Macbeth, published in 1990, served the profession as the formalism of Shakespeare's Early Tragedies translated into editorial practice, where every claim about what a play is doing must survive contact with the constitution of its text. The edition matters to the assessment of the Romeo and Juliet chapter because it certifies the discipline behind the experiment thesis. A critic whose notion of form were a licence to call any inconsistency a mode could not have sustained the exactitudes of a standard edition; Brooke sustained them across the decades that separate the two works.

### Q: How does Brian Gibbons's Arden introduction stand in relation to Brooke's reading?

Gibbons's introduction stands as the editorial ratification of the climate Brooke created. The Arden edition of 1980, whose lineation this article follows for its quotations, introduces the play to first readers as a work whose generic mixture is its defining structural fact, following the comic conventions of the first half into the tragic design they are made to serve. An edition's introduction occupies a special evidentiary position: it must tell a new reader what kind of play is in hand, briefly and without a monograph's evasions, and be right. That the standard editions describe a designed mixture where the editions of Bradley's century described a flawed tragedy with beautiful passages marks the distance the discipline travelled between 1904 and the settlement Brooke's book did most to establish.

### Q: Why does Brooke treat the golden statues as the play's final stage picture rather than as a sentimental extra?

Brooke treats the statues as the final stage picture because they complete the play's visual argument. His method reads the early tragedies through composed dispositions of bodies that fix meaning as an emblem does: Juliet at the window above Romeo in the orchard, the lovers on the bier in the tomb, and finally the promised statues, the fathers vowing images of the children in pure gold above the real bodies. The picture is not decoration on the reconciliation; it is the reconciliation's content, the lovers converted into civic treasure by the world that destroyed them, their value legible at last in the only denomination Verona trusts. A sentimental extra could be cut without loss. Remove the statues and the design loses its closing emblem, the emblem that shows what the mixture's resolution costs.

### Q: What is the hardest objection to calling the mixed form an experiment, and does it overturn Brooke?

The hardest objection is methodological: a criticism that refuses every single pattern risks calling whatever a play does its experiment, making failure impossible and the term empty. The objection has real force against careless uses of Brooke's pluralism, and it is answered from within his practice by naming the controls an experiment must show: an announced design, devices that recur, returns that invert, a systematic distribution of registers, and departures from the source that cut one way. Romeo and Juliet passes all five tests at maximum strength, so the objection disciplines the method without overturning its verdict on this play. Where a play offered no such patterning, the honest Brookean verdict would be unevenness; the method can fail a play, and that is what entitles it to pass this one.

### Q: How does Juliet's changing command of verse forms support Brooke's modal reading?

Juliet's progress through the play's registers is the modal reading plotted on a single figure. She begins in 1.3 speaking obedient tags inside her mother's periods, holds her half of a perfect sonnet against a stranger at the feast, conducts the orchard scene's negotiation by inventing and revising the language of contract, commands the epithalamium of 3.2 and the solitary aria of the potion speech in 4.3, and speaks the play's last private lines in the tomb. What grows, on Brooke's showing, is not a psychology in Bradley's sense but a command of utterance, mode by mode, and the tragedy completes itself when Verona's most accomplished speaker finds no honoured form left except silence and the dagger. The curve is measurable in the text's forms, which is what makes it evidence rather than impression.

### Q: What role does the feud play in Brooke's formal account, if the tragedy is built from lyric and comedy?

The feud is the tragedy's public body, the element that converts private lyric and comic machinery into catastrophe. In Brooke's modal terms it belongs neither to the lyric strand nor to the comic strand but governs their interference: it supplies the blocking force that comedy's fathers represent in miniature, armed here with swords, edicts and a death sentence legislated over the opening brawl. It is also the reason the lovers' completed forms cannot circulate: every message, errand and rite must cross the feud's territory, and the crossing is where the machinery fails. Formally, the feud is what the mixture is about, the social fact that makes a sonnet dangerous and a wedding clandestine, and its reconciliation over the bodies, conducted in competitive promises of gold, is the public mode absorbing the private one at the price the Prologue announced.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Brooke summary to argue that the play's mixture is design rather than defect?

An essay should use the summary as a checklist of auditable claims rather than as an authority. Begin from the table's verdict row, then test the rows beneath it against the text: the Prologue's announcement, the wedding's placement at the structural centre, the comic devices returning inverted in the potion plot, the choruses' timed withdrawal, the register map of sonnet, edict, wit and prose. Each row names the scenes where its claim can be verified or falsified, which is what separates Brooke's case from the older habit of praising the poetry and apologizing for the construction. The essay's strongest closing move is comparative: set the pattern beside Arthur Brooke's poem, where the same story without the mixture produces a moralized exemplum, and let the difference carry the conclusion that the mixture is the tragedy's engineered cause.
