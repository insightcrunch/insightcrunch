The Article 370 abrogation of August 2019 was executed through a sequence of instruments issued within forty-eight hours: a Presidential Order on 5 August 2019 that rewrote the interpretive clause of the Constitution as it applied to Jammu and Kashmir, a second Presidential declaration on 5 August 2019 that rendered Article 370 itself inoperative, a statutory resolution passed by both Houses of Parliament, and the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019, which divided the state into the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir and the Union Territory of Ladakh with effect from 31 October 2019.

## Two Readings of August 2019, and Why Each One Alone Fails

Standard treatments of the abrogation divide into two camps that rarely engage each other. The first camp reads August 2019 as a political decision: the Bharatiya Janata Party had carried the removal of Jammu and Kashmir's special status as a commitment from the Jana Sangh era through the party manifestos of 2014 and 2019, and a single-party Lok Sabha majority converted a longstanding pledge into action. The second camp reads August 2019 as a constitutional puzzle: the interesting questions, on this view, concern the mechanics of Article 367 and Article 370(3), the substitution of one assembly for another inside a constitutional clause, and the validity of a procedure that used the provision to be abolished as the instrument of its own abolition. Each reading captures a real dimension. Each reading, taken alone, misses the substance that made August 2019 a decisive moment in the constitutional history of the Indian Union.

The political-decision reading explains motive and timing but cannot explain form. A government holding 303 Lok Sabha seats after the 2019 general election possessed many routes toward the same end, including a constitutional amendment under Article 368 carried by the special majorities the ruling coalition commanded in the Rajya Sabha with allied support. The route actually chosen was narrower, faster and stranger: it ran through the text of Article 370 itself, and it depended on a chain of substitutions that began with the interpretive clause, Article 367. Why a government with parliamentary strength chose the interpretive route over the amendment route is a question the political reading cannot answer, because the answer sits inside constitutional procedure: the amendment route would have required confronting the question of whose consent Jammu and Kashmir had to give, and the interpretive route was designed to make that question answerable by institutions the Union government itself controlled in August 2019.

The constitutional-procedure reading explains form but cannot explain timing or consequence. Constitutional lawyers debated the Article 367 device for decades before 2019; A.G. Noorani, in Article 370: A Constitutional History of Jammu and Kashmir (2011), had mapped the clause-by-clause architecture of the special status and the contested history of its erosion long before the Presidential Orders were drafted. The device existed on paper through the Vajpayee years, the Manmohan Singh years and the first Modi term without being used. A reading that stays inside the text cannot explain why the device was activated on 5 August 2019 rather than in 2003 or 2016, nor can it explain the operational architecture that accompanied the legal instruments: the preventive detentions, the communication restrictions, the bifurcation of the state, and the four-year wait for a Supreme Court verdict that arrived on 11 December 2023.

The integrated reading defended in this article holds that the political and procedural dimensions were a single design. The abrogation reversed a specific, documented act of political restraint: the deferral of Article 370 in the National Agenda for Governance adopted by the National Democratic Alliance in March 1998, when coalition arithmetic forced the Bharatiya Janata Party to shelve the demand. The procedural route chosen in 2019 was the mechanism that made the reversal executable without the consent of any elected Jammu and Kashmir institution, because the state's legislature stood dissolved and its powers vested in Parliament under President's Rule. And the Supreme Court's unanimous validation in In Re Article 370, delivered on 11 December 2023, settled the constitutional question in the Union's favour on each contested point, even as the political question, the question of statehood, consent and consequence in Jammu, in the Kashmir Valley and in Ladakh, remained open through the assembly elections of October 2024 and beyond them. The namable claim of this article follows from that integration: the August 2019 abrogation reversed the 1998 deferral through a procedural route that bypassed the Constituent Assembly mechanism; the December 2023 validation settled the constitutional question and left the political question open.

### Did the abrogation need a constitutional amendment under Article 368?

No constitutional amendment under Article 368 was moved or passed. The Union government proceeded entirely through Presidential Orders issued under Article 370 itself, a statutory resolution under Article 370(3), and an ordinary statute, the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019, passed by simple majorities of members present and voting in each House on 5 and 6 August 2019.

The choice of route carried the argument of the entire operation. An Article 368 amendment would have placed the special status before Parliament as an open constitutional question and would have invited the objection that Article 370, by its own clause (3), had assigned the decisive recommendation to the Constituent Assembly of Jammu and Kashmir, a body dissolved in 1956. The interpretive route avoided reopening that assignment in terms. It amended the dictionary of the Constitution rather than the provision, changing what the words "Constituent Assembly" meant when read inside Article 370, and then acted on the changed meaning. Critics, among them Noorani in his final writings and the petitioners before the Constitution Bench, described the route as a fraud on the Constitution, a phrase with a long pedigree in Indian constitutional argument. Defenders, among them the Solicitor General Tushar Mehta in argument and the Union Home Minister Amit Shah on the floor of the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2019, described the route as the only reading that prevented a dissolved body from exercising a permanent veto over constitutional change. The Supreme Court, in December 2023, accepted the defenders' position on the procedure. The distance between those two descriptions is the distance this article measures.

## The Founding Bargain: Accession, Article 370 and the Promise of a Constituent Assembly

Article 370 entered the Constitution of India as the textual residue of a specific wartime bargain. Maharaja Hari Singh, the Dogra ruler of the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir, signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947 after tribal raiders backed from Pakistan had entered the state and advanced toward Srinagar. The Governor-General, Lord Mountbatten, accepted the accession on 27 October 1947, and Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar the same morning. The Instrument, in the standard form used for princely accessions, ceded only three subjects to the Dominion of India: defence, external affairs and communications. Mountbatten's acceptance letter added a political commitment that the accession question would be settled by a reference to the people once law and order had been restored, a commitment Jawaharlal Nehru repeated in his broadcast of 2 November 1947 and in subsequent statements to the United Nations Security Council in January 1948.

Those founding facts matter to the abrogation argument because every later position claimed descent from them. The founding tensions of the Indian Constitution, the religion-state tension and the federal-unitary tension among them, were both present at the creation of Article 370, as [the religion-state and federal-unitary founding tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) worked themselves out in a provision drafted to hold a Muslim-majority princely state inside a Union whose founding settlement was being written in the same months. Sheikh Abdullah, who led the Jammu and Kashmir National Conference and became the state's Prime Minister in March 1948, negotiated the terms of the relationship with Delhi through N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, the former Dewan of Kashmir who sat in Nehru's cabinet without portfolio and who became the draftsman of the provision.

Ayyangar moved the draft provision, numbered 306A in the Drafting Committee's numbering and renumbered Article 370 in the final text, in the Constituent Assembly of India on 17 October 1949. His speech of that date remains the primary record of the provision's intended character. Ayyangar told the Assembly that the provision was framed for the special circumstances of Kashmir: the state was not yet ripe for the complete integration applied to other princely states, the matter stood before the United Nations, and the people of the state, acting through their own Constituent Assembly, would determine the final shape of the relationship. The text he moved gave that promise a legal address. Article 370(3) provided that the President could declare the article inoperative, or operative with exceptions and modifications, by public notification, but only on the recommendation of the Constituent Assembly of the state. The clause built the consent of a named Kashmiri body into the termination procedure of the special status itself.

The structure of the full article deserves statement, because the 2019 procedure operated on each of its clauses. Article 370(1) limited Parliament's legislative power over Jammu and Kashmir to the Instrument of Accession subjects, extendable to other subjects only with the concurrence of the state government, and provided in clause (1)(d) that other provisions of the Indian Constitution could be applied to the state by Presidential Order with exceptions and modifications, again requiring concurrence for matters beyond the accession subjects. Article 370(2) required that concurrence given before the state Constituent Assembly met be placed before that Assembly for decision. Article 370(3) carried the termination mechanism and its Constituent Assembly condition. The article sat in Part XXI of the Constitution, headed "Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions", a placement the Supreme Court would treat as evidence of temporary character in 2023, and that defenders of the special status would counter by pointing to other long-lived provisions housed in the same Part.

The state Constituent Assembly whose recommendation clause (3) required was elected in 1951, framed the Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir, adopted it on 17 November 1956, and dissolved itself in January 1957 when that Constitution took effect on 26 January 1957. Between its election and its dissolution, that Assembly ratified the accession, endorsed the Delhi Agreement of July 1952 between Nehru and Sheikh Abdullah, which confirmed the special status architecture, and made no recommendation under Article 370(3). The legal consequence debated for the following six decades followed directly: the body named in the termination clause had ceased to exist without exercising the power the clause assigned to it. One school of argument, stated most fully by Noorani across five decades of writing, held that the termination power had lapsed with the body, leaving Article 370 permanent in practice because its ending mechanism had become impossible to operate. A second school, held by the Union government of 2019 and accepted by the Supreme Court in 2023, held that a constitutional power could not be extinguished by the disappearance of the recommending body, and that the recommendation function could be read, through interpretive amendment, as passing to the state's Legislative Assembly. The entire procedural history of August 2019 is an argument between those two schools conducted in the form of official instruments.

### Why did Article 370 require a recommendation from the state Constituent Assembly?

Clause (3) of Article 370 made the President's power to declare the article inoperative conditional on the recommendation of the Constituent Assembly of Jammu and Kashmir. Ayyangar's speech of 17 October 1949 explained the design: the final relationship between the state and the Union was to be settled by the state's own constitution-making body, not imposed from Delhi.

The asymmetric character of the arrangement placed Article 370 inside a wider founding design. The Constitution of 1950 did not treat all states identically; it carried special provisions for several regions and a general architecture in which the Union held residuary and emergency powers of unusual depth, the design analysed as [Article 370 and the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) within Indian federalism. Jammu and Kashmir stood at the far end of that asymmetry: alone among states it retained its own constitution, its own flag under the Delhi Agreement settlement, and a definition of permanent residents whose rights in land and public employment were protected first by the state constitution and after 1954 by Article 35A. The asymmetry was the point. It registered the terms on which a contested accession, in a state whose ruler was Hindu, whose majority population was Muslim, and whose territory was claimed in full by Pakistan, had been brought inside the Union in 1947 and held there through the first India-Pakistan war of 1947 to 1948.

## Article 35A, the 1954 Order and the Long Erosion Before 2019

The constitutional relationship that ended in August 2019 had been altered repeatedly across the intervening decades, and any honest account of the abrogation must record the alterations, because the parties to the 2023 litigation argued from them in opposite directions. Petitioners contended that decades of incremental application had hollowed the special status in practice while preserving its form, and that 2019 merely completed an erosion by unconstitutional means. The Union contended that the same decades demonstrated that Article 370 had always functioned as a flexible application device rather than a sovereignty compact, and that its final use in 2019 differed in scale rather than in kind.

The central instrument of alteration was the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 1954, issued by President Rajendra Prasad on 14 May 1954 under Article 370(1), with the concurrence of the state government then headed by Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad. The 1954 Order applied large portions of the Indian Constitution to the state with modifications. It also inserted Article 35A into the Constitution as applied to Jammu and Kashmir. Article 35A empowered the state legislature to define permanent residents and to reserve for them rights in employment under the state government, acquisition of immovable property, settlement and scholarships, and it barred challenge to such laws on the ground that they violated the fundamental rights of other Indian citizens. Article 35A was never placed before Parliament as a constitutional amendment; it existed solely as an annexure of the 1954 Presidential Order. Its vulnerability on that ground, that a new constitutional provision had been created by executive order under a clause permitting application with modifications, formed a second line of litigation that ran beside the Article 370 cases and was overtaken by the events of August 2019, when the superseding Presidential Order of 5 August 2019 replaced the entire 1954 framework and Article 35A ceased to operate with it.

Subsequent Presidential Orders extended further provisions of the Indian Constitution to the state across the 1950s and 1960s, including the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the Election Commission and, in 1965, the application of Article 356, under which the Union could assume the functions of the state government, and Article 357. By the count placed before the Constitution Bench in 2023, some forty-five Presidential Orders had applied, extended or modified constitutional provisions in relation to Jammu and Kashmir between 1950 and 2019, and the state had been governed under central intervention for long stretches, including the Governor's Rule and President's Rule periods of the 1990s. The office of Sadar-i-Riyasat, the elected head of state contemplated by the Delhi Agreement, was replaced by a Governor appointed by the President in 1965, and the title of the state's head of government was changed from Prime Minister to Chief Minister the same year. Defenders of the 2019 action cited this record as proof that integration had proceeded by consent forms for seventy years; critics cited the same record as proof that consent forms had been obtainable because the state governments giving concurrence after 1953 were, in the critics' phrase, installed rather than elected, a characterisation the National Conference and the petitioners pressed and the Union disputed.

The political prehistory inside the state ran alongside the legal one. Sheikh Abdullah was dismissed and arrested in August 1953, an event Kashmiri political memory treats as the founding breach of the Delhi settlement; he returned to office in 1975 under the Indira-Abdullah Accord, which recorded his acceptance of Article 370 as it stood after two decades of alteration and described the state's status as settled within the Indian Union. The armed insurgency that began in 1989, the exodus of Kashmiri Pandits from the Valley in 1990, the long counterinsurgency, and the successive elected governments of the National Conference, the Congress and, after 2002, the People's Democratic Party, all unfolded inside a constitutional structure whose formal text still promised a special relationship that its operational content no longer delivered. That gap between text and operation is the ground on which the events of 2019 landed, and it explains why the argument over August 2019 so often becomes an argument over which decade deserves the moral weight: 1949, when the promise was drafted; 1953, when its principal Kashmiri guarantor was jailed; 1954, when the application machinery began its work; or 2019, when the text itself was switched off.


## The 1998 Deferral: What the National Agenda for Governance Shelved

The political half of the integrated reading rests on a document: the National Agenda for Governance, adopted by the National Democratic Alliance in March 1998 when the Bharatiya Janata Party formed its first durable coalition government under Atal Bihari Vajpayee. The demand for the abrogation of Article 370 had stood in the programmes of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh since the 1950s, when Syama Prasad Mookerjee had campaigned against the permit system and the separate constitutional position of Jammu and Kashmir, and had died in custody in Srinagar in June 1953 after entering the state in defiance of the entry permit requirement. The demand passed into the Bharatiya Janata Party's manifestos after 1980. In 1998, however, the price of coalition was deferral. Regional partners whose support the Vajpayee government required, among them parties for whom the special status was either a commitment or an irrelevance they refused to spend capital upon, insisted that the alliance's contentious commitments be set aside. On that history, [the 1998 NDA's tactical retreat on Article 370](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) belongs beside the deferral of the Ram temple construction and the uniform civil code in the same document: three founding demands of the party, each postponed by name in the interest of governability.

The deferral's terms matter for the 2019 comparison. The National Agenda did not renounce the demand; it recorded that the alliance would govern on the agreed agenda and that the contentious items stood outside it. Vajpayee's governments of 1998 and 1999 to 2004 accordingly left Article 370 untouched, and in 2000 the Vajpayee cabinet formally rejected the autonomy resolution passed by the Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly under Farooq Abdullah, which had sought restoration of the pre-1953 position, without moving against the status itself. The pattern repeated the structure of coalition-era politics across the period: single-party ideological commitments were traded for coalition survival, and constitutional change of the first magnitude was deferred by parties that lacked the numbers to carry it alone. The Bharatiya Janata Party's manifesto for the 2014 general election restored the commitment in print, promising to discuss Article 370 with stakeholders and to pursue its abrogation, and the manifesto of 2019 repeated the pledge in the same programme that promised the abrogation's companion measures.

Between the promise and the execution stood an alliance that repeated the 1998 structure in miniature. The People's Democratic Party, led by Mufti Mohammad Sayeed and after his death in January 2016 by Mehbooba Mufti, entered coalition with the Bharatiya Janata Party in March 2015 on an Agenda of Alliance that described Article 370 as a settled provision and committed both parties to the constitutional status quo for the state. For three years the national commitment was again deferred, this time by a state-level coalition document rather than a national one. The withdrawal of the Bharatiya Janata Party from that coalition on 19 June 2018 ended the second deferral. Governor's Rule followed under Governor N.N. Vohra and then Satya Pal Malik; the Legislative Assembly, kept in suspended animation for five months, was dissolved by Governor Malik on 21 November 2018; President's Rule was proclaimed on 19 December 2018 and extended in July 2019. Each step was defended by its authors on stated grounds, security, governmental paralysis, the impossibility of a stable majority, and each step was attacked by Kashmiri parties and by constitutional critics as the deliberate manufacture of the institutional vacuum that August 2019 required. That characterisation is disputed. The sequence itself is not: by the summer of 2019, Jammu and Kashmir had no elected legislature, its legislative powers stood transferred to Parliament under Article 357 as applied during President's Rule, and its Governor held office at the pleasure of the President whose orders would carry the abrogation.

## The Architecture of 2014 to 2019: Why the Constraint Set Changed

The integrated reading requires the political conditions to be stated as precisely as the legal ones. The coalition constraint that produced the 1998 deferral disappeared in stages. In May 2014 the Bharatiya Janata Party won 282 Lok Sabha seats, the first single-party majority since 1984, and formed a government in which coalition partners were welcome but not necessary. The framework of [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) reads the years after 2014 as a renegotiation of the founding settlements, federal, secular and developmental, by a parliamentary architecture that no longer needed to purchase majorities with deferrals. Within that architecture, [the post-2014 governance architecture](/2019/07/14/2014-election-inflection-reanalysed/) displayed a recurring preference for concentrated executive instruments over negotiated legislative processes, a preference whose sharpest earlier demonstration was the demonetisation of 8 November 2016, executed by executive notification under the Reserve Bank of India Act without prior parliamentary debate, as [the unilateral-executive-action precedent](/2019/07/20/demonetisation-november-2016-politics/) of that episode established in the political memory of both supporters and critics.

The contrast with the Goods and Services Tax is instructive and cuts in favour of the integrated reading. The GST, launched on 1 July 2017, required a constitutional amendment, the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act 2016, because no interpretive route existed: the tax powers of the Union and the states were textually distributed, and only Article 368 could redistribute them. The amendment passed through ratification by state legislatures and the construction of the GST Council, [the cooperative-federalism architecture context](/2019/07/25/gst-2017-cooperative-federalism-test/) in which fiscal federalism was renegotiated by negotiation. Article 370 presented the opposite configuration. A textual route existed inside the provision itself, clause (1)(d) for application orders and clause (3) for termination, and the only missing element was a recommending authority whose voice the Union could supply. Where the constitutional text forced negotiation, the post-2014 government negotiated. Where the text offered an executive route, the government took it. The difference in method between 2016 and 2019 was therefore not a difference in disposition alone; it was a difference in the affordances of the constitutional text, and the August 2019 procedure was designed around those affordances by lawyers who had read the provision as carefully as Noorani had.

The internal preparation for August 2019 remains the least documented part of the record, and precision requires the limits of the record to be marked. The brief for this article identifies the internal correspondence of the Ministry of Home Affairs in June and July 2019, and the vetting of the procedural design by the office of the Solicitor General, as an under-cited primary source made partially visible through litigation. What the public record establishes is circumstantial but consistent: the extension of President's Rule in July 2019, the deployment of additional paramilitary forces to the Kashmir Valley in the final week of July 2019, officially explained by the government as a security precaution, the advisory of 2 August 2019 directing tourists and Amarnath pilgrims to leave the Valley, and the absence of any public consultation with Jammu and Kashmir political parties before 5 August. Detained leaders and petitioners later characterised the preparation as evidence that the security rationale was a cover for a constitutional operation. The government characterised the same facts as prudent management of a foreseeable law and order risk. Both characterisations rest on the same chronology; the chronology is the settled part.

### Who held the powers of the Jammu and Kashmir legislature in August 2019?

Parliament held them. After the dissolution of the Legislative Assembly in November 2018 and the proclamation of President's Rule in December 2018, the legislative powers of the state stood vested in Parliament under the constitutional provisions applied to the proclamation, and the Governor administered the state on the President's behalf.

## The Five August Days: The Instruments in Sequence

The chronology of 5 and 6 August 2019 can be stated with documentary precision, and it should be, because the argument over legitimacy often dissolves a sequence of distinct instruments into a single gesture attributed to a single will. The instruments were distinct, they issued from distinct authorities, and each carried a distinct legal function in the chain.

On the morning of 5 August 2019, before Parliament took up the Kashmir resolutions, the President of India, Ram Nath Kovind, issued the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 2019, numbered C.O. 272, under Article 370(1). The Order superseded the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 1954 as amended over the decades, and applied the entire Constitution of India to Jammu and Kashmir. Within that application, the Order added a new clause (4) to Article 367 as it applied to the state. Article 367 is the Constitution's interpretation clause; it governs how references in the Constitution are to be read. The new clause directed, among other readings, that the reference in Article 370(3) to the Constituent Assembly of the state be read as a reference to the Legislative Assembly of the state, and that references to the government of the state be read, during President's Rule, as references to the Governor. In a single instrument, the identity of the recommending body under the termination clause was changed from a body that had ceased to exist in 1956 to a body whose functions Parliament was exercising that morning.

Later on 5 August 2019, Union Home Minister Amit Shah moved in the Rajya Sabha a statutory resolution recommending that the President declare Article 370 inoperative, together with the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Bill 2019. The Rajya Sabha adopted the resolution and passed the Bill on 5 August by the margins recorded in its proceedings. The President then issued the declaration under Article 370(3), numbered C.O. 273, on 6 August 2019, declaring that Article 370 would cease to be operative except for a residual clause recording that the Constitution of India applied to the state in full. The Lok Sabha adopted the resolution and passed the Reorganisation Bill on 6 August 2019. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 received presidential assent on 9 August 2019 and took effect on the appointed day, 31 October 2019, when the state of Jammu and Kashmir ceased to exist and two Union Territories commenced: Jammu and Kashmir, with a legislative assembly on the model of Puducherry and Delhi, and Ladakh, administered directly by a Lieutenant Governor without a legislature.

The operational measures that accompanied the instruments were as much a part of the event as the instruments themselves. In the night of 4 and 5 August 2019, internet and telephone services in the Kashmir Valley were suspended, a curfew was imposed under section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the principal leaders of the Valley's parties were detained: three former chief ministers, Mehbooba Mufti, Omar Abdullah and, briefly at first and then for months, Farooq Abdullah, who was subsequently detained under the Public Safety Act in September 2019. Several hundred political workers, lawyers, traders and minors were detained in the weeks that followed, according to the figures placed by habeas corpus petitioners before the courts; the government maintained that detentions were preventive, limited and progressively relaxed. Landline services were restored in stages from late August 2019; postpaid mobile services in October 2019; mobile internet in the Valley only in February 2021, some eighteen months after its suspension, with fourth-generation services restored after the Lieutenant Governor's administration certified the security position. The Supreme Court, in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, decided on 10 January 2020, held that indefinite suspension of the internet was impermissible, that restriction orders had to be published and proportionate, and directed review of the orders then in force; the petitioners argued that the directions were not implemented in substance, and the government argued that they were.

## The Two-Step Route: A Procedural Map of the Abrogation

The findable artifact of this article, the two-step procedural map of the August 2019 abrogation, sets out the mechanism in the form in which a reader can cite it: step one rewrote the meaning of the termination clause, and step two exercised the termination power under the rewritten meaning. Each step combined a Presidential instrument with a parliamentary act, and each step was later defended and attacked on distinct grounds before the Constitution Bench.

| Step | Instrument and date | Constitutional basis | Function in the chain | In Re Article 370 (11 December 2023) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Step One, interpretation | Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 2019, C.O. 272, 5 August 2019 | Article 370(1)(d), application of constitutional provisions with modifications | Applied the full Constitution to the state and inserted Article 367(4), reading "Constituent Assembly" in Article 370(3) as "Legislative Assembly" and, during President's Rule, "Government" as the Governor | Upheld: modification of Article 367 was within the application power, and the reading did not amend Article 370 by a side door |
| Step One, voice | Parliament exercising the powers of the state legislature, 5 August 2019 | President's Rule under Article 356 as applied to the state, with Article 357 | Supplied the concurrence and recommendation voices that the modified text required from state institutions | Upheld: the exercise of state legislative functions by Parliament during President's Rule was constitutionally regular |
| Step Two, termination | Declaration C.O. 273, 6 August 2019, on the statutory resolution of both Houses | Article 370(3) as read through Article 367(4) | Declared Article 370 inoperative except a residual clause applying the Constitution in full; Article 35A fell with the superseded 1954 Order | Upheld: Article 370 was temporary, its termination power survived the dissolution of the state Constituent Assembly, and the declaration was valid |
| Step Two, reorganisation | Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019, assent 9 August 2019, in force 31 October 2019 | Article 3, Parliament's power to alter state boundaries and status | Bifurcated the state into the Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh | Upheld as within Article 3; the Court recorded the Union's assurance on restoration of statehood to Jammu and Kashmir and directed assembly elections by 30 September 2024 |

Reading the map as a whole exposes the design decision at the centre of the operation. The recommending voice for termination belonged, by the original text, to a Kashmiri constitution-making body. Step one replaced that body's name in the interpretive clause with the name of the state's ordinary legislature; step one's second half ensured that the ordinary legislature's voice, in August 2019, was Parliament itself, speaking for a dissolved assembly through the machinery of President's Rule; step two then pronounced the termination. Every link in the chain had a textual basis, and the chain as a whole produced an outcome the 1949 text had assigned to a consent that was never obtained from any Kashmiri body, elected or constituent. Both halves of that sentence were litigated for four years, and the Supreme Court accepted the first half as legally dispositive while acknowledging the second half as the political residue its judgment could not dissolve.

The doctrinal objections pressed against the map deserve their strongest statement. The petitioners before the Constitution Bench, led in argument by Kapil Sibal, Rajeev Dhavan, Dushyant Dave and others for Kashmiri parties, former civil servants and interveners, argued first that the Article 370(1)(d) power to apply provisions with modifications could not be used to modify Article 367 so as to alter the operation of Article 370(3), because a power granted by a provision could not be turned upon the provision's own termination conditions; second, that the state Constituent Assembly's dissolution had frozen clause (3), a position they drew from the logic that the Constitution's framers had made the Assembly's recommendation a condition precisely because it embodied the Kashmiri people's constituent will; third, that the concurrence of a Governor appointed by the President could not substitute for the concurrence of an elected state government, so that the 5 August Order rested on the Union consenting to itself; and fourth, that the conversion of a state into a Union Territory, as distinct from boundary alteration, exceeded Article 3 and offended the federal structure that S.R. Bommai and the basic structure doctrine protected. The Union, in the arguments of Attorney General K.K. Venugopal and Solicitor General Tushar Mehta, answered that Article 370 was by placement and design temporary; that interpretation clauses exist to be adjusted by the same power that applies provisions; that President's Rule constitutionally vests state functions in Union institutions for its duration, a principle settled across decades of practice; and that Article 3, read with the precedents of state reorganisation from 1956 onward, imposed no bar to the creation of Union Territories. The judgment of 11 December 2023 resolved each of the four objections against the petitioners.

### Was Article 35A removed by a separate instrument?

No separate instrument removed Article 35A. Article 35A had entered the constitutional order through the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 1954; when the Order of 5 August 2019 superseded the 1954 framework and applied the Constitution in full, Article 35A ceased to operate as part of that single substitution.


## The Reorganisation Act: Jammu, the Kashmir Valley and Ladakh After 31 October 2019

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 did more than change a status label, and its architecture has to be described region by region, because the three regions of the former state entered the new order with different histories, different demands and different institutional outcomes. The former state had always been a composite: the Kashmir Valley, overwhelmingly Muslim and the centre of the state's politics since 1947; Jammu, with a Hindu majority across its plains districts and substantial Muslim populations in its hill districts, whose regional parties had long complained of subordination to Valley-dominated governments; and Ladakh, the high-altitude region of Leh, predominantly Buddhist, and Kargil, predominantly Shia Muslim, whose representatives had demanded separation from the Kashmir administration since the late 1980s. A settlement that the Valley experienced as demotion could be, and in Leh was, received as emancipation. Any account that speaks of a single Kashmiri reaction to August 2019 has already falsified the record.

For Jammu and Kashmir as a Union Territory, the Act created a legislative assembly with powers over the State List subjects except public order and police, which remained with the Lieutenant Governor, on the pattern of the National Capital Territory of Delhi under Article 239AA in its weaker respects and of Puducherry. The Council of Ministers would aid and advise a Lieutenant Governor appointed by the President, who retained discretion in matters outside the assembly's competence and in cases of difference with the ministry. The Act reduced the former state's legislative council to abolition, set the assembly's strength at 107 seats rising to 114 after delimitation, reserved the state cadre of the civil services within the Arunachal Pradesh, Goa, Mizoram and Union Territories cadre, and applied the full body of Indian law, including the Right to Information Act and central commissions, that the special status had kept at the gate. The first Lieutenant Governor, Girish Chandra Murmu, took office on 31 October 2019; he was succeeded in August 2020 by Manoj Sinha, whose administration conducted the District Development Council elections of November and December 2020, the first electoral exercise of the new order.

For Ladakh, the Act created a Union Territory without a legislature, administered by a Lieutenant Governor, Radha Krishna Mathur being the first appointee. The Leh Apex Body, representing the Buddhist-majority Leh district's organisations, had demanded Union Territory status since 1989 and initially welcomed the separation; the Kargil Democratic Alliance, representing the Shia-majority Kargil district, had opposed bifurcation and continued to oppose it. By 2021 and 2022 both the Leh and Kargil bodies had converged on a shared four-point agenda: statehood for Ladakh, inclusion in the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution, protection of land and employment for residents, and a public service commission for the territory. The convergence is a standing reminder that the post-2019 settlement distributed satisfaction unevenly even among its initial beneficiaries, and that the political question the Supreme Court left open in 2023 had a Ladakhi chapter as well as a Kashmiri one. The Union Home Ministry constituted a high-powered committee on Ladakh's demands in January 2023; talks between the committee and the Leh and Kargil bodies proceeded intermittently, with the land and employment protections partially addressed through domicile-style residency rules for the territory.

In Jammu, the political reading divided along lines that preceded 2019. Organisations in Jammu city that had campaigned for full integration since the Praja Parishad movement of the early 1950s treated August 2019 as the completion of the accession; traders' bodies and land interests in Jammu subsequently raised the same anxieties about land and employment competition that the permanent resident protections had once managed, and the administration answered those anxieties, across all three regions, with the domicile rules of 2020. The Jammu and Kashmir Domicile (Procedure) Rules 2020 redefined eligibility for public employment and, in the amended land laws of October 2020, opened agricultural land purchase in defined categories beyond the former permanent resident class. Critics in the Valley and in Jammu described the land changes as demographic engineering; the administration described them as the ordinary operation of equal citizenship and investment policy. The description chosen tracks the constitutional theory chosen, which is precisely the integration of the political and the legal that this article defends.

## The Litigation Road to the Constitution Bench, 2019 to 2023

The constitutional challenge began within days. Petitions filed in August 2019 by the National Conference's Lok Sabha members Mohammad Akbar Lone and Hasnain Masoodi, by the Communist Party of India (Marxist) leader Mohammed Yousuf Tarigami, by advocates and former officials including the former interlocutor Radha Kumar and the former Air Vice Marshal Kapil Kak, and later by the People's Democratic Party and others, challenged C.O. 272, C.O. 273 and the Reorganisation Act. A three-judge bench referred the questions to a five-judge Constitution Bench in 2019, and the bench, after preliminary hearings in late 2019 and early 2020, declined in March 2020 to refer the matter to a seven-judge bench, holding, by a three-to-two division on the reference question, that the earlier five-judge decisions in Prem Nath Kaul (1959) and Sampat Prakash (1968) did not conflict in a manner requiring a larger bench. Those precedents mattered to both sides. Sampat Prakash v. State of Jammu and Kashmir had held that Article 370 continued to operate after the dissolution of the state Constituent Assembly and that the President's application powers under it remained available, a holding the Union read as confirming the provision's working flexibility and the petitioners read as confirming its permanence after 1957.

The substantive hearings waited through the pandemic years and began in earnest on 2 August 2023, continuing for sixteen days of argument. The bench comprised Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud and Justices Sanjay Kishan Kaul, Sanjiv Khanna, B.R. Gavai and Surya Kant. The Court heard the petitioners across the first week, the Union's defence led by the Attorney General R. Venkataramani and the Solicitor General, and a range of interveners including the Jammu and Kashmir High Court Bar Association and bodies supporting the abrogation, among them organisations of Kashmiri Pandits whose counsel pressed the argument that Article 370 had sheltered discrimination against women, against the Valmiki community settled in the state in 1957, against West Pakistan refugees of 1947 and against the children of women permanent residents who married outside the state. Those discrimination arguments were factually anchored in decided cases and in the Permanent Resident Certificate regime, and the Union placed them at the centre of its political defence; the petitioners answered that the removal of discrimination required no abolition of the federal settlement and that the cases cited had been progressively corrected by the Jammu and Kashmir High Court itself, notably in Sushila Sawhney (2002), which restored the rights of women permanent residents. Both answers are preserved in the record of argument, and the judgment, when it came, decided the case on structural grounds rather than on the discrimination record.

### Which earlier Supreme Court decisions framed the 2023 case?

Two five-judge decisions framed the field: Prem Nath Kaul (1959), on the powers of the state Constituent Assembly era authorities, and Sampat Prakash (1968), which held that Article 370 continued in operation after the state Constituent Assembly dissolved and that Presidential application orders could continue under it.

The judgment was delivered on 11 December 2023, and its architecture was as carefully staged as the procedure it reviewed. Three opinions issued: the judgment of Chief Justice Chandrachud for himself and Justices Gavai and Surya Kant; a concurring judgment of Justice Khanna; and a separate concurring opinion of Justice Kaul. The conclusions were unanimous. The Court held that Jammu and Kashmir retained no element of internal sovereignty after accession and the adoption of the Indian Constitution, the state's own constitution being subordinate in the scheme; that Article 370 was a temporary and transitional provision, evidenced by its placement in Part XXI, its marginal note and the circumstances of 1949; that the dissolution of the state Constituent Assembly did not render clause (3) impossible of operation, because the President's power under that clause was not extinguished by the disappearance of the recommending body; that the Article 367 modification route was a permissible exercise of the application power; that the exercise of the state government's concurrence function by the Governor during President's Rule, and of the legislature's functions by Parliament, was valid in law, with the Court declining to disturb the proclamation framework on the material before it; and that the Reorganisation Act's bifurcation was within Parliament's powers, the Court resting the statehood question on the Union's assurance, recorded in the Solicitor General's statement, that statehood would be restored to Jammu and Kashmir at the earliest, and directing that elections to the Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly be held by 30 September 2024. On Ladakh, the Court upheld the creation of the Union Territory, noting the petitioners' challenge to that limb had not been pressed with the same force.

Justice Kaul's separate opinion carried the element of the 2023 settlement that the integrated reading treats as decisive against the separation thesis. Concurring in the result, Justice Kaul recorded the human cost of the insurgency decades, referred to the displacement of Kashmiri Pandits and to violations by state and non-state actors since 1989, and recommended the constitution of a Truth and Reconciliation Commission to investigate and report on human rights violations by state and non-state actors in Jammu and Kashmir since the 1980s, so that the constitutional settlement might be accompanied by an accounting. No such commission had been constituted within the period covered by this article's evidence. The recommendation did not alter the operative holdings; it marked, from inside the majority's own bench, the judgement's awareness that a constitutional file can be closed while a political history remains unexamined.

## Noorani Against the Route, and the Scholars the Judgment Answered

The named disagreement this article must adjudicate has a documentary spine. In Article 370: A Constitutional History of Jammu and Kashmir (2011), A.G. Noorani assembled the record of the provision from the archives: the Ayyangar drafting history, the exchange of letters between Nehru and Sheikh Abdullah, the Delhi Agreement negotiations, and the successive application orders. Noorani's conclusion, argued with a lawyer's attention to instrument and date, was that Article 370 embodied a solemn compact, that the Constituent Assembly's recommendation condition was the compact's lock, and that the erosion of the provision after 1953 had proceeded by consent forms obtained from governments in Srinagar that lacked the constituent authority the lock required. He extended the argument after August 2019 in essays that described the Presidential Orders as unconstitutional in method whatever their political merits, and he maintained that position until his death in August 2024. The strength of Noorani's reading is its fidelity to the 1949 materials. Its weakness, which the Union's counsel exploited and the Court's reasoning reflected, is that it required a dissolved body to hold a perpetual veto and required the word permanent to be read into a provision housed in the Constitution's temporary part.

Anuradha Bhasin, executive editor of the Kashmir Times, approached the settlement from the opposite archive: the record of governance in the Valley after August 2019. Her petition of August 2019 against the communication restrictions produced the January 2020 judgment on internet suspension, and her subsequent essays documented the chilling of Kashmiri journalism, the use of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act and the Public Safety Act against reporters and political workers, and the hollowing of elected institutions during the Lieutenant Governor's administration. Bhasin's reading treats the procedural question as secondary to the democratic one: whatever the clauses permitted, a decision of that magnitude taken under blackout and detention failed the test of democratic legitimacy. The strength of that reading is its attention to the conditions under which consent and contest were possible in August 2019. Its weakness, as an adjudication of the constitutional question, is that legitimacy and legality answer different questions, and the Court was asked the second.

Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India (2021), placed the abrogation inside an ideological genealogy reaching from Mookerjee through the Jana Sangh's integration campaigns to the post-2014 programme, reading August 2019 as the execution of a seventy-year ideological commitment by a government that had finally acquired the parliamentary and institutional means. Jaffrelot's genealogy explains the timing and the selection of this commitment from among the party's shelved demands better than either the legal or the Valley-governance readings. Its limit is the mirror of the political-decision reading: genealogy explains why the demand endured, but not why the execution took the Article 367 form, and the form, as the four years of litigation showed, was where the constitutional fate of the operation was decided.

Adjudication among the three readings follows from their respective failures. The political-decision reading, of which Jaffrelot's genealogy is the most sophisticated version, is true about motive and insufficient about method. The constitutional-procedure reading, of which Noorani's constitutional history is the most formidable version, is true about the text's original allocation of consent and insufficient about the text's own machinery for change, machinery the Court found adequate to the use made of it. The democratic-legitimacy reading, Bhasin's, is true about the conditions of August 2019 and does not answer the question the courts had to decide. The integrated reading accepts the strongest element of each: the ideological motive from the genealogy, the consent architecture from the constitutional history, the conditions from the governance record, and adds the claim that binds them, that the procedure was selected because it converted a political majority in Delhi into every voice the constitutional text required, including the voice of Jammu and Kashmir itself. On the primary evidence, the instruments, the National Agenda for Governance, the President's Rule proclamations, the parliamentary debates of 5 and 6 August 2019 and the judgment of 11 December 2023, that claim is the one the documents sustain.

### Did the Supreme Court decide the question of statehood for Jammu and Kashmir?

The Court did not order a date for the restoration of statehood. It recorded the Union's assurance that statehood would be restored at the earliest opportunity, upheld the Reorganisation Act, and directed that assembly elections be conducted by 30 September 2024, a direction with which the Election Commission complied.

## The Debate in Parliament, 5 and 6 August 2019: The Argument as the Country Heard It

The two days of parliamentary debate are the public record on which the political reading and the procedural reading first collided, and the integrated account requires the debate's main lines to be set down. Union Home Minister Amit Shah opened in the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2019 by laying the Presidential Order, the statutory resolution and the Reorganisation Bill before the House together, a sequencing the opposition protested because the resolution's subject, Article 370, was being terminated by instruments members had received the same morning. Shah's substantive defence proceeded on four grounds: that Article 370 was temporary by its own text and placement; that it had obstructed the application of beneficial law, including reservations and the Right to Education, to the state's residents; that it had failed to prevent the insurgency, the corruption of the state's politics or the underdevelopment the government attributed to the Valley's dominant families; and that the bifurcation answered Ladakh's long-standing demand. He gave the assurance, repeated by the Solicitor General before the Constitution Bench four years later, that statehood would be restored to Jammu and Kashmir when the situation permitted, an assurance the judgment of December 2023 would record as part of its reasoning on the Reorganisation Act.

The opposition's case in the Rajya Sabha was led by the Leader of the Opposition, Ghulam Nabi Azad of the Congress, himself a former chief minister of the state, who argued that the government had abolished a constitutional settlement without a single Kashmiri voice in the room, the state's members being absent because its legislature stood dissolved and its Lok Sabha members from the Valley parties had not been consulted. Kapil Sibal, speaking for the Congress in the debate and later leading the petitioners' arguments before the Constitution Bench, framed the legal objection that would structure the litigation: a power to apply the Constitution with modifications had been used to amend the Constitution's meaning, and a clause designed to require Kashmiri consent had been operated by the Union's own appointees. The Trinamool Congress, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the Left parties opposed on federal grounds, while several parties outside the alliance, the Biju Janata Dal, the Bahujan Samaj Party, the YSR Congress and the Aam Aadmi Party among them, supported the government, a distribution that recorded how far the integration position had travelled beyond the ruling party's own benches by 2019. The Rajya Sabha adopted the resolution and passed the Bill on 5 August 2019 by 125 votes to 61 in the division recorded in its proceedings. The Lok Sabha debated on 6 August, with Prime Minister Narendra Modi describing the decisions as the correction of a historical wrong and the fulfilment of the constitutional promise of equality for the state's residents, and passed the Bill the same day by the large majority recorded in its proceedings.

Two features of the debate deserve emphasis in the adjudication. The first is that the government's floor defence was overwhelmingly political and moral, resting on integration, equality and development, while its legal defence, the Article 367 construction, was stated briefly and defended in detail only later, in court, by the law officers. The sequence supports the integrated reading's ordering of the dimensions: the political commitment selected the destination, the legal design selected the road, and each was publicly defended in the forum suited to it. The second feature is the debate's absences. No member spoke for an elected government of Jammu and Kashmir, because none existed; the National Conference's Lok Sabha members, Farooq Abdullah among them, were in Srinagar under the restrictions of that week, and the House proceeded on the constitutional business of a state whose representatives were, in the main, either absent or in detention. The petitioners returned to that absence across four years of litigation as the democratic core of their case. The government's answer, that the Constitution assigns the state's voice during President's Rule to Parliament and the Governor and that the House was the state's voice in law for those two days, is the answer the Supreme Court's reasoning in substance accepted. The debate thus contains, in miniature, the entire dispute this article has adjudicated: a form perfectly regular, a consent perfectly absent, and a country arguing ever since about which of those two facts describes August 2019.

## The Stance of the Judgment: Settled Law, Open Politics

The proposition that the December 2023 judgment settled the constitutional question requires one refinement. A Constitution Bench judgment settles the law unless a larger bench revisits it, and review petitions against In Re Article 370 were dismissed by the Court in May 2024, closing the ordinary avenues of challenge. In that sense the abrogation's legality is settled law: Article 370 stands inoperative, the Constitution of India applies to Jammu and Kashmir in full, and the Union Territory structure rests on a validated statute. The refinement is that the judgment settled the law by reasoning that itself preserved open questions. The Court accepted the Union's assurance on statehood rather than adjudicating a right to it; it recommended nothing on statehood timing; Justice Kaul's truth and reconciliation proposal pointed to an unprocessed history; and the elections direction implied a democratic sequence whose completion depended on executive action. A judgment that closes a case while recording an assurance about the losing side's central political demand has settled the law and scheduled the politics.

The political sequence then ran on the Court's timetable. Delimitation had preceded it: the Delimitation Commission for Jammu and Kashmir, constituted in March 2020 under Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai, issued its final order on 5 May 2022, redrawing the assembly map to 90 elected seats, 47 in the Kashmir division and 43 in the Jammu division, a reallocation that Valley parties characterised as weighting the map toward Jammu and the Commission defended by its stated criteria of population, geography and administrative units. Assembly elections, the first since 2014 and the first under the Union Territory framework, were held in three phases in September and October 2024. The National Conference, in alliance with the Congress, won the election; Omar Abdullah was sworn in as Chief Minister on 16 October 2024 at the head of a government whose first assembly session passed a resolution seeking the restoration of statehood and the safeguarding of the region's identity, land and employment rights in terms that stopped short of demanding the restoration of Article 370 in its verdict-era form. The Union government received the statehood demand without a timetable. The Lieutenant Governor retained control of police and public order, and the elected ministry governed inside the Reorganisation Act's limits, a working demonstration of the difference between the constitutional settlement, which was complete, and the political settlement, which the state's own elected leadership continued to describe as pending.

For the researchers who reconstruct episodes of executive decision-making from protected notes and scattered records, the archival habits praised in guides such as the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) have a particular relevance to Kashmir reporting, where the safety of a source can depend on the discipline of the notebook. The detail belongs in this article because the post-2019 record, detentions of journalists among it, forms part of the evidence on which the political question continues to be argued, and because the constitutional history of Article 370 will be written in the future from archives whose survival depends on such discipline in the present.

The general election of April to June 2024 supplied the first all-India verdict delivered after the judgment. The Bharatiya Janata Party campaigned on the abrogation as a settled achievement of integration; the Congress manifesto promised restoration of statehood to Jammu and Kashmir without promising restoration of Article 370, a distinction the National Conference noted when the parties allied for the assembly elections later that year; the National Conference's own manifesto promised to strive for the restoration of Articles 370 and 35A through the constitutional forums, while its government, once formed, directed its formal demands at statehood and at safeguards for land and employment. In the Lok Sabha results for the territory, the National Conference and the Bharatiya Janata Party divided the five seats of the two Union Territories between the Valley-and-Jammu pattern that has organised the region's politics since 2019, the Valley returning National Conference members and an independent who had campaigned from detention under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, Jammu returning Bharatiya Janata Party members, and Ladakh returning an independent supported by the Leh and Kargil bodies jointly. The pattern is the political question in electoral form: a constitutional settlement ratified by the courts, administered by the Union, and still being negotiated, constituency by constituency and resolution by resolution, by the people whose Constituent Assembly the termination clause had named in 1949. The negotiation continues through democratic instruments the settlement itself restored on the Court's timetable, which is the strongest evidence available for the proposition that December 2023 closed the file without closing the argument.

The external dimension closed more quietly than the internal one. Pakistan downgraded diplomatic relations in August 2019, suspended bilateral trade and made the restoration of the special status a stated precondition for normalisation, a position repeated by successive governments in Islamabad; India treated the changes as an internal constitutional matter and cited the Simla framework against internationalisation. China objected specifically to the creation of the Ladakh Union Territory, which it read as affecting the disputed boundary in Aksai Chin, and the Ministry of External Affairs rejected the objection as interference. Neither objection altered the instruments. The Line of Control ceasefire understanding of February 2021 between the Directors General of Military Operations reduced firing incidents along the frontier without touching the constitutional positions of any party, a separation of the military temperature from the constitutional file that mirrored, at the border, the separation of law from politics at home.


## What Changed on the Ground: Law, Land, Domicile and Administration

The constitutional instruments of August 2019 acquired their meaning for residents through a body of subordinate change that arrived in the following two years, and the integrated reading requires that record to be stated with the same care as the orders themselves. On 31 October 2019 the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act's appointed day, some 106 central laws became applicable to the two Union Territories in full, including the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, the National Commission for Minorities Act and the full jurisdiction of national commissions that the earlier application orders had admitted only in part. Simultaneously, 153 laws of the former state were repealed and 166 retained in adapted form, according to the adaptation orders issued by the Lieutenant Governor's administration. The Ranbir Penal Code, the state's separate criminal code since the Dogra period, gave way to the Indian Penal Code. The separate constitution of Jammu and Kashmir ceased to operate; the flag that had flown beside the national flag on state buildings since the Delhi Agreement settlement lost its official status; and the state holiday calendar lost the observances associated with the accession-era settlement, including Martyrs' Day on 13 July and the birthday of Sheikh Abdullah, while 26 October, Accession Day, entered the official calendar.

Land and domicile rules carried the deepest social consequences, because the permanent resident regime abolished with Article 35A had governed who could buy land, hold state employment and claim scholarships in the former state since 1927, when Maharaja Hari Singh's Hereditary State Subject notifications first codified the protections against settlement from outside. The Jammu and Kashmir Civil Services (Decentralisation and Recruitment) Act was amended in April 2020 to define domicile by fifteen years of residence, by study and examination criteria, or by registration as a migrant, and the Domicile Procedure Rules of May 2020 operationalised the definition. In October 2020 the administration notified amendments to land laws that removed the permanent resident condition for purchase of non-agricultural land and permitted transfer of agricultural land for defined purposes with official permission. The administration of Lieutenant Governor Manoj Sinha presented the changes as the removal of a discriminatory barrier and an invitation to investment, pointing to the industrial policy of 2021 and the investment proposals received at the Jammu and Kashmir Global Investors Summit process. The National Conference, the People's Democratic Party and the People's Alliance for Gupkar Declaration, the coalition of Valley and Jammu parties formed in August 2019 and formalised in October 2020 around the demand for restoration of the pre-August 2019 position, presented the same changes as the legal machinery of demographic alteration. The Gupkar Declaration's member parties contested the District Development Council elections of 2020 together and won a majority of seats in the Kashmir division, a result both camps cited: the alliance as proof of popular rejection, the Union government as proof that democratic processes functioned under the new order.

The administrative integration proceeded through personnel as much as through law. The state cadre officers of the Indian Administrative Service and Indian Police Service were merged into the AGMUT cadre in January 2021, ending the separate Jammu and Kashmir cadre that had embodied the state's administrative distinctness since the 1950s. Panchayat and municipal elections filled the local tier; the District Development Councils of 2020 created a third elected tier that had not existed under the state framework, with chairpersons elected in February 2021. Security indicators moved along their own track: the Ministry of Home Affairs' figures placed before Parliament recorded declining numbers of militancy-related incidents and of security force casualties in the years after 2019 relative to the preceding period, alongside continuing targeted killings of minorities and migrant workers in the Valley in 2021 and 2022 that the administration attributed to Pakistan-backed groups and the opposition attributed in part to the political vacuum. Each dataset belongs to the record; the argument concerns what the datasets measure, and on that argument the parties remain divided along the lines drawn in August 2019.

Education, employment and the courts absorbed quieter changes. The Jammu and Kashmir Official Languages Act 2020 recognised five official languages for the Union Territory, Kashmiri, Dogri, Urdu, Hindi and English, replacing the sole official status Urdu had held since the Dogra era, a change welcomed in Jammu as recognition of Dogri and received in the Valley as dilution of a shared administrative inheritance. Reservation policy was recast, with the economically weaker sections reservation of the Constitution's One Hundred and Third Amendment extended to the territory and, in 2024, the inclusion of additional communities in the Other Backward Classes list, including the Pahari ethnic group, a measure Jammu's hill districts had demanded for decades and Valley parties contested as electoral engineering ahead of the assembly elections. The Jammu and Kashmir High Court, renamed the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, exercised jurisdiction over both territories from Srinagar and Jammu benches, and the habeas corpus petitions of 2019 and 2020, several hundred at their height according to the Bar Association's filings, were progressively disposed of as detentions expired or were revoked, many without adjudication on the merits, a pattern the petitioners' counsel recorded as a grievance in itself.

## Why the Two Questions Cannot Be Separated: The Adjudication

The complication the brief assigns to this article is the strongest available counter to its thesis, and it deserves a direct answer. The counter runs as follows. Constitutional questions and political questions are different kinds of questions; courts answer the first and electorates the second; the judgment of 11 December 2023 answered the first completely; therefore the integrated reading mistakes a closed legal file for an open one, and the only remaining substance is the ordinary politics of statehood, development and regional grievance, which requires no constitutional theory to describe. On this view, August 2019 was a political decision that happened to use lawyers, and December 2023 ended the matter that made it distinctive.

Three features of the record resist that separation. The first is internal to the judgment. The Constitution Bench disposed of the statehood question by recording an executive assurance rather than by deciding a legal claim, which means the Court itself treated the central political consequence of the Reorganisation Act as a matter pending between the Union and the people of Jammu and Kashmir rather than as a question concluded by law. A separation thesis that rests on the judgment must explain why the judgment declined to perform the separation at exactly the point where law and politics met. The second feature is Justice Kaul's opinion. A concurring judge who recommends a truth and reconciliation process for the decades of violence is stating, in the restrained vocabulary of a judicial opinion, that the constitutional settlement and the political settlement travel on different clocks; the recommendation has no legal operation, and its presence in the reports is unintelligible unless the bench understood the case as deciding less than the whole of its subject. The third feature is the procedure's own logic. The Article 367 route was not an incidental vehicle for a political decision; it was the decision's enabling condition, chosen because the ordinary political route, an amendment debated as an amendment, or a negotiated settlement with Kashmiri parties of the kind attempted in 1952, 1975 and 2015, would either have failed or have produced a different outcome. Where the choice of legal form determines which political outcomes are available, the two domains are integrated in fact, whatever their separation in theory.

The comparison with the other decisive moments of the Modi era sharpens the point. Demonetisation in November 2016 was an executive act of vast consequence that required no constitutional instrument and generated no comparable constitutional litigation on its validity; its politics could be, and were, argued on economic evidence alone. The Goods and Services Tax required the full Article 368 process and years of negotiation through the GST Council; its politics were the politics of a bargain recorded in a constitutional text. Article 370's abrogation resembles neither. It used the Constitution's own text as the instrument, the state's own dissolved institutions as the voice, and a four-year judicial silence followed by unanimous validation as the seal. That combination, executive speed, textual ingenuity, institutional substitution and eventual judicial confirmation, is the integrated phenomenon, and it is why August 2019 will be studied beside the founding moments of the Republic rather than beside the ordinary controversies of a parliamentary term: beside, precisely, the Constituent Assembly settlement in which Ayyangar promised in October 1949 that the people of Jammu and Kashmir, through their Constituent Assembly, would have the final word on the relationship. The final word was eventually spoken. The integrated reading records who, in the end, was holding the pen.

The evidence discipline of this article should be stated plainly at its close. Settled facts have been stated as settled: the dates and numbers of the Presidential Orders, C.O. 272 of 5 August 2019 and C.O. 273 of 6 August 2019; the passage and assent of the Reorganisation Act and its appointed day of 31 October 2019; the composition of the Constitution Bench and the date and unanimity of its judgment; the delimitation order of 5 May 2022; the assembly elections of September and October 2024 and the swearing-in of the Omar Abdullah ministry on 16 October 2024. Disputed characterisations have been attributed to their holders at their strongest: fraud on the Constitution to the petitioners and to Noorani's school; perpetual veto of a dissolved body to the Union's counsel; demographic engineering to the Gupkar parties; discriminatory shelter to the interveners who defended the abrogation on equality grounds. The article's own claim stands between the attributions and is answerable to the documents: the August 2019 abrogation reversed the 1998 deferral through a procedural route that bypassed the Constituent Assembly mechanism, and the December 2023 validation settled the constitutional question while leaving the political question open. Every instrument cited for that claim exists in the public record, and the claim falls if the instruments are read to say otherwise.


## The Equality Argument: Article 35A, Discrimination Claims and Their Critics

No adjudication of August 2019 is complete without the equality argument, because for a substantial body of the abrogation's defenders the argument from equal citizenship, rather than the argument from integration, carried the moral weight, and the Constitution Bench heard it at length. The permanent resident regime, as codified by the state constitution and protected by Article 35A, distributed civil goods by descent. Women who were permanent residents lost, under the interpretation applied before 2002, their status on marrying a non-resident, and their children by such marriages inherited the disability, until the Jammu and Kashmir High Court's full bench in Sushila Sawhney (2002) held that a woman's permanent resident status survived her marriage, a correction the state legislature then attempted to reverse by the Permanent Resident (Disqualification) Bill of 2004, which lapsed amid protest. The Valmiki community, brought to the state in 1957 from Punjab as sanitation workers on the assurance of full citizenship, received permanent resident certificates endorsed for sanitation work alone, confining generations to a single occupation as a condition of residence. Refugees who had crossed from West Pakistan in 1947 and settled in Jammu voted in Lok Sabha elections for seven decades without the right to vote in state assembly elections or to own the land they farmed. Members of the Gorkha community settled since the nineteenth century and the children of permanent resident mothers and non-resident fathers carried analogous exclusions, each documented in petitions and committee reports placed before the courts.

The defenders of the abrogation, among them the intervening organisations of displaced Kashmiri Pandits and the Union in its written submissions, assembled these cases into a single proposition: Article 35A immunised a hereditary citizenship regime from the fundamental rights chapter of the Indian Constitution, and no reform internal to the special status had dislodged it in sixty-five years, because the regime's beneficiaries staffed the state legislatures that alone could amend it. The proposition had evidentiary force. The counter-argument, pressed by the petitioners and by scholars of the region, answered at three points. First, the discriminations were creatures of state legislation and interpretation, several already corrected or under correction by the state's own courts, and their cure required striking the discriminatory rules, an exercise the Supreme Court could have performed on the pending Article 35A challenge without touching Article 370. Second, the equality gained in 2019 was real but accompanied by the loss of the land and employment protections that the same regime had provided to the region's poorer residents against outside capital, a trade whose incidence fell unevenly across Jammu, the Valley and Ladakh. Third, the equality argument proved more than its users required: equal citizenship of the ordinary Indian kind is compatible with asymmetric federal protections, as the Constitution's own provisions for Nagaland under Article 371A, for Mizoram under Article 371G and for the Sixth Schedule areas demonstrate, so equality alone could not select abolition over recalibration. The judgment of December 2023 did not rest its result on the equality record, deciding instead on temporariness, procedure and parliamentary power. The equality argument therefore survives the litigation as a political argument in full strength, available to both sides: to defenders as the moral core of integration, to critics as proof that the chosen instrument exceeded the stated grievance. The integrated reading absorbs the point. A government confident only in its equality argument could have legislated against the discriminatory rules through the application power it used for seventy years; the decision to terminate the provision itself expressed the older integration commitment that the 1998 Agenda had shelved, and the equality record supplied that commitment with its most presentable public defence.

## The Roads Not Taken: Amendment, Negotiation and the Long Way Round

Counterfactual discipline is a test of causal claims, and the integrated reading survives it better than its rivals. Consider the roads available to a government in 2019 that wished to end the special status, and what each road would have produced.

The amendment road ran through Article 368. A bill to delete or rewrite Article 370, carried by two-thirds majorities, would have faced the objection that clause (3) made the state Constituent Assembly's recommendation a condition of the article's termination, and that an amendment abolishing the condition abolished by indirection what the condition protected. The objection might have failed; the amending power after Kesavananda Bharati (1973) is broad, bounded by the basic structure, and federalism's presence in that structure after S.R. Bommai (1994) cuts in directions scholars dispute. But the road would have taken time, would have required the government to defend the deletion as a constitutional amendment in both Houses and possibly before the Court on basic structure grounds, and would have conceded the premise that Article 370 stood outside the ordinary run of provisions until Parliament removed it by the Constitution's most solemn procedure. The government of 2019 declined that concession. The interpretive road inverted the premise: Article 370 contained its own ending, and the ending needed only the voices the text required, which the Union could lawfully supply. The choice between the roads was a choice about what kind of act the abrogation would be remembered as, an amendment imposed by a majority upon a settlement, or a settlement expiring by its own machinery. Governments that care about the second description will pay for it in ingenuity, and the Home Ministry's June and July 2019 preparation, so far as the litigation record reveals it, was the payment.

The negotiation road had three historical signposts. In 1952 the Delhi Agreement negotiated the status with the state's elected leadership and produced a settlement both capitals defended for a generation. In 1975 the Indira-Abdullah Accord negotiated with the same leadership after twenty-two years of estrangement and produced a settlement that left Article 370 formally intact and operationally transformed. In 2015 the Agenda of Alliance negotiated between the Bharatiya Janata Party and the People's Democratic Party produced a settlement that shelved the abrogation demand as the price of governing the state. Each negotiation traded the Union's maximum demand for Kashmiri participation in the outcome. By 2019 the party in Delhi assessed, on the evidence of its own statements, that negotiation had historically entrenched the status it sought to end, and that the participation it purchased was the participation of the very leaderships committed to the status. That assessment is contestable and was contested, by the detained leaderships themselves and by the former interlocutors who argued that a settlement without Kashmiri signatures would govern without Kashmiri consent. The assessment nonetheless explains the road taken with a completeness the purely legal and purely ideological readings cannot match: the procedure was selected to make negotiation unnecessary, because negotiation was the mechanism by which the demand had been deferred in 1952, in 1975, in 1998 and in 2015.

The long way round, gradualism through the application power, was the road of 1954 to 2018, and its destination is part of the record: a special status reduced to a shell whose remaining contents, the separate constitution's symbolism, the permanent resident regime and the bar on Union legislative competence, were precisely the contents its defenders valued most. The critics of August 2019 who describe the abrogation as a rupture must reckon with the gradualists' seventy years; the defenders who describe it as a formality must reckon with the detentions and the blackout that a formality would not have required. Between those reckonings the integrated reading stands, and it is offered here as the reading the documents sustain when the instruments, the agendas, the proclamations, the debates and the judgment are placed in a single sequence and read without flinching in either direction.


## The Word Temporary: Part XXI and the Interpretive Quarrel at the Case's Core

Beneath the procedural map sat a quarrel over a single adjective, and the Constitution Bench's resolution of that quarrel determined everything else. Article 370 stands in Part XXI of the Constitution, whose heading in the original text read "Temporary and Transitional Provisions" and in the amended text reads "Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions". The marginal note to Article 370 itself reads "Temporary provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir". The Union's argument built a syllogism on those labels: the framers placed the article among temporary provisions, named it temporary in its own margin, and provided a termination clause; a provision so housed and so labelled was a bridge, not a settlement, and the bridge's far end was full application of the Constitution. The petitioners answered that the temporariness of Article 370 attached to the circumstances of 1949, the war, the United Nations reference and the pending decision of the state's Constituent Assembly, and that the provision's function was temporary only until that Assembly decided; once the Assembly dissolved in 1956 without recommending termination, the temporary scaffolding fell away and the structure it had erected, the special relationship, stood permanent by the only decision the text authorised anyone to take. On the petitioners' reading, temporariness described the decision window, not the provision's lifespan. On the Union's reading, temporariness described the provision itself, and the window never shut.

The precedents supplied ammunition in both directions, which is why the 2020 reference order mattered. Sampat Prakash (1968) had held that Article 370 continued to operate after 1957 and that Presidential orders under it remained competent, a holding compatible with permanence and with continued temporariness alike. The State Bank of India v. Santosh Gupta (2016) had described Article 370 as having acquired a permanent character in observations the petitioners quoted and the Union distinguished as obiter. The Jammu and Kashmir High Court, in its 2015 judgment in Ashok Kumar's case concerning the state flag and in observations of the same period, had described Article 370 as permanent and beyond abrogation, reasoning that the Constituent Assembly condition could no longer be fulfilled. The Constitution Bench in 2023 resolved the quarrel for the Union's reading: temporary in placement, temporary in the marginal note, temporary in the understanding of the Indian Constituent Assembly's members who spoke to it in October 1949, and terminable by the clause (3) power notwithstanding the recommending body's dissolution, because a contrary reading would freeze a provision the framers had made terminable and would subordinate the Constitution's text to the accident of a body's lifespan. Justice Khanna's concurrence added the historical observation that the state Constituent Assembly's own choices, ratifying accession and framing a constitution that declared the state an integral part of India in its section 3, had expressed the constituent will on the relationship's fundamentals, leaving the termination mechanics to the text.

The quarrel's residue is worth recording because it governs how the judgment will be used in future federal disputes. If placement in Part XXI and a marginal note can characterise a provision as temporary against the argument that its own termination condition has become impossible, then other asymmetric provisions, Articles 371A and 371G for Nagaland and Mizoram, the Fifth and Sixth Schedules, will be read with attention to their termination clauses rather than to their character as negotiated compacts. The Union's counsel in 2023 were careful to distinguish those provisions, which carry no equivalent of clause (3), and the judgment's reasoning turned on the presence of the termination clause as much as on the adjective. The distinction is the judgment's own limiting principle: Article 370 was terminable because Article 370 said so, in a clause the article's defenders had treated for decades as the guarantee of the settlement's permanence. The guarantee and the ending were the same words. That irony, visible in the text since 17 October 1949, is the deepest layer of the integrated reading, and the last piece of primary evidence this article lays before its readers. A reader who follows the clause through the record, from Ayyangar's speech of October 1949 to the state Constituent Assembly's silence of 1951 to 1956, from Sampat Prakash in 1968 to the reference order of 2020 and the sixteen days of argument in August 2023, watches a single sentence perform every function the parties assigned to it in turn: promise, shield, obstacle and finally instrument. Constitutional provisions rarely lead so documented a life, and no account of Indian federalism that skips the biography of clause (3) can explain either the special status or its ending.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Article 370 abrogation?

Article 370 abrogation is the rendering inoperative of Article 370 of the Constitution of India, the provision that from 1950 had governed the constitutional relationship between the Union and Jammu and Kashmir. The President declared the article inoperative by the declaration numbered C.O. 273 on 6 August 2019, acting under Article 370(3) after the interpretive Order C.O. 272 of 5 August 2019 had applied the Constitution in full and recast the termination procedure. After the declaration, the Constitution of India applied to Jammu and Kashmir without exceptions, the state's separate constitution ceased to operate, Article 35A fell, and the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 converted the state into two Union Territories with effect from 31 October 2019.

### Q: What happened on 5 August 2019 in Kashmir?

On 5 August 2019 the President issued the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 2019, C.O. 272, which superseded the 1954 application framework, applied the entire Indian Constitution to the state and inserted clause (4) into Article 367 so that the Constituent Assembly named in Article 370(3) would be read as the Legislative Assembly. Union Home Minister Amit Shah then moved in the Rajya Sabha the statutory resolution recommending that Article 370 be declared inoperative, together with the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Bill; the Rajya Sabha adopted both the same day. Around the instruments, communications in the Kashmir Valley were suspended, a curfew was imposed and the principal Valley party leaders were detained.

### Q: How was Article 370 procedurally abrogated?

The procedure ran in two steps. Step one used Article 370(1)(d): the Presidential Order of 5 August 2019 modified Article 367, the interpretation clause, directing that the reference to the Constituent Assembly of the state in Article 370(3) be read as the Legislative Assembly, and that references to the state government be read as the Governor during President's Rule. Because the Legislative Assembly stood dissolved and its powers were exercised by Parliament under President's Rule, Parliament supplied the recommendation the modified clause required. Step two used Article 370(3): on the statutory resolution passed by both Houses, the President issued the declaration C.O. 273 on 6 August 2019 rendering Article 370 inoperative, leaving a residual clause that applied the Constitution to the state in full.

### Q: What is the J&K Reorganisation Act 2019?

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 is the statute, passed by the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2019 and the Lok Sabha on 6 August 2019, assented to on 9 August 2019 and brought into force on 31 October 2019, that divided the state of Jammu and Kashmir into two Union Territories. The Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir received a legislative assembly with competence over State List subjects except public order and police, under a Lieutenant Governor. The Union Territory of Ladakh received no legislature and direct administration by a Lieutenant Governor. The Act also adapted the body of state law, repealing 153 state laws and continuing 166 in modified form, and applied central laws to both territories in full.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court rule in 2023 on Article 370?

In In Re Article 370, decided on 11 December 2023, a five-judge Constitution Bench headed by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud unanimously upheld the abrogation. The Court held that Article 370 was a temporary provision, that Jammu and Kashmir retained no internal sovereignty after accession, that the dissolution of the state Constituent Assembly did not extinguish the President's power under Article 370(3), that the Article 367 interpretive route was valid, and that the Reorganisation Act fell within Parliament's powers. The Court recorded the Union's assurance that statehood would be restored to Jammu and Kashmir at the earliest and directed assembly elections by 30 September 2024. Justice Kaul, concurring, recommended a Truth and Reconciliation Commission for violations since the 1980s.

### Q: Why did Modi abrogate Article 370?

The Narendra Modi government acted on a commitment carried by the Bharatiya Janata Party and its predecessor, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, since the 1950s campaign of Syama Prasad Mookerjee against the state's separate constitutional position, restated in the party manifestos of 2014 and 2019. Coalition politics had forced the commitment's deferral in the National Agenda for Governance of 1998 and again in the Agenda of Alliance with the People's Democratic Party in 2015. The single-party majority of 2014, renewed with 303 seats in 2019, removed the coalition constraint, and the withdrawal from the state coalition in June 2018 followed by President's Rule placed the institutional voices the procedure required under Union control. The government defended the act as completing integration and ending discriminatory features of the permanent resident regime; opponents contested both the method and the purpose.

### Q: What changed in Kashmir after Article 370?

After August 2019 the Constitution of India applied to Jammu and Kashmir in full, the state constitution and official flag ceased to operate, and the permanent resident regime with its land and employment protections was replaced by the domicile rules of 2020. On 31 October 2019 the state became a Union Territory with an assembly but without control of police and public order, and Ladakh became a separate Union Territory without a legislature. Central laws including national commissions reached the territory unmediated, the state civil service cadre merged into the AGMUT cadre in 2021, a Delimitation Commission redrew the assembly map in 2022, and the first assembly elections of the new order in September and October 2024 returned a National Conference-led government headed by Omar Abdullah, with statehood restoration unresolved.

### Q: What was Article 35A and what happened to it in 2019?

Article 35A was a provision inserted into the Constitution as applied to Jammu and Kashmir by the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order 1954, without placement before Parliament as an amendment. It empowered the state legislature to define permanent residents and to reserve for them employment under the state government, acquisition of immovable property, settlement and scholarship rights, and it barred fundamental rights challenges to such laws by other citizens. Because Article 35A existed only as part of the 1954 Order's framework, it ceased to operate when the Order of 5 August 2019 superseded that framework and applied the Constitution of India in full. The separate constitutional challenge to Article 35A pending before the Supreme Court since 2014 was overtaken by that supersession.

### Q: Who was N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar and what role did he play in drafting Article 370?

N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar was a former Dewan of Kashmir who served as a minister without portfolio in Jawaharlal Nehru's cabinet and acted as the government's principal negotiator and draftsman for the Kashmir provision of the Constitution. On 17 October 1949 he moved the draft provision, then numbered 306A and finally Article 370, in the Constituent Assembly of India. His speech of that date is the primary record of the provision's intended character: he told the Assembly that the special circumstances of Kashmir, the war of 1947 to 1948, the reference to the United Nations and the pending decision of the state's own Constituent Assembly, required a temporary arrangement under which that Kashmiri body would determine the final shape of the relationship, the design embodied in the recommendation condition of Article 370(3).

### Q: What did the Delhi Agreement of 1952 settle between Nehru and Sheikh Abdullah?

The Delhi Agreement of July 1952, negotiated between Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Sheikh Abdullah, the head of the Jammu and Kashmir government, confirmed the architecture of the special status after accession. It recorded agreement that the state's constitutional relationship with the Union would rest on Article 370, that the state would retain its own flag alongside the Union flag, that the head of the state, the Sadar-i-Riyasat, would be elected by the state legislature rather than appointed, that the emergency provisions of the Indian Constitution would apply in limited form, and that the permanent resident protections would continue. The Jammu and Kashmir Constituent Assembly endorsed the agreement. Kashmiri political argument after 1953 treated the dismissal of Sheikh Abdullah in August of that year as Delhi's breach of the agreement; Union governments treated the agreement as a working arrangement within the Constitution rather than a treaty above it.

### Q: Why was the recommendation of the Jammu and Kashmir Constituent Assembly impossible to obtain after 1956?

Article 370(3) required the recommendation of the Constituent Assembly of Jammu and Kashmir before the President could declare the article inoperative. That Assembly was elected in 1951, framed the Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir, adopted it on 17 November 1956 and dissolved itself when the state constitution took effect on 26 January 1957. It made no recommendation under Article 370(3) at any point in its existence. After its dissolution no body bearing its name or its constituent character existed, so the recommendation the clause required could not be given in the form the text specified. The legal argument that followed for six decades concerned the consequence of that impossibility: whether the termination power had lapsed with the Assembly, as A.G. Noorani argued, or survived to be exercised through an interpretive substitution, as the Union argued in 2019 and the Supreme Court accepted in 2023.

### Q: What role did President's Rule play in the August 2019 procedure?

President's Rule supplied the institutional voices the procedure required. The Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly was dissolved by Governor Satya Pal Malik on 21 November 2018, and President's Rule was proclaimed on 19 December 2018 under the provisions applied to the state, transferring the legislature's powers to Parliament and the state government's functions to the Governor acting for the President. When the Presidential Order of 5 August 2019 read the Constituent Assembly of Article 370(3) as the Legislative Assembly, the body whose recommendation followed was a dissolved assembly whose functions Parliament exercised, and the concurrence of the state government was the concurrence of the Governor. Petitioners argued that the Union had thereby consented to itself; the Union answered that President's Rule constitutionally vests state functions in Union institutions, and the Supreme Court upheld the arrangement in December 2023.

### Q: What is the difference between the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir and the Union Territory of Ladakh under the Reorganisation Act?

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 gave the two territories different institutional forms. The Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir received a legislative assembly elected by its residents, with power to legislate on subjects in the State List of the Seventh Schedule except public order and police, and a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister aiding a Lieutenant Governor who retains discretion in excluded matters and in differences with the ministry. The Union Territory of Ladakh received no legislature; a Lieutenant Governor administers it directly under the President, with the elected Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Councils of Leh and Kargil continuing as local bodies. The asymmetry answered Ladakh's demand since 1989 for separation from Kashmir's administration, though Leh and Kargil bodies later jointly demanded statehood and Sixth Schedule protection for Ladakh.

### Q: What did Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul propose in his separate opinion in In Re Article 370?

Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul concurred with the Constitution Bench's unanimous conclusions upholding the abrogation, and added a separate opinion addressed to the human history behind the case. He recorded the suffering produced by the insurgency that began in 1989, including the displacement of Kashmiri Pandits from the Valley in 1990 and violations committed by state and non-state actors, and recommended that the Union constitute a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, on the pattern used in other divided societies, to investigate and report on human rights violations by state and non-state actors in Jammu and Kashmir since the 1980s. The recommendation carried no operative direction and did not affect the judgment's holdings. No such commission had been constituted within the period covered by the record on which this account rests, and Kashmiri parties and civil liberties organisations cited the unimplemented recommendation in their responses to the judgment.

### Q: How did the Anuradha Bhasin judgment of January 2020 treat the internet restrictions?

Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, decided by the Supreme Court on 10 January 2020 on the petition of the Kashmir Times editor Anuradha Bhasin and a companion petition, concerned the communication restrictions imposed in Kashmir from August 2019. The Court held that access to the internet through the medium of trade and expression enjoyed the protection of the Constitution's free speech and trade guarantees, that an indefinite suspension of internet services was impermissible, that orders restricting communications had to be published, reasoned and proportionate, and that the restriction orders in force had to be reviewed by the authorities. The Court did not strike down the restrictions itself or order immediate restoration. Petitioners argued in later proceedings that the directions were implemented in form rather than in substance; mobile internet in the Kashmir Valley, including fourth-generation services, was restored in stages concluding in February 2021.

### Q: What were the domicile rules introduced for Jammu and Kashmir in 2020?

The domicile rules replaced the permanent resident regime that Article 35A had protected. An amendment of April 2020 to the Jammu and Kashmir Civil Services (Decentralisation and Recruitment) Act defined a domicile of the Union Territory to include a person resident for fifteen years, a person who studied for seven years and appeared in the tenth or twelfth class examination in the territory, and children of central government and allied officials who served in the territory for ten years, among other categories. The Jammu and Kashmir Domicile (Procedure) Rules of May 2020 set the certification process through tehsildars. Eligibility for public employment in the territory was tied to the domicile certificate, and land law amendments of October 2020 removed the permanent resident condition for purchase of non-agricultural land. The administration presented the rules as equal citizenship; the Gupkar Declaration parties presented them as demographic alteration.

### Q: How did the Delimitation Commission of 2022 change the assembly map of Jammu and Kashmir?

The Delimitation Commission for Jammu and Kashmir, constituted in March 2020 under the chairpersonship of Justice Ranjana Prakash Desai, issued its final order on 5 May 2022. It redrew the Union Territory's legislative assembly at 90 elected seats, allotting 47 to the Kashmir division and 43 to the Jammu division, an increase of six seats for Jammu and one for Kashmir relative to the former state's elected map, and it reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and, for the first time in the territory, nine seats for Scheduled Tribes. The Commission stated criteria of population together with geography, area and administrative convenience. The National Conference, the People's Democratic Party and other Valley parties rejected the order as weighted toward Jammu and as an exercise conducted without an elected government in place; the Bharatiya Janata Party and Jammu-based parties welcomed it as correcting an historical imbalance between the divisions.

### Q: When did Jammu and Kashmir hold its first assembly elections as a Union Territory and who formed the government?

Jammu and Kashmir voted in its first assembly elections as a Union Territory in three phases on 18 September, 25 September and 1 October 2024, meeting the deadline the Supreme Court had set in its December 2023 judgment. The elections were also the territory's first assembly elections since 2014. The Jammu and Kashmir National Conference, contesting in alliance with the Indian National Congress, won the largest bloc of seats, and the alliance formed the government. Omar Abdullah, the National Conference vice president and a former chief minister of the state, was sworn in as Chief Minister on 16 October 2024. The new assembly's early resolutions sought restoration of statehood and safeguards for identity, land and employment, demands the Union government received without a timetable, and the elected ministry governed within the Reorganisation Act's limits, with police and public order retained by the Lieutenant Governor.

### Q: What was the Gupkar Declaration and which parties signed it?

The Gupkar Declaration was a joint statement issued on 4 August 2019, the day before the Presidential Orders, at the Srinagar residence of Farooq Abdullah on Gupkar Road, by the principal regional parties of Jammu and Kashmir together with national parties present in the state. Its signatories included the Jammu and Kashmir National Conference, the People's Democratic Party, the Indian National Congress's state leadership, the Communist Party of India (Marxist), the Jammu and Kashmir People's Conference at that stage and smaller regional parties. The declaration committed the parties to defend Articles 370 and 35A and the state's territorial unity. After the signatories' release from detention, the alliance was formalised in October 2020 as the People's Alliance for Gupkar Declaration, demanding restoration of the constitutional position as it stood on 4 August 2019, and the alliance won the largest share of seats in the District Development Council elections of 2020.

### Q: How did Pakistan and China respond to the August 2019 changes?

Pakistan responded to the August 2019 instruments by downgrading diplomatic relations with India, expelling the Indian High Commissioner, suspending bilateral trade and raising the matter at the United Nations Security Council, which discussed Kashmir in a closed consultation on 16 August 2019 without an agreed outcome statement. Successive governments in Islamabad made the restoration of Jammu and Kashmir's special status a stated precondition for the normalisation of relations, while India maintained that the changes were an internal constitutional matter and rejected third-party involvement under the Simla framework. China objected specifically to the creation of the Union Territory of Ladakh, stating that it affected territory under Chinese administration in the Aksai Chin region; India's Ministry of External Affairs rejected the objection. The February 2021 ceasefire understanding between the two armies' Directors General of Military Operations reduced firing along the Line of Control without altering any party's constitutional position.
