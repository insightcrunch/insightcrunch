The Benami Transactions Act rests on a simple and uncomfortable fact about Indian property holding: the name in the deed and the person who paid for the property are very often not the same person, and for most of the legal history of the subject the law had no reliable way of separating the innocent version of that arrangement from the fraudulent one. A father buys a house in his daughter's name out of taxed income and family affection. A trader buys the same house in his driver's name out of unaccounted cash so that no creditor, tax officer or estranged partner can find it. On paper the two transactions look identical, and a legal system that cannot tell them apart will either punish the father or license the trader. The statute examined here is Parliament's attempt, made twice, twenty eight years apart, to draw that line: first in 1988, when it prohibited benami transactions without building anything that could enforce the prohibition, and again in 2016, when it returned with a definition, a set of authorities, a power of attachment and confiscation, and criminal penalties of real severity.

![The Benami Transactions Act explained, from a dormant prohibition to the machinery of the amendment and the recalled Supreme Court judgment - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The second attempt is the one that matters in practice, and its story has a third act that most accounts get wrong. Armed with the machinery of 2016, the Income Tax Department attached property across the country, including property bought long before the amendment existed. In August 2022 the Supreme Court held in Union of India v. Ganpati Dealcom Pvt. Ltd. that the criminal provision of the original Act was unconstitutional and that the confiscation machinery could operate only prospectively. In October 2024 the same Court recalled that judgment in its entirety, because the constitutional question had been decided without ever having been argued, and restored the appeal for a fresh hearing. The consequence is a position of unusual honesty for a legal system: the two questions on which the whole enforcement edifice rests, whether the criminal provision is valid and whether confiscation can reach transactions completed before November 2016, are open questions, and any account that presents either answer as settled is misleading its reader. This pillar sets out the statute in full: what benami holding was before it was prohibited, what the 1988 Act said and why it could not act, what the 2016 amendment built, how a case moves through the machinery, what the courts decided and then undecided, and what a person holding family property in another's name can and cannot rely upon.

## Before the prohibition: a practice the law recognised

The word benami comes from Persian and means, in substance, without a name: a transaction in which property stands in one person's name while the consideration moves from another's pocket and the benefit flows back to the payer. For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries Indian courts did not treat the arrangement as inherently unlawful. Sections 81 and 82 of the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 gave the practice a statutory footing by recognising that where property was transferred to one person for consideration paid by another, and it was intended that the transferee hold for the payer's benefit, the transferee held in trust. Civil courts enforced that trust as a matter of routine. Benami purchases were a feature of family settlements, of land held against the uncertainties of partition, and of ordinary commerce in a society where joint family property and individual title sat uneasily together.

The reasons the practice flourished repay a moment's attention, because the amended Act's exceptions and offences are unintelligible without them. In a joint family system, property bought from family funds naturally stood in the name of the karta or of whichever member dealt with the outside world, and the line between family holding and benami holding was social rather than documentary. Land reform added a harder incentive: ceiling legislation in the States counted the land a person held, and holdings scattered across the names of relatives and dependents defeated the count. Agricultural land and tenancy laws that restricted who could buy or hold pushed purchases into the names of those who qualified. Creditors, partition disputes and the wish to keep a family's true wealth illegible to rivals and officials did the rest, and the revenue lost twice over, first because the income from such property escaped attribution and second because the transfers themselves escaped the record. A practice sustained by that many conveniences and that many evasions was never going to be reached by a statute that merely declared it prohibited.

The abuse ran alongside the convenience from the beginning. Property held in another's name defeats a creditor who cannot find it, defeats the revenue which cannot tax what it cannot attribute, defeats land ceiling legislation which counts only what a person visibly owns, and defeats an estranged spouse, a disinherited heir or a defrauded partner. Parliament's first intervention was narrow and indirect: section 281A, inserted into the Income-tax Act, 1961 in 1976, barred a person from suing to enforce rights in property held benami unless notice had been given to the income tax authorities, a disclosure condition designed to smoke the arrangement into the open rather than to prohibit it. The Law Commission took up the subject in its Fifty-seventh Report in 1973 and recommended comprehensive legislation against benami transactions, and the case for a prohibitory statute hardened as the scale of unaccounted wealth in real estate became a settled theme of economic policy. The interval between the Fifty-seventh Report and the Ordinance, fifteen years in which the recommendation sat unimplemented while section 281A's disclosure condition was worked around by the simple expedient of not suing, is the first appearance of the pattern this pillar tracks: benami reform in India has always been announced well before it was administered, and the distance between announcement and administration is where the practice lived. When the Ordinance came in 1988 it came as an urgency measure in the middle of a parliamentary session rather than as the considered product of the report process, and the statute that replaced it carried that haste in its structure, complete in its prohibitions and empty in its administration. What followed in 1988 was, in form, exactly the comprehensive prohibition the Commission had contemplated. In substance, as the next section shows, Parliament enacted the prohibition and stopped there.

## The Benami Transactions Act of 1988: a prohibition in nine sections

The immediate trigger was executive rather than parliamentary. On 19 May 1988 the President promulgated the Benami Transactions (Prohibition of the Right to Recover Property) Ordinance, 1988, which barred suits and defences founded on benami holdings, subject to two exceptions for property held by a coparcener of a Hindu undivided family and property held in a fiduciary capacity, and which repealed section 82 of the Indian Trusts Act, section 66 of the Code of Civil Procedure and section 281A of the Income-tax Act. The Ordinance's own title is a small history of the subject: it prohibited the right to recover property, attacking benami holding only through the courts' doors, and its critics in Parliament and in the press treated that as the vice of the measure, a prohibition of remedies against benami rather than of benami itself. The reference to the Law Commission and the One Hundred and Thirtieth Report that followed converted the criticism into a programme: the report contemplated a statute for all benami property, a criminal offence, and acquisition of the property by the State, and the replacing Bill adopted all three limbs. That genealogy matters to the pillar's argument, because the limb that was added last and debated least, acquisition, was the limb for which no administrative design was ever supplied, and the limb carried forward from the Ordinance, the civil bar, was the only one that functioned. The Act inherited its working part from an emergency ordinance and its unworking parts from a report's recommendations, and it never acquired the third thing it needed, an executive scheme beneath the text. The Ordinance drew criticism as half hearted, and the Law Commission was asked to examine the subject comprehensively; its One Hundred and Thirtieth Report, titled Benami Transactions: A Continuum, recommended a fuller statute covering all property, criminalising the transaction and providing for acquisition of benami property by the state. The Bill that replaced the Ordinance followed that design. The Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988, Act 45 of 1988, received assent on 5 September 1988; by section 1(3), sections 3, 5 and 8 came into force at once and the remaining provisions were deemed to have come into force on 19 May 1988, the date of the Ordinance. It had nine sections. Its brevity was not elegant economy. It was the whole of the law, and almost everything a working statute needs was missing from it.

Section 2(a) defined a benami transaction in a single line, as any transaction in which property is transferred to one person for a consideration paid or provided by another person, and section 2(c) defined property widely, as property of any kind, movable or immovable, tangible or intangible, including any right or interest in it. Section 3(1) then stated the prohibition: no person shall enter into any benami transaction. Section 3(2) carried a single family carve-out cast as a presumption, providing that a purchase by a person in the name of his wife or unmarried daughter was presumed, unless the contrary was proved, to have been made for the benefit of the wife or the unmarried daughter. Section 3(3) made the offence punishable with imprisonment for a term which could extend to three years, or with fine, or with both, and section 3(4) made that offence non-cognizable and bailable. Section 4 closed the civil courts to the real owner: no suit, claim or action to enforce any right in property held benami would lie at the instance of the person claiming to be the real owner, and no defence founded on such a right would be allowed either, with exceptions in section 4(3) for the Hindu undivided family coparcener and the fiduciary. The Supreme Court held in Mithilesh Kumari v. Prem Behari Khare in 1989 that the section 4 bar reached claims pending in appeal when the Act commenced, so the civil limb of the statute bit immediately and retrospectively on litigation already in train. Section 5 declared that all properties held benami shall be subject to acquisition by such authority, in such manner and after following such procedure as may be prescribed, and that no amount would be payable by way of compensation for any acquisition under the section. Section 8 conferred the power to make rules.

Read the nine sections again with a single question in mind: who was supposed to do anything under this Act? Section 4 needed nobody, because it operated in courts that already existed, and it worked from the first day. Section 3 created an offence, but a non-cognizable one, and the Act designated no agency to investigate benami holdings, receive information about them or build a case. Section 5 promised acquisition, but everything depended on the words as may be prescribed: the authority, the manner and the procedure were all left to rules, and the rules that would have prescribed an acquiring authority and a procedure were never made. No rules under the Act supplied the missing machinery at any point in the twenty eight years that followed. The Government of India conceded as much before the Supreme Court in the Ganpati Dealcom proceedings, accepting that sections 3 and 5 of the 1988 Act remained only on paper and were never implemented on the ground. A prohibition, an offence without an investigator, and a power of acquisition without an acquirer: that was the benami law from 1988 to 2016.

### Why did the 1988 benami Act never produce an acquisition of property?

Because the power in section 5 was incomplete on its face. It made benami property liable to acquisition by an authority, in a manner and by a procedure that were all to be prescribed by rules, and no rules prescribing any of the three were ever made, so there was never a lawful acquirer in existence.

The dormancy deserves one further distinction, because it explains why the failure went unnoticed for so long. The statute was not silent in the courts. Section 4 was litigated constantly: benami pleas were raised and rejected in partition suits, recovery suits and family disputes across the country, and the case law on what makes a transaction benami, on the burden of proof resting on the person who alleges it, and on the intention of the payer as the governing test, grew steadily under the 1988 Act. The Supreme Court's tests in cases such as Bhim Singh v. Kan Singh in 1980, decided under the pre-statute law and absorbed into the statutory era, and Jaydayal Poddar v. Bibi Hazra in 1974 on burden of proof, were applied by courts deciding benami questions throughout the period. A reader of the law reports would have seen a living subject. A reader of the statute book would have seen something else: a criminal offence that generated no enforcement apparatus and an acquisition power that never acquired anything. The benami prohibition was, in the enforcement sense, a dead letter carried in a living body of civil doctrine, and the difference between those two states is the analytical key to everything that happened in 2016 and after.

## The civil bar at work: section 4 in the courts

Because section 4 was the only limb of the 1988 Act with an institution attached to it, its career repays attention, both for what it did and for the irony of whom it protected. The bar extinguished the real owner's access to court in both directions: he could not sue to enforce any right in property held benami, and he could not set up his real ownership as a defence when the benamidar, or someone claiming through the benamidar, sued him. The repeals that accompanied the Act had closed the older escape routes in advance: section 82 of the Indian Trusts Act, which had let courts treat the name holder as trustee for the payer, was repealed, as were section 66 of the Code of Civil Procedure, which had barred suits against certified auction purchasers on benami grounds, and section 281A of the Income-tax Act, whose notice condition had been the previous, disclosure based approach. The design was coherent on its own terms. If the real owner could neither sue nor defend, the benami arrangement became a trap for its own creator: the payer's money bought property that the law would treat, in any courtroom, as the benamidar's.

The irony is that the trap protected the arrangement as often as it punished it. A benamidar who simply kept the property was unreachable by the one person who knew the truth, and the State, which might have broken the arrangement open, had no machinery with which to do so. In family litigation the bar cut in directions its drafters could only partly have intended: in partition and succession disputes, a plea that property standing in one member's name was really the joint family's or another member's purchase invited the section 4 bar where the plea amounted to a claim of real ownership against the paper title, while the two exceptions in section 4(3), for the Hindu undivided family coparcener claiming that the property was held for the family's benefit and for the fiduciary holding for his beneficiary, preserved the two channels through which genuine collective and trust holdings had always flowed. Courts spent the 1990s and 2000s working the boundary between a barred benami claim and a permitted plea, between the benami purchase the statute condemned and the joint family or fiduciary holding it exempted, and the resulting body of decisions is the true working law of benami for the period. Mithilesh Kumari settled the temporal question early, in 1989: a litigant whose benami claim or defence was pending, even in appeal, when the Act commenced lost it, because the bar spoke to the maintainability of the proceeding rather than to the validity of the original purchase.

One further consequence deserves recording, because it shaped the politics of 2016. Twenty eight years of section 4 litigation taught Indian families a specific lesson about benami holding: the risk was private, not public. Property in another's name was dangerous because the name holder might turn, or his heirs might refuse, or a creditor of his might attach, and thousands of arrangements were undone or defended in civil courts on exactly those risks. No family arranged its affairs against the risk of the State, because the State had never come. When the machinery of 2016 arrived and was pointed at holdings of that vintage, the grievance that powered the retrospectivity litigation was precisely this settled expectation: not that benami holding was lawful, section 3 had said otherwise since 1988, but that its unlawfulness had only ever had private consequences, and that the public consequence was being introduced against transactions structured in reliance on a generation of public inaction. The Supreme Court's eventual engagement with the subject cannot be understood without that expectation in view.

## A prohibition is not a law until someone can enforce it

This series has a running argument about the ways Indian statutes fail, and the benami Act supplies a failure mode distinct from the ones catalogued so far. The familiar failure is non-commencement: Parliament enacts a statute and the executive never notifies it into force, so the law exists as print and nothing more, the pattern examined in this series' account of the [Whistle Blowers Protection Act](/2012/01/01/whistle-blowers-protection-act-2014/). The benami Act did not fail that way. It was in force from 1988, every section of it, and its civil limb genuinely changed outcomes in court. It failed at the next level down. A prohibition in the criminal and confiscatory sense is not a self executing statement of policy; it is a promise that three further things exist: someone authorised to find the prohibited conduct, a procedure by which that person can act against property or liberty, and a forum before which the affected person can answer. The 1988 Act supplied none of the three for its offence and its acquisition power. The namable claim of this pillar is therefore precise: a prohibition is not a law until someone can enforce it, and between 1988 and 2016 India had a benami prohibition that was, in that strict sense, not a law at all.

The claim matters because it disciplines the reading of what happened in 2016. The amendment of that year is usually described as strengthening the benami law, a word that implies a working statute made more potent. The accurate description is different: 2016 was the first benami law. Everything the amendment added, the definitions with their exceptions, the four authorities, the notice and attachment procedure, the adjudication, the tribunal, the differentiated offences, was not an enhancement layered on a functioning base but the entire operative content of the regime, attached to a prohibition that had waited twenty eight years for it. That is also why the retrospective question, examined later in this pillar, proved so destructive. Because the prohibition had existed on paper since 1988, governments could argue that the machinery of 2016 merely completed an old sentence; because nothing had ever been enforced under the paper prohibition, property holders could answer that a sentence completed after twenty eight years, against transactions closed in reliance on the law's silence, was in substance a new law aimed backwards. Both arguments begin from the same fact, the dormancy, and the dormancy is therefore not background colour in this story. It is the origin of the litigation that would eventually reach the Supreme Court twice.

## The road back: the failed Bill of 2011 and the amendment of 2016

Parliament knew about the gap long before it filled it. In his budget-era reform programme the then Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee introduced the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Bill, 2011 in the Lok Sabha on 18 August 2011, a consolidating Bill that would have replaced the 1988 Act with a complete statute containing a definition, confiscation, authorities drawn from the Income Tax Department and an appellate mechanism. The Standing Committee on Finance examined it and reported on it, but the Bill was never passed, and it lapsed on the dissolution of the Fifteenth Lok Sabha in 2014. The design choice visible in the 2011 Bill, replacement rather than repair, was abandoned by the next government. On 13 May 2015 the Finance Minister Arun Jaitley introduced the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Bill, 2015, which kept the 1988 Act alive and rebuilt it from within, a choice with a legal consequence that later litigation would test: the prohibition of 1988 and the machinery of 2016 would form one continuous statute, and the argument that the machinery completed an existing prohibition rather than created a new one was built into the very structure of the reform.

The contrast between the two attempts is instructive beyond the choice of vehicle. The 2011 Bill, as introduced by Pranab Mukherjee, proposed a fresh statute to consolidate and amend the law, prohibiting the holding of property in benami, restricting the right to recover or transfer it, and providing a mechanism and procedure for confiscation, with authorities drawn from the Income Tax Department and confiscation free of compensation, and it died the ordinary death of an unpassed money adjacent measure, lapsing when the Fifteenth Lok Sabha dissolved without the Bill having been taken up for passage after committee examination. The 2015 Bill kept the architecture the 2011 exercise had worked out, the four authorities, the adjudication model, the confiscation without compensation, and changed the container: rather than replace the 1988 Act, it amended and renamed it, preserving the 1988 commencement date in the statute's very title, the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988. That preservation was defended as continuity, and continuity had a consequence the drafters may or may not have priced in. Because the operative Act claims descent from 1988, every transaction since 1988 sits inside the same statute as the machinery, and the question whether the machinery follows the prohibition backwards through the whole life of the Act was left to be answered by implication rather than by an express transitional provision confining it. A single clause stating that Chapter IV applied only to transactions entered into after commencement would have settled the largest litigation the Act produced. Its absence is the most consequential drafting decision in the entire benami story.

The Bill's own statement of objects and reasons had identified the defects of the 1988 Act in terms that conceded this pillar's central claim: the absence of provisions for the vesting of confiscated property in the Central Government, the absence of an appellate mechanism, and the failure of the rule making and implementation framework, three admissions that together describe a prohibition without machinery. The Bill was examined by the Standing Committee on Finance and, after the government's amendments were moved, passed by the Lok Sabha on 27 July 2016 and by the Rajya Sabha on 2 August 2016. It received the assent of the President on 10 August 2016 as the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Act, 2016, Act 43 of 2016, and was brought into force on 1 November 2016 by notification S.O. 3289(E) dated 25 October 2016, accompanied by the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions (Amendment) Rules of 2016. The amendment renamed the parent Act the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988, and expanded it from nine sections to seventy two. It was also, deliberately, one part of a package aimed at unaccounted wealth from three directions at once: the Black Money Act of 2015, examined in this series at [the Black Money Act 2015 explained](/2012/09/01/black-money-act-2015-explained/), had taken the offshore limb a year earlier, the benami amendment took property held in other names at home, and a week after the amendment commenced, on 8 November 2016, the withdrawal of the high denomination currency notes forced unaccounted cash toward the banking system, where benami deposits would themselves become attachments under the new law. The sequencing was the policy: close the offshore route, build the domestic property machinery, then shake the cash economy and watch what surfaced.

## What counts as a benami transaction under the amended Act

Section 2(9) of the amended Act replaces the one line definition of 1988 with four limbs, and the structure of the definition is the first thing a careful reader should absorb, because three of the four limbs need no family relationship, no secret and no paper trail of the ordinary kind. Clause (A) captures the classic case: a transaction or arrangement where property is transferred to, or is held by, a person, and the consideration has been provided or paid by another person, and the property is held for the benefit or future benefit, direct or indirect, of the person who provided the consideration. The three elements are standing separated from payment, and payment separated from benefit: the name on the property, the source of the money and the destination of the benefit point to different persons. The words transaction or arrangement, repeated through the clause, carry weight of their own: the definition is not confined to registered transfers or documented purchases, and the classic benami holding, an oral family understanding standing behind a formally regular deed, is as much within clause (A) as a papered scheme, because what the clause examines is the separation of payment, title and benefit rather than the form in which the parties recorded their understanding. Clause (B) captures a transaction carried out or made in a fictitious name. Clause (C) captures a transaction where the owner of the property is not aware of, or denies knowledge of, such ownership, the parked name who may not even know the property exists. Clause (D) captures a transaction where the person providing the consideration is not traceable or is fictitious. An Explanation appended to the clause then removes a familiar conveyancing arrangement: allowing possession to be taken or retained in part performance of a contract of the kind described in section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 is not a benami transaction where the consideration has been provided by the person allowed into possession, stamp duty on the transaction has been paid, and the contract has been registered.

Around the definition the Act builds its cast. Benami property, in section 2(8), means any property which is the subject matter of a benami transaction, and includes the proceeds from such property, so the net follows value rather than form: sale proceeds, converted assets and substituted holdings remain within reach. The benamidar, in section 2(10), is the person or fictitious person in whose name the property is held or transferred. The beneficial owner, in section 2(12), is the person, whether his identity is known or not, for whose benefit the property is held by the benamidar. The vocabulary is worth fixing early because the statute's moral geometry lives in it: the benamidar lends a name and may be a willing accomplice, a paid name lender, a dependent relative or, under clause (C), an unknowing stranger; the beneficial owner is the centre of gravity of the offence, the person the machinery exists to expose; and the property is the anchor that lets the State act in rem, against the asset, whether or not it can first convict anyone. The three limbs beyond clause (A) map onto the field reality the department described in its first statements. Clause (B), the fictitious name, is the limb of shell companies and invented purchasers, the pattern in the fifty acre acquisition where shareholders were name lenders and directors fictitious. Clause (C), the owner unaware of or denying the ownership, is the limb of the parked name: the employee in whose account deposits were placed during the currency withdrawal, the domestic worker or driver in whose name a flat stands, a person who learns of his property from the department's notice and whose denial of knowledge, paradoxically, completes the definition against the arrangement. Clause (D), the untraceable or fictitious payer, is the limb for layered holdings in which the money's origin has been deliberately dissolved. A benamidar in the clause (C) pattern occupies the statute's most exposed human position: the proceedings are in his name, the attachment freezes property on his title, and his defence, that he knew nothing, is simultaneously the fact that brings the transaction within the definition, so that his protection lies not in denial but in the adjudication's focus on the beneficial owner and in the absence of any benefit flowing to him.

Chapter II then restates the civil consequences in their amended form, and the restatement is easy to underrate because the sections kept their numbers. Section 3 prohibits entering into a benami transaction and, by the amended sub section (3), punishes a transaction entered into on or after the amendment's commencement in the manner provided in Chapter VII, leaving the older transactions to the confiscation debate rather than to prosecution. Section 4 carries forward the bar on the real owner's right to recover the property or to raise his ownership in claim or defence. Section 5, which in 1988 had promised an acquisition that never came, is rewritten as a declaration that property held benami shall be liable to be confiscated by the Central Government, a change of verb that marks the change of regime: acquisition sounded in the State taking property for a purpose, confiscation sounds in the State seizing property for a wrong, and the migration of the word between 1988 and 2016 tracks the migration of the theory underneath it. Section 6, new in substance, forbids the benamidar to re-transfer the property to the beneficial owner or anyone acting on his behalf and makes a contravening transfer null and void, sealing the private exit from the arrangement. Read together, the four sections leave a benami holding with no lawful future: it cannot be enforced by the payer, cannot be surrendered back by the holder, and is liable to be taken by the State, so that the only lawful courses the statute contemplates are the exceptions at entry and disclosure and defence once a notice arrives. That separation of asset proceedings from personal guilt, generous to enforcement and troubling to property rights, runs through every later section and both Supreme Court encounters.

The reach of the word property completes the definition's architecture. Section 2(26) defines property as assets of any kind, whether movable or immovable, tangible or intangible, corporeal or incorporeal, including any right or interest in it and any legal document or instrument evidencing title, and, where the property is capable of conversion into another form, the property in its converted form together with its proceeds. The definition matters because benami holding in practice is only sometimes a house in a driver's name. The first enforcement wave reached bank deposits placed in the accounts of employees, shares held through name lenders and fictitious directors, and cash converted into other assets, and each of those targets depends on the breadth of section 2(26) read with the proceeds language in the definition of benami property. A department that can follow value through conversion can pursue the arrangement rather than the asset; a holder who sold the original property and banked the price has changed the form of the exposure, not escaped it. The breadth also explains why the definition's fourth limb, the untraceable or fictitious payer, does independent work: in deposit and share cases the department often cannot identify who in fact paid, and the limb lets the case proceed on the structure of the holding rather than on the identity of the person behind it, at the price of placing the explanatory burden, in practice, on the name holder who can be found.

## The exceptions: where family property stands

The exceptions to clause (A) are where the statute meets Indian family life, and they are drafted as conditions rather than as sentiments. A transaction is not benami where the property is held by a karta or a member of a Hindu undivided family for the benefit of the family, and the consideration has been paid out of the known sources of income of the family. It is not benami where the property is held by a person standing in a fiduciary capacity for the benefit of another person towards whom he stands in that capacity, a category the Act illustrates by naming a trustee, an executor, a partner, a director of a company, a depository or a participant under the Depositories Act, 1996, and any other person notified by the Central Government. It is not benami where an individual holds property in the name of a spouse or of any child, and the consideration has been paid out of the known sources of income of the individual. And it is not benami where an individual holds property in the name of a brother or sister or a lineal ascendant or descendant, where the individual and the named relative appear as joint owners in any document, and again the consideration has been paid out of known sources.

The Hindu undivided family exception deserves a word of its own, because the HUF is the one Indian institution in which separated name and separated benefit are not an aberration but the design. Property of the family is held by the karta, managed by him, and enjoyed by members whose names appear on no deed, and a statute that treated every such holding as benami would have criminalised the ordinary constitution of millions of families. The exception therefore protects holdings by a karta or a member for the benefit of the family where the consideration came from the family's known sources, and its boundary is the boundary of the family fund: a purchase from the karta's personal and unaccounted money, in a member's name, for that member's separate benefit, is outside the exception however the family describes it. The fiduciary exception performs the parallel service for the institutional world: trustees hold for beneficiaries, partners for firms, directors for companies, depositories for investors, and the exception, by naming those capacities and letting the Central Government notify others, keeps the definition aimed at concealment rather than at the separation of legal and beneficial title on which all modern property holding depends.

Two hinges do the real work. The first is the phrase known sources of income, which appears in three of the four exceptions and converts a question about family affection into a question about documentation: the purchase escapes the definition not because the relationship is close but because the money's route can be accounted for within the family's taxed and recorded resources. Money that cannot be placed within known sources fails the exception even between a husband and wife, and money that can be so placed succeeds even though the holder and the payer differ. The second hinge is the joint ownership condition in the sibling and lineal relative exception, which is narrower than most families assume. A son who buys property solely in his father's name, or a sister who funds a flat held solely in her brother's name, is outside the fourth exception however genuine the arrangement, because the payer's name does not appear as joint owner in the document; the statute protects siblings and ascendants and descendants only when the paper shows both names. Against the 1988 position the movement is double sided. The older Act presumed a purchase in the name of a wife or unmarried daughter to be for her benefit, a presumption the amendment abandons; the amended Act instead protects a defined set of relationships defined by source of funds, and it extends protection sideways to siblings and upwards and downwards along the line of descent in a way the 1988 text never did, while withdrawing any automatic presumption. Families who arranged property under the older understanding, in a wife's sole name with mixed or undocumented funds, hold the class of assets over which the retrospective litigation would be fought.

### Is buying property in your wife's name a benami transaction?

Not by itself. The amended Act expressly excludes property held by an individual in the name of a spouse, where the consideration has been paid out of the known sources of income of that individual. The exposure begins where the funds cannot be shown to come from known sources, because the exception protects the documented purchase, not the relationship alone.

The burden of proof doctrine inherited from the older law continues to matter inside the new definition. The person who alleges that a transaction is benami must prove it, and the governing test remains the intention with which the arrangement was made, assessed through the source from which the purchase money came, the nature and character of possession after the purchase, the motive for giving the property a benami colour, the position and relationship of the parties, the custody of the title deeds and the conduct of the parties after the sale, the factors the Supreme Court gathered in Bhim Singh v. Kan Singh. The department's notice under the amended Act must therefore do more than point at a mismatch between name and payment; it must build, from banking records, income records and possession, a case that the property is held for the payer's benefit and that no exception carries the transaction out of the definition. The exceptions are not decorative. In adjudication they are the ground on which most genuine family arrangements win, and the department's early enforcement history, examined below, shows notices failing precisely where the source of consideration was explained.

## Why detection is hard: deeds, mutation and the paper of ownership

Every benami case is decided on records, and the condition of India's property records explains both why the practice flourished and why the machinery proceeds as it does. A sale deed registered under the Registration Act, 1908 records the parties the parties choose to present: the deed names the purchaser, recites the consideration and is registered on payment of stamp duty, and nothing in the registration process inquires whose money paid the price or whose benefit the purchase serves. Revenue records and mutation entries, which govern the fiscal visibility of land, follow the deed and record the holder for the purposes of land revenue; mutation is a fiscal entry, not a determination of title, and it replicates the deed's name without testing it. The banking system holds the one record that routinely contradicts the deed, the trail of the consideration, and it is no accident that the amended Act staffs its machinery from the Income Tax Department and arms its officers with powers to compel production of books and documents: the department is the one institution that habitually sees the money side of transactions whose paper side sits in the registration and revenue records. A benami inquiry is therefore an exercise in joining three partial records, the deed that names the holder, the revenue entry that repeats the name, and the money trail that points elsewhere, and the adjudication succeeds or fails on whether the three can be made to tell one story. Families arranging property in good faith defeat inquiries at exactly this joint: where the deed, the funding trail and the tax filings align, there is no second story to tell.

## How a transaction is proved benami: burden, intention and the classic tests

Because the definition turns on benefit and intention, the law of proof decides more benami cases than the text of section 2(9) does, and that law was built by the courts long before the machinery existed. Its first principle, stated in Jaydayal Poddar v. Bibi Hazra in 1974 and repeated in every later restatement, is that the burden of proving a transaction benami rests squarely on the person who alleges it, and that the proof required is strict: it is not enough to raise a suspicion, however strong, or to show that the ostensible owner had no independent means, because the law starts from the position that the person named in the document is the owner. The second principle is that intention is the essence of the inquiry, the intention of the person who contributed the purchase money, gathered from the surrounding circumstances rather than from any single fact. The Supreme Court's most cited gathering of those circumstances, in Bhim Singh v. Kan Singh in 1980, lists the source from which the purchase money came, the nature and character of possession of the property after the purchase, the motive if any for giving the transaction a benami colour, the position of the parties and their relationship, the custody of the title deeds after the sale, and the conduct of the parties in dealing with the property after the sale. Later benches restated the same inquiry in fuller form: the three judge bench era restatements in cases such as P. Leelavathi v. V. Shankarnarayana Rao in 2019 repeat that the source of consideration is a material but not conclusive circumstance, that possession and enjoyment and the payment of taxes and charges matter, that the custody of the title deeds matters, and that a party who sets up a benami case must stand or fall on the proof he adduces, since the weakness of the defence is no substitute for the proof of the allegation.

Two distinctions inside the doctrine do practical work under the amended Act. The first is between a benami transaction and a sham transaction: in a sham transaction the document was never intended to operate at all and no title passes, while in a benami transaction the transfer is real and title genuinely vests in the benamidar, which is why the consequences differ and why the department's case is not advanced by showing that the parties called the arrangement fictitious among themselves. The second is the shifting of the evidentiary burden. The legal burden stays with the allegation throughout, but once the department or the claimant establishes the foundational facts, separated payment, separated benefit, possession retained by the payer, title deeds retained by the payer, the practical burden of explanation moves to the holder, and it is at that point that the exceptions and the known sources record do their work. Read the machinery of Chapter IV through this doctrine and its design clarifies: the section 24 notice is the department's foundational case reduced to writing, the reply is the holder's explanation, and the adjudication is the testing of the Bhim Singh circumstances against documents, bank trails, possession and conduct. The statute of 2016 did not invent a new law of benami proof. It gave the old law an officer, a forum and consequences.

## The machinery the amendment built

Chapter III of the amended Act answers the question the 1988 Act never faced: who acts. Section 18 names four authorities. The Initiating Officer, an Assistant Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner of Income-tax, is the officer who forms the reason to believe that a person has entered into a benami transaction and sets the case in motion. The Approving Authority, an Additional Commissioner or a Joint Commissioner of Income-tax, is the senior check on the Initiating Officer, whose prior approval is required before the sharpest interim step, provisional attachment, can be taken. The Adjudicating Authority, appointed under section 7, is the quasi judicial forum that decides, after hearing the parties, whether the property is benami property and whether attachment should be confirmed toward confiscation. The Administrator, an Income-tax Officer, receives, manages and takes possession of property that stands confiscated. Section 19 gives these authorities, for the purposes of the Act, the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure when trying a suit, including powers of discovery and inspection, enforcing attendance and examining on oath, and compelling production of books of account and documents. The design borrows wholesale from tax administration: the investigators are income tax officers, the approving layer is their hierarchy, and the adjudicator stands outside the department's chain of command in form, though the Act originally filled the adjudicating and service provisions through sections 8 to 17, a block of provisions omitted by the Finance Act, 2021 when the adjudicating functions were consolidated.

Because a new tribunal and adjudicating structure could not be staffed overnight, the Act arrived with a transitional engine borrowed from its neighbours. Section 71 provided that until the Adjudicating Authority and the Appellate Tribunal were constituted under the benami Act itself, the Adjudicating Authority under the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002 and the Appellate Tribunal under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act, 1976 would discharge the corresponding functions under this Act. Notifications of 25 October 2016, S.O. 3290(E) and S.O. 3288(E), operationalised the arrangement by designating the income tax officers and the money laundering adjudicating machinery for benami work from the commencement date. The borrowing was practical and consequential. Practical, because the department could issue notices within days of commencement using experienced officers and an adjudicator already deciding attachment cases under the money laundering statute. Consequential, because the benami caseload landed on institutions built for other laws, and the Appellate Tribunal that hears benami appeals is the same tribunal whose money laundering and forfeiture docket shapes its capacity, a structural constraint that returns in the assessment at the end of this pillar. The borrowed design then hardened into the permanent one. The amended Act had originally carried its own provisions, in sections 8 to 17, for the appointment, qualifications and service conditions of the Adjudicating Authority and its members, contemplating a benami adjudicator of the Act's own; the Finance Act, 2021 omitted that entire block of provisions, leaving the section 71 arrangement, adjudication by the money laundering authority and appeal to the forfeiture tribunal, as the durable structure rather than a stopgap. A reader mapping the amended Act should therefore picture its forums as shared institutions with three statutory masters, and its delays and listing pressures as the predictable arithmetic of that sharing.

## The map of the amended Act

For a reader opening the statute, its eight chapters divide the work cleanly. Chapter I, sections 1 and 2, contains the short title, the commencement scheme and the twenty nine definitions on which everything else depends. Chapter II, sections 3 to 6, states the prohibition of benami transactions in section 3, the prohibition of the right to recover property held benami in section 4, the liability of benami property to confiscation in section 5, and the prohibition on the benamidar re-transferring the property to the beneficial owner in section 6. Chapter III, sections 7 to 23, constitutes the authorities and their powers, with section 18 naming the four authorities and section 19 arming them with civil court powers. Chapter IV, sections 24 to 29, is the procedural engine room: notice, provisional attachment, reference, adjudication, confiscation and the Administrator's management of confiscated property. Chapter V, sections 30 to 49, constitutes the Appellate Tribunal, its procedure and the appeals that lie to it under section 46 and onward to the High Court under section 49. Chapter VI, sections 50 to 52, provides for Special Courts and trial. Chapter VII, sections 53 to 55, contains the offences and the sanction requirement. Chapter VIII, sections 56 to 72, carries the miscellaneous provisions that practitioners cite most after the definitions: section 57, which makes transfers after a section 24 notice null and void; section 61, which makes the offences non-cognizable; section 68, the rule making power; section 71, the transitional provision that staffed the forums; and section 72, the repeal and saving clause under which the Ordinance era provisions were swept away. Two dates organise the whole map: 19 May 1988, from which the original Act's operative provisions ran, and 1 November 2016, the commencement of the amendment, and almost every dispute in the case law is, at bottom, a dispute about which side of the second date a transaction or a holding falls on.

## How a benami case moves, from notice to confiscation

Chapter IV supplies the procedure, and its shape is a funnel with judicialised checkpoints. It begins in section 24. Where the Initiating Officer, on the basis of material in his possession, has reason to believe that any person is a benamidar in respect of a property, he may, after recording reasons in writing, issue a notice to the benamidar calling upon him to show cause why the property should not be treated as benami property; where the beneficial owner is known, a copy of the notice goes to him as well. The notice is the foundation document of the entire proceeding, and the courts and the tribunal have treated the recorded reasons as examinable: a notice that merely recites suspicion, or that proceeds on a transaction falling within an exception, is vulnerable from the first day. Where the Initiating Officer is of the opinion that the person in possession may alienate the property during the notice period, he may, with the previous approval of the Approving Authority, provisionally attach the property in the manner provided in the Second Schedule to the Income-tax Act, 1961. As enacted in 2016, the officer then had ninety days from the issue of the notice to decide whether the provisional attachment should continue or be revoked, and, where attachment was ordered or continued, fifteen days from that order to draw up a statement of the case and refer it to the Adjudicating Authority. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 recast these intervals from 1 October 2024, giving the noticee a reply window extendable up to three months and framing the attachment decision and the reference in periods of four months and one month counted from the end of the month in which the notice issued and the order was passed, a legislative acknowledgment that the original timetable had proved too tight for the record building the statute demands.

Two features of the opening steps have generated a disproportionate share of the litigation and deserve precise statement. The first is the standard the Initiating Officer must meet. The phrase reason to believe carries a settled meaning in Indian fiscal law: the belief must be honestly held, it must rest on relevant material in the officer's possession, and the reasons must be recorded in writing before the notice issues. A court examining the notice does not ask whether it would itself have believed, and it does not weigh the sufficiency of the material as an appellate forum would; it asks whether material existed, whether it bore a rational connection to the belief that a benami transaction had occurred, and whether the officer applied his mind to it. Notices have failed that examination where they recited the definition without material, where the material showed a transaction inside an exception, and where the reasons were recorded after the event or borrowed wholesale from another proceeding. The second feature is the character of provisional attachment. It is effected in the manner provided in the Second Schedule to the Income-tax Act, 1961, the machinery used for recovery of tax dues, which for immovable property works by an order prohibiting transfer or the creation of a charge and for movable property by seizure or prohibitory orders to custodians such as banks. It freezes; it does not vest, and between attachment and adjudication the holder keeps title and loses only the power to deal, which is why the approval of the Approving Authority and the ninety day decision point in the original scheme were the statute's interim safeguards rather than formalities.

The timetable's history is itself instructive. The scheme enacted in 2016, notice, a ninety day window for the attachment decision, a reference within fifteen days and adjudication within a year of the reference, assumed a record that could be assembled at departmental speed, and in operation the short intervals invited procedural challenge and extension seeking that consumed the forums' time. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 lengthened the front of the funnel from 1 October 2024: the noticee's time to reply, which the recast frames at thirty days extendable up to three months, the attachment decision moved to four months counted from the end of the month in which the notice issued, and the reference to one month counted from the end of the month in which the attachment order was passed. Legislatures rarely lengthen their own enforcement timetables without cause, and the recast is best read as an official acknowledgment that the quality of benami adjudication depends on records, bank trails, title histories and family documentation, that take months rather than weeks to build and test.

Adjudication follows under section 26. The Adjudicating Authority issues notice to the benamidar, the beneficial owner, any person holding an interest in the property and other interested persons, considers the replies, the material and the evidence, hears the parties, and passes an order holding the property to be benami property and confirming the attachment, or holding that it is not and revoking the attachment; the Act sets an outer limit of one year from the end of the month in which the reference is received for that adjudication to conclude. Where the order holds the property benami, section 27 moves to the consequence: after giving the persons concerned an opportunity of being heard, and subject to the order of the Appellate Tribunal on appeal, the Adjudicating Authority passes an order confiscating the property, whereupon all rights and title in the property vest in the Central Government free from all encumbrances and, in the words of section 27(3), no compensation shall be payable in respect of the confiscation. The Act protects one figure inside this severity: a person who acquired an interest in the property in good faith, for adequate consideration and without knowledge of its benami character before the section 24 notice, is not to lose it to confiscation. Sections 28 and 29 then hand the confiscated property to the Administrator, who manages it, takes possession of it, and deals with it subject to the Central Government's directions; and section 57 freezes the aftermath of a notice by providing that a transfer of attached property after the issue of a section 24 notice is null and void for the purposes of the Act.

### Who decides whether a property is benami?

The Adjudicating Authority under section 7 decides, after notice, replies, evidence and a hearing under section 26, and its order is appealable to the Appellate Tribunal under section 46. The Initiating Officer only opens the case and attaches provisionally; he cannot finally hold a property to be benami property or confiscate it.

Two features of this design deserve emphasis before the offences are reached. The first is that the track to confiscation is civil and proprietary: it proceeds against the property, on the civil standard, through an authority appointed under the statute, and it requires no conviction, no charge sheet and no proof of the evasive purpose that the criminal limb demands. The second is the compensation rule. Indian property law elsewhere treats state taking as an event with a price: the land acquisition framework examined in this series at [the LARR Act 2013 explained](/2013/11/01/larr-act-2013-explained/) is built, whatever its other failures, around compensation and rehabilitation as the constitutional price of compulsory acquisition under Article 300A. Benami confiscation inverts the premise deliberately: the property, in the statute's theory, was never rightfully the holder's to lose, and the payer's claim is the very claim the Act refuses to recognise, so nothing is payable. Whether a taking without compensation on that reasoning is a legitimate classification or a punishment wearing civil dress was one of the questions the Supreme Court confronted in 2022, resolved in the property holder's favour, and then, by the recall of 2024, confronted no longer.

What adjudication examines, in the run of cases, is a record of a recognisable kind. The department's material typically begins outside the family altogether: a search or survey under the Income-tax Act that surfaces a deed in a third name, banking records showing consideration moving from an account that is not the purchaser's, deposits made during the currency withdrawal of November 2016 into accounts whose holders could not explain them, or shareholding records in which the named holders are employees, drivers or persons of no visible means. Against that material the holder's answer is assembled from the opposite direction: the payer's returns and bank statements placing the consideration within known sources, the relationship documents engaging an exception, possession and rent records showing who in fact enjoyed the property, the custody of the original title deeds, and the treatment of the property in the parties' own tax filings over the years, since a payer who has declared the property's rent as his own income for a decade has made the department's benefit case in his own hand, while a purchaser in whose returns the property and its income consistently appear has evidence of ownership that the paper title alone would not supply. Adjudicating Authorities revoke attachments where that answering record is coherent, and confirm where it is improvised, which is the practical meaning of the burden doctrine described earlier: the statute's funnel is wide at the notice stage by design, and it is the documentary record, not the allegation, that decides who comes out of it.

## After confiscation: the Administrator and the afterlife of the property

The statute's least examined figure is the one who inherits its victories. On a confiscation order, sections 28 and 29 charge the Administrator, an Income-tax Officer designated for the purpose, with the management of the confiscated property: he receives it, takes possession of it, and holds and deals with it subject to the conditions and directions the Act and the Central Government prescribe, and all rights and title stand vested in the Central Government free from all encumbrances from the date of the order. The administrative reality beneath that clean vesting is untidy in ways the drafters of acquisition statutes would recognise. Confiscated benami property is frequently occupied, by the benamidar's family, by tenants inducted before or after the notice whose transfers the Act treats differently, or by the beneficial owner's own household, since the paradigm case is a family home standing in the wrong name. It is frequently encumbered in fact if not in law, by mortgages granted to banks whose protection the Act recognises where the loan was made in good faith for consideration before notice, a qualification that has required the forums to work out, property by property, whether a lender's security survives a confiscation that is expressed to vest the asset free of encumbrances. And it is frequently illiquid and litigated for years while the appeals run, during which the Administrator is less a receiver of value than a caretaker of contested assets. None of this diminishes the confiscation power's deterrent logic, but it qualifies the fiscal story told about the first enforcement wave: attachment and even confiscation convert concealed ownership into State held property on paper long before they convert it into realised public value, and the distance between the two is measured in the Administrator's registers rather than in the department's press statements.

## The offence side: prosecution under the amended Act

Section 3 as amended keeps the prohibition and routes its punishment to Chapter VII for transactions entered into on or after the commencement of the amendment, a textual detail of real importance: Parliament itself confined criminal punishment under the new dispensation to transactions of the new era, whatever might be argued about the civil machinery. Section 53 is the operative offence. Where a person enters into a benami transaction in order to defeat the provisions of any law, or to avoid payment of statutory dues, or to avoid payment to creditors, the beneficial owner, the benamidar and any other person who abets or induces any person to enter into the transaction are guilty of the offence of benami transaction, punishable with rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than one year and extending to seven years, and with fine which may extend to twenty five per cent of the fair market value of the property. The three purposes are the mens rea of the offence: the State must prove not merely that name and money diverged but that the divergence was engineered to defeat a law, a statutory due or a creditor. Section 54 punishes the informant's mirror image: a person who furnishes false information or a false document to an authority, knowing it to be false, in any proceeding or inquiry, faces rigorous imprisonment from six months to five years and fine up to ten per cent of the fair market value of the property. Section 55 bars prosecution for the offences under section 3, section 53 and section 54 without the previous sanction the section requires, section 61 makes the offences non-cognizable, and Chapter VI establishes Special Courts for trial, with section 51 directing that the trial be concluded within six months from the date of filing of the complaint. The sanction requirement performs the same office in this Act that it performs across Indian fiscal legislation: it interposes a senior, record based decision between the investigating officer's conclusion and the citizen's arraignment, filtering out prosecutions in which the civil case has collapsed or the purpose element of section 53 cannot honestly be alleged, and its absence is a defect that goes to the root of a prosecution rather than a curable irregularity, so that a complaint filed without the required sanction is liable to fail at the threshold whatever the merits beneath it. Chapter V completes the remedial ladder: an appeal under section 46 to the Appellate Tribunal within forty five days of the adjudication order, and an appeal under section 49 to the High Court within sixty days on a substantial question of law. The Special Courts complete the trial design: under section 50, the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, designates a Court of Session as a Special Court for the trial of offences under the Act for a specified area or class of cases, and appeals and revisions from that court lie to the High Court as under the Code of Criminal Procedure.

The drafting contrast between the two offence regimes is deliberate and should not be missed. The 1988 offence attached to the act of entering into a benami transaction as such: no purpose, no knowledge and no consequence needed to be proved, and the same provision that awaited the professional concealer awaited the father who bought in his unmarried daughter's name outside the presumption's protection. The 2016 offence in section 53 attaches only where the transaction was entered into in order to defeat a law, avoid a statutory due or avoid payment to creditors, so that the prosecution must plead and prove a purpose that the definition of the transaction itself does not require. Parliament thereby wrote into the criminal limb the mens rea whose absence from the original offence the Supreme Court's 2022 judgment treated as a constitutional vice, while leaving the civil limb, confiscation, free of any purpose requirement. Whether that asymmetry is a principled division, property proceedings correcting title and criminal proceedings punishing intent, or an unstable compromise that takes the most where proof is thinnest, is the analytical question the restored appeal leaves behind it, and it will outlast the timing dispute that has dominated the litigation so far.

In practice the visible enforcement of the Act has run overwhelmingly through the civil track, and the structure of the criminal track explains why. A prosecution requires the sanction contemplated by section 55 before it can be instituted, a complaint before a Special Court, proof beyond reasonable doubt of the section 53 purpose, and a trial that section 51 directs to be concluded within six months of the complaint, a direction that sits uneasily with the pace of sessions trials generally. The government's own periodic statements of the enforcement record are correspondingly framed in the vocabulary of the civil track, notices, provisional attachments, confirmations and confiscations, rather than of convictions. The consequence is a regime whose deterrent reputation rests on asset consequences imposed on the civil standard, with imprisonment held in reserve for the cases the department chooses to carry through the criminal courts, an allocation that the property holder experiences as severity without a criminal trial and that the constitutional critique of the Act treats as the heart of the matter.

The contrast between the two tracks is the statute's central tension in miniature. Confiscation requires the State to show that a property answers the definition of benami property, and no more; the honest cousin whose name was used without the evasive purpose of section 53 can still lose the property if the definition and no exception fit. Prosecution requires the evasive purpose and carries imprisonment, but only for the new era's transactions and only with prior sanction. A regime that takes property easily and imprisons with difficulty may be defensible as asset recovery, or indefensible as punishment by instalments, and the constitutional attack on the 2022 docket was framed exactly in those terms. Before the courts are reached, the scale of what the machinery did in its first years belongs in the record, with its sources named, because the figures were contested from the start and deserve to be carried as claims rather than as facts.

## The first enforcement wave, in the government's own numbers

The machinery did not have to hunt blind. The department organised dedicated benami prohibition units within its investigation establishment and fed them from the information systems the tax administration already ran: search and survey operations that surfaced deeds in third names, annual information returns and banking data that exposed deposits inconsistent with the account holder's profile, and, in the months after the currency withdrawal, the deposit records of the note exchange period itself, in which large cash deposits into the accounts of employees, relatives and shell entities stood out against those holders' known incomes. The notice under section 24 was therefore rarely the beginning of the department's knowledge; it was the point at which an information trail, often years in the building, was converted into a statutory proceeding, and the recorded reasons enclosed with the early notices characteristically recited the search findings, the deposit data or the statement of a name lender recorded in some other proceeding.

The figures that follow are the government's, given by the government, and are reproduced as attributed claims about the department's own activity. In a statement issued through the Press Information Bureau on 11 January 2018, the Income Tax Department reported that provisional attachments had been made in more than nine hundred cases involving properties valued at more than Rs 3,500 crore, of which immovable properties accounted for more than Rs 2,900 crore, and that the Adjudicating Authority had confirmed five attachments of properties valued at more than Rs 150 crore. The same statement illustrated the docket's variety: bank accounts of persons who deposited cash into the accounts of employees and associates during the withdrawal of high denomination notes, farmhouses and flats bought in the names of employees and drivers, and a fifty acre parcel acquired in the name of a company for beach tourism projects, attached where the shareholders were found to be name lenders with fictitious directors fronting for an undisclosed beneficial owner. In a written reply in the Rajya Sabha on 31 July 2018, the Minister of State for Finance, Shiv Pratap Shukla, placed provisional attachments at more than sixteen hundred cases involving property worth more than Rs 4,300 crore, with immovable property accounting for more than Rs 3,400 crore. Two cautions attach to these numbers and apply to every similar figure in the benami debate. A provisional attachment is an opening position, not a finding: it records that an officer had reason to believe, not that an adjudicator held the property benami after hearing, and the distance between the attachment figure and the confirmed figure in the January 2018 statement, five confirmations against more than nine hundred attachments, measures how provisional the wave was. And the docket's most consequential feature was temporal: a large share of the notices, including the one that would carry the statute to the Supreme Court, attacked transactions completed years before the machinery existed, on the department's theory that benami holding being a continuing state, property still held benami after 1 November 2016 was within reach whatever the date of purchase.

The composition of the wave also taught an early lesson about the Act's breadth that the bare figures conceal. The properties attached were not only the farms and flats of the classic benami imagination. Because the definition of property in section 2(26) runs to assets of every kind and follows value through conversion, the notices reached bank balances built up in the names of employees during the currency withdrawal, shareholdings in companies whose directors and shareholders were found to be name lenders, and deposits and investments representing the proceeds of property long since sold. The department was, in effect, using the benami statute as a general instrument against unexplained holding patterns across the financial system, a use the text supports and one that multiplied both the statute's deterrent signal and its litigation surface, since every extension beyond the paradigm case, land in a relative's name bought with the payer's taxed or untaxed money, generated its own challenges on definition, exception and proof. The five confirmations recorded in the January 2018 statement against more than nine hundred provisional attachments should be read in that light: not as failure, since adjudication on a one year timetable had barely begun, but as a measure of how much of the wave was position taking whose fate would be decided in the forums the Act had created.

## Ganpati Dealcom: the judgment of August 2022

The test case came from Kolkata. M/s Ganpati Dealcom Pvt. Ltd. had purchased immovable property in May 2011, in the era when the benami law consisted of the dormant 1988 text. In August 2017 the Initiating Officer issued notice under section 24(1) of the amended Act against the company in respect of that purchase. The Calcutta High Court quashed the notice, holding that the 2016 amendment could not be applied retrospectively to a transaction of 2011. The facts were, for a test case, unusually clean: a single completed purchase, five years before the machinery existed, attacked six years after completion by an officer whose office the purchase predated, with no suggestion that anything had happened in the intervening years except the continued holding of the property. Everything therefore turned on the legal characterisation of that continued holding and of the amendment itself, and nothing turned on disputed facts, which is why the appeal travelled to the Supreme Court as a pure question of law and why its framing, prospectivity or retrospectivity, was always going to organise the answer. The department's position required the Court to treat the amendment as procedural machinery attached to an old prohibition and the holding as a continuing state; the company's position required it to treat the amendment as a substantive code of offences and forfeitures and the purchase as a closed event. The 2022 bench adopted the company's characterisation almost in full. The Union of India carried the matter to the Supreme Court, and a three judge bench of Chief Justice N. V. Ramana, Justice Krishna Murari and Justice Hima Kohli decided Civil Appeal No. 5783 of 2022 on 23 August 2022, in a judgment reported at (2023) 3 SCC 315. The only question the bench framed for itself was whether the 2016 amendment Act was prospective or retrospective in operation, a framing that would matter enormously when the judgment was later recalled.

The conclusions, gathered at the end of the judgment, went well beyond an answer to that question. The Court held that section 3(2) of the unamended 1988 Act, the criminal provision as the Court referred to it, was unconstitutional for being manifestly arbitrary, and that the offence provision as it emerged after the 2016 amendment suffered the same infirmity when measured against Article 20(1) of the Constitution for transactions of the earlier era. It held that section 5 of the unamended Act, the in rem forfeiture provision, was unconstitutional as manifestly arbitrary, a power of taking that had stood in the statute book for thirty four years without the procedure that might have disciplined it. It held that the 2016 amendment was substantive rather than procedural or declaratory, creating new offences and a new confiscation regime, and that confiscation under the amended section 5, being a punitive consequence in substance, could apply only prospectively. It held consequently that the authorities could not initiate or continue prosecution or confiscation in respect of transactions entered into before the amended Act was brought into force, naming 25 October 2016 in the conclusions, the date of the notification that brought the amendment into force on 1 November 2016, and that all such prosecutions and confiscation proceedings in respect of earlier transactions stood quashed. It expressly left open the constitutionality of in rem forfeiture under the 2016 Act on grounds other than retrospectivity. The reasoning wove several strands together: the 1988 provisions as born infirm, vague in their criminal content, devoid of procedural safeguards and incapable of implementation, as the government's own concession established; the doctrine that a statute creating offences or imposing penalties is presumed prospective and that Article 20(1) forbids conviction under an ex post facto law; the crystallisation of rights and liabilities when a transaction closed under the regime of its time; and the character of confiscation, which the Court treated as forfeiture with a penal sting rather than as a neutral recovery of mislabelled title. For twenty six months those conclusions were the law applied by high courts and tribunals across the country, and proceedings founded on pre-amendment transactions were closed in reliance on them in large numbers.

The reasoning behind the conclusions deserves its own record, because the restored appeal will have to meet it if the questions are ever argued properly. On the criminal provision, the bench fastened on the character of the original section 3 offence: a prohibition of entering into any benami transaction, punishable with imprisonment, in a provision whose governing definition reached the most ordinary family purchase and which carried no requirement of guilty intention, so that a citizen could not tell from the text which side of the criminal line a household arrangement fell on, the vice the doctrine of manifest arbitrariness exists to catch. On section 5, the bench reasoned from the provision's own emptiness: a power to acquire property without compensation, whose authority, manner and procedure were never prescribed in thirty four years, was not a law of acquisition but an uncanalised threat of one, and its presence in the statute book could not ground proceedings under its successor. On retrospectivity, the bench treated the amendment's changes as creating new offences and new liabilities rather than declaring old ones, held that the rights and liabilities of parties to closed transactions had crystallised under the regime in force when they acted, and characterised confiscation, a forfeiture of property to the State following an official determination of wrongdoing, as punitive in substance, attracting the same presumption of prospectivity that governs penal law, fortified where punishment is concerned by Article 20(1) itself. The judgment also drew strength from the government's own concession that sections 3 and 5 of the 1988 Act were never implemented, treating the concession as an admission that the proceedings launched after 2016 rested on provisions that had lived only on paper. Each strand is contestable, and the Union's review petition contested the procedure rather than the strands, but any fresh bench will inherit this reasoning as the position to be displaced, which is why accounts that treat the recall as having restored the department's original confidence misread the litigation's posture.

Two details of the 2022 conclusions repay attention because practitioners built on them for twenty six months. The first is the date. The conclusions barred prosecution and confiscation for transactions entered into before 25 October 2016, the date of the notification by which the amendment was brought into force, although the amendment in fact commenced on 1 November 2016, so that the judgment's own protective line and the statute's commencement line stand a week apart, a discrepancy that mattered to any transaction executed in that interval and that a fresh adjudication will have to tidy. The second is the fate of the department's textual argument. The tribunal's working law before 2022 had rested on the disjunctive reading of section 2(9)(A), transferred to, or is held by, under which the holding of property by a benamidar after commencement sufficed whatever the date of transfer. The 2022 conclusions, by confining the machinery to transactions entered into after commencement, displaced that reading in substance without a clause by clause engagement with it, and the recall has left the reading in the same suspended state as everything else in the judgment's wake: available to the department as its best textual argument, rejected in effect by the only Supreme Court bench to consider the matter, and undecided as a matter of binding law.

## The recall: October 2024

The Union sought review, and on 18 October 2024 a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, Justice J. B. Pardiwala and Justice Manoj Misra, in Review Petition (C) No. 359 of 2023, reported at 2024 SCC OnLine SC 2981, recalled the 2022 judgment in its entirety and restored the civil appeal for fresh adjudication before a bench to be nominated by the Chief Justice. The ground was not that the conclusions were wrong on the merits; the recalling bench expressed no opinion on the constitutionality of any provision. The ground was anterior and structural: there had been no challenge to the constitutional validity of sections 3 and 5 of the unamended Act in the proceedings, the only question framed was the prospective or retrospective operation of the amendment, and the vires of a statutory provision could not be adjudicated in the absence of a lis, pleadings and contest on that question. A judgment that strikes down provisions nobody had attacked, in an appeal that asked only about timing, had in the review bench's assessment travelled outside the controversy the parties had placed before the Court. The recall therefore did something rarer and more disorienting than a reversal: it vacated the answers without endorsing the opposite answers. The unamended provisions are not valid because the 2022 judgment fell; they are unadjudicated. The confiscation machinery is not confirmed against older transactions; its reach is undecided. Paragraph 7 of the recall order added a practical wrinkle by granting liberty to parties aggrieved by proceedings disposed of in reliance on the 2022 judgment to seek review of those proceedings, and the fate of that liberty would be tested, and narrowed, within thirteen months.

The procedural shape of what follows from the recall is worth stating exactly, because it determines what the restored appeal can and cannot decide. The recall order restored the civil appeal to the position it occupied before August 2022 and recorded that the findings returned in the recalled judgment were not to influence the outcome of the appeal on its fresh adjudication. For the constitutional questions to be decided in that appeal, a party must raise them as a party: the Union defending the provisions, or the property holder attacking them, with pleadings that put vires in issue and a bench constituted to hear a constitutional challenge. If the restored appeal is argued, as the original was, only on the question of prospective or retrospective operation, the likely outcome is a judgment on timing that again leaves validity untouched, and the manifest arbitrariness reasoning of 2022 would remain what it has become, an influential and unadopted critique. The recall also left the status of the struck down provisions in a deliberately suspended state: sections 3 and 5 of the unamended Act stand in the statute book neither condemned nor absolved, and their successor provisions operate under a cloud that only the fresh adjudication can lift. That suspension, more than any single holding, is the legal weather in which every benami notice issued since October 2024 has been framed.

## After the recall: reopening refused at the top, contested below

The test arrived in Union of India v. Virendra Amrutbhai Patel, decided on 4 November 2025 by a bench of Justice B. V. Nagarathna and Justice Augustine George Masih. The sequence beneath that decision is the recall's whole problem in miniature. Benami proceedings against the respondents had been quashed by the Gujarat High Court in reliance on Ganpati Dealcom; the Union's challenge to that quashing was dismissed by the Supreme Court in April 2024, in the period when the 2022 judgment was the governing law; the judgment was then recalled in October 2024; and the Union sought review of its own April 2024 dismissal on the footing that the authority supporting it had ceased to exist, pointing to the liberty in paragraph 7 of the recall order as its invitation. The bench's refusal, resting on K. L. Rathi Steels, holds the line between two kinds of legal change: the law that decides a case is the law of its time, and a later reversal, even the extraordinary recall of the very judgment relied on, does not convert a concluded order into an erroneous one for the purposes of review. The bench declined, expressing its inability to agree with the liberty granted in paragraph 7 of the recall order, and following the three judge bench decision in Government of NCT of Delhi v. K. L. Rathi Steels Ltd., (2024) 7 SCC 315, which held, on the Explanation to Order XLVII Rule 1 of the Code of Civil Procedure, that a subsequent reversal or modification of the law declared by a superior court is not by itself a ground for review of an order concluded under the earlier law. The Union's review petition was dismissed. The position that emerges is layered and must be stated exactly. The restored Ganpati appeal awaits fresh adjudication, and until it is heard, the constitutionality of the unamended provisions and the temporal reach of confiscation remain open questions at the level of the Supreme Court. Orders of the Supreme Court itself that were closed in reliance on the 2022 judgment will not be reopened merely because that judgment was recalled. Below that level, the Appellate Tribunal has treated the recall as reviving benami proceedings that had been closed in reliance on the recalled judgment, reasoning that tribunal orders founded solely on a judgment that no longer exists cannot survive its recall, while other forums have applied the Rathi Steels logic against reopening; the restoration of concluded tribunal proceedings is accordingly contested ground, and a person whose case was closed between August 2022 and October 2024 cannot be told, without qualification, either that the closure is safe or that it is gone. The Gauhati High Court, in proceedings arising out of that restoration practice, has expressed doubt about the tribunal restoring appeals of its own motion and about the jurisdiction to do so, and the question of the tribunal's power remains pending, so that even the procedural aftermath of the recall is unsettled at two levels at once. The tribunal's own reasoning, in the restoration matters, proceeded on the principle that a judgment recalled is a judgment effaced, so that orders passed solely because that judgment bound the forum lost their foundation when it fell; the objection pressed against restoration is the mirror image of the Rathi Steels principle, that an order correct when passed is not falsified by later doctrine, extended by the objection that a tribunal which has disposed of an appeal has exhausted its authority over it. Between those two principles the working law at tribunal level is unsettled in the most literal sense, with outcomes depending on the forum seised of the restoration question, and practitioners advising on cases closed in the 2022 to 2024 window must audit the procedural history of the individual matter rather than rely on any general rule.

The doctrine the Supreme Court applied in Virendra Amrutbhai Patel deserves a precise statement, because it will govern any similar attempt to unsettle closed benami matters. The Explanation to Order XLVII Rule 1 of the Code of Civil Procedure provides that the fact that the decision on a question of law on which a judgment is based has been reversed or modified by a subsequent judgment of a superior court in another case is not a ground for review of that judgment. The three judge bench in K. L. Rathi Steels applied that Explanation to refuse reviews founded on a later change in the law, and the two judge bench in the benami matter followed it to hold that the recall of Ganpati Dealcom, being a later development in the law, did not entitle the Union to reopen an order that had been correctly passed under the law as it stood when it was passed. Finality, on this reasoning, is not a technicality layered over the merits; it is the rule that lets litigation end at all, and it protects the citizen who won under yesterday's law exactly as it protects the department when the movement of doctrine runs the other way.

### Has the Supreme Court settled whether the benami law reaches older transactions?

It has not. The 2022 judgment that answered the question, confining confiscation and prosecution to the era after the amendment, was recalled in full in October 2024 and is no longer good law. The appeal stands restored for fresh adjudication, and the constitutionality of the provisions and the temporal reach of the machinery are open questions awaiting that hearing.

## The case for the machinery, and the case against its reach

The stance this pillar is required to take begins by giving each side its full strength, because the benami question is a genuine contest between two constitutional goods: the State's claim against concealed wealth, and the citizen's claim that closed transactions are closed. The enforcement case, argued by the Union throughout, runs as follows. Benami holding is not an event completed at purchase like a sale; it is a state of concealment that continues for every day the property is held in the wrong name, and a machinery statute that reaches property held benami on the day it commences reaches a continuing state, not a past act. The prohibition itself dates from 1988, so no holder after 1988 can claim surprise that the arrangement was unlawful; only the remedy is new, and remedies are the classic matter of procedural law, which ordinarily governs pending and present situations alike. The Appellate Tribunal gave this argument a textual form by holding that the words transferred to, or is held by, in section 2(9)(A) are disjunctive, so that a property purchased before 2016 but held by the benamidar after the commencement date answers the definition on its holding limb. Deterrence completes the case: a machinery confined to fresh transactions would leave the entire accumulated stock of benami property, the very evil the statute exists against, permanently immunised by its own age.

The case against the reach, which the 2022 bench substantially accepted, answers each point. Confiscation without compensation is punishment in substance whatever its civil label, and Article 20(1) and the presumption against retrospectivity protect completed transactions from new punitive consequences; the amendment's own section 3(3) confines prosecution to transactions entered into after commencement, which is Parliament conceding for the criminal limb exactly the prospectivity the department denied for confiscation. Rights and defences crystallised under the law as it stood: a purchase of 2011 was made against a statute whose only working limb was a civil bar in private litigation, and the parties ordered their affairs, their limitation periods and their records on that footing. The unamended offence and acquisition provisions were, on the government's own concession, unimplemented paper, and provisions never operationalised for thirty four years are a frail foundation for retroactive severity. The tribunal's holding limb reading, finally, proves too much: on that logic no benami transaction, however ancient, ever escapes, because property held is always being held, and a construction that makes the temporal question disappear has answered it by assumption. The honest summary, which the recall compels, is that the Supreme Court has held both that these questions answer themselves against retrospectivity and that they were never properly before it; the law awaits a bench that will decide them on pleadings that squarely raise them, and until that hearing the department proceeds against older transactions at the risk that the eventual answer is the one given in 2022.

There is, in fairness, a middle position available to the restored bench, and the structure of the Act itself suggests it. Parliament separated the consequences: prosecution it confined by text to the new era, confiscation it left textually unbounded, and adjudication it built as a present inquiry into a present holding. A bench could hold that the confiscation machinery may examine a holding as it stands, but that confiscation as a consequence must rest on the law of the transaction's own time in one respect, the exceptions and defences available when the arrangement was made, while applying the new procedure to prove them; or it could distinguish between property whose benami character was complete and spent at purchase and arrangements continued, renewed or exploited after commencement through rent collection, further transfers or conversion of proceeds, treating the post commencement acts as the statutory trigger. No party's headline argument owns this middle ground, and benches deciding a framed question tend toward the poles the framing offers, but the existence of the middle is worth recording because the litigation's next decade may be spent in it: a jurisprudence that protects the closed family purchase of 2005 while pursuing the arrangement actively worked in 2018 would answer most of the fairness objection and most of the enforcement case at once, at the cost of a line that only case by case adjudication could draw.

## Benami is not money laundering, and not only a tax problem

The benami machinery shares furniture with the money laundering regime, the same Adjudicating Authority in the transitional design and the same Appellate Tribunal, and the two are routinely confused, so the distinction belongs in a pillar of this kind. The Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002, examined in this series at [the PMLA 2002 complete guide](/2013/03/01/pmla-2002-complete-guide/), attaches property that represents the proceeds of a scheduled crime: its trigger is criminal origin, its machinery follows a predicate offence, and its provisional attachment and confiscation are keyed to the laundering of tainted value. The benami Act requires no predicate offence at all. Its trigger is a structure, name separated from money separated from benefit, and it reaches property bought with fully lawful and taxed funds where the holding pattern fits the definition and no exception rescues it, just as it fails to reach criminal wealth held in the criminal's own name. The two statutes therefore cross without coinciding: proceeds of crime parked in an employee's name invite both regimes, and a family's documented purchase in a spouse's name invites neither. The procedural temperaments of the two regimes also differ in ways that matter to the person facing both. Attachment under the money laundering statute is provisional for a statutory period and is tested before the same Adjudicating Authority on the question whether the property is involved in money laundering, with confiscation tied to the conclusion of the criminal trial; attachment under the benami Act is provisional until the Adjudicating Authority decides the reference, and confiscation follows the adjudication itself, subject to appeal, without waiting for any trial. A property can therefore be lost under the benami Act on a timetable that the money laundering regime would not permit for the same asset, which is one reason the benami notice is frequently the more urgent document on a family's table even where a money laundering allegation sounds graver. A third regime touches the same facts from a different angle, since property whose cost cannot be explained may be assessed as unexplained investment or unexplained money under the [Income Tax Act 1961 complete guide](/2012/07/01/income-tax-act-1961-complete-guide/), with the tax consequence falling on the payer even where benami proceedings are never opened. A notice under any one of the three is a reason to examine exposure under all of them, and a defence built for one, the known sources documentation that answers the benami exception question, frequently supplies the answer the others are looking for.

## Benami in the share register: the companies parallel

One field in which the benami pattern migrated deserves separate notice, because Parliament has attacked it twice from different directions. The classic benami asset was land, but the 50 acre company acquisition in the department's own first statement, in which the shareholders were name lenders and the directors fictitious, illustrates the corporate form of the pattern: shares held by persons of no means, funded by an undisclosed principal, giving the principal control of a company and of whatever the company owns without his name appearing in any register. The benami Act reaches that pattern directly, since shares are property within section 2(26) and a shareholding funded by another for that other's benefit answers clause (A) of the definition. The Companies Act, 2013 reaches it from the register side: sections 89 and 90 require the significant beneficial owner of shares, the individual who ultimately owns or controls a prescribed threshold of the holding, to declare that interest, maintain a register of such owners and file declarations with the Registrar, so that concealment in the share register is itself a compliance violation independent of any benami proceeding. The two regimes illustrate the difference between a prohibition with machinery and a disclosure system: the companies framework assumes the holding and compels its attribution, while the benami framework proves the attribution and then confiscates the holding. Investigators work them together, the register supplying the map of who claims what, the benami notice testing whether the map is true.

## What the law means for ordinary family arrangements

For the large constituency that holds property across family names for reasons of affection, convenience or estate planning rather than concealment, the amended Act converts informal habits into evidentiary questions, and the practical discipline it rewards is documentation assembled before it is needed. The purchase deed, the bank trail showing the consideration moving from the payer's account, the payer's income records placing that consideration within known sources, the family's own record of why the property was held as it was, and, for siblings and ascendants and descendants, the presence of the payer's name as joint owner in the document, together answer almost every question a notice can ask; kept together and kept intact, ideally in a single private file of the kind described in [VaultBook's account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), they turn a section 24 notice from an existential threat into a correspondence that closes. The danger cases cluster where the record cannot be rebuilt: cash contributions within families, contributions from relatives whose incomes were never documented, property bought in a mother's or a brother's sole name on family money passing through several hands. In each, the arrangement may be innocent in fact and still sit outside the exceptions as drafted, because the exceptions reward traceable money between specified relations and nothing else. This pillar offers legal information about a statute in flux, not legal advice on any person's holding, and a family facing a notice or contemplating a rearrangement of titles needs counsel on its own documents rather than on general propositions, not least because the central temporal question examined above remains undecided and the value of regularising a holding depends in part on an answer no court has finally given.

Three recurring arrangements deserve specific mention because they generate a disproportionate share of anxiety under the Act. The first is the purchase in a woman's name encouraged by fiscal policy itself: several States charge lower stamp duty on property registered in a woman's name, and families have for years bought the family home in the name of a wife, mother or daughter for that saving and for security, arrangements that sit comfortably inside the spouse exception where the consideration is the individual's documented income, and that become exposed only where the money trail leaves the family's accounted resources. The second is the parent funded purchase for a child and the child funded purchase for a parent, both common in a housing market where first homes are family projects: the spouse and child exception covers the downward direction where the payer is the individual and the holder a child, while the upward direction depends on the fourth exception and its joint ownership condition, so that a son who buys a flat for his mother in her sole name, however transparent the funding, has arranged his family outside the exception's text and must rely on displacing the definition's benefit limb instead. The third is money arriving from relatives abroad, where the funding trail crosses the foreign exchange framework and the documentation standard rises accordingly: the exception asks for known sources, and a remittance that cannot be documented through banking channels fails the test for reasons that have nothing to do with the family's honesty. In each case the statute's real demand is the same, that family property arrangements be arranged on paper as carefully as they are arranged in fact, a demand that is easy to state and was, for the decades in which the prohibition slept, almost never enforced against anyone.

## Confiscation without compensation: the Article 300A question underneath

Beneath the timing dispute sits a constitutional question that the litigation has circled without yet deciding on proper pleadings: whether the State may take property under this Act, finally and without compensation, on an administrative determination that its holding pattern was benami. Property ceased to be a fundamental right with the Forty fourth Amendment, and Article 300A provides instead that no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law. The Supreme Court's reading of that article, in decisions such as K. T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka in 2011, treats it as a real constraint rather than a formality: the law authorising deprivation must serve a public purpose, must not be arbitrary or unfair, and the principles it lays down for compensation, where compensation is part of the scheme, must not be illusory. Confiscation under section 27 tests each element. The public purpose is easily stated, in the deterrence of concealed ownership and the recovery of property held against the revenue and creditors. The compensation element is answered by the classification at the statute's heart: the property, the argument runs, was held in a transaction the law prohibited, the real owner's claim to it is the claim section 4 refuses to hear, and a person cannot demand the price of losing what the law says was never legitimately his to hold in that form. The arbitrariness element is where the pressure concentrates, because the taking follows not a conviction but an adjudication on the civil standard, because the definition reaches arrangements entered into for mixed family reasons, and because the severity of losing an entire property, purchased perhaps with money only partly unexplained, is fixed by the statute without gradation.

The opposing considerations are not trivial and a balanced pillar should state them at strength. Forfeiture of the instruments and proceeds of wrongdoing without compensation is a familiar and accepted feature of Indian law, in customs, in narcotics legislation and in the forfeiture chapter of the money laundering regime's parent statutes, and benami confiscation can be read as belonging to that family rather than to the family of land acquisition, where compensation is the price of taking admittedly legitimate property for public use. The adjudication is not unreviewable: it follows notice, evidence and hearing, with two appeals, one on facts and law to a tribunal and one on substantial questions of law to a High Court. And the definition's exceptions, the bona fide purchaser protection and the section 53 purpose requirement on the criminal side together mark a legislature attempting calibration rather than confiscation at large. The 2022 bench, examining the unamended forfeiture provision, held the arbitrariness objection good; the recall withdrew that examination without disturbing the force of the question. When the restored appeal is heard on proper pleadings, Article 300A and the manifest arbitrariness doctrine will be the ground on which the confiscation power is finally tested, and the outcome will matter beyond benami, to every Indian statute that takes property by adjudication rather than by acquisition.

## The honest assessment

One durable effect of the amended Act is visible outside the courtrooms altogether, in conveyancing behaviour. Since 2016, advisers structuring family purchases have had to draft for the exceptions: names added as joint owners where the sibling and lineal exception requires them, gifts of money for purchases documented as gifts with bank transfers rather than passed in cash, a child's home bought with a parent's declared funds and the paper kept to prove it. The exceptions operate, in other words, as a code of conduct for family property, rewarding the documented arrangement in advance and penalising only at the adjudication those who cannot reconstruct one. That is a species of success prohibition statutes rarely achieve, because it operates without a single prosecution: the statute changed the default paperwork of the Indian family purchase. Set against it stands the figure the Act has never convincingly answered, the arrangement made in the decades of dormancy on the paperwork habits of those decades, whose holders face a documentary standard that their transactions predate and cannot retrospectively satisfy, and whose treatment is the moral core of the restored appeal.

What works in the amended Act should be recorded plainly. The definition is a genuine improvement on 1988: four articulated limbs, proceeds followed through conversions, exceptions stated as conditions, and a fiduciary carve out that lets trusts, partnerships and companies function without every directorship becoming a benami allegation. The machinery is real on paper and was real in operation within weeks of commencement, with named authorities, a prior approval check on provisional attachment, a reasoned adjudication on a statutory timetable and a two level appeal. The offence is drafted with a mens rea that the 1988 text lacked, requiring an evasive purpose and prior sanction, so that prosecution is hard to launch casually even where confiscation is easy. And the statute forced a national conversation about the most ordinary concealment in Indian property holding, which the figures of the first wave, whatever their provisional character, show was not a marginal practice.

What does not work is equally plain. A confiscation power without compensation, exercisable on the civil standard through an appointed authority, sits at the edge of what property rights under Article 300A are usually taken to permit, and its legitimacy was impugned by the one Supreme Court bench that examined the old provisions, in a judgment later withdrawn for procedural reasons rather than answered. The temporal reach of the regime, the question on which thousands of notices depend, has been litigated for the better part of a decade and stands exactly where it stood in 2016, awaiting a hearing, while proceedings have been opened, closed, reopened in forums below and refused reopening at the top. The borrowed adjudicating and appellate machinery carries other statutes' dockets alongside this one. And the exceptions, for all their precision, protect a paperwork ideal of the Indian family, salaried, documented, jointly titled, that diverges from the cash threaded arrangements by which much family property actually moved in the decades the prohibition slept. The verdict of this pillar is the claim with which its analysis began. The 1988 Act failed because a prohibition is not a law until someone can enforce it. The 2016 amendment built the someone, the procedure and the forum, and thereby created a real law, whose unfinished business is no longer machinery but legitimacy: a final word, from a bench squarely seized of the questions, on how far back a real law may reach.

Until that word comes, the practical position can be summarised in three open fronts, and the summary is offered as the accurate state of the subject rather than as a hedge. On validity, the criminal and forfeiture provisions of the unamended Act and the confiscation design of the amended Act stand unadjudicated, condemned in a recalled judgment and defended in a review that won on procedure. On temporal reach, the department's continuing holding theory and the property holders' crystallised rights theory both remain available to the bench that hears the restored appeal, and notices against older transactions are issued and defended in that uncertainty. On concluded proceedings, the recall has revived what forums below are willing to revive and has left untouched what the Supreme Court closed with its own hand, a division that will produce its own appellate traffic. A statute whose prohibition is thirty eight years old, whose machinery is a decade old and whose central questions are unanswered is not a curiosity; it is a fair specimen of how Indian economic legislation matures, in long dormancy, sudden activation and slow constitutional settlement, and the benami Act's remaining chapters will be written in that order.

Read across the rows, the table states the pillar's whole argument in its compressed form. The substantive prohibition barely changed between the first row and the second; what changed was the presence of machinery, and with it the practical meaning of the same words. The third row shows a court attempting to confine that machinery to its own era, and the fourth shows the confinement withdrawn without the machinery being validated, leaving the second row's enforcement running under the first row's unresolved questions. A benami holding is therefore best understood not by asking what the law says, which has been tolerably clear since 1988, but by asking which of the four states governs the transaction in question, a question whose answer, for the oldest and most numerous holdings, the restored appeal has yet to supply.

A word should be added about the sources on which an account of this statute must rest, because the benami literature is unusually contaminated by the litigation it describes. Commentary written between November 2016 and August 2022 commonly assumed the machinery's reach over older transactions, because the department was exercising it; commentary written between August 2022 and October 2024 commonly stated the 2022 holdings as the law, because they were; and material written since the recall sometimes treats the recall as a ruling for the department, which it is not. The only stable ground is the text of the Act as amended, the text of the two Supreme Court orders with their procedural postures kept distinct, and the government's figures carried as the government's claims. This pillar has been written on that ground throughout, and readers checking its propositions against the statute book and the two reported orders will find the disagreements located where this pillar has located them: not in what the documents say, but in what follows from them.

## How to read this Act

A reader approaching the statute for the first time, whether as a student, an adviser or a holder of family property, will get more from it by reading against the grain of its chapter order. Start with the exceptions inside section 2(9), because they define the lawful space and most real arrangements are decided there. Read section 3(3) next, for the boundary of the criminal law, and then Chapter IV as a single procedural narrative from notice to confiscation, noting where the burden of explanation moves. Read the two Supreme Court episodes last and in the right order: the conclusions of the 2022 judgment for the strongest statement of the case against the machinery's reach, the recall order of 2024 for why that statement is not law, and the Virendra Amrutbhai Patel order of 2025 for the limits of reopening what the recalled judgment closed. Hold throughout the distinction this pillar has pressed: between the prohibition, which is old and was for decades unenforced, the machinery, which is a decade old and entirely real, and the legitimacy questions, which are the unfinished business that the restored appeal exists to settle. The Act's short title, with its retained year of 1988, will try to persuade the reader that the regime is older and more settled than it is. The reader who resists that persuasion has understood the statute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a benami transaction?

A benami transaction is an arrangement in which property is transferred to, or held by, one person while the consideration for it is paid or provided by another, and the property is held for the immediate or future benefit, direct or indirect, of the person who paid. Section 2(9) of the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, 1988 extends the definition to three further cases: a transaction carried out in a fictitious name, a transaction whose owner is unaware of or denies the ownership, and a transaction whose payer is untraceable or fictitious. The person in whose name the property stands is the benamidar, and the person who provided the consideration and takes the benefit is the beneficial owner. Specified family and fiduciary holdings paid for out of known sources of income are excluded from the definition altogether.

### Q: Why was the benami law of 1988 unenforceable for decades?

Because the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988 prohibited the transaction without creating anyone able to act against it. Section 5 made benami property liable to acquisition by such authority, in such manner and after following such procedure as may be prescribed, and the rules that would have prescribed the authority, the manner and the procedure were never made at any point in the Act's first twenty eight years. Section 3 created an offence, but the Act named no investigating agency, and the offence was non-cognizable and bailable. Only section 4, the bar on the real owner's suits and defences, operated in practice, because it worked through courts that already existed. The Government conceded before the Supreme Court in the Ganpati Dealcom proceedings that sections 3 and 5 remained only on paper and were never implemented on the ground.

### Q: What machinery did the 2016 benami amendment create?

The Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Act, 2016 expanded the 1988 Act from nine sections to seventy two and built a complete enforcement structure. It created four authorities under section 18: the Initiating Officer, an Assistant or Deputy Commissioner of Income-tax who issues the notice under section 24; the Approving Authority, an Additional or Joint Commissioner whose prior approval is required for provisional attachment; the Adjudicating Authority, which hears the case and decides whether the property is benami; and the Administrator, who manages confiscated property. It added a procedure of notice, provisional attachment, adjudication under section 26 and confiscation under section 27, appeals to the Appellate Tribunal and the High Court, Special Courts for trial, offences in Chapter VII, and, through section 71, the transitional use of the money laundering Adjudicating Authority and the forfeiture Appellate Tribunal until benami forums were constituted.

### Q: Can benami property be confiscated?

Yes. Under section 27 of the amended Act, where the Adjudicating Authority holds a property to be benami property after adjudication under section 26, it may, after giving the persons concerned an opportunity of being heard and subject to the order of the Appellate Tribunal on appeal, pass an order confiscating the property. On confiscation, all rights and title in the property vest in the Central Government free from all encumbrances, and section 27(3) provides that no compensation shall be payable in respect of the confiscation. The Act protects a person who acquired an interest in the property in good faith, for adequate consideration and without knowledge of its benami character before the section 24 notice issued. Whether confiscation can reach transactions completed before the amendment commenced in November 2016 is a separate question, and it remains undecided after the recall of the Ganpati Dealcom judgment.

### Q: Does the benami law reach transactions before 2016?

That question is open. The Income Tax Department has proceeded on the theory that benami holding is a continuing state and that the words transferred to, or is held by, in section 2(9)(A) reach property still held by a benamidar after 1 November 2016 whenever it was bought, a reading the Appellate Tribunal has accepted. In August 2022 the Supreme Court held in Ganpati Dealcom that the confiscation machinery operates only prospectively and that proceedings for earlier transactions stood quashed. In October 2024 the Court recalled that judgment in full and restored the appeal for fresh adjudication, so the 2022 answer no longer holds the field and no final answer has replaced it. Prosecution stands differently: section 3(3) confines punishment under the offence provisions to transactions entered into on or after the commencement of the amendment.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in Ganpati Dealcom?

In Union of India v. M/s Ganpati Dealcom Pvt. Ltd., decided on 23 August 2022 by a bench of Chief Justice N. V. Ramana, Justice Krishna Murari and Justice Hima Kohli and reported at (2023) 3 SCC 315, the Supreme Court held that section 3(2) of the unamended 1988 Act was unconstitutional for manifest arbitrariness, that the offence provision after amendment suffered the same infirmity against Article 20(1) for earlier transactions, that section 5 of the unamended Act was unconstitutional as manifestly arbitrary, that the 2016 amendment was substantive and not merely procedural, and that confiscation under the amended Act applies prospectively only, so that prosecution and confiscation for transactions entered into before the amendment was brought into force could not be initiated or continued and stood quashed. The judgment was recalled in its entirety in October 2024.

### Q: Why was the Ganpati Dealcom judgment recalled?

Because the constitutional questions it answered had never been placed before the Court. In Review Petition (C) No. 359 of 2023, a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, Justice J. B. Pardiwala and Justice Manoj Misra held on 18 October 2024 that there had been no challenge to the constitutional validity of the unamended provisions in the proceedings, that the only question framed in the appeal was whether the 2016 amendment operated prospectively or retrospectively, and that the vires of a statutory provision cannot be adjudicated in the absence of a lis, pleadings and contest between the parties on that question. The bench recalled the 2022 judgment in its entirety, restored Civil Appeal No. 5783 of 2022 for fresh adjudication before a bench to be nominated by the Chief Justice, and expressed no opinion on the constitutionality of the provisions.

### Q: Which transactions are exempt from the benami definition?

Four categories within clause (A) of section 2(9) are excluded. Property held by a karta or a member of a Hindu undivided family for the benefit of the family, paid for out of the family's known sources of income, is excluded. Property held by a person in a fiduciary capacity, such as a trustee, executor, partner, director of a company or a depository or participant under the Depositories Act, 1996, for the benefit of the person towards whom that capacity is held, is excluded. Property held by an individual in the name of a spouse or any child, paid for out of the individual's known sources of income, is excluded. Property held by an individual in the name of a brother, sister or lineal ascendant or descendant is excluded where both names appear as joint owners in a document and the consideration came from known sources. An Explanation also excludes possession taken in part performance of a registered contract under section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 where stamp duty has been paid.

### Q: What is the difference between a benamidar and a beneficial owner?

The benamidar, defined in section 2(10), is the person, real or fictitious, in whose name benami property is held or to whom it is transferred: the name on the deed, the account or the share certificate. The beneficial owner, defined in section 2(12), is the person for whose benefit the property is held by the benamidar, whether or not his identity is known. The two roles carry different exposure. Proceedings are opened in the benamidar's name because the property stands in it, and attachment fastens on the property he holds, yet the inquiry is aimed through him at the beneficial owner, who provided the consideration, takes the benefit and is the principal accused under the offence provision in section 53. A benamidar may be a willing accomplice, a paid name lender, a dependent relative, or, where the definition's third limb applies, a person who does not know the property exists.

### Q: What do the words known sources of income mean in the benami exceptions?

The phrase is the hinge on which three of the four exceptions in section 2(9) turn, and it directs attention from the relationship between the parties to the traceability of the money. Consideration is paid out of known sources where the payer can account for it from income and resources that are disclosed and documented, through bank records, income tax records and the family's accounted funds, rather than from cash or receipts that cannot be placed. The exceptions therefore protect a documented arrangement and not a relationship as such: a purchase in a spouse's name funded from the individual's taxed salary and banked savings qualifies, while a purchase in the same name funded from unexplained cash does not, and in adjudication the practical burden falls on the holder to produce the paper trail that places the consideration within known sources.

### Q: Is property bought in a wife's name always a benami transaction?

No. Property held by an individual in the name of a spouse is expressly excluded from the definition in section 2(9) where the consideration has been paid out of the known sources of income of that individual, and purchases funded from a husband's or wife's taxed and documented income are the ordinary case the exception was written for. The position under the original 1988 Act was framed differently, as a presumption that a purchase in the name of a wife or unmarried daughter was made for her benefit unless the contrary was proved. Exposure arises where the source condition fails: where the consideration cannot be shown to come from known sources, where the spouse's name fronts for a third person's money, or where the arrangement was designed to defeat a law, a statutory due or a creditor, in which case a notice under section 24 can issue and the Adjudicating Authority decides on the evidence.

### Q: Who is the Initiating Officer under the benami law?

The Initiating Officer is one of the four authorities named in section 18 of the amended Act and is an Assistant Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner of Income-tax. He is the officer who opens a benami case: where, on the basis of material in his possession, he has reason to believe that a person is a benamidar in respect of a property, he records his reasons in writing and issues the show cause notice under section 24(1), sending a copy to the beneficial owner where that owner is known. With the previous approval of the Approving Authority he may provisionally attach the property where alienation is feared, and where he orders attachment or its continuation he draws up a statement of the case and refers it to the Adjudicating Authority. His powers are investigative and interim; the final determination that property is benami, and confiscation, belong to the Adjudicating Authority.

### Q: What happens after a notice is issued under Section 24 of the benami Act?

The notice calls on the benamidar to show cause why the property should not be treated as benami property, and the noticee replies with the documents and explanation on which he relies. If the Initiating Officer fears the property may be alienated, he may attach it provisionally with the Approving Authority's approval; as enacted in 2016 he then had ninety days from the notice to decide whether the attachment should continue, and fifteen days from that order to refer the case to the Adjudicating Authority, intervals the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 recast from 1 October 2024 into periods counted by months. The Adjudicating Authority issues notices to the benamidar, the beneficial owner and interested persons, hears the parties and passes an order under section 26 confirming or revoking the attachment, within the outer limit of one year from the end of the month of the reference. Confiscation under section 27 follows a confirming order, subject to appeal.

### Q: Why must the Initiating Officer take approval before attaching a property?

Because provisional attachment is a freezing of property on an officer's reason to believe, before any adjudication, and section 24(3) builds a hierarchical check into that step: the Initiating Officer may attach provisionally only with the previous approval of the Approving Authority, an Additional Commissioner or a Joint Commissioner of Income-tax senior to him. The approval requirement serves two purposes. It forces a second, senior reading of the material and the recorded reasons before a person's dealings with property are frozen, and it creates a documented decision that the benamidar can test in the adjudication and in appeal, since an attachment ordered without the required approval, or on material that discloses no benami character, is vulnerable to being set aside. The check is internal to the department rather than judicial, which is why the quality of the reasons recorded at the notice stage has featured so prominently in challenges to attachment.

### Q: What is the punishment for entering into a benami transaction?

Section 53 makes it an offence to enter into a benami transaction in order to defeat the provisions of any law, or to avoid payment of statutory dues, or to avoid payment to creditors. The beneficial owner, the benamidar and any person who abets or induces the transaction are guilty of the offence, which is punishable with rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than one year and extending to seven years, and with fine which may extend to twenty five per cent of the fair market value of the property. Two limits matter. Prosecution requires the previous sanction contemplated by section 55, and the offences are non-cognizable under section 61. And by section 3(3), punishment under the offence provisions attaches to transactions entered into on or after the commencement of the 2016 amendment, so the criminal limb does not reach back to transactions of the earlier period even where confiscation is argued for.

### Q: What is the penalty for giving false information under the benami Act?

Section 54 punishes a person who is required to furnish information under the Act and who provides or furnishes false information or a false document to any authority or officer, in any proceeding, inquiry or the like, knowing it to be false. The punishment is rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than six months and extending to five years, together with fine which may extend to ten per cent of the fair market value of the property in respect of which the false information or document was furnished. The offence is aimed at the paper defences on which benami arrangements depend: fabricated source documents, invented loan records and false explanations of consideration. As with the principal offence in section 53, a prosecution under section 54 cannot be instituted without the previous sanction required by section 55, and the offence is non-cognizable under section 61.

### Q: Where does an appeal lie against an order of the Adjudicating Authority?

An appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal under section 46, within forty five days from the date of the order of the Adjudicating Authority, by any person aggrieved, including the benamidar, the beneficial owner and the Initiating Officer where the order goes against the department. A confiscation order under section 27 is itself expressed to be subject to the order of the Appellate Tribunal on the appeal, so confiscation does not settle until the tribunal has spoken or the appeal period has run. From the Appellate Tribunal a further appeal lies to the High Court under section 49, within sixty days, on a substantial question of law. In the transitional design of section 71, the Appellate Tribunal for the purposes of the benami Act is the tribunal constituted under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act, 1976, which also hears appeals under the money laundering statute.

### Q: How do benami proceedings differ from money laundering proceedings under the PMLA?

The two regimes attach property for different reasons. Proceedings under the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002 are anchored to a predicate offence: the property attached must represent the proceeds of a scheduled crime, and the machinery follows the criminal origin of the value. Benami proceedings require no crime in the background at all. Their trigger is a holding structure in which the name on the property, the source of the consideration and the destination of the benefit diverge, and they can reach property bought with lawful and taxed funds where no exception in section 2(9) applies, just as criminal wealth held in the criminal's own name lies outside them. The forums overlap, since the money laundering Adjudicating Authority and the forfeiture Appellate Tribunal were designated for benami work by section 71, and the same property can face both regimes where criminal proceeds are parked in another's name, but the questions asked in each are distinct.

### Q: Can a benamidar transfer the property back to the beneficial owner?

No, and a transfer made in defiance of the bar is ineffective. Section 6 of the amended Act provides that the benamidar shall not re-transfer the property to the beneficial owner or to any other person acting on his behalf, and that where a property is re-transferred in contravention of that prohibition, the transfer is null and void. The bar closes the obvious escape route by which a benami arrangement under pressure would be unwound into the payer's own name once a department inquiry begins. It sits alongside two related rules: section 4 bars the beneficial owner from suing to recover the property or raising the benami arrangement as a claim or defence, and section 57 provides that a transfer of property after the issue of a notice under section 24 is null and void for the purposes of the Act. A regularisation of title therefore cannot be achieved by a private deed between benamidar and beneficial owner.

### Q: Did the bar on benami suits apply to cases already pending when the 1988 Act commenced?

Yes. In Mithilesh Kumari v. Prem Behari Khare, reported at AIR 1989 SC 1247, the Supreme Court held that section 4 of the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988 applied to claims and defences in proceedings pending when the Act commenced, including at the appellate stage, so that a party could not continue to press a benami claim or defence that the new statute barred. The reasoning treated the bar as governing rights in litigation rather than creating a fresh substantive liability for a completed transaction, and it made section 4 the one limb of the 1988 Act that bit immediately across the court system. The holding carries two statutory exceptions in section 4(3), for property held by a coparcener of a Hindu undivided family for the benefit of the family and for property held by a person in a fiduciary capacity, to which the bar does not extend.
