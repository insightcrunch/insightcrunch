The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, enacted as Public Law 107-155 and printed at 116 Stat. 81, reached the statute books through H.R. 2356 of the 107th Congress, the House vehicle known by the names of its principal sponsors, Representatives Christopher Shays of Connecticut, a Republican, and Martin Meehan of Massachusetts, a Democrat. Its Senate companion, S. 27, carried the names of Senators John McCain of Arizona, a Republican, and Russ Feingold of Wisconsin, a Democrat, a pairing that gave the entire enterprise its more familiar shorthand, McCain-Feingold, in the Senate and Shays-Meehan in the House. President George W. Bush signed the measure on March 27, 2002, completing a legislative sequence that had consumed the better part of a decade and had survived repeated failure in one chamber or the other before a procedural maneuver in the House and a direct Senate vote on the House text finally placed an identical bill on a president's desk for signature.

The statute rested on two central provisions that together defined its purpose and its controversy. The first barred national party committees from raising and spending soft money, the funds raised outside the limits and source prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, and it extended parallel restrictions to federal candidates and officeholders and to the financing of federal election activity by state and local parties. The second regulated electioneering communications, broadcast advertising that referred to a clearly identified federal candidate within defined periods before a primary or a general election, and barred corporations and labor organizations from paying for such communications from general treasury funds. Those two provisions emerged from a seven year effort that began in 1995, when McCain and Feingold issued a joint call for reform, and that proceeded through committee investigations, failed cloture votes, two House passages that died in the Senate, a presidential campaign that carried the issue to a national audience, and a corporate scandal in the final months that contemporaneous reporting credited with pushing the bill forward.

![The McCain-Feingold Act of 2002, the statute that banned national party soft money and regulated pre-election broadcast advertising - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-71.webp)

A profile of this statute must therefore carry three intertwined accounts rather than a single story of enactment. It must explain the two titles in their statutory detail, the soft money prohibition and the electioneering communication regime that Congress constructed in response to the financing practices of the 1996 and 2000 election cycles. It must reconstruct the procedure that forced enactment after ordinary committee and leadership channels had produced an unfavorable report and a rival bill, a discharge petition that assembled a majority of the House against the preferences of its leadership. And it must trace the judicial arc that followed, in which a closely divided Supreme Court sustained the central provisions in *McConnell v. FEC* in 2003 and later decisions narrowed or invalidated parts of the structure Congress had built, leaving the soft money ban in force on its enacted text while the advertising restrictions underwent substantial revision in subsequent litigation.

## A Bill That Took Seven Years to Reach a President

### Why did the bill stall in the Senate for so long?

The bill stalled because Senate opponents used the filibuster to require sixty votes for cloture, a threshold reform supporters never reached in the nineteen nineties, even after House passage, while disagreements over soft money and issue advertising kept a durable blocking coalition intact across several Congresses and election cycles in both parties.

The effort began in 1995 with a joint call for reform by McCain and Feingold, published as an op-ed that announced a shared project between a Republican senator from Arizona and a Democratic senator from Wisconsin. That bipartisan sponsorship mattered from the outset because campaign finance reform divided both parties internally, separating members who regarded large unregulated contributions as a corrupting influence from members who regarded restrictions on political spending as a burden on speech and on the practical work of party organization. A bill carried by one party alone could be dismissed by its opponents as a partisan device designed to disadvantage the other side in the next election. A bill carried jointly by members of both parties was harder to characterize in that manner, although joint sponsorship alone supplied no votes beyond the two names attached to it, and the arithmetic of the Senate determined the fate of the proposal from the first Congress in which it appeared to the last.

The 1996 election cycle supplied the catalyst that transformed a stated reform program into a legislative campaign with an evidentiary record. During that cycle the national parties raised and spent soft money on broadcast issue advertising at an unprecedented scale, demonstrating to legislators, journalists, and investigators that the distinction between regulated campaign funds and unregulated party funds had become a central feature of federal elections rather than a peripheral accounting practice. Money raised outside the limits and prohibitions of federal law paid for advertising that voters encountered as campaign communication, even when the advertisements avoided the words that triggered the legal definition of express advocacy. For supporters of the McCain and Feingold proposal, the 1996 cycle proved that the existing framework permitted the parties to operate two systems at once, one subject to contribution limits, disclosure, and source restrictions, and another largely outside those constraints. For opponents, the same cycle illustrated the demand for political funds in a system of expensive broadcast campaigns and the risk that new restrictions would redirect money toward groups even less accountable than the parties.

The Senate Governmental Affairs Committee, under its chairman, Senator Fred Thompson, gave the 1996 experience a formal investigation in hearings held in 1997 on the fundraising practices of that cycle. Those hearings placed the mechanics of soft money solicitation and spending into a congressional record, with testimony and documents that reform supporters could cite in later debate as findings rather than as allegations. The investigation also demonstrated the limits of exposure as a legislative strategy. A committee could establish that national parties had solicited large contributions and had directed the proceeds toward advertising and party activity with federal consequences, and members could deplore particular episodes in strong language, without producing the sixty Senate votes required to close debate on a reform bill. The hearings sharpened the factual dispute and narrowed the range of plausible denial about how the system operated, but the institutional obstacle that awaited the bill on the Senate floor remained unchanged by anything a hearing room could produce.

That obstacle was the filibuster, and its arithmetic governed the middle years of the campaign. Senate cloture required sixty votes, and reform supporters repeatedly failed to assemble that number. The 1998 cloture vote captured the position with precision, ending at 51 to 48 in favor of closing debate, with all 45 Democrats and 6 Republicans supporting cloture, a clear majority of the senators voting and a clear shortfall against the supermajority threshold the rule imposed. Nine votes separated a majority coalition from the authority to proceed to a final vote that the same coalition might well have won. The composition of the 51 also explained the difficulty of closing the gap through ordinary persuasion, because the supporting bloc already contained near unanimity in one party and only a small fraction of the other, so each additional vote had to come from a senator whose party colleagues were overwhelmingly on the opposing side and whose objections often concerned the substance of the soft money ban or the advertising provisions rather than the details of drafting. Repeated failure under those conditions carried its own cost, since each defeated cloture attempt allowed opponents to argue that the Senate had considered the question and had declined to act, even though a majority had voted in favor of acting.

While the Senate blocked action, the House demonstrated that a majority for a version of the reform existed in at least one chamber. The House passed Shays-Meehan versions of the bill in 1998 and again in 1999, establishing that the pairing of a Republican and a Democratic sponsor could hold a floor coalition together in the chamber that operates by majority rule and that lacked a sixty vote threshold for closing debate. Both House bills then died in the Senate, a sequence that clarified the location of the veto point in the legislative process. The problem for reform supporters was not drafting a bill capable of passing a chamber, nor was it persuading a national audience that soft money presented a policy question worthy of attention. The problem was the interaction between a Senate supermajority requirement and a committed minority large enough to deny cloture, Congress after Congress, regardless of action elsewhere in the Capitol. House passage in successive Congresses kept the proposal alive as a legislative vehicle and as a public commitment by its sponsors, but passage alone could not compel the Senate to reach a final vote on the merits.

The 2000 presidential campaign altered the political environment in which that Senate arithmetic operated, because McCain carried the issue nationally as a candidate for the Republican presidential nomination. A proposal that had been debated as a Senate bill acquired a campaign identity, presented to Republican primary voters and to a broader electorate as a test of willingness to confront the financing practices of the political parties themselves. That national exposure did not by itself amend Senate procedure or convert opponents, and supporters entered the 107th Congress in 2001 facing the same institutional requirements that had defeated earlier versions. The campaign nevertheless changed the cost calculation for continued obstruction by raising the visibility of the subject beyond the circle of specialists, committee staff, and organized interests that had followed the bill through its earlier iterations. When S. 27 was introduced on January 22, 2001, and debated over two weeks with 22 amendments adopted before passage on April 2, 2001, by 59 to 41, the Senate result reflected a chamber in which the reform coalition had grown close to the threshold that had eluded it in 1998, although the House route would still prove necessary to produce a final enactment.

The collapse of Enron in late 2001 added momentum in the final months of the effort, at a moment when the House bill faced committee resistance and required an unusual procedural effort to reach the floor. Contemporaneous reporting credited the scandal with pushing the bill forward, and the connection that reporting drew was political rather than technical, since Enron had no drafting role in provisions written years earlier in response to party soft money and issue advertising. A major corporate failure that became a subject of congressional investigation and extensive press coverage reminded legislators and voters that large corporate contributions and access to policymakers belonged to a single broader debate about money and influence in government. For fence sitting members weighing the discharge petition and the final House vote in early 2002, the scandal supplied a reason to act promptly that the hearings of 1997 and the cloture failures of 1998 had not supplied on their own. The seven year interval between the joint call of 1995 and the signature of March 27, 2002, thus reflected no single cause of delay, but a succession of barriers, evidentiary development, procedural arithmetic, chamber disagreement, and national politics, each of which had to align before a bill that commanded simple majorities could survive every veto point in the process.

The structure of the dispute also helps explain why a majority position could remain a losing position for so long. Supporters framed soft money as a circumvention of the contribution limits that had governed federal elections since the Federal Election Campaign Act, arguing that a donor barred from giving a large sum directly to a candidate could give a far larger sum to a national party and achieve a comparable political effect through party advertising and party activity. Opponents framed the same transactions as support for party building and grassroots organization, activities that parties had long conducted with funds raised under state law and that the 1979 amendments to the federal statute had placed partly outside the federal definitions of contribution and expenditure. Those competing characterizations were not merely rhetorical ornaments on a vote count; they determined how individual senators assessed the risk of a ban. A senator persuaded that soft money corrupted the candidate contribution system could accept a prohibition as a repair to an existing framework, while a senator persuaded that parties required flexible funds to compete in expensive elections could regard the same prohibition as a handicap imposed on the most accountable participants in the system, with money predictably migrating toward independent groups that answered to no candidate and no party membership. Because both arguments drew support inside each party, party leaders could not deliver a unified bloc for cloture through discipline alone, and the bipartisan sponsorship that lent the bill credibility also confirmed that the division cut across the usual lines of Senate organization.

The repeated Senate failures had a further institutional consequence that prolonged the effort beyond the arithmetic of any single vote. Each Congress required the work to begin again, with a bill introduced, a committee record assembled or renewed, and floor time negotiated against competing measures whose sponsors could invoke the same scarcity of legislative days that reform opponents invoked against McCain-Feingold. Sponsors and staff who carried the proposal from one Congress into the 107th Congress had to preserve a coalition whose members faced reelection, retirement, and shifting committee assignments, while opponents needed only to preserve a blocking minority of sufficient size in the chamber where the supermajority rule applied. That asymmetry between construction and obstruction is a familiar feature of Senate legislating, but the campaign finance record gave it an unusually clean illustration, because the House twice supplied evidence that a majority existed for passage and the Senate twice supplied evidence that a majority was insufficient. The eventual Senate passage of S. 27 in April 2001, after two weeks of debate and the adoption of 22 amendments, therefore marked a genuine change in the chamber position, even though the margin of 59 to 41 remained one vote below the figure associated with cloture and even though enactment still required the House bill, the discharge petition, and a second Senate vote on a different text almost a year later.

## The Discharge Petition: Procedure as the Deciding Vote

### What does a discharge petition do in the House?

A discharge petition allows a majority of House members to force a bill out of a committee that has refused to report it favorably, placing the measure on the calendar for floor consideration despite opposition from committee leaders and the majority party leadership controlling the normal legislative schedule for that chamber session.

The need for such a device arose from the reception that Shays-Meehan received in the Committee on House Administration during the 107th Congress. The measure had first been offered as H.R. 380 and was reintroduced as H.R. 2356 on June 28, 2001, the same day on which the committee ordered Shays-Meehan reported unfavorably and ordered the rival Ney-Wynn bill, H.R. 2360, reported favorably. That paired action stated the institutional position with unusual clarity. The committee of jurisdiction had examined the reform bill, had declined to endorse it, and had advanced an alternative measure in its place, which under ordinary practice would have positioned the rival bill for floor consideration and left the unfavorably reported measure without a viable path to a vote. An unfavorable report is not a formal death sentence for a House bill, since the full chamber retains the constitutional authority to legislate on a subject regardless of a committee recommendation, but the committee system and the leadership control of scheduling ordinarily make such a report decisive in practice, a reality that an account of [the committee system and its markup process](/2022/03/01/committee-system-and-markups/) helps explain for readers tracing how measures advance or expire before reaching the floor.

Supporters therefore turned to the discharge petition, a mechanism whose logic is simple and whose successful use is rare because its requirements expose every signer to pressure from leadership and committee officials. The petition required 218 signatures, a majority of the House, gathered member by member through late 2001 and into January 2002. Each signature was a public commitment by a member to set aside the normal order of business and to compel consideration of a bill that the responsible committee had reported unfavorably, and the accumulation of signatures proceeded slowly precisely because members understood that signing placed them in open disagreement with the scheduling decisions of their own chamber leadership. The number itself distinguished the discharge effort from an ordinary expression of sentiment in a letter or a cosponsorship list, since 218 signers constituted the same majority that would be required to pass the bill if every signer held firm, and leadership could count the signatures as reliably as supporters could. When Shays announced success on January 24, 2002, the announcement therefore conveyed more than progress toward a procedural goal; it demonstrated that a floor majority for consideration already existed and had been documented in a form that the House could not easily disregard.

The rarity of the achievement underscored its significance in the history of the chamber. The petition on H.R. 2356 was only the second successful discharge petition since the 1980s, a record that reflects the strength of the institutional forces arranged against the device. Committees guard their gatekeeping role because the division of labor in a large chamber depends on committees screening, amending, and reporting measures before the full membership votes, and party leadership guards the floor schedule because control of the agenda allows a majority party to advance its program and to protect its members from difficult votes. A successful petition overrides both forms of control at once, which explains why members who might vote for a bill on final passage sometimes hesitate to sign a petition that brings the bill forward against leadership wishes. In the campaign finance fight, the successful petition also reversed the meaning of the committee action of June 28, 2001, converting the unfavorable report from an effective veto into a documented obstacle that a majority had deliberately chosen to surmount. Procedure in that sense was not a preliminary detail to the substantive vote that followed, but a deciding vote in its own right, taken signature by signature over a period of months.

Once the signatures landed, leadership scheduled the bill, and the House proceeded to a debate that matched the length of the struggle preceding it. Debate ran nearly 17 hours across February 13 and 14, 2002, a marathon sitting in which supporters defended the soft money ban and the electioneering communication provisions against amendments and arguments that had been refined over seven years of committee hearings, floor fights, and campaign exchanges. The duration mattered because it allowed the House to consider the measure as the major legislation its sponsors claimed it to be, rather than as a procedural protest discharged for symbolic effect. Members addressed the role of national parties as conduits for large contributions, the experience of the 1996 and 2000 cycles, and the constitutional objections that opponents predicted the courts would later entertain. A debate of that length also tested the coalition that the petition had assembled, since a majority assembled to force consideration might fracture over particular provisions during amendment, and the sponsors had to hold together Republicans willing to defy much of their party and Democrats willing to accept restrictions that many of their own party organizations regarded with apprehension.

The House passed the bill on February 14, 2002, by 240 to 189, a margin that confirmed the petition count as an accurate measure of floor strength while also revealing the partisan shape of the coalition. Democrats supported passage by 198 to 12, and Republicans opposed it by 41 to 176, with the independent vote split at one to one, so the enactment coalition rested on an overwhelming Democratic majority joined by a Republican minority that included the bill's own House sponsor. That distribution explains both the bill's success and the difficulty that had preceded it in a chamber under Republican leadership, since a measure opposed by most members of the majority party ordinarily lacks a route through committee and lacks a place on the schedule. The discharge petition had supplied the route, and the final tally supplied the justification that supporters offered for using it, namely that a genuine majority of the whole House favored the bill and had been prevented from voting on it only by intermediate institutional controls. Opponents could respond that those controls existed to filter measures and to structure deliberation, but after a 240 vote majority had formed in public, the argument for continued delay lost the procedural protection that the committee report had provided during the preceding summer and autumn.

The Senate then chose the course that brought the long effort to a conclusion without further negotiation between the chambers. Rather than going to conference, the Senate took up the House bill directly and passed it on March 20, 2002, by 60 to 40, accepting the text of H.R. 2356 as the final legislative product. Direct passage avoided the risks that a conference committee would have introduced at the end of a seven year campaign, including reopened disputes over provisions that one chamber had accepted only as part of a delicate compromise and the possibility of further delay in the Senate under the same cloture arithmetic that had defeated earlier versions. The tally itself carried symbolic weight for supporters, because 60 votes was the number that cloture had demanded and that the 1998 effort, at 51 to 48, had failed to reach; on final passage in 2002 the reform coalition met that figure exactly. The Senate vote also preserved the identity of the House vehicle, so the bill presented to the president was the Shays-Meehan measure that the discharge petition had rescued, a continuity that tied the procedural history of the House directly to the enacted law.

The decision to proceed by discharge rather than by negotiation over the rival bill also shaped the character of the measure that reached the Senate. Ney-Wynn, as the bill reported favorably by the Committee on House Administration, represented the alternative that the committee majority preferred, and supporters of Shays-Meehan had to decide whether compromise within committee offered a plausible route to a soft money restriction with meaningful force or whether the committee product would dilute the central prohibition beyond recognition by its original sponsors. By gathering signatures for the original House bill instead, supporters preserved the text they had carried through earlier Congresses and forced a comparison on the floor between enactment of that text and rejection of it, rather than a comparison between two committee drafts whose differences would have been negotiated largely outside public view. The petition thus protected the substance of the proposal at the same time that it overcame the scheduling obstacle, and that dual function accounts for the intensity of the opposition the device provoked. Opponents of the bill lost not only a vote on policy when the 218th signature was recorded, but also the opportunity to reshape the policy through the ordinary committee process that had produced an unfavorable report for one bill and a favorable report for its rival on the same day.

The final House and Senate votes also settled a question about representation that the long delay had left open. Skeptics could argue during the years of Senate blockage that professed support for reform in speeches and campaign statements cost members little, because the filibuster or the committee system would prevent a binding decision. The discharge petition removed that shelter in the House by converting private inclination into a signed public act, and the roll call votes of February 14 and March 20, 2002, removed it in both chambers by recording each member and each senator for or against the same statutory text. Whatever later courts made of particular provisions, the legislative record closed with majorities in both houses and a presidential signature attached to an identifiable bill, H.R. 2356, whose path through the 107th Congress could be traced from an unfavorable report through a rare procedural revolt to enactment. That traceable path became part of the statute's history in later litigation and commentary, because it showed Congress acting deliberately, after extended debate and repeated failure, rather than attaching a campaign finance rider to an unrelated measure in the closing hours of a session.

President George W. Bush signed the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act on March 27, 2002, and accompanied the signature with stated reservations about the constitutionality of the broad ban on issue advertising. That qualification placed the executive branch on record with a doubt that opponents of Title II shared and that the expedited judicial review provisions of the statute were designed to test without prolonged uncertainty. Signature with reservations nevertheless constituted enactment, and the general effective date of November 6, 2002, the day after the 2002 general election, deferred the new regime until the electoral cycle then in progress had concluded, while the contribution limit changes applied to contributions made on or after January 1, 2003. The sequence from an unfavorable committee report in June 2001 to a presidential signature in March 2002 therefore depended on a chain of decisions in which no link was dispensable, from the members who signed in sufficient number to reach 218, to the leadership that scheduled the discharged bill, to the House majority that sustained it through nearly 17 hours of debate, to the Senate that accepted the House text without a conference. Remove the petition from that chain, and the record supplied by the committee action, the rival bill, and the seven preceding years points toward another session ending with a reform majority unable to obtain a vote in the chamber where its bill had been reported unfavorably.

## The First Title: National Parties Cut Off From Soft Money

### What made soft money different from a campaign contribution?

Soft money differed because it sat outside the limits and source prohibitions that governed contributions for federal elections. The money came from sources barred from contributing directly and arrived in amounts federal law would not have allowed, after party building rules placed certain spending beyond the statutory definitions.

The distinction began as a matter of statutory definition. The amendments enacted in 1979 to [the Federal Election Campaign Act](/2021/09/01/federal-election-campaign-act-1971/) placed state and local party grassroots and party building spending outside the statutory definitions of contribution and expenditure. Grassroots activity built a party organization at the local level, maintained lists, recruited volunteers, and sustained routine operations between elections. By placing spending for those purposes outside the two definitions that triggered federal regulation, Congress left a space in which money could be raised and spent for party purposes without passing through the federal limits and source prohibitions that applied to contributions and expenditures themselves.

Administrative rulings then gave that space a practical mechanism. The Federal Election Commission permitted parties to allocate spending between federal and nonfederal accounts. Allocation mattered because much party activity resisted a clean separation. A party committee maintained one office, employed one staff, and communicated with voters whose ballots contained federal, state, and local contests at the same time. Under the allocation approach, a party divided the cost of shared activity between an account subject to federal rules and an account governed by state law, according to the federal and nonfederal character of the activity. The federal account held money raised under federal limits and source prohibitions. The nonfederal account held money raised under state law, which in many circumstances allowed amounts and sources that federal law excluded.

The growth figures traced a channel that expanded from a modest supplement into a major source of party finance. The Brennan Center placed party soft money at about 45 million dollars in 1988. The Congressional Research Service, drawing on Federal Election Commission data, placed the figure at 262 million dollars in 1996 and at about 495 million dollars in the 2000 cycle. The 1996 figure stood at nearly six times the 1988 figure, and the 2000 figure approached double the 1996 figure within one additional interval of four years.

Scale alone did not explain congressional concern, because the character of the money mattered as much as the amount. A contribution to a federal candidate or to a party federal account carried defined conditions. Federal law set a ceiling on how much a single source could give, and federal law barred certain sources from giving at all. Those two features, a limit and a prohibition, formed the basic architecture of the regulated system. Soft money operated beyond both features at once. A source could provide an amount that would have exceeded the federal ceiling if the money had entered a federal account, and a source excluded from the federal system could participate through the nonfederal account if state law allowed the transaction. The money then supported party activity in an election environment where federal and nonfederal contests shared the same voters, the same calendar, and often the same communications. The separation was therefore legal and accounting based, resting on account labels and allocation formulas, while the practical effect of the spending reached voters deciding federal races.

The 1996 election cycle gave that concern a concrete setting. National parties raised and spent soft money on broadcast issue advertising at a scale described in the record as unprecedented, and the Senate Governmental Affairs Committee, under Senator Fred Thompson, investigated 1996 fundraising practices in hearings held in 1997. Title I of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act answered that record with a prohibition directed at the national committees themselves, rather than with a narrower adjustment to allocation percentages or reporting detail.

New section 323 of the Federal Election Campaign Act, added by sections 101 to 103 of the reform act, supplied the prohibition in comprehensive terms. National party committees were barred from soliciting, receiving, directing, or spending funds outside the limits and source prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act, for any purpose. Each verb in that formulation closed a different route around the rule. A bar on soliciting prevented a national committee from asking for nonfederal money in the first place, so the prohibition reached the request and not merely the completed transfer. A bar on receiving prevented acceptance of such money offered without a prior request. A bar on directing prevented a committee from steering nonfederal money toward another spender or another account while retaining practical control over its use, so formal distance from the final expenditure did not remove the transaction from the prohibition. A bar on spending prevented a committee from using nonfederal funds already in hand. The closing phrase, for any purpose, removed the argument that a particular use was too organizational, too local, or too remote from a federal candidate to count. Under section 323, the source and amount rules followed the national committee across all of its activities, and no purpose based justification restored access to nonfederal funds.

The breadth of that language represented a change in regulatory strategy. The earlier system had attempted to sort party spending by purpose, treating some purposes as federal and others as nonfederal, and then allocating shared costs between the two categories. Section 323 abandoned purpose sorting for national committees. A national committee operated entirely within federal limits and source prohibitions, regardless of whether a given expenditure supported a federal candidate directly, supported state and local activity, or maintained the organization itself. Enforcement no longer depended on contesting the classification of each shared cost. The question became whether the national committee had solicited, received, directed, or spent money outside the federal framework at all.

Section 323 also reached beyond committee treasuries to individual federal candidates and officeholders, in subsection (e). Federal candidates and officeholders were barred from soliciting or receiving soft money in connection with a federal election, and parallel limits governed solicitation connected with state and local elections. A prohibition limited to committees would have left open the possibility that the individuals with the greatest personal interest in an election, and often the greatest capacity to attract large donations, could request nonfederal money for use by others. A candidate asking a supporter for a large nonfederal contribution could achieve much of the practical result of raising the money directly, even if the payment passed to a party committee or another organization. By barring solicitation and receipt by candidates and officeholders themselves, subsection (e) treated the request as part of the regulated transaction, because a donor who answers a candidate request understands the source of the request and the candidate understands the source of the funds. The parallel limits for state and local elections prevented a federal officeholder from using the standing of a federal position to raise nonfederal money for contests nominally outside the federal system.

For state, district, and local party committees, Title I took a different approach built around a defined category of conduct called federal election activity. State and local committees retained a dual existence under state and federal law, but the statute required payment for federal election activity with federal funds or with a permitted mix of federal funds and a limited category of state law funds. The definition therefore determined when federal money rules attached to a committee that also conducted genuinely state and local work. Each element of the definition identified a form of activity close enough to a federal election to justify federal funding conditions, even when a state or local committee carried out the activity.

The first element covered voter registration activity conducted in the 120 days before a regularly scheduled federal election. Timing supplied the rationale, because registration conducted within that window directly enlarged the electorate that would vote in that federal contest. The statute drew the line by calendar rather than by proof of intent, so a committee did not need to aim registration at a particular federal candidate for the activity to fall within the category. The second element covered voter identification, the process of determining which individuals were likely supporters, likely opponents, or likely voters at all. Identification work built the lists that later mobilization depended upon, and in a cycle containing a federal contest, those lists served federal candidates as readily as state candidates. The third element covered get out the vote activity, the direct effort to move identified supporters to cast ballots. A voter mobilized for a state contest on a shared ballot also encountered the federal contests on that ballot.

The fourth element covered generic campaign activity, meaning party promotion that supported the party as a whole rather than a named candidate. Advertising the party label benefited every candidate carrying that label, federal candidates included, so a benefit did not need to name a federal candidate to affect a federal election. The fifth element covered public communications that referred to a clearly identified federal candidate, the most direct form of shared spending, because a communication of that kind promoted or opposed a federal candidacy in practical effect regardless of which committee paid for the communication. The sixth element covered services provided by employees who spent more than 25 percent of paid time on activity connected with a federal election. The employee provision treated staff time as a resource subject to the same funding logic as purchased advertising, so a committee could not avoid federal funding requirements by assigning the cost of personnel heavily engaged in federal election work to a nonfederal account. Together, the six elements described a perimeter around the federal election itself, drawn through timing, function, content, and personnel.

The permitted mix rested on the Levin funds allowance, named for Senator Carl Levin and enacted in section 323(b). Under the allowance, state and local parties could pay for specified federal election activities that did not refer to a federal candidate using funds raised under state law, subject to a ceiling of 10,000 dollars per source per year, and subject to allocation under Federal Election Commission rules. Every condition narrowed the allowance. The activity had to fall within the specified categories. The activity could not refer to a federal candidate, so Levin funds could not finance a communication naming the person whose election gave the activity its federal character. The money had to be raised under state law, so the allowance operated within a state regulatory framework rather than outside regulation altogether. No single source could provide more than 10,000 dollars in a year for this purpose, so the allowance excluded the very large donations that had defined the soft money problem at the national level. Allocation under Commission rules governed the division between federal funds and Levin funds for qualifying activity. The allowance therefore functioned as a measured accommodation for state and local party work, particularly voter registration, voter identification, get out the vote, and generic campaign activity conducted without reference to a federal candidate, while denying state and local committees any route back to unlimited nonfederal financing.

The contrast between the national rule and the Levin allowance showed the structure of the compromise within Title I. National committees received no allowance at all, because national committees existed to influence federal elections and because the largest soft money figures in the record involved national party fundraising. State and local committees received a bounded allowance, because those committees conducted elections and party work that federal law had never fully governed. The closer a committee and an activity stood to the federal election, the stricter the funding rule became.

Title I also ended donations to national parties under the building fund exemption. Under the older framework, donations for party headquarters and building purposes had occupied an exempt position, allowing national parties to accept funds for facilities outside the ordinary federal limits. The exemption rested on a distinction between money spent to influence an election and money spent on the physical plant of a party organization. Ending the exemption applied the same reasoning that supported section 323 as a whole. A building housed the staff who conducted federal election work and hosted the operations that planned federal campaigns. Donors capable of financing facilities occupied a position of access and gratitude similar in kind to donors financing other party operations, even if the funded asset was real property rather than advertising time. After Title I, the rule for national committees contained no building exception, no allocation share, and no Levin style allowance, and the comprehensiveness of the phrase for any purpose was confirmed as intentional rather than incidental.

The result was a redefinition of the national party as a wholly federal financial actor. Before the reform act took effect on its general effective date of November 6, 2002, a national committee operated two financial personalities at once, raising regulated hard money for federal purposes and nonfederal soft money for allocated and exempt purposes. After Title I, the committee operated one financial personality, defined entirely by federal limits and source prohibitions. State and local committees retained a narrower duality, governed by the federal election activity definition and the Levin ceiling. The first title therefore removed a parallel system and left a small, capped, and supervised remnant for state and local grassroots work that avoided reference to a federal candidate.

## The Second Title: A New Category Called the Electioneering Communication

### Why did Congress regulate advertisements that never mention voting?

Congress acted because advertisements that avoided words of voting could still shape a federal race when broadcast near an election to the voters who would decide it. Issue advocacy spending rose sharply during the 1990s, while the express advocacy line left that spending outside funding and disclosure rules.

The problem began with a line drawn around the words an advertisement used. Under the express advocacy framework that governed federal regulation before the reform act, a communication fell within the central federal rules when the communication expressly advocated the election or defeat of a candidate, using language that directed viewers or listeners to vote in a particular way. A communication that discussed an issue, praised or criticized an officeholder record, and urged the audience to contact the officeholder or consider the record could avoid that classification by omitting words of explicit voting instruction. The distinction depended on vocabulary rather than on audience, timing, or electoral effect. Two advertisements could reach the same voters, in the same district, during the same final weeks of the same campaign, with the same practical message about the same candidate, and receive entirely different legal treatment because one advertisement said to vote against the candidate and the other described the candidate record and left the instruction unstated.

The spending figures showed that the distinction carried substantial money. The Congressional Research Service estimated issue advocacy spending at about 135 million dollars in 1996, rising to between 275 million dollars and 340 million dollars in 2000. The range in the 2000 estimate itself conveyed part of the problem, because spending outside the disclosure system could be estimated only within broad bounds rather than counted from complete reports. Even the lower end of the 2000 range stood at more than double the 1996 estimate. The growth paralleled the growth of party soft money during the same period, and the two developments were connected in practice, because national parties raised and spent soft money on broadcast issue advertising at unprecedented scale in the 1996 cycle and outside organizations also used issue framed advertising to address voters during federal campaigns. Money that could not enter the regulated system as a contribution, and could not be spent by certain sources as express advocacy, could still purchase broadcast time under an issue label.

Title II addressed that route by defining a new category, the electioneering communication, in sections 201 to 204 of the reform act, through a series of elements that each had to be present. Congress built the definition from objective features that a broadcaster, a sponsor, a regulator, or a court could verify from the communication itself and from distribution records, without reconstructing the private intention of the sponsor. The definition therefore replaced a vocabulary test with a structural test. A communication could discuss a genuine public issue and still fall within the category if the communication met every element, because the regulatory consequences rested on proximity to an election and identification of a candidate rather than on a judgment that the stated issue was insincere. A communication that failed any single element fell outside the category, however electoral its tone might appear. The elements operated as a set of gates, and the funding and disclosure rules of Title II applied only after a communication passed through every gate.

The first element concerned medium. An electioneering communication had to be a broadcast, cable, or satellite communication. The medium element focused the category on the forms of mass communication that reached large electorates through purchased or distributed programming signals, the same forms that had carried the issue advertising growth of the 1990s. Print material, mail, telephone contact, and other forms of communication stood outside this particular definition, whatever other provisions of federal law might govern those forms. The choice of medium also made the remaining elements workable, because broadcast, cable, and satellite distribution generated records of when a communication aired and where the signal could be received, so the timing and targeting elements could be applied from evidence rather than from estimation.

The second element concerned content, stated in narrow terms. The communication had to refer to a clearly identified federal candidate. Reference to a candidate supplied the connection between an issue discussion and a specific federal election. An advertisement about a policy question, however vigorous, remained outside the category if the advertisement named no federal candidate and depicted no federal candidate in a manner that clearly identified the candidate. Conversely, an advertisement that clearly identified a federal candidate entered the field of possible regulation even if the remainder of the script discussed legislation, public policy, or an official record. The element did not require praise or criticism. A favorable portrayal, a hostile portrayal, and a portrayal framed as neutral information all satisfied the reference element, because the definition asked whether the candidate was identified rather than whether the sponsor had taken a stated position on the candidacy.

The third element concerned timing. The communication had to be publicly distributed within 60 days before a general election or within 30 days before a primary election, and the statute also encompassed the convention and caucus periods within its timing framework. The two windows reflected a judgment about proximity. A candidate advertisement distributed a year before an election addressed an audience that included many people who might never vote in that election. The same advertisement distributed during the final two months before a general election reached voters in the act of forming a choice, amid the concentrated communication of the campaign itself. Congress drew the general election window at twice the length of the primary window, matching the greater scale and duration of general election campaigning. Distribution, rather than production or purchase, supplied the operative moment, so an advertisement prepared earlier in the year became an electioneering communication when the sponsor placed the advertisement before the public inside the defined window.

The fourth element concerned audience. The communication had to be targeted to the relevant electorate, defined as capable of being received by 50,000 or more persons in the district or state served by the candidate. For a candidate for the House of Representatives, the relevant geography was the district. For a candidate for the Senate, the relevant geography was the state. The standard measured capability of reception rather than proof that 50,000 persons actually watched or heard the communication. A broadcast signal or cable distribution reaching the threshold population satisfied the element even if ratings data showed a smaller actual audience for a particular airing. That choice made the element administrable, because signal reach and market population could be determined from distribution facts, while actual attention could never be measured with comparable precision. The threshold also confined the category to communications of electoral significance in geographic terms. A communication referring to a Senate candidate but distributed only in a market incapable of reaching 50,000 persons in that state fell outside the definition, while a communication referring to a House candidate and distributed across a market reaching 50,000 or more persons in the district fell inside the definition, assuming the medium, reference, and timing elements were also present.

Taken together, the four elements described a broadcast advertisement, naming a federal candidate, aired near that candidate election, before the voters empowered to decide that election. The definition captured the practical profile of the 1990s issue advertisement that had concerned Congress, while excluding communications that lacked any one of the features that made such an advertisement a campaign instrument in practical effect. A candidate advertisement confined to a negligible audience remained outside, and a candidate advertisement in print remained outside this category. Congress attached the consequences of Title II only to communications satisfying the complete combination.

The principal consequence for funding appeared in section 203. Corporations, including incorporated nonprofit organizations, and labor organizations were barred from paying for electioneering communications from general treasury funds. Such organizations could finance electioneering communications through separate segregated funds, the political action committee structure recognized under federal law. The distinction between a general treasury and a separate segregated fund carried the regulatory weight of the provision. A general treasury accumulated money from the ordinary operations of an organization, gathered for purposes unrelated to a particular federal election. Spending from that treasury on a candidate identifying broadcast near an election placed the full accumulated resources of the organization behind an electoral message. A separate segregated fund, by contrast, collected voluntary political contributions for political use, under federal limits and disclosure, from a restricted class of contributors connected to the organization. Section 203 therefore did not bar corporations or labor organizations from sponsoring electioneering communications. Section 203 required sponsorship through the segregated fund rather than through the treasury, so the funds behind a covered communication came from the regulated political channel rather than from the general resources of the organization.

Inclusion of incorporated nonprofit organizations within section 203 closed a specific route around the funding rule. A nonprofit corporation could accumulate substantial treasury resources and, absent inclusion, could sponsor candidate identifying broadcast advertising near an election while a business corporation or a labor organization faced the treasury prohibition. The statute treated corporate form rather than profit purpose as the operative characteristic for this provision, because an incorporated organization held a treasury gathered under the advantages of the corporate structure, whatever mission the organization pursued. The segregated fund option remained available on the same terms, so an incorporated nonprofit organization with supporters willing to contribute to a political fund could finance covered communications from that fund. The boundary of the restriction followed the boundary of the electioneering communication definition, so treasury funds remained available for communications outside the definition, including communications outside the timing windows, communications in other media, and communications that identified no federal candidate.

The second consequence of the definition concerned disclosure, under section 201. A person whose disbursements for electioneering communications exceeded 10,000 dollars in a calendar year became subject to reporting requirements administered by the Federal Election Commission. The reports identified the disbursements made for the communications and identified contributors of 1,000 dollars or more, with accelerated reporting near elections, so information reached the public on a schedule closer to the distribution of the communications themselves rather than after the election had concluded. Disclosure addressed a distinct concern from the concern addressed by the funding rule. Even where a sponsor lawfully financed a covered communication through a segregated fund or from a permissible source, voters hearing the advertisement had an interest in knowing the financial source behind the message. A communication sponsored under the name of an organization told the audience the label the sponsor had chosen, but the label alone revealed little about which contributors had financed the specific advertising effort. Section 201 required the money trail to be reported once spending passed the threshold. The 10,000 dollar threshold confined the duty to sponsors whose spending reached a level of electoral significance, and the 1,000 dollar contributor threshold identified contributors large enough to matter to an assessment of sponsor interests.

Accelerated filing near elections gave the disclosure system its practical force. A report filed after voting ended documented history but informed no choice. A report filed on an accelerated schedule during the final period of a campaign allowed journalists, opposing campaigns, and voters to connect a broadcast communication to the source of its financing while the communication continued to circulate and before ballots were cast. The acceleration requirement therefore tied the timing of disclosure to the timing element of the underlying definition, because proximity to an election changed the character of the activity in both provisions. Spending near an election demanded funding through regulated channels under section 203, and spending near an election demanded prompt public documentation under section 201. Section 203 governed where the money could come from, and section 201 governed what the public learned about where the money had in fact come from, for any sponsor whose disbursements crossed the statutory threshold.

Congress also wrote a backup definition, supplied by Senators Olympia Snowe and James Jeffords as part of the Title II framework. The backup definition covered communications that promoted, supported, attacked, or opposed a candidate, and the backup definition was written to apply only if the primary definition were invalidated by a court. The structure revealed congressional awareness that the primary definition pressed against a constitutional boundary, because regulation of communications that merely identified a candidate near an election extended federal authority into territory the courts had not previously approved in the same form. If a court struck the primary definition, the backup definition would have taken effect as a fallback reaching communications defined by promotional or oppositional character, a formulation closer to the older express advocacy tradition. The backup definition did not operate alongside the primary definition and did not expand coverage while the primary definition stood. The backup definition remained dormant text, held in reserve against a judicial outcome that would otherwise have left Title II without a working definition at all.

That judicial outcome did not occur on the schedule Congress had guarded against. The primary definition survived judicial review in 2003, when the Supreme Court decided *McConnell v. FEC* and sustained the electioneering communication provisions against facial attack, including the section 203 funding restriction and the disclosure provisions. Because the primary definition survived, the Snowe-Jeffords backup definition never operated. No sponsor became subject to the promote, support, attack, or oppose formulation as the governing test, and no report or funding decision turned on the backup language. The operative category remained the structural definition built on medium, candidate reference, timing, and audience reach, rather than a definition built on whether a communication promoted or opposed a candidate in tone.

Title II therefore rested on a recharacterization of the regulatory question. The older framework had asked what an advertisement said about voting, and sponsors had answered that question through careful drafting that omitted the decisive words. The new framework asked where an advertisement appeared, whom the advertisement identified, when the advertisement reached the public, and how many of the relevant voters could receive the advertisement. A sponsor could no longer exit the funding and disclosure system by editing a script, because script voting language no longer controlled classification. The electioneering communication stood between a contribution and a general discussion of public affairs, a category constructed for communications that functioned as campaign advertising in every verifiable respect except the presence of an instruction to vote. Sections 201 to 204 built the funding rule, the disclosure rule, and the reserve definition around that category, and the category itself became the central contribution of the second title to the structure of federal campaign finance law as enacted in 2002.

## The Supporting Machinery: Disclaimers, Higher Limits, and the Self-Funder Clause

The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 rested on two headline prohibitions, the soft money ban in Title I and the electioneering communication regime in Title II, but the statute also carried a dense body of supporting machinery in Title III and Title IV. That machinery governed how advertising identified its sponsors, how much individuals could give under the raised hard money limits, how the Federal Election Commission received and published filings, how misconduct in solicitation and inaugural fundraising was treated, and how a candidate who financed a campaign from personal resources altered the position of an opponent. Title IV then supplied the provisions that held the statute together if a court invalidated a part of the design, fixed the dates on which the new rules took hold, and set the path that constitutional litigation would follow.

Section 311 supplied the advertising identification rules through a rewrite of section 318 of the Federal Election Campaign Act. The rewrite addressed sponsorship identification on election-related advertising generally, so that communications connected to elections carried information about responsibility rather than circulating without an accountable source. By tying identification to the advertising itself, section 311 placed the disclosure at the moment of persuasion, in the same communication that asked for a vote or shaped an impression of a candidate, instead of leaving the information only in a report filed with the commission. The candidate approval statement formed the most distinctive part of the design because the statement converted sponsorship identification into a personal act of responsibility by the candidate whose campaign benefited from the message.

The candidate approval statement, known by the stand by your ad label, required a candidate to claim a communication in a form that an audience could see or hear. For television, the statute prescribed two alternative forms of appearance. The candidate could appear in a full-screen view at the close of the communication and state approval of the communication, or the candidate could be heard in an unaccompanied voice-over at the close while stating approval. The placement at the close mattered because the approval statement closed the message, so that the final impression left with a viewer paired the content of the advertisement with the candidate who accepted responsibility for that content. The full-screen view left no ambiguity about identity, since the candidate filled the screen rather than appearing in a small inset or in a group image. The unaccompanied voice-over alternative left the candidate's voice alone, without competing voices, so that a viewer could attribute the statement to the candidate rather than to a narrator, an announcer, or an endorser. For radio, the rule took a parallel form suited to a medium without images. The candidate's voice identified the candidate and stated approval of the communication. Identification by voice served the same function as the full-screen image on television, because a radio audience had no visual cue and relied on the voice itself, together with the name spoken in that voice, to connect the approval to a particular person.

The approval statement altered the incentives surrounding campaign advertising because a candidate who had to appear or speak at the close of an advertisement and state approval owned the advertisement in a public and personal sense. The disclaimer machinery regulated attribution rather than content, since the statute did not dictate the tone, the argument, or the evidence that an advertisement presented. That distinction ran through Title III, since the raised limits in section 307 set amounts, the filing rules in section 306 set methods of delivery and publication, and the approval statement in section 311 set a form for claiming responsibility.

### How do the raised limits adjust over time?

The raised limits adjust through consumer price index indexing tied to a 2001 base period, with each adjusted figure rounded to the nearest 100 dollars, so the per candidate limit rose from 2,000 dollars to 2,100 dollars for the 2005 to 2006 cycle before later adjustments followed.

Section 307 carried the contribution changes, and the section paired higher amounts with an indexing mechanism so that the relationship between the statutory figures and the economy would not depend entirely on fresh legislation each time prices moved. The individual limit on contributions to a candidate rose from 1,000 dollars per candidate per election to 2,000 dollars per candidate per election. The doubling applied election by election, a structure that treated a primary and a general election as separate elections for limit purposes under the framework that the statute amended. The figure of 2,000 dollars became the base amount to which indexing later applied, and the first indexed result illustrated the mechanism in concrete terms. For the 2005 to 2006 cycle, the indexed per candidate limit stood at 2,100 dollars. The increase of 100 dollars over the statutory base reflected the rounding rule as well as the price movement, since an indexed amount rounded to the nearest 100 dollars moved in steps rather than in exact proportion to each small change in the index.

The annual limit on contributions to a national party committee rose from 20,000 dollars to 25,000 dollars. That change raised the amount that an individual donor could lawfully direct to a national party committee within a calendar year under the hard money system at the same time that Title I closed the soft money channel through which national parties had previously received funds outside the limits and source prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act. The pairing was deliberate in structural terms. Congress removed an unlimited channel, within the source rules, and raised a limited channel, so that national party committees would raise support under contribution limits, source prohibitions, and disclosure rather than through the separate nonfederal accounts that the soft money system had sustained. The 25,000 dollar figure therefore cannot be read in isolation. The figure formed one part of a substitution, in which regulated money replaced unregulated money as the intended foundation for party activity, and the biennial aggregate then placed an outer boundary around the total that a single donor could spread across candidates and committees in a two year cycle.

Section 307 created a new biennial aggregate of 95,000 dollars. Within that aggregate, no more than 37,500 dollars could go to candidates, and no more than 57,500 dollars could go to other committees. The two subceilings added to the aggregate itself, since 37,500 dollars and 57,500 dollars together made 95,000 dollars, and the division between the subceilings expressed a judgment about the balance between direct support for candidates and support for committees. Inside the 57,500 dollar ceiling for other committees, a further inner limit of 37,500 dollars applied to committees other than national party committees. The inner limit prevented a donor from concentrating the entire committee portion of the aggregate in a single category of committee outside the national party structure, and the layering of an aggregate, two subceilings, and an inner ceiling showed how section 307 regulated distribution as well as total amount. A donor could reach the aggregate only by respecting each layer, and a contribution that satisfied a per recipient limit could still fail the aggregate structure if the pattern of giving across a biennium crossed a subceiling.

The party committee limit connected to Senate candidates doubled from 17,500 dollars to 35,000 dollars. That figure governed the combined party committee support associated with a Senate candidate under the provision that section 307 amended, and the doubling matched the pattern of the other increases in the section, in which Congress raised amounts that had remained fixed while campaign costs and the scale of fundraising had grown. Each figure in section 307 served a different recipient or a different pattern of giving. The per candidate per election limit governed a single contribution relationship. The national party committee limit governed annual giving to a particular kind of committee. The biennial aggregate governed a donor across an entire cycle. The Senate party committee figure governed a party side channel of support for a class of candidates whose races operated across entire states. Indexing then applied across the raised structure, using the consumer price index with 2001 as the base period, so that the base period preceded the operative life of the new limits and anchored the calculation to a fixed reference point rather than to a moving year chosen at each adjustment.

Rounding to the nearest 100 dollars gave the indexed limits an administrative simplicity that exact figures would have lacked, since a limit stated in a round figure could be communicated to donors, candidates, treasurers, and committees without a table of irregular amounts. The first indexed per candidate limit of 2,100 dollars for the 2005 to 2006 cycle demonstrated the practical output of the design. The contribution limit changes themselves applied to contributions made on or after January 1, 2003, a date that separated the new amounts from the general effective date of November 6, 2002 and gave the raised limits a clean starting point at the beginning of a calendar year and a new election cycle.

The smaller provisions of Title III addressed the administration of the system and several specific points of abuse or opacity that did not fit inside the soft money and electioneering communication titles. Section 306 required standardized electronic filing software and prompt posting of filings on the internet by the Federal Election Commission. Standardization addressed the form in which reports arrived, since reports prepared through a common software framework could be received, processed, and compared on consistent terms rather than through a collection of incompatible formats. Prompt internet posting addressed the speed with which filed information reached the public. Disclosure that arrived after an election, or that remained difficult to obtain during a campaign, lost much of the value that justified the reporting burden, and section 306 treated timely public access as part of the disclosure obligation rather than as an administrative afterthought. Together, the software requirement and the posting requirement moved disclosure toward a system in which a filing became a public record in usable form shortly after submission.

Section 308 dealt with inaugural committees, a subject adjacent to campaign finance because an inaugural committee raised money in connection with the assumption of office rather than in connection with an election contest itself. The section required disclosure of donations to an inaugural committee of 200 dollars or more, so that donations at or above that threshold entered a public record. The threshold of 200 dollars set the point at which anonymity ended for inaugural giving under the provision, and donations below that point fell outside the disclosure trigger that the section stated. Section 308 also barred foreign national donations to inaugural committees, extending a source prohibition into the inaugural setting. The pairing of disclosure and a source bar in a single section reflected the same combination that the broader statute used elsewhere, since Congress repeatedly joined transparency about amounts and sources with categorical exclusions for sources that the statute placed outside lawful participation.

Section 309 barred fraudulent misrepresentation in the solicitation of campaign funds. A solicitation that misrepresented a material fact in order to obtain campaign money attacked the system from a different direction than an excessive contribution or an undisclosed expenditure. Fraud in solicitation deceived the donor, so that consent to give rested on a false premise, and fraud also risked diverting support from the candidate or committee that a donor intended to assist toward an operation that traded on confusion or false claims of authority. A bar on fraudulent misrepresentation therefore protected donors as well as the integrity of campaign finance records, since money raised through deception entered the system under a false description of purpose or affiliation. Sections 312 to 315 then supplied enforcement weight for Title III and for the amended statute more broadly. Those sections raised penalties, set a limitations period, and included sentencing provisions. A limitations period fixed the window within which enforcement could proceed for covered conduct, and a defined window served both the government and persons accused of violations by tying prosecution to a period stated in the statute rather than to open ended exposure. Raised penalties and sentencing provisions addressed the consequences after a violation was established, and the combination of a limitations period with stronger penalties balanced prompt enforcement against punishment proportionate to the gravity that Congress assigned to campaign finance offenses.

The Millionaire's Amendment addressed a problem that contribution limits created in races involving a candidate who financed a campaign largely from personal funds. A candidate who spent personal wealth on a campaign did not depend on contributions in the way that an opponent depended on contributions, and the ordinary per contributor limit therefore constrained the opponent's fundraising while placing no equivalent constraint on the self-funder's personal spending. Congress answered with a conditional adjustment rather than with a restriction on the self-funder. In the House version, section 319, codified as section 441a-1 of the Federal Election Campaign Act, the mechanism turned on a threshold of 350,000 dollars in personal funds spending by a House candidate. When a House candidate's personal funds spending exceeded that threshold, consequences followed for the opposing candidate. The opposing candidate could accept individual contributions at up to three times the ordinary limit, so that a contributor who would otherwise have faced the standard per candidate ceiling could give at a multiplied ceiling in a race where the threshold had been crossed. The opposing candidate could also benefit from increased party coordinated spending, which raised the support that a party could supply in coordination with the campaign beyond the level that would otherwise have applied.

Notification duties completed the House mechanism. The self-funder bore escalating notification duties at stated thresholds, so that the commission, the opponent, and other participants in the race learned of personal funds spending as spending rose rather than only after reports revealed the full scale of self-financing at a later stage. Notification gave the triggered benefits a factual foundation, since the opponent's ability to accept contributions at up to three times the ordinary limit and to receive increased coordinated party spending depended on a determination that personal funds spending had crossed the 350,000 dollar threshold and, under the escalating structure, on knowledge of further spending beyond that point. The design therefore linked information to consequence. Disclosure alone would have told an opponent that a race had changed without supplying additional capacity to respond, while an automatic adjustment without notification would have created uncertainty about when the higher limits took effect. Section 319 joined the two, with duties on the candidate whose spending triggered the adjustment and with benefits that ran to the opposing candidate rather than to the self-funder.

A parallel Senate mechanism operated under a different measure of scale. Senate races varied enormously in cost and electorate because states varied in population, and a single fixed dollar threshold that suited a House district would not have measured personal funds spending sensibly across states of very different sizes. The Senate mechanism therefore used a formula keyed to the voting-age population of the state. The formula scaled the trigger to the size of the electorate that a Senate candidate addressed, so that personal funds spending was judged against a benchmark connected to the population from which voters in that state were drawn. The parallel structure preserved the logic of the House provision while adapting the measurement to the constitutional difference between a representative elected from a district and a senator elected statewide. In both settings, the amendment left the self-funder free to spend personal funds and instead changed the limits that governed everyone else in the race once the stated trigger was crossed, a choice whose constitutional fate a later section of the litigation record would decide.

Title IV closed the statute with provisions about the statute itself. Section 401 supplied severability, a statement that the invalidation of one part of the act would not automatically bring down the remainder. Severability mattered for a statute of this breadth because Titles I, II, and III contained many distinct provisions, from the soft money ban to disclaimer rules to filing software requirements, and a defect in a single provision did not necessarily reflect a defect in provisions that operated independently. A severability clause instructed a reviewing court to preserve the parts of the statute that could function without the invalid part, subject to the court's own analysis, rather than treating the whole enactment as a single indivisible bargain. Section 402 supplied effective dates, the provisions that fixed when the new rules governed conduct. The general effective date was November 6, 2002, the day after the 2002 general election, a choice that kept the new regime from changing the rules in the middle of the election cycle then concluding and that placed the operative start at the opening of the next cycle. The contribution limit changes applied to contributions made on or after January 1, 2003, as section 307 operated within that broader timing structure. Section 403 then designed the judicial review process for constitutional challenges. Actions under section 403 were filed in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia, heard by a three-judge court, expedited in every way, and subject to direct appeal to the Supreme Court. The design concentrated constitutional litigation over a federal election statute in a single district, placed the first hearing before a court of three judges rather than a single judge, accelerated each stage, and removed the intermediate court of appeals from the path between the district court and the Supreme Court. That design explained much about the speed of the litigation that followed enactment, and the litigation record in turn tested nearly every major choice that Titles I, II, and III contained.

## The Judicial Arc: Sustained, Narrowed, Then Struck in Part

The constitutional litigation over the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act began immediately after enactment on March 27, 2002, and the first Supreme Court decision arrived in December 2003, a pace that the expedited review design in section 403 had made possible. Challengers attacked the act across its range, from the soft money restrictions of Title I to the electioneering communication provisions of Title II to peripheral provisions that reached minors and party spending choices. The result in *McConnell v. FEC* sustained the central structure against facial attack, struck two peripheral provisions, and declined on standing grounds to reach the raised limits and the Millionaire's Amendment in that posture. Later cases then changed the practical scope of what the first decision had sustained. An as-applied challenge in the Wisconsin Right to Life litigation confined section 203 to a narrower class of communications, *Davis v. FEC* struck the Millionaire's Amendment that *McConnell* had not reached, and *Citizens United v. FEC* overruled part of *McConnell* itself and invalidated the treasury funds prohibition at the heart of section 203.

The plaintiffs who opened the arc numbered more than 80 across the consolidated cases, and Senator Mitch McConnell stood among the leaders of the challenge that gave the principal case its name. The scale of the plaintiff group reflected the scale of the statute, and consolidation allowed a single three-judge court to receive the full set of claims rather than leaving the statute to be tested in fragments across multiple districts. The three-judge court ruled in May 2003, in a decision reported at 251 F. Supp. 2d 176, with divided results across the many provisions under attack. A divided outcome of that kind left the central questions open in practical terms, and the direct appeal placed the entire structure before the Supreme Court for resolution at a single sitting.

### Why did the first constitutional decision arrive so soon after enactment?

The first decision arrived quickly because section 403 channeled constitutional challenges into the District Court for the District of Columbia before a three judge court, required expedition at every stage, and allowed direct appeal to the Supreme Court, compressing litigation that ordinarily would have taken years into months.

The Supreme Court heard four hours of argument at a special sitting on September 8, 2003, an allocation of time and a scheduling choice that matched the breadth of the consolidated record. Four hours of argument allowed counsel for the many grouped claims and defenses to address soft money, electioneering communications, disclosure, and the peripheral provisions within one proceeding, and the special sitting placed the argument outside the ordinary rhythm of a term that would otherwise have delayed consideration. The Court decided the case on December 10, 2003, as *McConnell v. FEC*, 540 U.S. 93. A five justice majority sustained the core of the statute. Justices Stevens and O'Connor jointly authored the opinion on Titles I and II, and Justices Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer joined that majority. Joint authorship by Justices Stevens and O'Connor placed the central opinion in the hands of two justices whose agreement defined the outcome on the soft money and electioneering communication questions, and the five justice alignment held on the holdings that mattered most to the statute's operation in the cycles that followed.

On soft money, the majority sustained the restrictions of section 323, the provision added by Title I, as a closely drawn anticircumvention measure under the contribution framework associated with *Buckley*. The reasoning treated the soft money ban as a measure aimed at the circumvention of contribution limits rather than as a direct restriction on independent speech. Large donations to national parties, on that view, created a risk that the contribution limits governing candidates would lose force, because money given to a party in unlimited amounts from otherwise prohibited or restricted sources could support the candidates whom the party existed to elect. A closely drawn anticircumvention justification therefore allowed Congress to close a channel through which the base limits could be undermined, provided the closing measure fit the circumvention problem with the closeness that the majority found in section 323. The holding sustained not only a prohibition on national party committees receiving and spending soft money but also the surrounding structure that governed federal candidates and officeholders and that set the funding rules for federal election activity by state, district, and local party committees. The result left Title I intact as the governing law for party finance after the November 6, 2002 effective date.

On Title II, the majority sustained section 203 against facial attack and sustained the electioneering communication disclosure provisions against facial attack as well. A facial posture placed a heavy burden on the challengers, since a facial claim asked the Court to hold that a provision could not constitutionally operate rather than that a provision had operated unconstitutionally in a particular application. Section 203 barred corporations, including incorporated nonprofit organizations, and labor organizations from paying for electioneering communications from general treasury funds, while leaving open the use of separate segregated funds. Sustainment of that prohibition in *McConnell* preserved the distinction between treasury funds and segregated funds as a constitutional line for the period that followed the decision, and sustainment of the disclosure provisions preserved the reporting duties that section 201 had attached to electioneering communication disbursements above the statutory threshold. President George W. Bush had stated reservations about the constitutionality of the broad ban on issue advertising when the president signed the act on March 27, 2002, and the facial decision in December 2003 resolved that stated doubt, for the time covered by the decision, in favor of validity.

The majority did not sustain everything. The Court struck two peripheral provisions. Section 318, which banned campaign contributions by individuals aged 17 or younger, fell unanimously, so that no justice in the case voted to sustain the minors contribution ban. The forced choice provision of section 213 fell as well. That provision would have required a party, after nominating a candidate, to choose between coordinated expenditures and independent expenditures in support of that candidate. The invalidation of the forced choice rule preserved a party's ability to engage in both forms of spending under the otherwise applicable rules rather than forcing an election between the two modes at the point of nomination. The two invalidations shared a peripheral position in the statute. Neither the minors ban nor the forced choice rule formed part of the soft money prohibition or the electioneering communication definition, and the loss of those provisions therefore left the central titles standing while removing two rules that reached conduct at the edges of the design.

A separate majority in *McConnell* held that the plaintiffs lacked standing to attack the raised hard money limits and the Millionaire's Amendment in that facial posture, so the Court did not reach the merits of those provisions. The standing ruling had lasting consequences for the sequence of later litigation. Section 307 raised limits that many participants in the system welcomed rather than resisted, and a challenge to higher limits by plaintiffs whom the Court found lacked the required stake could not proceed in that case regardless of the arguments offered against the amounts. The Millionaire's Amendment likewise survived *McConnell* without a merits ruling, since the standing holding removed section 319 from the set of provisions that the Court evaluated in 2003. Survival without a merits ruling differed from sustainment on the merits. Section 203 carried an affirmative facial endorsement from December 2003, while the Millionaire's Amendment carried only the absence of a decision, and that difference left section 319 exposed to a later challenge brought by a plaintiff with a concrete stake in a race where the amendment operated. Dissents by Chief Justice Rehnquist and by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas attacked the core holdings in part, and the dissenting opinions preserved arguments against the soft money and electioneering communication holdings that later majorities would revisit in narrower and then in broader forms.

The narrowing began with *FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life, Inc.*, 546 U.S. 410 (2006), a per curiam decision that held that the facial ruling in *McConnell* did not foreclose as-applied challenges to section 203. That opening mattered because a speaker whose communication fell within the electioneering communication definition could argue that application of the treasury funds prohibition to a particular communication violated the First Amendment even though the provision had survived facial attack. The fuller ruling arrived in *FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life, Inc.*, 551 U.S. 449 (2007), decided on June 25, 2007, by five votes to four. Chief Justice Roberts supplied the controlling analysis, joined in its reasoning by Justice Alito, while Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas concurred in the judgment and would have overruled *McConnell* on the point, and Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer dissented. The controlling analysis confined section 203 to communications that were the functional equivalent of express advocacy, meaning communications susceptible of no reasonable interpretation other than as an appeal to vote for or against a candidate. The organization's advertisements, which addressed Senate filibusters of judicial nominees, were held to be genuine issue advertisements entitled to the exemption that the analysis produced. The advertisements therefore could be financed without the section 203 treasury funds bar, not because the definition of electioneering communication had changed, but because the constitutional reach of the prohibition had been narrowed to a class defined by the absence of any reasonable interpretation apart from an electoral appeal. The relationship between that narrowing and the broader sequence of decisions receives extended treatment in [the companion account of the major campaign finance cases](/2021/11/15/campaign-finance-supreme-court-cases/), which places the Wisconsin litigation between the facial sustainment of 2003 and the invalidations that followed.

A plaintiff with a direct stake then brought the provision that *McConnell* had not reached before the Court. In *Davis v. FEC*, 554 U.S. 724 (2008), decided on June 26, 2008, Justice Alito wrote for a five justice majority that struck sections 319(a) and 319(b), the House Millionaire's Amendment. The majority treated the amendment as a burden on the self-funder's speech that the anticorruption interest did not justify, and the leveling rationale, under which Congress adjusted limits in order to equalize the resources available to candidates in a race, was rejected as a basis for the burden. The holding followed from the structure of section 319 itself. The amendment did not limit the self-funder directly, yet the triggered benefits for an opponent, contributions at up to three times the ordinary limit and increased party coordinated spending, operated because the self-funder had spent personal funds above the 350,000 dollar threshold, and the majority concluded that a regime that penalized the exercise of a candidate's own spending in that manner could not stand on the grounds offered. After the decision, the Federal Election Commission announced that the analysis precluded enforcement of the Senate mechanism as well, and the commission ceased enforcing the amendment and its forms, so that the parallel formula keyed to voting-age population fell in practice together with the House provision that the Court had invalidated in terms.

*Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), decided on January 21, 2010, completed the arc from sustainment to partial overruling. Justice Kennedy wrote for a five justice majority in a case that arose from *Hillary: The Movie*, a feature length film critical of a presidential candidate, and from advertisements for the film. After initial argument, the Court ordered reargument on whether *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* and the part of *McConnell* that had sustained section 203 should be overruled. Reargument of that breadth signaled that the case would not be confined to an as-applied exemption of the kind produced in the Wisconsin litigation. The Court held the federal ban on corporate independent expenditures and the section 203 treasury funds prohibition unconstitutional, overruling *Austin* and the corresponding part of *McConnell*. The ban on direct corporate contributions to candidates was not at issue in the case and remained in place. The disclaimer and disclosure requirements were upheld by eight votes to one.

The sequence from 2003 to 2010 therefore left the statute in a divided condition that no single decision described on its own. Title I entered the post litigation period with the facial endorsement of *McConnell* intact on the soft money question. Title II entered the same period with a definition that had been sustained facially in 2003, narrowed as applied in 2007, and stripped of its treasury funds prohibition in 2010. The Millionaire's Amendment, unreached in 2003 because of standing, was invalidated in 2008, and the raised limits of section 307, also unreached in 2003, continued as the operative contribution structure. Severability under section 401 supplied the statutory basis on which the surviving provisions continued to operate after individual provisions fell.

## The Holding That Survived the Decision That Struck

Most retellings of *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), stop at the part of the judgment that removed a prohibition. The decision, issued on January 21, 2010, held the federal ban on corporate independent expenditures unconstitutional and struck the prohibition in section 203 of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act on the use of general treasury funds for electioneering communications. In reaching that result, the five-justice majority overruled *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* (1990) and the corresponding part of *McConnell v. FEC* that had sustained section 203 in 2003. That account is accurate as far as it goes, and it describes the part of the case that changed what corporations and labor organizations could pay for directly. It does not describe the whole judgment. In Part IV of the same opinion, a different and much larger majority upheld the statute's disclosure and disclaimer requirements as applied, by eight votes to one. The statute that lost its central advertising prohibition in that courtroom on that morning kept, by a margin that crossed the divisions visible everywhere else in the case, the machinery that required spending to be identified, reported, and labeled.

The asymmetry matters because the two halves of the decision rested on different theories about what government may do when money enters an election. The expenditure half treated a ban on independent spending from corporate treasuries as a suppression of speech that the Constitution did not permit. The disclosure half treated reporting and identification as a different kind of burden, one tied not to silencing a speaker but to informing an audience. A spender remained free, after the judgment, to pay for the communication. What the spender could not do, under the provisions sustained in Part IV, was pay for a covered communication while keeping the fact and the source of that payment outside the public record created by the statute. The distinction between prohibiting speech and requiring its attribution runs through the entire campaign finance structure that the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act built, and *Citizens United* preserved that distinction even as the majority dismantled a prohibition that the Court had sustained seven years earlier in *McConnell v. FEC*.

### What does the surviving disclosure system require a spender to reveal?

A spender whose electioneering communication disbursements exceed ten thousand dollars in a calendar year must file reports with the Federal Election Commission identifying those disbursements and each contributor of one thousand dollars or more, with accelerated reporting required as an election approaches under the statute.

The provision behind that answer is section 201 of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, which added disclosure duties to the Federal Election Campaign Act framework for electioneering communications. The underlying category, defined in Title II in sections 201 to 204, covered a broadcast, cable, or satellite communication that referred to a clearly identified federal candidate, that was publicly distributed within 60 days before a general election or 30 days before a primary, and that was targeted to the relevant electorate, meaning capable of being received by 50,000 or more persons in the district of the candidate for a House race or in the state of the candidate for a Senate race. Section 203 addressed who could pay for such communications and from which funds. Section 201 addressed a separate question, namely what a person who made disbursements for such communications had to place on record once a threshold amount had been crossed. The threshold and the contents of the report gave the system its shape. Reports went to the Federal Election Commission. Reports identified the disbursements themselves and identified contributors at the stated level. The timing rules tightened near an election, when the information carried the greatest practical value to voters evaluating messages that arrived in the closing phase of a campaign.

Section 311 supplied the companion requirement that operated at the point of contact between a communication and its audience. Section 311 rewrote the disclaimer provision of the Federal Election Campaign Act, section 318 in that act numbering, to require sponsorship identification on election related advertising. A viewer or listener did not have to locate a commission filing to learn the most basic fact about a message. The communication itself carried an identification of its sponsor. For candidate advertising, section 311 also carried the approval statement that became widely known through its spoken formula. For television, the candidate had to appear in a full screen view or be heard in an unaccompanied voice over at the close stating approval of the communication. For radio, the candidate voice identified the candidate and stated approval. The disclaimer system and the reporting system therefore worked at two different distances from the voter. One placed information on the advertisement itself, in the moment of persuasion. The other placed information in a public filing, where journalists, opposing campaigns, researchers, and interested citizens could examine amounts, timing, and sources in greater detail than any on screen statement could contain.

In *Citizens United*, the majority sustained those provisions as applied to the film and advertisements at issue in the case, a feature length film critical of a presidential candidate titled *Hillary: The Movie*, and to related advertising. The stated ground was informational. The majority rested on the governmental interest in providing the electorate with information about the sources of election related spending. Disclosure, on that reasoning, allowed voters to evaluate a message with knowledge of the interests standing behind it, rather than against spending that left no trace in the public record. The interest was not framed as an interest in equalizing voices or in reducing the quantity of speech in the system. It was framed as an interest in an informed electorate, and that framing explains why justices who divided sharply over the expenditure prohibition converged on the disclosure question. A justice could conclude that government lacked power to ban the expenditure and still conclude that government held power to require the expenditure to be reported and the resulting communication to be labeled.

Justice Thomas stood alone against that conclusion. His dissent on the disclosure point rested on the protection of anonymous speech and on the risk that compelled identification exposed speakers and contributors to retaliation. On that view, a name attached to a political position did not merely inform an audience. A name created a target. The cost of disclosure fell not in filing fees or paperwork but in the prospect that an employer, a neighbor, a customer, or an organized opponent would answer a political contribution with economic or social punishment, and that the prospect alone would deter participation by persons unwilling to accept that exposure. The argument drew its force from a long American practice of anonymous and pseudonymous political argument, and from the premise that the freedom to speak included, in at least some settings, a freedom to decide whether a name accompanied the speech. The eight justice majority did not accept that the risk, on the record and posture before the Court, defeated the informational interest for the provisions as applied. The one justice dissent did not accept that an informational interest justified a system that made identification the price of participation at the stated thresholds. Both positions survived the decision in recognizable form, and legislative debates over disclosure thresholds, contributor identification, and donor privacy in the years after 2010 repeatedly returned to the same collision that Part IV recorded.

The near disappearance of Part IV from standard accounts of *Citizens United* has several causes that reinforce one another. The first is simple narrative economy. A decision that overruled a precedent of twenty years, freed corporate treasuries for independent expenditures, and arrived after a reargument ordered on the fate of that precedent presents a dramatic story with a clear protagonist and a clear casualty, and compression favors the dramatic element. A holding that left an existing reporting system in place lacks a casualty. Nothing was struck, no filing stopped, no disclaimer vanished from a screen, and the absence of disruption reads, in a summary, as an absence of news. The second cause is structural. Accounts of the case organized around the question of whether corporations hold speech rights find Part IV peripheral, because the disclosure holding did not turn on a prohibition and did not require the majority to define the outer limit of corporate speech in the same way. The third cause is practical and, over time, the most consequential. Political actors planning campaigns after January 2010 had to absorb the expenditure holding immediately, because it changed what could be bought. The disclosure holding changed nothing about daily compliance, because it preserved duties already in force since the statute general effective date of November 6, 2002, and an unchanged duty tends to be filed as background rather than as a holding.

That filing habit distorts the legal record. A reader who encountered only the compressed account would conclude that the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act advertising title failed in full in 2010, when the judgment in fact divided the title into a prohibition that fell and a transparency system that stood with one dissent. The distortion runs in the other direction as well. A reader who encountered only Part IV would miss the scale of what the expenditure holding removed. The complete decision held both results at once, in a single opinion, on a single day, and the statute profile that omits either result misdescribes the law that emerged from the case. The ban on direct corporate contributions to candidates, the fact sheet of the case records, was not at issue and remained in place, a further reminder that *Citizens United* decided specified questions about independent expenditures and about disclosure rather than the whole field of campaign finance in a single stroke. Precision about the disclosure holding is therefore not a footnote to the famous holding. Precision about the disclosure holding is part of the holding, affirmed by the broadest coalition the case produced.

## Did the Statute Fail? The Record of the Two Titles

A familiar verdict holds that the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act failed, that money routed around its restrictions, and that the statute survives mainly as a monument to an intention that events defeated. That verdict treats the statute as a single undertaking with a single fate. The enacted law contained distinct titles that operated through distinct mechanisms, faced distinct challenges, and produced distinct records. Title I restricted soft money, the funds raised and spent outside the limits and source prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act. Title II regulated electioneering communications, defined the category, restricted one source of payment for such communications in section 203, and imposed disclosure in section 201. A judgment about failure that merges those titles into one assessment answers a question the statute text never posed. The record, examined title by title, supports a narrower and more defensible conclusion.

The soft money title presents the clearest record because its command was categorical and its object was identifiable. Section 101 to section 103 of the statute, adding section 323 to the Federal Election Campaign Act, provided that national party committees could not solicit, receive, direct, or spend funds outside the limits and prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act, for any purpose. Federal candidates and officeholders could not solicit or receive soft money in connection with a federal election, and parallel limits reached specified activity connected to state and local elections. State, district, and local party committees had to pay for federal election activity with federal funds or with a permitted mix defined by the statute, and the Levin fund mechanism in section 323(b) allowed a bounded exception for specified activities that did not refer to a federal candidate, using funds raised under state law of up to 10,000 dollars per source per year, allocated under commission rules. Title I also ended donations to national parties under the old building fund exemption. The ban operated on the enacted text, and commission enforcement of section 323 continued on that text after the statute took effect.

Measured against its own object, the title accomplished the removal it ordered. The national party soft money channel did not return after November 6, 2002, the general effective date of the statute. That statement describes a specific channel with a specific history. Soft money had grown from about 45 million dollars in 1988 to 262 million dollars in 1996 and to about 495 million dollars in the 2000 cycle, a growth that supplied much of the case for the legislation. After the ban, the parties did not resume that channel, and the absence was not a pause measured in a single quiet cycle. In the first full cycle under the ban, the 2004 cycle, the six national party committees raised about 1.21 billion dollars in hard money, funds raised under the limits and prohibitions of federal law. That total exceeded the roughly 1.07 billion dollars of hard money and soft money combined that the national committees had raised in the 2000 cycle, according to Federal Election Commission figures compiled at the Brookings Institution from data assembled by Anthony Corrado. The comparison answers the prediction, heard throughout the long legislative fight, that the national parties would wither once soft money ended. The parties adapted to the regulated channel at greater scale than the combined channels had produced in the last cycle before the ban. Whatever criticisms the post 2002 party finance system attracted, the claim that Title I failed to close the channel it named, or that its closure disabled the institutions it regulated, does not survive the figures from the first full cycle under the law.

The advertising title produced a different record, and honesty about that record requires separating the restriction from the title that contained it. Section 203 barred corporations, including incorporated nonprofit organizations, and labor organizations from paying for electioneering communications from general treasury funds, while allowing payment from separate segregated funds. The Supreme Court sustained that restriction against facial attack in *McConnell v. FEC* in 2003, narrowed its practical reach through the as applied ruling in *FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life* in 2007, and struck the treasury funds prohibition in *Citizens United* in 2010. Once the restriction fell, spending that the restriction had blocked or channeled did not vanish from elections. Other channels carried the spending, and the identity of those channels changed across the decade in ways that a full account of [the consequences of the statute for later campaign practice](/2021/12/15/campaign-finance-law-impact/) traces in detail. In the 2004 cycle, section 527 organizations became a prominent channel for large donations, drawing funds into a form organized under the tax code rather than under the party structure that Title I had regulated. Later in 2010, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, applying *Citizens United* in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC*, held contribution limits inapplicable to groups that made only independent expenditures, and independent expenditure only committees, known as super PACs, derived from that ruling. A critic measuring Title II by a single ambition, the reduction of outside spending near elections, finds ample material in that sequence.

That measurement, however, is a measurement of one provision against an ambition broader than its text, and the same habit that merged the titles returns in a new form when a whole title is judged by its restriction alone. Title II also contained the disclosure system of section 201, and that system was not struck in 2003, in 2007, or in 2010. The Supreme Court sustained the electioneering communication disclosure provisions against facial attack in *McConnell*, and eight justices sustained the disclosure and disclaimer requirements as applied in *Citizens United*. The advertising title therefore did not share a single fate within its own pages. Its prohibition rose, narrowed, and fell across three decisions. Its transparency provisions endured each decision, including the decision that ended the prohibition. Nor did Title I stand apart from judicial testing while Title II absorbed the litigation. The same *McConnell* judgment that sustained section 203 also sustained the soft money restrictions of section 323 as a closely drawn anticircumvention measure, by a five justice majority, and no later decision in the sequence restored national party soft money or displaced section 323 from the enacted text.

Weighed title by title, the failure verdict requires premises that the record does not supply. The verdict requires that a statute fails when regulated actors find lawful channels outside a struck restriction, even though the channel the statute closed by name stayed closed. The verdict requires that adaptation by regulated institutions counts as defeat, even though the national parties raised more regulated money in 2004 than regulated and unregulated money combined in 2000. The verdict requires that a title be counted as lost in full when one provision falls, even though the disclosure provisions in the same title were affirmed by eight justices in the very decision that struck the restriction. None of those premises describes a standard applied to legislation in other fields, where a statute that loses a provision to constitutional litigation while its remaining titles operate is described as partially invalidated, not as defunct. The measured conclusion follows from the record rather than from sympathy for the statute design. A statute whose first title operates in full and whose disclosure requirements were affirmed by eight justices is inaccurately described as defunct.

## Two Arguments the Statute Has Never Reconciled

The litigation over the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, from the consolidated challenges decided in *McConnell v. FEC* in 2003 through *Citizens United* in 2010, never settled the normative dispute that produced the statute. Courts resolved the validity of particular provisions in particular postures. The underlying arguments, stated most fully by the participants who carried the legislative and legal fight, continued past each judgment, because each argument began from a premise about democratic government that the opposing premise did not concede. A statute profile serves the record best by stating each argument at full strength, in the terms its holders chose, without arranging a winner that the history did not produce.

### The anti-corruption case, as its holders state it

Senators John McCain and Russ Feingold, the Senate sponsors whose names the bill carried through a campaign that began with a joint call for reform in 1995, stated the case in terms of what large contributions to political parties actually purchased in the system that preceded the statute. On that account, the evil of soft money was never limited to a provable exchange of a contribution for a specific official act. Large contributions to parties purchased access, the opportunity to be heard at length by officeholders who controlled decisions of great economic consequence, and access of that kind, sold at prices that ordinary citizens could not pay, created at least the appearance of corruption in the eyes of a public asked to trust that outcomes turned on merits rather than on donations. The appearance mattered independently of any prosecutable bargain, because a citizenry convinced that government answered to its largest funders withdrew the confidence on which representative institutions depended. Supporting organizations, including Common Cause and the Brennan Center for Justice, developed the same case with the record of the 1990s as evidence, pointing to a fundraising system in which national parties raised soft money at a scale that reached about 495 million dollars in the 2000 cycle and in which the solicitation of very large contributions by officeholders and party leaders formed a routine feature of governing rather than an aberration at its edge.

The legal architecture of the anti-corruption case rested on the framework of *Buckley v. Valeo* and on the concept of circumvention. Contribution limits, under that framework, served a permissible purpose because large contributions to candidates carried a danger of quid pro quo corruption and its appearance that expenditure limits did not carry in the same way. The sponsors and supporting organizations argued that a contribution limit system contained a structural vulnerability that Congress had power to close. If federal law limited what a donor could give a candidate and limited what a donor could give a party for federal purposes, but allowed the same donor to give without limit to the same party for purposes the party itself defined as nonfederal, the limit on the party contribution existed largely on paper. Money moved through the unregulated account supported the shared enterprise of the party, freed regulated funds for other uses, and passed through hands that solicited it with full knowledge of the donor identity and the donor interests. A guard against circumvention, on that reasoning, was not an expansion of the anticorruption interest into new territory. A guard against circumvention was the condition of the original limits retaining any effect at all, and the soft money restrictions of section 323 were defended, and in *McConnell* sustained, as a closely drawn measure of exactly that kind. The electioneering communication provisions drew support from a related strand of the same case. Broadcast advertising that named a candidate in the weeks before an election, paid for from corporate or union treasuries or by undisclosed sources, functioned in the campaign as campaign advertising functioned, whatever label its sponsor preferred, and a system that regulated the financing of express appeals while ignoring the financing of their functional equivalents invited the same circumvention in a second form.

### The free expression case, as its holders state it

Senator Mitch McConnell, the lead plaintiff among more than 80 plaintiffs in the consolidated challenges filed under the expedited review provision immediately after enactment, stated the opposing case from the premise that money spent on political communication stood close to the center of the freedom the First Amendment protected. Political speech, on that account, required resources to reach an electorate at scale, and a restriction on the funds available for speech operated in practice as a restriction on the speech itself, measured in advertisements never broadcast, arguments never heard, and candidates never criticized at the moment criticism carried the greatest consequence. The American Civil Liberties Union, which challenged the electioneering communication restrictions, brought a distinct institutional voice to the same side of the case, an organization whose defense of speech rights extended to speakers whose positions the organization did not share. The electioneering communication provisions drew the sharpest form of the objection. A communication that named a federal candidate and reached the candidate electorate within 60 days of a general election or 30 days of a primary fell within the regulated category by reason of timing and reference alone, even when the communication addressed a pending vote in Congress, a nomination under debate, or another matter of public business on which citizens had reason to address officeholders precisely because an election approached and accountability remained possible.

The incumbency strand of the free expression case answered the anti-corruption case at its foundation. Rules governing campaign finance, on that account, were written by the officeholders who competed under them, and the suspicion that attached to large contributions attached with equal logic to the motives of legislators designing the system in which challengers had to raise funds and build recognition against opponents who began with the visibility of office. Restrictions that burdened the financing of outside voices and party activity fell hardest on those who lacked an incumbent platform, a press corps attentive to daily activity, and a record that generated coverage without payment. When speakers fell silent near elections because a communication had become legally hazardous, the electorate lost information at the point in the cycle when attention peaked and choices hardened. A voter who never heard an argument, on that reasoning, suffered a concrete injury that an abstract interest in the appearance of integrity did not outweigh, and disclosure mandates offered no complete answer, because compelled identification carried its own deterrent weight for speakers who valued anonymity or feared reprisal. The two cases therefore divided over first premises that no evidentiary record could fully bridge. One side located the democratic injury in the influence that large sums secured before an election was held. The other side located the democratic injury in the speech that regulation suppressed before a vote was cast. The statute enacted in 2002 embodied the first premise in its operative titles, the courts sustained parts of that embodiment and struck others, and the second premise survived each defeat in Congress and each loss in court to be argued again in the next case, on the next provision, with the same force its holders brought to the consolidated challenges of 2003.

## Reading the McCain-Feingold Statute as a Whole

The procedural route of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act supplies the profile lesson in institutional design, because the statute reached the floor of the House of Representatives only after a majority of the House signed a discharge petition to free it from committee. The Committee on House Administration had ordered the Shays and Meehan bill reported unfavorably on June 28, 2001, while reporting the rival Ney and Wynn bill favorably, and the ordinary gatekeeping of the committee system pointed toward the outcome that had ended House passed versions in the Senate in earlier years and Senate efforts at cloture throughout the 1990s. The discharge petition gathered signatures through late 2001 and reached 218, a majority of the House, by late January 2002, only the second successful discharge petition since the 1980s. Leadership then scheduled the bill, debate ran nearly 17 hours across February 13 and 14, 2002, and passage followed by 240 votes to 189. A statute that became law through that route teaches a working lesson in committee gatekeeping that no account of its substance replaces. A committee majority can hold a bill against a chamber majority for a long period, and the chamber majority possesses, in the discharge mechanism, a remedy that succeeds rarely enough to mark the occasions when a cross party coalition wants a vote more than it fears the leadership displeasure that signing invites.

Read as a whole in the form that litigation and time produced, the statute that emerged from that route retains operative parts across each of its concerns. The soft money title operates on the enacted text of section 323. The disclaimer requirements of section 311 accompany election related advertising with sponsorship identification and the candidate approval statement. The raised and indexed base limits of section 307, which lifted the individual per candidate limit from 1,000 dollars to 2,000 dollars and applied indexing with 2001 as the base period, remained the foundation of the contribution structure even after the biennial aggregate limits fell in *McCutcheon v. FEC* in 2014, a judgment in which the base per recipient limits were not at issue. The disclosure system of section 201 stands affirmed in the 2010 judgment in *Citizens United*. Those provisions, enacted as amendments to the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, were later editorially recodified from title 2 to title 52 of the United States Code in 2014, a change of address rather than of substance that a reader tracing a section number across editions of the Code must follow to avoid concluding that a provision vanished when its citation changed. A reader comparing the 2002 statute against the 1971 act it amended gains the clearest view of that whole structure from [a side by side account of the two laws](/2022/01/01/feca-vs-bcra-campaign-law/), and a reader building a durable record of the profile profits from keeping the enacted text, the three judge court and Supreme Court judgments in *McConnell* and *Citizens United*, and the commission reporting forms in a single organized file, a task for which a structured study record through [the VaultBook legislation study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) suits the material well. The study path that follows the statute logic runs from the 1979 amendments and commission rulings that opened the soft money channel, through the text of sections 101 to 403, to the sequence of *McConnell*, *Wisconsin Right to Life*, *Davis v. FEC*, *Citizens United*, and *McCutcheon*, reading each judgment for the provisions sustained as carefully as for the provisions struck.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did McCain-Feingold ban?

The statute banned two flows of money. First, national party committees were prohibited from soliciting, receiving, directing, or spending any funds outside the contribution limits and source prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act, which ended party soft money, and federal candidates and officeholders were barred from soliciting such funds in connection with an election. Second, corporations, including incorporated nonprofit organizations, and labor organizations were prohibited from paying for electioneering communications from their general treasury funds, although their political action committees remained free to pay. The statute did not ban independent expenditures by individuals, spending by political action committees, or broadcast advertising outside the defined pre-election windows.

### Q: What is an electioneering communication under McCain-Feingold?

An electioneering communication is a broadcast, cable, or satellite communication that refers to a clearly identified federal candidate, is publicly distributed within sixty days before a general election or thirty days before a primary election, and is targeted to the relevant electorate. Targeting means the communication can be received by fifty thousand or more persons in the candidate's district, for a House candidate, or in the candidate's state, for a Senate candidate. The definition does not require the communication to urge a vote for or against anyone. Falling inside the definition triggered two consequences under the statute as enacted: the communication could not be paid for from corporate or union general treasury funds, and a spender whose disbursements passed ten thousand dollars in a calendar year had to file disclosure reports with the Federal Election Commission.

### Q: How did a discharge petition pass McCain-Feingold?

A discharge petition does not pass a bill; it breaks the gatekeeping that keeps a bill from the floor. In the House, a petition signed by two hundred eighteen members, an absolute majority, discharges a committee from further consideration of a measure or of the special rule for considering it. Supporters of the Shays-Meehan bill gathered signatures through two thousand one and reached two hundred eighteen in January two thousand two, only the second successful discharge effort since the nineteen eighties. Faced with a signed majority, the House leadership scheduled the bill, debate ran nearly seventeen hours across February thirteenth and fourteenth, and the House passed the bill on February 14, 2002, by two hundred forty votes to one hundred eighty-nine.

### Q: What is the stand by your ad rule in McCain-Feingold?

The rule is the candidate approval statement that section 311 of the statute wrote into the disclaimer law. A federal candidate's authorized advertising must state that the candidate approved the communication. In a television advertisement, the candidate must appear in a full-screen view or be heard in an unaccompanied voice-over at the close of the advertisement while the approval statement runs, and a written statement appears on screen. In a radio advertisement, the candidate's own voice identifies the candidate and states the approval. The provision sat inside a broader rewriting of sponsorship identification for election-related advertising, and its stated purpose in the debate was accountability: a candidate who had to claim an advertisement personally would answer for its content personally.

### Q: Did McCain-Feingold raise contribution limits?

Yes. The statute is remembered for its prohibitions, but section 307 raised the limits on regulated giving at the same time. The amount an individual could give a candidate rose from one thousand dollars to two thousand dollars per election. The annual limit on an individual's contributions to a national party committee rose from twenty thousand dollars to twenty-five thousand dollars. A new biennial aggregate capped an individual's total contributions at ninety-five thousand dollars, divided between candidates and other committees. The per-candidate limit and the aggregate were then indexed to inflation, using the consumer price index with two thousand one as the base period, so the two thousand dollar figure became two thousand one hundred dollars for the two thousand five to two thousand six cycle.

### Q: What was the Millionaire's Amendment in McCain-Feingold?

The Millionaire's Amendment was a mechanism for candidates running against opponents who financed campaigns from personal wealth. The House version, section 319, was triggered when a candidate's spending of personal funds passed a threshold of three hundred fifty thousand dollars. Once triggered, the opposing candidate could accept individual contributions at up to three times the ordinary limit and could benefit from increased coordinated spending by the party, while the self-financing candidate acquired escalating duties to notify the Federal Election Commission and the opponents as personal spending rose. A parallel Senate mechanism used a formula based on the voting-age population of the state. The Supreme Court struck the House provision in *Davis v. FEC* in two thousand eight, and the Commission treated the reasoning as disabling the Senate mechanism as well.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court uphold McCain-Feingold at first?

Yes, in the main. In *McConnell v. FEC*, decided on December 10, 2003, a five-justice majority sustained both core titles against facial challenge: the soft money restrictions were upheld as a closely drawn measure against circumvention of the contribution limits, and the electioneering communication provisions, including the treasury funds prohibition and the disclosure rules, were upheld as well. Justices Stevens and O'Connor jointly wrote the opinion on the two titles. The Court struck two peripheral provisions, the ban on campaign contributions by persons aged seventeen or younger, which fell unanimously, and the rule forcing parties to choose between coordinated and independent expenditures. It declined, for want of standing, to reach the raised contribution limits and the Millionaire's Amendment.

### Q: How long did McCain-Feingold take to pass?

Seven years. Senators John McCain and Russ Feingold opened the campaign in nineteen ninety-five, and President George W. Bush signed the statute on March 27, 2002. In between, the effort crossed four Congresses. The Senate defeated repeated attempts to end filibusters against the bill, including a cloture vote of fifty-one to forty-eight in nineteen ninety-eight. The House passed versions of the bill in nineteen ninety-eight and nineteen ninety-nine that died in the Senate. The Senate finally passed its version on April 2, 2001, and the House bill that became law passed on February 14, 2002, after supporters forced it to the floor with a discharge petition. Few modern statutes have required a comparably sustained campaign of reintroduction, amendment, and procedural maneuver across so many sessions.

### Q: What is the public law number of the McCain-Feingold Act?

The statute is Public Law 107-155. The number records that it was the one hundred fifty-fifth public law enacted by the one hundred seventh Congress. It is printed at 116 Stat. 81 in the Statutes at Large. The enacted vehicle was H.R. 2356 of the one hundred seventh Congress, the House bill sponsored by Representatives Christopher Shays and Martin Meehan, rather than the Senate companion, S. 27, which carried the names of Senators John McCain and Russ Feingold. Its formal short title is the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, and lawyers and the Federal Election Commission cite it as BCRA. The public law number, the Statutes at Large citation, and the bill number together identify the enactment exactly in any legal database.

### Q: When did McCain-Feingold take effect?

The statute's general effective date was November 6, 2002, the day after that year's general election. Congress wrote the delay into section 402 so that the rules governing an election cycle would not change in the middle of it, and so that party committees holding soft money raised under the old regime faced a fixed date after which those funds could no longer be spent on federal election activity. Two qualifications complete the picture. The changes to contribution limits made by sections 102 and 307 applied to contributions made on or after January 1, 2003, rather than to the closing weeks of two thousand two. And the machinery of Title IV, including severability and the expedited judicial review of section 403, took effect on enactment itself, which is why constitutional challenges were filed within days of the signing.

### Q: Who sponsored McCain-Feingold in the Senate and in the House?

In the Senate, the sponsors were John McCain, a Republican from Arizona, and Russ Feingold, a Democrat from Wisconsin. In the House, the sponsors were Christopher Shays, a Republican from Connecticut, and Martin Meehan, a Democrat from Massachusetts. Each chamber's pair joined a Republican to a Democrat, a structure the sponsors maintained deliberately across the seven years of the effort, through repeated reintroductions in both chambers. The Senate bill was S. 27 of the one hundred seventh Congress, and the House bill, ultimately the enacted vehicle, was H.R. 2356. The statute's two common names preserve the two pairs: McCain-Feingold names the Senate sponsors, and Shays-Meehan names the House sponsors whose version became law.

### Q: What is soft money, and why did Congress move against it?

Soft money was money raised by political parties outside the limits and source prohibitions of the Federal Election Campaign Act: unlimited contributions from individuals, and contributions from corporate and union treasuries that federal law barred from candidate elections, accepted because the money was ostensibly for party building rather than for electing federal candidates. The channel grew from an exemption the nineteen seventy-nine amendments created for state and local party grassroots activity, widened by Federal Election Commission allocation rulings. Party soft money rose from about forty-five million dollars in nineteen eighty-eight to about four hundred ninety-five million dollars in the two thousand cycle. Congress acted on the stated ground that the channel had become a route around the contribution limits: donors gave six-figure sums to parties, parties spent on advertising that affected federal races, and the limits on what a donor could give a candidate were defeated in practice.

### Q: What did Citizens United change about McCain-Feingold?

*Citizens United v. FEC*, decided on January 21, 2010, removed the statute's advertising restriction. The Court held, by five votes to four, that corporations and unions could not be barred from spending general treasury funds on independent political speech, struck the treasury funds prohibition of section 203, and overruled *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* and the part of *McConnell v. FEC* that had sustained section 203. Everything else in the statute stood outside the holding. The ban on direct corporate contributions to candidates was not at issue. The soft money prohibition was untouched. And the Court upheld the statute's disclaimer and disclosure requirements by eight votes to one, with Justice Thomas alone dissenting on that part of the case, so the identification machinery of the statute survived the decision that eliminated its best-known restriction.

### Q: Is McCain-Feingold in effect?

In large part, yes. The soft money title operates on its enacted text: national party committees remain barred from raising or spending funds outside federal limits, and the state party rules for federal election activity remain in place. The disclaimer and stand by your ad requirements govern candidate and independent advertising. The contribution limits the statute raised operate in their indexed form, although the biennial aggregate ceiling was struck by *McCutcheon v. FEC* in two thousand fourteen. What no longer operates is the second title's funding prohibition, struck in *Citizens United* in two thousand ten after being narrowed in two thousand seven, and the Millionaire's Amendment, struck in *Davis v. FEC* in two thousand eight. The surviving provisions sit in title 52 of the United States Code as part of the Federal Election Campaign Act that the statute amended.

### Q: Where did campaign money go after the soft money ban?

The money moved rather than disappeared. The parties themselves adapted first: in the two thousand four cycle, the six national party committees raised about one point two one billion dollars in regulated hard money, more than the roughly one point zero seven billion dollars of hard and soft money combined that they had raised in two thousand. Alongside the parties, organizations formed under section 527 of the tax code became a prominent channel for large donations in two thousand four, accepting funds outside the party structure. After the advertising restriction fell in two thousand ten, the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit held in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* that contribution limits could not be applied to groups making only independent expenditures, and spending flowed to the independent-expenditure-only committees known as super PACs. Each channel sits outside the national party committees that the statute closed to soft money.

### Q: What are Levin funds under McCain-Feingold?

Levin funds are the limited allowance that Senator Carl Levin's amendment wrote into the state party provisions of section 323. A state, district, or local party committee must generally pay for federal election activity with money raised under federal limits. The Levin allowance permits the committee to pay for specified categories of that activity, voter registration, voter identification, get-out-the-vote activity, and generic campaign activity, partly with funds raised under state law instead, provided no single source gives more than ten thousand dollars in a year for that purpose and the funds are allocated under Federal Election Commission rules. The allowance does not extend to public communications that refer to a clearly identified federal candidate. The design gave state parties a bounded way to use state-raised money for grassroots work while the national soft money channel was closed entirely.

### Q: How did the votes on McCain-Feingold split in the House and the Senate?

The House passed the bill on February 14, 2002, by two hundred forty votes to one hundred eighty-nine. Democrats supported it by one hundred ninety-eight to twelve, Republicans opposed it by one hundred seventy-six to forty-one, and the independent members divided one to one, so passage rested on a Democratic majority joined by a Republican minority voting against the position of the House Republican leadership. The Senate passed the House bill on March 20, 2002, by sixty votes to forty, taking the measure up directly rather than sending it to a conference committee. The Senate had passed its own companion the previous spring, on April 2, 2001, by fifty-nine votes to forty-one, after two weeks of debate in which twenty-two amendments were adopted.

### Q: Why is the statute called both McCain-Feingold and Shays-Meehan?

Because it had a sponsoring pair in each chamber, and congressional naming practice attaches a bill to the members who carried it. Senators John McCain and Russ Feingold led the effort in the Senate from nineteen ninety-five onward, and their names attached to the enterprise in public discussion years before enactment. Representatives Christopher Shays and Martin Meehan carried the companion bill in the House, where the decisive procedural fight occurred, and their names attach to the House version. The version that became law was the House bill, H.R. 2356, so Shays-Meehan is the technically precise label for the enacted text, while McCain-Feingold remains the more common name because the Senate pair originated and personified the campaign. The formal short title, the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, belongs to neither pair and is the name used in legal citation.

### Q: How did McCain-Feingold arrange for fast constitutional review?

Section 403 of the statute built the courthouse route into the law itself. Any action seeking declaratory or injunctive relief against the constitutionality of a provision had to be filed in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia and heard by a court of three judges. The courts were directed to expedite the action in every way, and an appeal from the three-judge court ran directly to the Supreme Court, skipping the court of appeals entirely. Challenges were filed within days of the signing on March 27, 2002, led by Senator Mitch McConnell among more than eighty plaintiffs across the consolidated cases. The three-judge court ruled in May two thousand three, the Supreme Court heard four hours of argument at a special sitting that September, and the decision in *McConnell v. FEC* issued on December 10, 2003, about twenty months after enactment and before the first presidential election held under the statute.

### Q: What should a student remember about McCain-Feingold for an exam?

Five points carry the statute. First, its identity: the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, Public Law 107-155, signed on March 27, 2002, and effective on November 6, 2002. Second, its two core provisions: a ban on national party soft money, which survives, and a bar on corporate and union treasury funding of electioneering communications, which does not. Third, its procedure: the bill reached the House floor only after supporters gathered two hundred eighteen signatures on a discharge petition, a device that succeeds very rarely. Fourth, its judicial arc in order: sustained in *McConnell v. FEC* in two thousand three, narrowed in *FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life* in two thousand seven, its Millionaire's Amendment struck in *Davis v. FEC* in two thousand eight, and its advertising restriction struck in *Citizens United* in two thousand ten. Fifth, the point most often missed: the *Citizens United* decision upheld the statute's disclosure and disclaimer requirements by eight votes to one.
