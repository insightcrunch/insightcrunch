This page tracks every layoff round at The Boeing Company from 2022 onward in one place, and it is updated each time the company runs another one. It records what each round cut, where, and under what stated reasoning, alongside the standing machinery that governs how Boeing separations work: how notice is given under the WARN Act and the company's union contracts, how severance is calculated for represented and non-represented staff, what happens to health coverage, and how recall rights operate when production rises again. Event coverage ages quickly; the record below is built to stay accurate as the history grows.

The cumulative picture is five tracked rounds in five years. The largest, announced in October 2024, was a company-wide programme to reduce the workforce by about 10 percent — roughly 17,000 positions, on the company's own figure — executed in waves of WARN notices through the following year. The newest, announced on November 9, 2026, cuts about 5 percent of the Commercial Airplanes division, an estimated 2,500 roles against the division's published headcount of 50,494, because the production increases those employees were hired to support have slipped into 2027. Between those two bookends sit a small space-programme reduction in February 2025 that the company largely mitigated before it landed, and two corporate-function rounds in 2022 and 2023 that removed about 2,150 finance and human resources positions between them, roughly a third of that work moving to an outside contractor in India. Summed across the five rounds, the tracked total is roughly 21,800 announced reductions, and the composition of that number matters as much as its size: Boeing sometimes publishes its own figure, sometimes only a percentage, and sometimes only a statutory filing, so this tracker labels every figure with the strength of its source, from WARN filings and company statements at the top to arithmetic estimates by news outlets below them.

A note on how this page is kept. The ledger begins at 1 January 2022 by design: Boeing's earlier reductions, above all the pandemic programme of 2020 that shed roughly 30,000 jobs, are part of the company's history and are summarised in the snapshot, but they predate the documentary standard the ledger applies. A round enters the ledger only when the company announces it, when a WARN or state mini-WARN filing establishes it, or when two independent outlets report the same reduction of at least 100 roles with a consistent scope. Rounds that are known but cannot be verified to that standard are named in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified rather than silently dropped, and they are promoted into the ledger if the evidence arrives. When a new round qualifies, it is added at the top of the ledger, the cumulative table is recomputed, and the date at the head of this page is updated; nothing already in the ledger is removed, and a corrected figure is amended in place with the correction visible in the round's own section.

## Company Snapshot

Boeing is one of the two largest manufacturers of commercial aircraft in the world and one of the largest defence contractors in the United States, founded in 1916 in Seattle and headquartered since 2022 in Arlington, Virginia. Its business runs in three divisions. Boeing Commercial Airplanes builds the 737, 767, 777 and 787 families, principally in Washington state and South Carolina, and is the division whose production rates drive most of the company's employment. Boeing Defense, Space & Security builds military aircraft, satellites and the Space Launch System core stage for NASA, with major sites in St. Louis, Huntsville and New Orleans. Boeing Global Services sells maintenance, training, parts and digital services across both fleets. Around those divisions sits a large enterprise layer — engineering, fabrication, finance, human resources and information technology — and it is the interaction between the divisions' production plans and that enterprise layer that produces most of the rounds in this ledger: when a production rate moves, staffing built for the old rate becomes surplus, and when corporate functions grow faster than the factories they support, they become targets in their own right.

The workforce trajectory frames everything that follows. Boeing reported approximately 142,000 employees at the end of 2021, still close to its pandemic trough. It then hired at extraordinary speed: approximately 156,000 at the end of 2022, approximately 171,000 at the end of 2023, approximately 172,000 at the end of 2024 and approximately 182,000 at the end of 2025, with about 14 percent of the workforce outside the United States and roughly 72,000 union members — about 40 percent of all employees — at the end of 2025, according to the company's annual filings. Commercial Airplanes employed 47,948 people at the end of 2023, 50,640 at the end of 2024 and 50,494 at the end of 2025. The single most important fact about Boeing's layoff record in this period is that it runs against that rising line. Every round in the ledger was executed by a company that was, in net terms, growing — hiring factory and engineering staff for production increases while removing staff whose work was tied to rates, programmes or functions that were shrinking. Net growth is not evidence that the rounds were small. It is evidence that Boeing's reductions in this period were reallocations as much as contractions, and the ledger therefore counts roles eliminated in identified rounds, never the net movement of the total, which also absorbs hiring, attrition, acquisitions and divestitures and can be derived from neither.

The structural pressure behind the two largest rounds is the production-rate problem that has governed Boeing since 2019. Two fatal crashes of the 737 MAX in 2018 and 2019 killed 346 people and grounded the type worldwide; production was suspended and then restarted at low rates under regulatory constraint. In January 2024, a door plug blew out of an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 in flight, and the Federal Aviation Administration capped 737 production at 38 aircraft a month while it examined Boeing's quality system. The company spent 2024 and 2025 working back upward under supervision, reached the low forties per month in 2026, and planned to stabilise at 47 a month — until, in October 2026, chief executive Kelly Ortberg told investors that the increase would take longer than expected, that the 47 rate would not be reached until early 2027, that the step to 52 a month had moved to the second half of 2027, and that the company would miss its 2026 delivery target for the 737. The 787 programme carries a smaller version of the same story, holding at about eight aircraft a month against a planned ten, with wing production named in reporting as a constraint. A workforce hired and trained for 47 aircraft a month is, on a plan that now delivers that rate a year later, a workforce the plan cannot carry — which is the November 2026 round stated in one sentence, and the reason this tracker treats production-rate announcements as the leading indicator of Boeing layoff rounds.

The financial position converts that operational problem into a staffing one. Boeing lost money in every year from 2019 onward on the measures its filings emphasise, burned $1.3 billion of cash in the third quarter of 2024 alone, and carried the costs of the 53-day machinists' strike of September to November 2024, the regulatory production caps, and a heavy debt load accumulated through the crisis years. The October 2024 programme was announced in the same breath as those results and framed by Ortberg as a financial reset: staffing aligned to financial reality, priorities reset, a leaner and more focused organisation. By the third quarter of 2026 the commercial division was still loss-making — an operating loss of $697 million on revenue of about $10.7 billion in the quarter — even as the defence and services divisions were profitable, and the company's shares had fallen about 25 percent during 2026. Against that sits the paradox every account of the November 2026 round has to hold: demand is not the problem. Boeing's total backlog reached $745 billion in the third quarter of 2026, and the commercial backlog alone stood at a record $612 billion covering more than 6,300 aircraft, with 300 net orders booked in that quarter alone. Boeing is cutting staff while holding more sold work than at any point in its history, because the constraint is its own factories' ability to build, not its customers' willingness to buy. That inversion — layoffs caused by too little output rather than too little demand — is what distinguishes Boeing's rounds in this ledger from the demand-driven reductions more common elsewhere in the industry.

Leadership forms part of the standing context because Boeing's rounds in this period are associated with a specific management succession. Kelly Ortberg, an aerospace executive recruited from retirement, became president and chief executive in August 2024, in the middle of the production and labour crises, and the October programme was his signature early act — announced ten weeks into his tenure, in the same message as the programme deferrals and the preliminary losses. Stephanie Pope, who had run the services division and then the commercial division's operations before becoming its president and chief executive, issued the November 2026 memo from the division she had led through the rate recovery. The two announcements share a rhetorical signature worth recording: both state the operational fact first (a rate, a financial reality), derive the staffing consequence in a single step, and commit to support measures in the same document. Neither was accompanied by a published headcount beyond the percentage given. A tracker learns to read this company's announcements accordingly: the percentage is the announcement; the headcount, where one exists, is assembled afterwards from division totals, filings and union briefings, and its assembly is documented in each round section rather than smoothed over.

The services division is the snapshot's last structural element, because it explains part of the company's resilience and part of its staffing logic. Boeing Global Services sells the aftermarket — maintenance, spare parts, training, digital tools — across both the commercial and military fleets, and its revenue follows the installed base of aircraft already flying rather than the rate at which new ones are built. It was profitable in the third quarter of 2026, as was defence, while the commercial division lost money, and it sits outside the November 2026 round entirely. A company whose aftermarket grows while its factories slow will predictably protect the aftermarket's staffing and take its corrections in the production divisions, which is the distribution the current round shows. The division also carries the company's largest population of employees whose work is tied to other operators' fleets — airlines, lessors and air forces — and its historical layoff exposure has been correspondingly lower and steadier than the manufacturing divisions'. Where future rounds touch services, that fact will itself be the story, and the snapshot records the baseline now so that such a change is visible when it comes.

The union landscape is the second structural fact, and it has no equivalent at most companies in this programme's scope. Roughly two in five Boeing employees are union members. The International Association of Machinists District 751 represents more than 30,000 production workers in the Puget Sound region under an agreement running to September 2028, ratified in November 2024 after the 53-day strike, and its members build the 737, 767 and 777. The Society of Professional Engineering Employees in Aerospace represents roughly 17,000 engineers and technicians, the population most exposed in both the 2024 programme's first wave and the 2026 round's December notices, under a contract ratified in October 2026 carrying a guaranteed 10 percent increase on ratification and annual raises of 4 percent. Other machinists' districts cover St. Louis and other defence sites. Union representation changes the mechanics of a Boeing layoff in ways the standing section sets out: layoff benefits for represented staff are written into contracts and published by the unions themselves, seniority and recall rights govern who goes and who can return, and the unions act as an independent documentary source — it was SPEEA's account of its November 2026 meeting with Boeing managers, reported by Reuters, that established the December 9 notice date and the absence of any voluntary programme in the newest round. A tracker of Boeing layoffs therefore reads three records at once: the company's announcements, the state's WARN filings, and the unions' communications, and the three do not always arrive in that order.

Geography concentrates the record the same way it concentrates the company. Washington state is Boeing's centre of gravity — about 66,000 employees before the November 2024 notices, and more than 65,000 at the end of 2025 — spread across the 737 plant at Renton, the widebody plant at Everett, the fabrication division at Auburn and engineering centres throughout Puget Sound. South Carolina builds the 787 at North Charleston. Defence work anchors St. Louis, Huntsville and the Michoud Assembly Facility in New Orleans, where the Space Launch System core stage is built. Wichita, Kansas, rejoined the company's perimeter when the Spirit AeroSystems acquisition closed in December 2025, returning the fuselage supplier's workforce of roughly 13,000 in Kansas to Boeing ownership after two decades outside it — one reason the reported total workforce rose by about 10,000 during 2025 even as the 2024 programme's later waves were still executing. India is the largest offshore engineering concentration, centred on the Boeing India Engineering and Technology Center in Bengaluru, which appears in this ledger twice in opposite roles: as the destination for finance and human-resources process work outsourced to Tata Consultancy Services in 2022 and 2023, and as a site that itself took about 180 cuts in March 2025 as part of the 2024 programme. The documentary consequence mirrors the Oracle pattern in this programme's pilot tracker: United States rounds generate WARN filings with sites, counts and effective dates, while offshore movements reach the record through company statements and press reporting of a lower evidentiary grade, and the sourcing labels in the ledger are calibrated to that difference.

Boeing has reduced its workforce many times before 2022, and those episodes belong to the snapshot rather than the ledger. The largest was the pandemic programme: in April 2020, with air travel collapsing, Boeing announced a reduction of about 10 percent of its workforce, and across 2020 and 2021 the company shed roughly 30,000 jobs through voluntary layoff offers, involuntary layoffs and attrition, taking the total from 161,100 at the end of 2019 to about 141,000 at the end of 2020. The 737 MAX grounding had already slowed hiring and production before the pandemic arrived, and the two crises compounded. Earlier still, the company ran repeated information-technology reductions — about 1,500 positions in the Puget Sound region from 2013, and about 600 more eliminated when IT work was outsourced to Dell in 2021 — establishing the outsourcing mechanism that the finance function would follow in 2022 and 2023. The institutional pattern these episodes formed is the one the ledger keeps meeting: Boeing staffs aggressively for production rates and programmes it expects, reduces through a mixture of voluntary offers, attrition and involuntary notices when the expectation moves, and rehires when rates recover — a cycle its workforce and its unions plan around, and one whose costs, in lost experience and retraining, the company's own quality reviews have since examined. The tracker records the cycle because it explains the design of the machinery in the standing section, in particular the recall rights that exist precisely because Boeing so often needs the same skills back.

The quality and regulatory regime is the layer beneath the production problem, and it belongs in the snapshot because it sets the speed limit every staffing plan runs into. After the January 2024 door-plug accident — in which a fuselage panel separated from an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 in flight, with no fatalities — the Federal Aviation Administration capped 737 output at 38 aircraft a month, increased its on-site presence in Boeing's factories, and required the company to build and execute a comprehensive corrective action plan before any rate increase would be approved. Each subsequent increase, including the move through the low forties during 2026, was granted step by step against demonstrated stability rather than against the company's schedule preference. The Department of Justice proceedings arising from the 2018 and 2019 crashes, the company's 2024 agreement to plead guilty to a fraud charge and the subsequent judicial scrutiny of that agreement, and a $243 million fraud settlement figure carried in the period's reporting, all belong to the same ledger of institutional consequence. For this tracker's purposes the point is narrower: Boeing's production rates are no longer set by Boeing alone. A workforce plan built on an assumed rate is a plan built on a regulatory decision, and the November 2026 round is what happens when the decision arrives later than the plan assumed. The company's staffing therefore carries a regulatory risk that its competitors, building under ordinary oversight, do not carry in the same form — and its layoff rounds will continue to be timed by approvals as much as by orders until that changes.

The balance sheet completes the frame. Boeing entered the tracked period carrying the debt accumulated through the grounding, the pandemic and the production crises, and its 2024 results included charges across its programmes measured in the billions alongside the $1.3 billion of cash consumed in the third quarter alone. In late October 2024, days after announcing the reduction programme, the company raised approximately $21 billion in one of the largest equity offerings ever completed by a company in its position — a recapitalisation that repaired the balance sheet at the price of substantial dilution, and that investors accepted on the strength of the same backlog that makes the layoff rounds look paradoxical. The sequence matters for reading the ledger: the 2024 programme was not a company running out of money in the simple sense, because a company that can raise $21 billion of equity in a week is not illiquid; it was a company whose cost structure, at capped production rates, could not cover its obligations at the staffing it carried, and which chose to repair that structure from both ends at once — capital in through the offering, cost out through the programme. The 2026 round is the same logic at division scale, without the crisis: the commercial division's $697 million third-quarter operating loss is the gap the 5 percent is meant to narrow while the rates recover.

The defence and space division deserves its own paragraph in the snapshot because its exposure runs on a different clock. Its work is programme-funded: a position exists because a contract funds it, and it ends when the contract is revised, descoped or completed. The Space Launch System round of February 2025 is the ledger's example — a reduction sized not by the division's overall health, which was sound, but by one programme's political and budgetary risk. The division's portfolio carries several such exposures at any time: development programmes approaching production decisions, production programmes approaching their final deliveries, and space programmes whose budgets are set annually by a legislature. Reductions in this division therefore tend to arrive as the SLS round did — programme by programme, sized in the hundreds, documented by the WARN filings of the affected sites — rather than as division-wide percentages, and a reader of this tracker should expect future defence entries to follow that shape. The reported-but-unverified defence item recorded below, an unconfirmed report of about 300 roles in February 2026, fits the same shape; it failed the evidence standard, not the plausibility test, and it will be promoted if filings or corroborated reporting establish it.

Three reported items are recorded here as reported-but-unverified, because they are known to this tracker but do not meet its evidence standard for a ledger entry. First, in February 2026, reporting attributed to a single anonymous source described a planned reduction of about 300 roles in Boeing Defense, Space & Security; Boeing responded to that reporting without confirming any figure, and no WARN filing or second outlet has established a reduction of 100 or more roles with a consistent scope, so it is not carried in the ledger. Second, Boeing filed five small WARN notices in California in April 2026 covering 19 workers in total, recorded as site closures effective June 12, 2026; the filings are genuine documents, but at 19 roles they sit far below this tracker's 100-role threshold and are treated as routine site-level filings rather than a round. Third, during the machinists' strike of September to November 2024, Boeing placed non-union staff on rolling temporary furloughs; furloughs are suspensions of work, not separations, and are excluded from the ledger by definition — the furloughs were suspended once the October 2024 programme was announced — and the same exclusion applies to the workforce actions taken in the same period by Spirit AeroSystems, which was then a separate company and did not become part of Boeing until December 2025. Each of these items will move into the ledger if filings or corroborated reporting establish it as a qualifying round, and the distinction between the two treatments — documented and reported — is the tracker's core discipline.

One further boundary belongs in the snapshot because it will otherwise be misread in the ledger. Two of the five rounds — February 2023 and November 2026 — were announced by the company as combined figures covering both involuntary layoffs and attrition, and Boeing did not publish the split in either case; the October 2024 programme likewise combined layoffs with attrition, reduced hiring and subsidiary sales. Where that happens, this tracker records the announced figure as announced, labels it as a combined figure in the table and in the round's own section, and never restates it as a count of layoffs alone. The WARN-documented portions of those rounds — the involuntary, site-level separations that statutory filings capture — are stated separately inside each round section, so a reader who wants only the documented layoff count can find it, and a reader who wants the company's announced scale can find that too, and the two are never added together or substituted for one another. That discipline is the Boeing version of the rule this programme applies everywhere: the figure is only ever as strong as its source, and the label travels with the number wherever the number is used.

Read as a sequence, the five rounds describe a company whose reductions follow its production plan the way a shadow follows an object. The 2022 and 2023 rounds were the corporate centre being repriced — finance and human resources work simplified, standardised and in part moved to a contractor — executed while the factories were hiring as fast as they could. The 2024 programme was the crisis round: a tenth of the company, announced in the middle of a strike, against losses and a regulatory cap on its best-selling product. The 2025 space reduction was the smallest and strangest, a warning of 400 positions that negotiation with the customer reduced to fewer than 200 before the notices landed. And the 2026 round is the purest rate round of the five: no strike, no crash, no collapsing demand — a record order book, a slower factory, and a division staffed for a plan that moved a year to the right. The documentary quality of the ledger varies across the sequence in a pattern worth naming. The rounds with filings (2024, and 2025 in its WARN portion) are known to the site and the effective date. The rounds known by announcement (2022, 2023) are known by their rationale and their combined totals but not by their layoff-versus-attrition split. And the newest round is known, so far, by a percentage and a division headcount published for a different purpose, from which every outlet's figure is arithmetic. That gradient is not a defect of this tracker. It is a property of how Boeing discloses, and the table's labels exist to keep the gradient visible rather than average it away.

**Reconciling the table with the filings.** Readers comparing this table with Boeing's reported headcounts will notice that the two move in opposite directions for most of the period, and the reconciliation is worth stating once, here, rather than leaving it to inference. The company's reported workforce rose from about 142,000 at the end of 2021 to about 182,000 at the end of 2025 — a net increase of 40,000 across the very years in which this ledger records about 21,800 announced reductions. Both numbers are true, because they measure different things. The ledger counts roles eliminated in identified rounds. The filings count the net of everything: rounds, yes, but also the largest hiring campaign in the company's recent history — about 15,000 added in 2022 and 10,000 planned for 2023 alone, concentrated in engineering and manufacturing — plus ordinary attrition and rehiring, plus the Spirit AeroSystems acquisition, which returned roughly 13,000 Kansas employees to the company's perimeter in December 2025, minus divestitures such as the 2025 sales of the digital aviation services businesses, whose staff transferred to the buyer rather than leaving employment. A year can therefore contain a 17,000-position reduction programme and still end with a higher reported workforce than it began, as 2025 did. Where the two records appear to conflict, the filings are not evidence against the announced figures; they are evidence that hiring and acquisition ran ahead of the cuts, which the company's own statements about growing engineering and manufacturing headcount throughout the period independently confirm. Both measures are carried in this tracker, each for what it proves, and neither is ever substituted for the other.

One comparison frames the whole table, because Boeing does not build aircraft alone in the world. Its duopoly counterpart, Airbus, spent the same period hiring against a production ramp it was executing more smoothly, and its own workforce actions of these years were concentrated in its defence and space division rather than in commercial production. The contrast is instructive for reading Boeing's ledger: nothing in the structure of the aircraft business required the commercial division of the American manufacturer to cut in 2024 and again in 2026 while holding a record backlog. The requirement came from the company's specific position — the grounding's aftermath, the regulatory cap, the quality system's recovery, the debt — and the rounds in this table are the workforce trace of that specific position rather than of the industry's cycle. A tracker organised by company, as this programme is, exists precisely to keep that distinction: the industry context explains the weather, but the ledger records what one company did in it, under its own constraints, by its own stated reasoning.

The rounds also interact, and the interactions are part of the record. The October 2024 programme was still executing when the SLS warning landed in February 2025 — the Huntsville site filed WARN notices under both actions within three months, 158 workers in November 2024 under the programme and 71 in February 2025 under the space reduction — and a reader working from the filings alone could mistake the second for a late wave of the first. They are separate actions with separate causes, and the ledger keeps them separate for that reason: the programme was a company-wide financial reset, the SLS action a single programme's contract risk, and the same site can appear in both without either entry absorbing the other. The November 2026 round interacts with the 2024 programme differently: it is the first Commercial Airplanes reduction since the programme, it falls on a division the programme's later waves also touched, and it arrives under the production plan the programme was supposed to make affordable. Where a site's or a population's history spans rounds, the round sections cross-refer rather than repeat, and the table's rows remain what they are — five announcements, five evidence bases, one cumulative total.

Two further figures frame the table. The first is the WARN floor: aggregators of state filings count 62 Boeing WARN notices filed between November 2024 and April 2026 naming 3,823 workers in the states that publish counts — the documented, site-level, involuntary core of the 2024 programme and the small filings that followed it, and a floor under that programme's United States layoff count rather than a measure of the programme itself, most of which executed through attrition, unfilled roles, international sites and transfers that no WARN filing captures. The second is the severance charge: Boeing's annual report booked $295 million of severance in connection with the 2024 programme, as reported from the filing — the closest thing to an official price tag the programme carries, and a figure that belongs to the whole programme rather than to any single wave of it. ## November 2026: The Commercial Airplanes Rate Reduction

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Monday, November 9, 2026, Stephanie Pope, president and chief executive of Boeing Commercial Airplanes, told the division's employees by memo that the division would reduce its staffing by approximately 5 percent through a combination of attrition and involuntary layoffs, with notifications beginning in December. The first notice date is December 9, 2026, established not by the company directly but by the Society of Professional Engineering Employees in Aerospace, whose officials were given the date by Boeing senior engineering managers at a meeting on Thursday, November 19, and reported it to Reuters. Separations follow the notice period described in the standing section, so the round's employment effect lands in early 2027 even though its announcement, and this ledger entry, belong to 2026.

**Roles cut.** Boeing has published no headcount for this round, and every number attached to it is arithmetic. The division employed 50,494 people at the end of December 2025, the most recent division headcount the company has published; 5 percent of that base is approximately 2,525, and the figure carried in this tracker — about 2,500 — is the estimate that Reuters, CNBC, Bloomberg and other outlets derived from it. It is labelled an estimate in the cumulative table and throughout this section, and it will be amended in place if WARN filings or a company figure establish the round's actual size. Two further qualifications travel with the number. The 50,494 base was eleven months old when the memo was written, so 5 percent of the division as it stood in November 2026 could be somewhat higher or lower. And the 5 percent is a combined figure covering involuntary layoffs and attrition together; Boeing has not said how many of the roughly 2,500 reductions will be layoffs and how many will be positions left unfilled as people leave, and this tracker does not guess the split. Roles left unfilled are recorded here as part of the announced combined figure, stated as such, and are never added to any layoff count.

**Share of workforce.** The reduction is approximately 5 percent of the Commercial Airplanes division by construction. Against the company's total workforce of approximately 182,000 at the end of 2025, the estimated 2,500 roles represent about 1.4 percent — a division-scale action, not a company-wide one, and about half the proportional size of the October 2024 programme within its much narrower perimeter.

**Divisions and geographies.** The round is confined to Boeing Commercial Airplanes; the defence and services divisions, both profitable in the third quarter of 2026, are not part of it. Within the division, Boeing managers told SPEEA that employment at all Commercial Airplanes sites is under review, but that layoffs will not necessarily impact all sites, and that the cuts will be targeted — focused on which work statements are ending or can be ended — rather than across-the-board. Reporting identified the Renton, Washington 737 operation and the division's Puget Sound engineering base as the centre of exposure, and the North Charleston, South Carolina 787 operation as within the slowed programmes, but Boeing has published no site distribution, and none is asserted here. Staff in Wichita, Kansas, and Tulsa, Oklahoma were told they are not being considered for layoffs, voluntary separation or early retirement, according to trade-press summaries of the company's internal communications. The two unions frame the open questions: IAM District 751, whose machinists build the aircraft, said it had been notified but did not know how many of its members would be affected; SPEEA, whose engineers and technicians received the largest share of notices in the 2024 programme's first wave, put its unanswered questions — how many members, which roles, and whether December 9 would be the only notice date — on the record through Reuters.

**The company's stated reason.** Pope's memo attributed the reduction to the production reset, in the sentence every account of the round has carried: adjusting production schedules and reducing aircraft output, she wrote, puts the division "in an overstaffed position," and staffing would be aligned to the revised business plan. The reset behind the sentence is specific. Boeing began raising 737 production from 42 aircraft a month in the second quarter of 2026 with regulatory approval, expecting to stabilise at 47 a month by the summer; on the third-quarter earnings call on October 27, 2026, chief executive Kelly Ortberg moved that target to early 2027, pushed the subsequent step to 52 a month into the second half of 2027, and confirmed the company would miss its 2026 delivery goal of 500 737s. The 787 line, at about eight aircraft a month against a plan of ten, with wing production reported as a constraint, forms the second half of the same picture. The division's third-quarter result — a $697 million operating loss on revenue of about $10.7 billion, with 161 aircraft delivered — is the financial form of the same fact. Boeing's position, stated by a spokesperson to Reuters on November 20, is that the company continues "to work through an established process to ensure our workforce can support our customer commitments," and is committed to treating everyone with dignity and respect and communicating details to impacted employees once decisions are finalised. The tracker records the framing as the company gives it: a staffing correction caused by output, announced against a record $612 billion commercial backlog of more than 6,300 aircraft — a reduction whose cause is the factory, not the order book.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filing covers this round yet; the filings will follow the December notices, and the tracker's practice is to record them in this section as they appear, with jurisdiction, notice date and site. The December 9 notice date sits more than 60 days after the November 9 announcement, and Boeing's established practice in the 2024 programme was to keep notified employees on the payroll through the statutory period, so the round's structure is consistent with the federal WARN Act's 60-day requirement and with the state mini-WARN laws that apply in several of the division's locations. Whether the notices, when they land, are concentrated enough at any single site to trigger site-specific thresholds is a question the filings themselves will answer.

**Severance and notice terms.** The memo, as reported, commits the company to company-sponsored outplacement services, layoff benefits and support through the Employee Assistance Program for affected employees. No round-specific severance formula has been published; the standing terms in the section below — the reported one-week-per-year standard for non-represented staff, and the contract tables for SPEEA and IAM members — are the terms of record until the December notices document otherwise. Two features of this round's design are established and belong here. There will be no voluntary layoff programme and no buy-outs: Boeing managers told SPEEA so directly, a departure from the company's pandemic-era practice of opening voluntary offers first. And the company left the round's scope formally open: asked whether December 9 would be the only notice date, managers told the union Boeing did not know. That open scope is recorded as a fact about this round; it is not treated as evidence of a further round, and no unannounced round is entered in this ledger on the strength of it.

**Sources.** This section rests on the November 9, 2026 memo from Stephanie Pope as published by Business Insider and reported by Reuters, CNBC and Bloomberg on November 9–10, 2026; Reuters' November 20, 2026 report of the SPEEA meeting and the company's response; Boeing's published division headcount of 50,494 at December 31, 2025; the company's third-quarter 2026 results and October 27, 2026 earnings-call statements as reported by Reuters, TheStreet and trade press; and WARN aggregator records confirming no filing for this round as of November 21, 2026.

**The paradox at the centre of the round.** Every previous round in this ledger can be explained by something shrinking: a function's workload, a programme's budget, a company's cash. This round is explained by something growing more slowly than planned. Boeing holds more than 6,300 commercial aircraft on order, booked 300 net orders in the third quarter of 2026 alone — including the largest 737 MAX order any leasing company has placed — and expects its customers to wait years for delivery slots. The employees being released were, in large part, hired or retained to build aircraft faster: the division's headcount rose from 47,948 at the end of 2023 to above 50,000 as the company staffed for rates of 47 and then 52 aircraft a month. The reduction therefore measures the distance between two plans — the ramp Boeing announced and staffed for, and the ramp its factories and supply chain can actually sustain — rather than any failure of demand for its product. That is also why the round's deepest question, which the December notices will begin to answer, is compositional: a reduction taken from overhead and ending work statements leaves the division able to accelerate when the factories stabilise, while a reduction taken from experienced production and engineering staff consumes the very capacity the delayed ramp will need in 2027. SPEEA's published frustration — that Boeing's own managers, ten days after the announcement, had not determined how many union members would be affected or which roles would be cut — is part of the record because it bears on that question: at the time the union spoke, the targeting the company described had not yet been resolved into names.

**The rate economics underneath the memo.** A production rate is a staffing equation before it is a headline. Each additional 737 per month carries with it a train of mechanics, inspectors, engineers, planners, parts handlers and support staff across Renton and the supply chain behind it, and Boeing staffed through 2025 and into 2026 for a line moving from 42 aircraft a month toward 47 and then 52. Holding at the low forties instead — with the 47 rate now expected in early 2027 and 52 not until the second half of that year — leaves the division carrying roughly the staffing of the higher plan against the output of the lower one for a period measured in years, not weeks. The 5 percent is the company's chosen correction to that gap, and its size relative to the gap is a judgement the filings will eventually test: the division delivered 161 aircraft in the third quarter of 2026 and missed the year's 737 delivery goal of 500, while its wage costs had just been reset upward by the two labour agreements described below. A smaller correction would have preserved more ramp capacity at a higher cash cost; a larger one would have repeated, in miniature, the error the industry remembers from 2020, when Boeing shed roughly 30,000 jobs into a downturn and then spent years rehiring and retraining for the recovery. The tracker records the correction's size and stated basis; the judgement embedded in it belongs to the company's history now, and the delivery and rate figures of 2027 are where it will be assessed.

**The supplier dimension.** One structural change separates this round from its 2024 predecessor: the fuselage supplier is now inside the company. Spirit AeroSystems, which builds the 737 fuselage in Wichita, spent the 2024 strike as an independent company furloughing its own workers in response to Boeing's shutdown, and its actions then were not Boeing's. The acquisition's closure in December 2025 brought roughly 13,000 Kansas employees into Boeing, which is why the company's reported workforce rose in a year of reductions — and it means the production system's largest historical shock-absorber, an external supplier whose workforce flexed with Boeing's rates at arm's length, is now part of the same employment perimeter the rounds are counted in. The November 2026 communications reflect the new geometry: Wichita and Tulsa staff were told they are not being considered for this round, a reassurance that would have been another company's to give two years earlier. Future rate-driven rounds will distribute differently for the same reason — the boundary of "Boeing layoffs" itself moved in December 2025, and this tracker applies the boundary as it stood at each round's date.

**The contract dimension.** The timing of the round against the division's two labour agreements is part of its documented context. SPEEA's members ratified a new four-year contract in early October 2026, weeks before the memo, carrying a guaranteed 10 percent wage increase on ratification and annual raises of 4 percent with a possible further 2 percent tied to performance. Boeing managers told the union the cuts were not retaliation for that contract, and the denial is recorded here because the union placed the question on the record. The machinists' agreement, ratified in November 2024 after the strike and running to September 2028, frames the other side of the division's labour cost. Neither agreement prevents the round — layoff provisions are a standing part of both contracts, and the standing section describes the benefit and recall machinery they contain — but together they mean the division is reducing headcount in the same season it has raised the price of the headcount that remains, which is the arithmetic of a company paying more per aircraft worker while planning to build aircraft more slowly. The tracker records that arithmetic without adjudicating it; it is the context in which the round's severance costs, and its effect on the division's cost per delivery, will be measured in the company's later filings.

**The language of overstaffing.** The word at the centre of Pope's memo has a history at this company, and the tracker records it because the continuity is diagnostic. In October 2024, Ortberg described the 17,000-position programme as a correction to overstaffing; in November 2026, Pope's memo placed the commercial division "in an overstaffed position" against its revised plan. In both cases the surplus is defined relative to a plan — a financial structure in the first case, a production schedule in the second — and not relative to the work available, which in 2026 includes a record backlog. The framing matters beyond rhetoric: a workforce described as surplus to a plan can be restored by the plan's revision, which is the mechanism recall rights exist to serve, whereas a workforce described as surplus to demand implies a permanence neither round has claimed. Readers comparing Boeing's rounds with those at other companies in this programme will find the same word doing different work elsewhere — at Oracle, in the pilot tracker, reductions were framed as roles made redundant by technology, a claim about the work itself. Boeing's claim is about the timetable. The December notices will be written in the same vocabulary, and the distinction between the two claims is the surest guide to what the company expects to happen next: timetables, unlike technologies, are expected to recover.

**The 787 and Charleston dimension.** The round's second programme is quieter than the 737 and structurally different. The 787 Dreamliner is built exclusively at North Charleston, South Carolina — a non-union site, unlike the unionised Puget Sound factories — and its rate has held at about eight aircraft a month against a plan of ten, with wing production named in reporting as one of the constraints on the wider system. A rate held flat is not a rate cut, and the staffing consequence differs accordingly: a plant staffed for ten a month and building eight carries the difference as surely as Renton carries its own, but the South Carolina workforce holds no collective agreement of the Puget Sound kind, so its separations, if they come, will run through the company's plan terms rather than a contract table, and its WARN filings — South Carolina publishes them, as the 2024 programme's 220 first-wave notices and 67 December notices from North Charleston showed — will be the round's record there. The tracker notes the asymmetry because it will shape the documentary trail: the same round will be described by union documents in Washington and by state filings alone in South Carolina, and the two records will arrive at different speeds with different detail. The division's widebody engineering, concentrated in Everett alongside the 767 and 777 lines, sits between the two programmes — exposed to the 737's rate through the division's shared overhead, and to the 787's through its own programme staffing — and the December notices will show how the company allocated the correction across those exposures.

**What happens next, on the documented sequence.** The round's remaining documentary events are predictable from the standing sequence this tracker records, and they are listed here so the section can be completed as they occur. The December 9 notices will name the first population; the WARN filings that accompany them will give the first site-level counts and effective dates, converting the estimated 2,500 into a documented floor. The company's fourth-quarter and annual reporting will show the commercial division's year-end headcount against the 50,494 base, and any severance charge the round generates will appear in the filings as the 2024 programme's $295 million did. The market's first verdict is already in the record — the shares fell about 1 percent on the announcement, a muted response next to the near-10 percent fall on the October earnings that preceded it, suggesting investors had priced the production problem before the staffing consequence arrived — and the analysts' framing, in the coverage the tracker rests on, divides between those who read the round as discipline applied early and those who read it as the 2020 error in miniature: capacity released just before it is needed. Both readings are recorded here as the round's documented reception. Neither is this tracker's position; the tracker's position is the ledger, and the ledger's next entries for this round will be filings, not arguments.

## February 2025: The Space Launch System Reduction

**Announcement and effective dates.** On February 7, 2025, Boeing informed the employees of its Space Launch System programme that the company was preparing for the potential elimination of approximately 400 positions by April 2025. A Boeing spokesperson confirmed the warning the same day: the company had told the team of "the potential for approximately 400 fewer positions by April 2025," to align with revisions to the Artemis programme and cost expectations, and 60-day notices of involuntary layoff would be issued to impacted employees in the coming weeks in accordance with the WARN Act. The programme's vice president addressed roughly 800 SLS employees that day. The reductions that survived mitigation took effect in April 2025, on the timetable the warning set: the WARN filings described below carry an effective date of April 18, 2025.

**Roles cut.** This round is recorded at 160 to 200 roles, and the width of that small range is the residue of an unusual sequence in which the company talked its own number down. The February 7 warning put approximately 400 positions at risk. Five days later, after discussions with NASA, Boeing wrote to the programme's employees — in a February 12 letter from the programme manager, reported by Bloomberg — that the company had "successfully mitigated a majority of the previously announced workforce reductions" and expected to proceed with fewer than 200 involuntary layoffs, redeploying employees where possible. The statutory record then documented the floor: a WARN notice filed in Alabama on February 18, 2025 covering 71 workers at Huntsville, effective April 18, 2025, and a notice filed in Louisiana on February 15, 2025 covering 89 workers at New Orleans — 160 positions between the programme's two principal sites. The ledger therefore carries 160 to 200: the WARN-documented 160 as the floor, the company's stated fewer-than-200 as the ceiling, and the 400 figure recorded in this section as what it was — a pre-mitigation warning, not a count of roles cut. Any account that cites 400 as the size of this round is citing the warning, and the distinction is the reason this round has its own section rather than a line in someone else's.

**Share of workforce.** Against a company workforce of approximately 172,000 at the end of 2024, 160 to 200 roles are under 1 percent — the smallest round in this ledger by an order of magnitude. Within the programme the proportion was far larger: the SLS workforce at Boeing numbered in the low thousands across its sites, and the positions at risk in the February warning represented a significant fraction of the programme's staffing, which is why a company-negligible reduction was a programme-defining event.

**Divisions and geographies.** The round fell entirely within Boeing Defense, Space & Security's space business, on the Space Launch System programme for which Boeing is NASA's prime contractor for the core stage, the upper stage work and the avionics. The programme is managed from Huntsville, Alabama, where Boeing's space operations sit alongside NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center, and the core stage is built at NASA's Michoud Assembly Facility in New Orleans, Louisiana — the two sites named in the WARN filings. Programme employees also work at other Boeing and NASA sites nationally, and the company did not publish a site distribution beyond the filings; none is asserted here beyond them.

**The company's stated reason.** Boeing's framing, in its February 7 statement, was alignment "with revisions to the Artemis program and cost expectations." The revisions in question were prospective: a new federal administration was reviewing the Artemis programme, whose cost and schedule record — including a NASA inspector general's findings on the programme's expense and on Boeing's upper-stage work — had made the SLS a candidate for cancellation or restructuring, and Boeing's contracts faced renewal decisions. No cancellation had been announced when Boeing acted, and the company described its own move as preparatory: it was required, if reductions came, to give 60 days' notice, and it issued the warning so that the notice clock could run while the programme's future was decided. The mitigation that followed — the majority of the warned positions preserved after discussions with NASA, and contract revisions finalised on terms that kept more of the workforce — is part of the stated record, and the round is therefore documented here as a reduction sized by a negotiation: warned at 400, negotiated to under 200, filed at 160.

**WARN Act posture.** This is the one round in the ledger in which the WARN Act was not merely complied with but was itself the mechanism of the announcement: Boeing's February 7 warning to employees was explicitly framed as the start of the statutory process, with 60-day notices to follow, and the Alabama and Louisiana filings in mid-February 2025 are the round's primary documents. The filings give this small round the hardest numbers in the ledger relative to its size — notice dates, sites and an effective date of April 18, 2025 — and they are the reason the round qualifies for the ledger at its documented floor even though its headline figure collapsed during mitigation.

**Severance and notice terms.** No round-specific severance terms were disclosed for the SLS reduction. The company stated that it was seeking opportunities to redeploy employees across the company to minimise job losses — a commitment that formed part of the mitigation arithmetic, since a redeployed employee is neither a layoff nor an unfilled role — and the standing terms in the section below govern the separations that did occur. Terms were not separately disclosed beyond that commitment, and this section records them as not disclosed rather than filling the gap from other rounds' practice.

**Sources.** This section rests on Boeing's February 7, 2025 statement as reported by SpaceNews, Bloomberg, The Register and FLYING Magazine; the company's February 12, 2025 mitigation letter as reported by Bloomberg and summarised by Virginia Business; and the Alabama and Louisiana WARN listings for February 2025 (71 workers at Huntsville effective April 18, 2025; 89 workers at New Orleans) as aggregated from the state agencies' published records.

**The Artemis context.** The programme whose risk produced this round is NASA's Space Launch System, the heavy-lift rocket at the centre of the Artemis campaign to return astronauts to the Moon, for which Boeing builds the core stage at Michoud and the avionics, and is developing the more powerful upper stage for later missions. The programme's cost record is the background of the February warning: a NASA inspector general's accounting put the agency's overall Artemis investment on a path to $93 billion through 2025, with the SLS itself at $23.8 billion spent through 2022, and further findings attributed hundreds of millions in cost overruns to Boeing's upper-stage work. A programme with that record, entering a new administration's budget review with its contracts up for revision, presented its prime contractor with a quantifiable risk: if the programme were cancelled or descoped, hundreds of programme-funded positions would end on a timetable the WARN Act would not let the company improvise. Boeing's February 7 warning was the hedge against that risk — notices prepared so that the statutory clock could run — and the mitigation that followed was the hedge being partially unwound when the customer's revisions proved smaller than the worst case. The round is therefore best understood as an insurance premium partly refunded: 400 positions placed under notice of risk, fewer than 200 finally taken, 160 of them filed. It is the only round in this ledger whose size was set by a negotiation that happened after the announcement and before the separations, and its section preserves all three numbers in their proper roles — warned, agreed, filed — because collapsing them into one figure, in either direction, misstates what occurred.

**Why this round is in the ledger at all.** A reduction of 160 to 200 roles at a company of 172,000 sits close to this tracker's floor, and its inclusion deserves a stated reason. It qualifies on two of the programme's three evidence routes at once: the company announced it in its own words on February 7, 2025, and WARN filings document its execution. It also carries information the larger rounds do not. It is the only round in the ledger driven by a single government programme's contract risk rather than by production rates or corporate cost, the only one whose size was materially changed by negotiation with the customer after announcement, and the only one in the defence and space division — a reminder that the division whose profitability sheltered it in 2026 runs programme-by-programme exposure of its own. The tracker's rule is that a documented round is recorded at its documented size, neither inflated to its warning figure nor discarded for being small, and this section is that rule applied at the ledger's lower edge.

## October 2024: The Ten Percent Programme

**Announcement and effective dates.** On October 11, 2024, Kelly Ortberg, Boeing's president and chief executive of two months, told employees in a company-wide message that Boeing would reduce the size of its total workforce by about 10 percent over the coming months — roughly 17,000 positions, including executives, managers and employees — and announced the decision alongside preliminary third-quarter results showing $1.3 billion of cash consumed in the quarter and a loss of $9.97 per share. The same announcement deferred the 777X programme's first delivery to 2026 and set the end of 767 freighter production. This is one announced round executed in waves, and the ledger treats it as one: the waves below are its execution, not separate rounds. First notices were delivered in mid-November 2024, with the first separations effective January 17, 2025; further waves followed in December 2024 and into 2025.

**Roles cut.** The programme's figure is the company's own: about 10 percent of a workforce of roughly 170,000, universally rendered as approximately 17,000 positions, and carried in this tracker as a company figure. Boeing never published a final executed total, and the composition of the 17,000 was mixed by design — Ortberg and the company described the reduction as coming through involuntary layoffs together with attrition, reduced hiring and the sale of subsidiaries, without publishing the proportions. The documented involuntary core is smaller and exact in the way WARN records are: by November 20, 2024, notices covering 3,509 workers had been announced across seven states, and aggregators of state filings count 62 Boeing WARN notices filed between November 2024 and April 2026 naming 3,823 workers in the states that publish counts. Those filings are the floor of the programme's United States layoff count, not its measure: the balance executed through attrition and unfilled roles, at international sites that file nothing, and through transfers of staff to buyers of divested businesses, none of which a WARN notice captures. The tracker therefore carries the round at the company's announced 17,000, labels it a company figure for a combined programme, and states the WARN floor separately wherever the programme's layoffs alone are discussed. The two numbers answer different questions and are never substituted for each other.

**Share of workforce.** About 10 percent of the company's workforce at announcement — the largest proportional reduction in this ledger, and the largest single announced round in Boeing's history since the pandemic programme recorded in the snapshot.

**Divisions and geographies.** The programme was company-wide by design and landed unevenly in a documented pattern. Washington state took the largest share: the state's WARN listing of November 15, 2024 covered 2,192 workers effective January 17, 2025, with a further small batch effective December 20, 2024 bringing the state's first-wave total to the 2,199 the Seattle Times reported. Of the Washington total, 438 members of SPEEA — 218 engineers and 220 technicians — received notices in the first week, the detail that broke the company's framing within days: Ortberg had told analysts the reduction would focus on overhead and non-essential activities and that the intent was not to take people off production or out of the engineering labs, yet several hundred engineers and production workers were in the first wave, and reporting from inside the defence engineering organisations described targeted reductions of roughly 10 percent across engineering groups supporting military programmes in St. Louis and elsewhere. South Carolina received 220 notices in the first wave and a further 67 at North Charleston in December. The December wave added a second Washington filing of 396 workers effective February 21, 2025, and the California filings: about 500 workers expected across the state's sites, including 144 at El Segundo, 179 across two Seal Beach facilities, 115 at Long Beach and 57 at Huntington Beach. Huntsville, Alabama filed for 158 workers in November 2024; Mesa, Arizona for 184; Virginia sites for 68; and filings in Oregon, Colorado, Pennsylvania and Missouri completed the seven-state first wave. The programme's international execution surfaced later and more thinly: in March 2025, about 180 jobs were cut at the Boeing India Engineering and Technology Center in Bengaluru, reported at the time as part of the 17,000 programme. The machinists of IAM District 751, who had struck from September 13 to November 4, 2024 and ratified their contract days before the first notices, were largely not hit in the early waves — the company had suspended the rolling furloughs it imposed during the strike when the programme was announced, and the Seattle Times reported in December 2024 that returning machinists had not been among the laid off — a distribution that reflected both the production workforce's scarcity and the programme's stated aim at overhead, even as the engineering notices qualified that aim.

**The company's stated reason.** Ortberg's message and the company's statements framed the programme as a financial and organisational reset. The company had to align its staffing levels with its financial reality; it would "reset priorities and create a leaner, more focused organization," in his words on the October earnings call, concentrating the reduction on overhead and activities that could be consolidated. On the same call he addressed the strike directly: it did not cause the layoffs, which he described as a result of overstaffing. The context the statements invoked was the company's position in the autumn of 2024 — years of losses since 2019, the January 2024 door-plug accident and the regulatory production cap that followed it, $6.2 billion of cash lost in the third quarter on the strike's arithmetic as analysts estimated it, and a balance sheet carrying the debt of the crisis years. The tracker records that framing as given, and records alongside it the two qualifications the documentary record supplies: the first wave's inclusion of engineers and production staff, against the overhead framing; and the fact that the programme was announced while 33,000 of the company's own machinists were on strike against it, a juxtaposition that fixed the round's public meaning regardless of the causation the company asserted.

**WARN Act posture.** The programme is the most thoroughly WARN-documented action in this ledger. Boeing filed notices state by state as the waves rolled: Washington's filings of November 15 and December 9, 2024; California's cluster of December 6, 2024 filings; South Carolina, Alabama, Arizona, Virginia and the remaining states on their own dates — 62 notices in the aggregator count between November 2024 and April 2026, naming 3,823 workers where states publish numbers. The notice pattern complied with the federal Act's structure in form: wave-one notices delivered in mid-November 2024 carried a January 17, 2025 effective date, and notified employees remained on the payroll through the interval. The filings also expose the Act's limits as a measuring instrument, which this tracker states once here rather than in every section: WARN captures site-level involuntary separations above statutory thresholds in the United States, and nothing else — not attrition, not hiring freezes, not international reductions, not the staff who left with sold businesses — so for a programme deliberately executed through all of those channels at once, the filings document the core and understate the whole by an amount the company has not published.

**Severance and notice terms.** The terms reported for this programme are the fullest in the ledger, because the Associated Press and the Seattle Times documented them at the time and the unions publish their own schedules. Eligible employees received career transition services and subsidized health care benefits for up to three months; severance was typically about one week of pay for every year of service, as Reuters reported it; and notified workers remained on the payroll for the roughly two months between notice and separation. Boeing's annual report booked $295 million of severance in connection with the programme, as reported from the filing — the programme's official price tag, and a figure that covers the whole programme rather than any single wave. Represented employees' terms ran through their contracts: the union-published schedules set out in the standing section — SPEEA's table, with its maximum of 26 weeks for an ordinary layoff and its higher work-movement scale, and the machinists' contract layoff article — governed the two largest populations, which is why the programme's severance cannot be stated as one formula and is not stated as one here.

**Sources.** This section rests on Boeing's October 11, 2024 announcement and preliminary third-quarter results as reported by the Associated Press and Reuters; Ortberg's October 2024 earnings-call statements as reported at the time; the Washington, California, South Carolina, Alabama, Arizona and Virginia WARN filings of November and December 2024 as published by the state agencies and aggregated by Manufacturing Dive and WARN trackers; SPEEA's statements on its members' notices; Associated Press reporting on the programme's severance, health subsidy and transition terms; and March 2025 reporting of the Bengaluru wave as part of the programme.

**The strike, the furloughs and the announcement's timing.** The programme cannot be read apart from the labour conflict it interrupted. The machinists' strike began on September 13, 2024, after members rejected a tentative agreement, and it idled the Puget Sound factories that build the 737, 767 and 777; analysts estimated its cost to the company at between $50 million and $150 million a day. During the strike Boeing imposed rolling temporary furloughs on non-union staff — one week in four for many — which the October announcement suspended, because a company moving to permanent reductions no longer needs temporary ones. The furloughs are not counted in this round: they were suspensions, most furloughed staff returned, and the tracker excludes them by definition, recording them here because the announcement's own logic referenced them. The strike settled on November 4, 2024, with a contract carrying substantial wage increases and running, in its successor form, to 2028; the first layoff notices reached engineers eleven days later. Ortberg's insistence that the strike did not cause the layoffs is part of the record, and so is the sequence, and the tracker leaves the two side by side as it found them: a programme whose stated cause was overstaffing and financial reality, announced at the moment of the company's maximum labour conflict, and executed first against the salaried workforce that had kept working through it.

**The defence division inside the programme.** Although the programme's public face was the commercial crisis, its filings and reporting reach into the defence business, and that reach is recorded here because it qualified the company's overhead framing in a second direction. Reporting from inside the St. Louis defence engineering organisations in November 2024 described targeted reductions of roughly 10 percent across the engineering groups supporting military programmes — the F-15 and F/A-18 fighters and the P-8 maritime patrol aircraft among them — with research and development work and production programmes alike expected, in one senior engineering manager's words to reporters, to "bleed a little." The Huntsville filing of 158 workers in November 2024, from the site that houses the space and missile-defence work, and the Mesa, Arizona filing of 184, belong to the same pattern. The tracker records this as reporting rather than as a company breakdown, because Boeing published no division split for the programme; but the pattern is consistent across the filings and the accounts, and it establishes the point the round section's framing paragraph makes from the other side: a programme announced against overhead reached, in execution, into engineering organisations on both the commercial and military sides of the company, and its announced 10 percent was applied inside divisions as a target to be met rather than as a description of where the surplus sat. That is a normal property of company-wide percentage programmes — the percentage is set at the centre and the composition is decided in the divisions — and it is why this tracker reads the filings, and not only the announcement, before characterising any round.

**The execution tail.** A programme announced in October 2024 did not finish in 2024, and its tail is part of its record. The first wave's separations took effect on January 17, 2025; the second wave's, from the December notices, on February 21, 2025; and smaller filings continued through the year as individual sites and functions reached their targets — the pattern the WARN aggregators capture in their count of 62 notices running to April 2026, most of them small, several of them in single digits, as the programme's remainder worked through the company's long tail of sites. The Bengaluru reduction of about 180 engineering roles in March 2025 belonged to this tail and was reported as the programme's, a reminder that the announced 10 percent was a global figure whose international execution surfaced only in local reporting. The tail matters to the ledger for one practical reason: it is the mechanism by which an announced figure becomes unmeasurable. By the time a programme is in its third wave, its notices are indistinguishable in the filings from ordinary site adjustments, and no honest total of its executed layoffs can be assembled from public documents — which is why this tracker carries the programme at its announced figure with its WARN floor stated, and does not pretend to an executed count the record does not contain. Programmes of this design are, in that specific sense, announced precisely once and executed indefinitely, and the distinction between the announcement and the execution is the most important thing a reader can hold about the largest entry in this ledger.

**The programme decisions inside the announcement.** The October 11 announcement was a package, and its non-workforce elements explain part of the workforce element. The 777X — the company's new widebody, already years late — had its first delivery deferred to 2026, which removed a production ramp the Everett workforce had been staffed toward and converted its engineering population, in part, into overhead against a later date. The 767 freighter's production was set to end after the remaining orders are delivered in 2027, closing a programme whose line had run for four decades and whose workforce would wind down with it. And the preliminary results published the same day recorded the charges that made the quarter's loss what it was, across the commercial and defence portfolios. A reduction programme announced inside a package like that is not solely a headcount decision: it is the workforce expression of a portfolio being re-timed — programmes pushed right, one programme ended, rates capped — with the staffing following the schedule. That is also why the programme's execution stretched across more than a year of notices: the schedule it was adjusting to kept moving, and the waves of filings through 2025 are the documentary trace of a company re-timing itself in instalments. Days after the announcement, the approximately $21 billion equity raise described in the snapshot completed the package from the capital side; the programme and the offering were the two halves of one repair, and the ledger records them together for that reason.

**What the programme did not do.** The reconciliation in the cumulative section states the arithmetic, and this section states the organisational fact behind it: the programme did not shrink Boeing. The reported workforce at the end of 2024, 172,000, was higher than at the end of 2023, and by the end of 2025 it stood at about 182,000 — the reduction programme and the largest hiring-and-acquisition expansion in the company's recent history ran concurrently, in different divisions, for different reasons. The programme's own levers included the sale of subsidiaries, executed in 2025 with the disposal of the digital aviation services businesses, whose employees transferred to the buyer: a reduction in Boeing's headcount that appears in no WARN filing and cost no one a job at the moment it occurred. A reader who wants to know what the programme changed should therefore look where the notices actually landed — in the salaried and engineering populations of the Puget Sound, in the corporate functions, and in the specific sites the filings name — rather than at the company's total, which the programme was never, in the event, large enough to move against the combined weight of hiring and acquisition. That is not a smallness judgement: 3,823 WARN-named workers is the documented core of a programme whose announced scope was 17,000, and for the individuals and sites in the filings, the programme was the largest workforce event of their working lives. It is a statement about what kind of instrument the programme was — a reallocation executed at crisis scale, inside a company that was simultaneously growing somewhere else.

## February 2023: The Finance and Human Resources Reductions

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Monday, February 6, 2023, Boeing confirmed a Seattle Times report and announced that it expected about 2,000 reductions during 2023, primarily in its finance and human resources functions, through a combination of attrition and layoffs. The layoffs were to begin in April 2023, followed by two further waves at roughly 60-day intervals, on reporting of the company's plan at the time. The round is one announced action executed across the year, and the ledger carries it as one round.

**Roles cut.** The company's figure is about 2,000 reductions, and it is a combined figure: Boeing announced the total as attrition and layoffs together and never published the split, and this tracker records it as announced — a combined reduction of about 2,000 positions — and never as 2,000 layoffs. The internal composition was reported by the Seattle Times from the company's own briefings: about 1,500 of the company's roughly 5,800 finance positions, about a quarter of the function, and up to 400 human resources positions, about 15 percent of that department's staff. Approximately one third of the eliminated positions were to be outsourced to Tata Consultancy Services in Bengaluru, India; the remainder were eliminated outright. The outsourced third requires the same care as the attrition split: a role moved to a contractor is a Boeing role eliminated, and it is counted in the company's announced figure, but the work itself continued under another employer, and the round section records that rather than treating every eliminated position as work that ceased.

**Share of workforce.** About 2,000 reductions against a workforce of approximately 156,000 at the end of 2022 represent about 1.3 percent of the company — and about a quarter of the finance function, which is the proportion that describes the round's actual incidence.

**Divisions and geographies.** The round fell on the enterprise functions, company-wide, rather than on any division: finance first, human resources second, with the company's communications at the time placing information technology's own streamlining alongside them as part of the same corporate-simplification drive. The work that survived in transferred form moved to Bengaluru, where Tata Consultancy Services took over finance and accounting process work and, in the human resources case, the transactional process work, while Boeing retained the generalist staff who work directly with employees at its factories and engineering offices — the dividing line the company drew, in its communications director's explanation, between process work that could be shared with an outside partner and judgement work that stayed. The reductions were concentrated in the United States salaried population, in the Puget Sound and at the company's other corporate sites.

**The company's stated reason.** Boeing's framing was simplification. Its statement said the finance team was planning for lower staffing levels as it simplified processes, improved efficiency and shared select work with an outside partner, and its communications director observed that over time some of the company's corporate functions had grown quite large. The announcement was made in the same season as a hiring campaign of the opposite sign — the company had grown its workforce by about 15,000 during 2022 and planned to hire about 10,000 more in 2023, concentrated in engineering and manufacturing — and Boeing presented the two movements as one policy: resources moved from the corporate centre to the factories and programmes. The tracker records the framing as given, and notes the context that makes it legible: the finance function's leadership had been reorganised the previous year under a transformation mandate, the September 2022 round below was this round's first instalment, and the outsourcing partner named in 2023 was the same one engaged in 2022.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filing defines this round; it was executed in waves sized and distributed so that the statutory record is fragmentary, and the round rests on the company's announcement — the first of this programme's three evidence routes — rather than on filings. That documentary thinness is itself recorded: a 2,000-role reduction at a company of Boeing's size can pass almost entirely outside the WARN system when it is spread across sites, functions and a calendar year, combined with attrition, and partly executed as transfers to a contractor, and this round is the ledger's standing example of that property.

**Severance and notice terms.** Terms for this round were not separately disclosed by the company, and this section records them as not disclosed; the standing terms in the section below are the terms of record for the period. No voluntary programme specific to this round was announced.

**Sources.** This section rests on Boeing's February 6, 2023 statement as reported by Reuters and the Associated Press; the Seattle Times' reporting of the function's internal composition, the Tata Consultancy Services outsourcing share and the wave timetable; and the company's workforce figures in its annual filings.

**The functions as they stood.** The scale of the two functions explains why they, and not others, carried the corporate-simplification drive. Finance numbered about 5,800 positions company-wide before the round — a function built up through the years when Boeing's reporting, programme accounting and compliance obligations were expanding, and staffed, in the company's own assessment, beyond what its simplified processes required. Human resources carried the transactional apparatus of a company that had just hired 15,000 people in a single year: recruiting operations, onboarding, payroll and benefits administration, much of it process work of the kind that transfers to a contractor most readily, which is why the function's generalists — the staff who sit with employees and managers at the sites — were explicitly retained while the process layer was shared with the partner. Information technology, the third function named in the company's streamlining statements, had already been through two outsourcing waves in the previous decade and appears in this round as context rather than as a counted population. The round's incidence inside the functions — a quarter of finance, about a seventh of human resources — is the figure that describes what the affected organisations experienced, and it is recorded alongside the company-wide 1.3 percent wherever the round's scale is summarised, because the two percentages describe the same event at different distances and a tracker that carried only the smaller one would misdescribe it.

**The round in the company's longer pattern.** The finance and human resources reductions of 2023 were not an improvisation. Boeing had been moving transactional corporate work to outside partners for a decade — information technology positions from 2013, a further IT outsourcing in 2021, low-level finance work shared with an earlier partner before Tata Consultancy Services took the larger mandate — and the 2022–2023 sequence applied the established method to the two functions that remained: simplify the process, standardise what can be standardised, transfer the standardised work, and eliminate the positions the transfer and the simplification leave behind. The method's signature is that the announced figure arrives as a combined attrition-and-layoffs total, because a function being wound down over a year sheds people by every route at once — resignations not replaced, retirements, transfers, and notices — and the company has no operational reason to separate the routes even though a tracker of layoffs has every reason to want them separated. The ledger's treatment of this round, announced figure carried as announced and labelled combined wherever it appears, is the compromise the evidence supports, and it is applied identically to the November 2026 round, where the same signature appears in a production division rather than a corporate function.

## September 2022: The Finance Reductions

**Announcement and effective dates.** On September 20, 2022, Boeing announced that it would eliminate about 150 finance positions in the United States during 2022, simplifying its corporate structure and directing more resources to manufacturing and product development. The first layoff notices were to go out in October 2022. It is the smallest company-announced round in this ledger and the first entry in it, and it is recorded as a distinct round because it was distinctly announced — its own statement, its own scope and its own calendar — five months before the larger finance and human resources action that followed and absorbed its logic.

**Roles cut.** The company's figure is 150 positions, all in the finance function, in the United States. Boeing said at the time that about 150 jobs nationwide would be cut in the first batch, with more to come in later years — a phrasing that makes this round, in retrospect, the announced first instalment of the finance transformation the February 2023 round then executed at thirteen times the scale. The 150 are recorded as the company stated them; whether every one of the 150 was an involuntary layoff, as against a position eliminated through the reorganisation the announcement described, was not itemised by the company, and the figure is carried as a company figure for positions eliminated.

**Share of workforce.** Against a workforce of approximately 142,000 at the end of 2021 and 156,000 at the end of 2022, 150 positions are under 1 percent of the company — about a tenth of one percent — and roughly 2.5 percent of a finance function that then numbered about 6,000 company-wide. The round's significance is not its size but its position: it opened the tracked period, it established the mechanism, and it was announced by a company that had added about 10,000 employees in the first part of that same year.

**Divisions and geographies.** The round fell on the finance organisation in the United States, with finance and accounting work identified for transfer to Tata Consultancy Services in India; the company's statement also placed information technology staffing reductions alongside the finance action as part of the same streamlining of corporate functions. Employees learned the shape of the change at a virtual all-hands meeting of non-union corporate staff, at which the outsourcing was announced, followed within days by a reorganisation that moved finance staff into new organisations under new managers — an execution detail reported by the Seattle Times at the time and recorded here because it became the template for how the function's later, larger round was also handled: the structure announced first, the individuals notified after.

**The company's stated reason.** Boeing's framing was streamlining: the finance team was planning for lower staffing levels as it simplified processes, improved efficiency and shared select work with an outside partner, and the company said it would assess future impacts as the process continued in the coming years. The stated destination of the savings was the product side of the business — more resources to engineering, production and product development. The tracker records the framing as given. It also records the context the company's own statements supplied: the reductions were unrelated, a spokesman said, to the headquarters relocation from Chicago to Arlington, Virginia completed that year, and they were announced by a chief financial officer, appointed in 2021, whose tenure intensified the focus on the finance function's cost — context that belongs to the record because the same function's reductions recur, at larger scale, in the next entry of the ledger.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filing defines this round; at 150 positions distributed across a national function, it sat below the thresholds that generate a documentary trail, and it rests entirely on the company's announcement — the first evidence route in this programme's standard. It is the ledger's limiting case: the smallest round a company announcement alone can establish at or above the 100-role threshold.

**Severance and notice terms.** Terms for this round were not separately disclosed, and this section records them as not disclosed. The standing terms in the section below are the terms of record for the period.

**Sources.** This section rests on Boeing's September 20, 2022 announcement as reported by Reuters and the Wall Street Journal; the Seattle Times' reporting of the Tata Consultancy Services outsourcing, the all-hands announcement and the finance reorganisation; and the company's workforce figures in its annual filings.

**The finance function at the moment of the announcement.** The finance organisation Boeing began cutting in September 2022 numbered about 6,000 people company-wide before the reductions, by the company's own account at the time — a function that had grown through the crisis years, when programme accounting, government-contract compliance and the reporting obligations of a company under regulatory and legal scrutiny all expanded at once. Its leadership had been reorganised the previous year, with a finance transformation mandate attached explicitly to the role created to run finance and accounting. The September announcement was that mandate's first public act: 150 positions, an outsourcing partner named, a reorganisation of the function's reporting lines executed within days of the all-hands at which the change was announced, and a stated intention to assess further impacts in the coming years. Read against the function's size, the 150 were a proof of method rather than a material saving — about 2.5 percent of the function — and the announcement's real content was the method itself: processes simplified first, work shared with the partner second, positions eliminated third. The February 2023 round, five months later, applied the identical sequence to the whole function and to human resources beside it. The ledger's two finance entries are therefore one story told in two announcements, and they are kept as two rounds because the company announced them as two — different dates, different scopes, different stated figures — which is the tracker's rule for where one round ends and the next begins.

**Why a 150-role round opens the ledger.** The tracker's threshold is 100 roles, and this round clears it by fifty. Its inclusion is deliberate and is defended here once, for all small rounds: a tracker that begins only when a reduction becomes large cannot show how reductions begin, and Boeing's method — visible in 2022 in miniature and in 2023 and 2024 at scale — is to announce a direction with a small, specific, function-level action, execute it through a reorganisation and an outsourcing transfer, and then extend it. The September 2022 statement said, in terms, that more would come in the coming years; the February 2023 statement was the more. A reader who starts the ledger here can watch the mechanism assemble. A reader who starts it in 2023 meets the same mechanism fully formed and mistakes it for a response to circumstances it predated. The small rounds are the ledger's memory, and this one is kept for that reason.

## How Cuts Work at Boeing

**Two systems, one company.** Boeing separations run through two different systems depending on who is being separated, and almost everything distinctive about the company's layoff practice follows from that fact. Roughly 40 percent of the workforce is represented by unions — the machinists of IAM District 751 and its sister districts, the engineers and technicians of SPEEA, and smaller units elsewhere — and for those employees the terms of a layoff are not set by the company at the time of the round. They are written into collective bargaining agreements negotiated years earlier: the benefit formula, the notice practice, the order in which employees are released, and the recall rights that govern who is called back when work returns. The unions publish their own layoff-benefit summaries, which makes Boeing's represented severance one of the few such schedules in American industry that an employee can read in full before any round is announced. The other 60 percent — managers, executives and non-represented salaried staff — are covered by the company's own layoff benefits plan, whose terms are known through the company's statements, its regulatory filings and reporting at the time of each round rather than through a single published schedule. This tracker states the two systems separately wherever terms are given, because a single "Boeing severance" figure that averaged them would describe neither.

**Notice.** The federal WARN Act requires employers of Boeing's size to give 60 calendar days of notice before a mass layoff or plant closing, and Boeing's documented practice is to run its large rounds on the statute's clock: notices delivered on a stated date, an effective date roughly 60 days later, and the notified employees remaining on the payroll through the interval — in the 2024 programme's first wave, notices in mid-November 2024 against a January 17, 2025 separation date, with most recipients not required to work the period out. State mini-WARN laws in several of Boeing's locations impose their own thresholds and, in some states, longer notice, and the company's filings track those jurisdictions individually, which is why the programme's WARN record is a shelf of state filings rather than a single federal document. Below the statutory thresholds, notice is a matter of company or contract practice rather than law, and the smaller rounds in this ledger — the finance reductions of 2022 and 2023 — were executed with no WARN trail at all. The newest round follows the statutory pattern in advance: announced November 9, 2026, with its first notices set for December 9, the separations they start will carry their own 60-day clocks into early 2027.

**Severance for non-represented staff.** The reported standard, documented in the 2024 programme and consistent with the company's earlier practice, is severance of about one week of pay for each year of service. The formula's caps and floors are documented in the union schedules described below and in secondary summaries of the company's plan — a minimum measured in weeks and a maximum of 26 weeks is the shape consistently reported — and this tracker attributes the standard as reporting rather than as a company-published table, because Boeing does not publish one for non-represented staff. Alongside severance, the documented 2024 package included subsidized health care benefits for up to three months and career transition services, and the November 2026 memo commits the company to outplacement services, layoff benefits and Employee Assistance Program support in the same pattern. Executives are a separate case, and uniquely for this tracker, a documented one: the Boeing Executive Layoff Benefits Plan, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission and amended effective January 1, 2025, provides an executive who experiences a layoff event with one year of base salary plus the executive's target incentive amount — a benefit an order of magnitude beyond the one-week-per-year standard, and a standing fact about the distribution of the company's layoff costs that the filings, rather than the company's announcements, are what disclose.

**Severance under the contracts.** For SPEEA's engineers and technicians, the union's published layoff-benefit table, as summarised in secondary coverage of the union's documents, runs three ways. An ordinary layoff pays one week of pay per year of service to a maximum of 26 weeks, with three months of subsidized COBRA coverage. A voluntary layoff pays on the same scale but as a lump sum against a release, and surrenders recall rights. A work-movement layoff — where the work itself is moved rather than ended — pays two weeks per year of service, with a minimum of 26 weeks and a maximum of 60, and six months of COBRA subsidy: the contract prices the company's decision to move work at roughly double the price of ending it. For IAM District 751's machinists, the union's published guidance on its contract's layoff article describes one week of base rate plus the cost-of-living adjustment for each full year of service, up to 26 weeks, with six months of medical coverage where the member continues to pay their share of the premium; that guidance predates the contract ratified in November 2024 and is recorded here as the union's published description of the structure rather than as the current agreement's text. Both schedules are union documents, not company ones, and this tracker cites them as such — while noting that in practice they are the operative terms for the two largest populations any Boeing round touches, and that Boeing's own statements have not contradicted them.

**Seniority, retention and recall.** In the represented workforce, who is released in a layoff is governed substantially by seniority and retention rules written into the contracts: employees are categorised by their experience and performance records, and release runs broadly from the least senior upward within a job classification, with bumping rights that let a senior employee displaced from one role claim another held by someone junior. The consequence is that a Boeing layoff in the represented population is partly predictable by its targets weeks before notices land — a property no at-will round has — and that the population actually separated skews younger and shorter-service than the population notified in a purely discretionary process. The mirror image of release is recall: represented employees laid off from Boeing carry recall rights for a contract-defined period, under which the company must offer re-employment in their classification before hiring externally. Recall rights exist because Boeing's cycle demands them. A company that cuts for a production rate it expects to reach eighteen months later has a structural interest in being able to reassemble the same workforce, and the contracts institutionalise that interest — the November 2026 round, taken explicitly against a ramp now scheduled for 2027, is the kind of action recall rights were designed around, and whether the December notices' recipients are back on the line when the 47 rate arrives is one of the measures by which the round will eventually be judged.

**Voluntary programmes and attrition.** Boeing's reduction toolkit has historically opened with voluntary instruments. In the 2020 pandemic programme the company offered a voluntary layoff package — a lump sum of one week of pay per year of service, up to 26 weeks, with subsidized COBRA for a limited period — to eligible employees in participating areas, reserving the right to decline volunteers whose skills it needed to keep. Voluntary offers reduce the involuntary count, concentrate separations among employees nearest retirement, and cost the company the experience it can least spare when too many accept; the balance between those effects is the standing argument about their use. The November 2026 round breaks with the pattern in a documented way: Boeing managers told SPEEA there will be no voluntary layoffs or buy-outs in the Commercial Airplanes reduction, making it an involuntary-and-attrition action by design. Attrition is the toolkit's quietest instrument and appears in the announced figures of three of this ledger's five rounds: a hiring freeze on selected roles, vacancies left unfilled, retirements not replaced. It produces no notices, no filings and no severance cost, which is precisely why companies prefer it and why this tracker insists on labelling combined figures as combined — an announced reduction of 2,000 or 2,500 that mixes attrition with layoffs describes a smaller number of separations than the headline carries, by an amount the company in these cases chose not to publish.

**Outsourcing and divestiture.** Two of Boeing's reduction mechanisms remove positions from the company without ending the work. Outsourcing transfers a function to a contractor — Tata Consultancy Services in Bengaluru for finance and human-resources process work in 2022 and 2023, Dell for information technology work in 2021 — and the Boeing positions are eliminated while the work continues elsewhere, a distinction the ledger preserves in the round sections because the human and documentary consequences differ: outsourced staff may be offered roles with the incoming contractor, and no WARN filing reliably captures a transfer. Divestiture removes whole businesses: the October 2024 programme named subsidiary sales among its levers, and the 2025 sales of the digital aviation services businesses transferred their employees to the buyer, reducing Boeing's reported headcount with no separations at all at the moment of sale. Both mechanisms are recorded in this tracker when the company's announced figures include them, labelled as what they are, because a tracker that counted only pink slips would understate the company's announced reductions, and a tracker that counted transfers as layoffs would overstate its separations. The labels, once again, are the compromise the evidence supports.

**Reductions outside the United States.** The machinery described above is American, because Boeing's centre of gravity is American, but the company employs about 14 percent of its workforce abroad and its rounds have international components that run on local law and leave thinner records. India's position in this ledger is the standing example: the Bengaluru engineering centre took about 180 cuts in March 2025 as part of the 2024 programme, documented in Indian and wire reporting rather than in any filing, because India has no WARN equivalent that publishes site-level notices; and the same city received the finance and human-resources process work whose transfer eliminated positions in the United States in 2022 and 2023. A single location can therefore appear on both sides of the ledger — gaining transferred work in one round, losing programme work in another — and the tracker records each appearance on its own evidence rather than netting them. European and other international reductions in Boeing's rounds surface, where they surface at all, through works-council processes and local reporting of a grade below the American record. The sourcing labels in the round sections mark that gradient wherever an international figure is used, and the cumulative table's totals inherit the caution: they sum announced and documented figures whose documentary quality varies by geography, which is a property of the company's footprint, not an error in the arithmetic.

**The sequence of a Boeing round.** The five rounds in this ledger, for all their differences of cause and scale, executed in a recognisable order, and the order is recorded here because it tells a reader where in the sequence a newly announced round currently stands. First comes the signal: a production-rate revision, a programme decision, a function's transformation mandate — usually visible in the company's statements weeks or months before any workforce figure exists. Then the announcement, which at Boeing takes one of three forms: a chief executive's message for company-wide actions, a division head's memo for divisional ones, or a statement to the press for function-level ones. Then, for represented populations, the union phase: briefings at which the operative facts — notice dates, the presence or absence of a voluntary programme, the targeting principle — are established, and at which the unions ask the questions their members will ask, on the record. Then notices, running on the WARN clock where the thresholds are met, with the state filings appearing as the notices land. Then the payroll interval, in which notified employees remain on the books, generally without working. Then separation, severance under the applicable system, and the start of recall rights for represented staff. And finally the filings' verdict: the workforce totals and severance charges in the next annual report, which confirm or complicate the announced figures a year or more after the event. The November 2026 round, at the time this tracker was created, stands between the union phase and the notices: announcement made, union briefed, December 9 set. A reader who knows the sequence knows what document arrives next, and this tracker's updates follow the same order — notices and filings first, totals when the filings carry them.

**How the company communicates a round.** Boeing's communication pattern across the five rounds is consistent enough to be part of the machinery. The announcement is always written before it is spoken: a chief executive's message, a division head's memo, or a statement issued to the press, in language that leads with the operational or financial fact and derives the staffing consequence from it. Individuals are never named in the announcement, and numbers, where the company gives them at all, are percentages or combined totals rather than site counts. Managers are briefed after the announcement and before the notices, and the unions are briefed in the same interval — in November 2026, ten days separated the memo from the SPEEA meeting at which the operative facts were established, and the company's managers at that meeting had not yet resolved the round's incidence, a gap the union placed on the record. Affected employees learn their own status at the notice stage, not the announcement stage: between the two, a Boeing round exists as a population-level fact that everyone in the perimeter can read and no one inside it can yet apply to themselves. That interval — announcement without names — is a deliberate feature of the company's method, visible in 2022's all-hands and reorganisation, in 2024's five weeks between announcement and first notices, and in 2026's month between memo and December 9. The tracker records the interval in each round section because it is part of what a round is, for the people inside it, before it is a number in this ledger.

**The paper trail.** A Boeing round leaves four kinds of record, in descending order of hardness. The WARN filings are hardest: state-published, site-specific, dated, and limited to the involuntary United States core. The company's filings are next: annual reports that fix the workforce totals and, in 2024, booked $295 million of severance for the programme, and the executive plan document that prices executive separations. The company's announcements are third: authoritative for intent, scope and stated reason, and — as the 2023 and 2026 rounds show — sometimes the only record of a round's total, expressed as a combined figure whose split the company keeps. The unions' communications are fourth and in one respect first: they are the only record written from inside the affected population, they publish the benefit terms the company does not, and in November 2026 they established the round's operative date before any filing existed. This tracker's sourcing labels exist to keep those four records distinct. When a future round is announced, the sequence to expect — on the evidence of the five rounds recorded here — is a company statement or a union communication first, WARN filings as notices land, and the financial filings last, with the executed total, if the company ever publishes one, arriving in the annual report of the following year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many layoff rounds has Boeing run since 2022?

Five rounds meet this tracker's evidence standard. In order: the September 2022 finance reductions (150 positions, company figure); the February 2023 finance and human resources reductions (about 2,000, company figure); the October 2024 company-wide programme (about 17,000 positions, company figure, executed in waves into 2025); the February 2025 Space Launch System reduction (160 to 200 roles, documented in WARN filings and a company statement); and the November 2026 Commercial Airplanes reduction (about 5 percent of the division, estimated at roughly 2,500 roles). A round is counted only when the company announced it, a WARN filing establishes it, or two independent outlets reported the same reduction of at least 100 roles with a consistent scope.

### Q: What is the largest layoff round Boeing has run since 2022?

The October 2024 programme, by a wide margin. Announced on October 11, 2024 by chief executive Kelly Ortberg, it targeted a reduction of about 10 percent of the company's total workforce — roughly 17,000 positions, including executives, managers and employees. It was executed in waves of WARN notices beginning in mid-November 2024 and continuing into 2025, across at least seven states in its first wave alone, and Boeing's annual report booked $295 million of severance in connection with it. The next largest round in the ledger, the November 2026 Commercial Airplanes reduction, is estimated at about 2,500 roles — roughly one seventh of the 2024 programme's announced size.

### Q: How many jobs is Boeing cutting in the November 2026 round?

Boeing has not said. The company's November 9, 2026 memo, from Commercial Airplanes chief executive Stephanie Pope, announced a reduction of approximately 5 percent of the division's staffing through attrition and involuntary layoffs, without a headcount. The division employed 50,494 people at the end of 2025, the most recent figure Boeing has published, so 5 percent works out to roughly 2,500 roles — an estimate produced by news outlets' arithmetic, not a company figure. This tracker carries it as an estimate and will amend it in place if WARN filings or a company statement establish the round's actual size. The split between involuntary layoffs and attrition inside the 5 percent has also not been disclosed.

### Q: Why is Boeing cutting jobs when it has a record order backlog?

Because its constraint is production, not demand. Boeing's commercial backlog stood at a record $612 billion in the third quarter of 2026, covering more than 6,300 aircraft, and the company booked 300 net orders in that quarter alone. But the factories cannot build at the rate the staffing assumed: the 737 MAX increase to 47 aircraft a month, planned for the summer of 2026, was pushed to early 2027, the step to 52 a month moved to the second half of 2027, and the 787 line held at about eight a month against a plan of ten. Division chief Stephanie Pope's memo put the logic in one line — adjusting production schedules and reducing output leaves the division overstaffed against its revised plan. The employees being released were largely hired or retained to build aircraft faster than the factories can now build them.

### Q: How much severance does Boeing pay?

It depends on which of Boeing's two systems covers the employee. For non-represented staff, the reported standard — documented in the 2024 programme — is about one week of pay for each year of service, with subsidized health care for up to three months and career transition services. For represented staff, the unions publish the schedules: SPEEA's table pays one week per year of service up to a maximum of 26 weeks for an ordinary layoff, and two weeks per year, between a 26-week minimum and a 60-week maximum, where work is moved rather than ended. The machinists' contract layoff provision, per the union's published guidance, pays one week of base rate plus the cost-of-living adjustment per full year, up to 26 weeks. Executives sit under a separate plan filed with the SEC: one year of base salary plus the target incentive amount.

### Q: How much notice do Boeing employees get before a layoff takes effect?

In the large rounds, about 60 days, because the federal WARN Act requires 60 calendar days of notice before a mass layoff and Boeing runs its big programmes on that clock: in the 2024 programme, notices delivered in mid-November 2024 carried a January 17, 2025 separation date, and notified employees generally remained on the payroll through the interval. State mini-WARN laws can add requirements in particular jurisdictions, and Boeing files state by state accordingly. Smaller rounds executed below the statutory thresholds, such as the finance reductions of 2022 and 2023, carry no WARN trail, and their notice followed company and contract practice rather than the statute. The November 2026 round follows the statutory pattern: announced November 9, 2026, first notices on December 9, 2026, with separations following the notice period.

### Q: What happens to health coverage after a Boeing layoff?

The documented pattern from the 2024 programme is subsidized health care benefits for up to three months after separation, with coverage continuing under COBRA thereafter at the former employee's expense. The union schedules differ in detail: SPEEA's published table provides three months of subsidized COBRA for an ordinary layoff and six months where work is moved, and the machinists' union guidance describes six months of medical coverage where the member keeps paying their share of the premium. The November 2026 memo commits the company to support through its Employee Assistance Program alongside layoff benefits and outplacement services. Specific terms attach to the contract or plan covering each employee, and the tracker records the documented schedules rather than a single company-wide answer, because none exists.

### Q: Are Boeing's layoffs governed by union contracts?

For about 40 percent of the workforce, yes — roughly 72,000 Boeing employees were union members at the end of 2025, according to the company's annual filing. The machinists of IAM District 751 build the commercial aircraft in the Puget Sound region; SPEEA represents about 17,000 engineers and technicians; other districts cover defence sites. Their contracts set the layoff benefit formulas, the seniority-based order of release, and recall rights, and the unions publish those terms themselves — which is why Boeing's represented severance is unusually visible. The remaining workforce is covered by the company's own layoff benefits plan. The unions are also an independent source of record: it was SPEEA's account of a November 2026 meeting with Boeing managers, reported by Reuters, that established the December 9, 2026 notice date and confirmed there would be no voluntary layoff programme in that round.

### Q: What are recall rights, and do Boeing employees have them?

Recall rights are a contractual promise that a laid-off employee will be offered re-employment in their classification before the company hires externally, for a period defined in the contract. Boeing's represented workforce holds them under its collective bargaining agreements, and they exist because Boeing's business is cyclical in a specific way: the company repeatedly cuts staff against a production rate it still intends to reach later, and then needs the same skills back when the rate arrives. The November 2026 round is a case in point — a reduction taken because the move to 47 aircraft a month slipped to early 2027, against a plan that still targets 52 a month in the second half of 2027. Whether the round's recipients return under recall when the ramp resumes is one of the measures by which the round will eventually be judged. Non-represented staff do not hold contractual recall rights of the same kind.

### Q: Did the 2024 machinists' strike cause the 17,000-job programme?

Boeing's chief executive said it did not. Kelly Ortberg told analysts in October 2024 that the strike had not caused the layoffs, which he described as a result of overstaffing, and the programme was framed as a financial reset — staffing aligned to the company's financial reality after years of losses and the regulatory cap on 737 production. The sequence is nonetheless part of the record: the programme was announced on October 11, 2024, while about 33,000 machinists were on strike, and the rolling furloughs Boeing had imposed on non-union staff during the strike were suspended once the permanent reductions were announced. The strike settled on November 4, 2024, and the first layoff notices — which fell mainly on salaried staff and engineers, largely sparing the returning machinists — followed about eleven days later. This tracker records the company's stated causation and the documented sequence side by side.

### Q: What do Boeing's WARN filings show, and what do they miss?

The filings show the involuntary, site-level core of the large rounds with statutory precision: the Washington filing of November 15, 2024 named 2,192 workers with a January 17, 2025 effective date; a second Washington filing added 396 that December; and aggregators count 62 Boeing notices between November 2024 and April 2026 naming 3,823 workers in the states that publish counts. The filings miss everything that is not a threshold-crossing involuntary separation at a United States site: attrition and unfilled roles, hiring freezes, international reductions such as the roughly 180 Bengaluru cuts of March 2025, employees transferred to buyers of divested businesses, and rounds executed below the thresholds, which includes both finance rounds in this ledger. For a programme executed through all of those channels at once, the WARN total is a floor, not a measure — which is why this tracker always states it as one.

### Q: Was the February 2025 Space Launch System round really 400 jobs?

No — 400 was the warning, not the outcome. On February 7, 2025, Boeing told its Space Launch System team that approximately 400 positions could be eliminated by April 2025 as the Artemis programme's future was reviewed. Five days later, after discussions with NASA, the company wrote that it had mitigated a majority of the announced reductions and expected to proceed with fewer than 200 involuntary layoffs, redeploying staff where possible. The WARN filings then documented 160 positions: 71 at Huntsville, Alabama and 89 at New Orleans, Louisiana, effective April 18, 2025. This tracker records the round at 160 to 200 — the filed floor and the company's stated ceiling — and treats the 400 figure as what it was, a pre-mitigation warning that negotiation with the customer reduced before the notices landed.

### Q: Why did Boeing outsource finance and human resources work to India?

The company's stated reason was simplification: the finance team was planning for lower staffing as it simplified processes, improved efficiency and shared select work with an outside partner, and about a third of the eliminated positions in the February 2023 round moved to Tata Consultancy Services in Bengaluru. The pattern was established before it was announced at scale — about 150 finance positions went in September 2022 under the same framing, and information technology work had been outsourced in earlier waves, including a 2021 move to Dell. Boeing drew a line between process work, which could be shared with a partner, and the generalist staff who work directly with employees, who stayed. The announced totals for both rounds are combined attrition-and-layoff figures; Boeing did not publish the split, and the tracker carries them as announced.

### Q: How does this tracker decide whether a Boeing reduction counts as a round?

Three evidence routes, any one of which qualifies a round: the company announces a workforce reduction itself, in a statement, memo, earnings communication or filing; a WARN Act or state mini-WARN filing by the company is on record; or two independent outlets report the same reduction of 100 or more roles with a consistent scope. Figures are taken from the strongest source available — filings first, then company statements, then reputable reporting — and estimates are labelled as estimates wherever they appear. Reductions that are known but fail the standard are named in the company snapshot as reported-but-unverified rather than silently omitted: the reported reduction of about 300 defence and space roles in February 2026, sourced to a single anonymous account and never confirmed by the company, sits in that category. Furloughs, being suspensions rather than separations, never qualify.

### Q: How can Boeing's total workforce rise in years when it is cutting thousands of jobs?

Because the rounds and the growth happen in different parts of the company at the same time. Boeing's reported workforce rose from about 142,000 at the end of 2021 to about 182,000 at the end of 2025, across the same years in which this ledger records roughly 21,800 announced reductions. The filings count the net of everything: the rounds, but also hiring campaigns that added about 15,000 people in 2022 alone, concentrated in engineering and manufacturing; ordinary attrition and rehiring; the Spirit AeroSystems acquisition, which returned roughly 13,000 Kansas employees to Boeing's perimeter in December 2025; and divestitures, whose staff transferred to buyers. The ledger counts roles eliminated in identified rounds; the filings count the net. Neither can be derived from the other, and this tracker never substitutes one for the other.

### Q: Were Boeing's 2024 furloughs during the strike counted as layoffs?

No. During the machinists' strike of September to November 2024, Boeing placed non-union employees on rolling temporary furloughs — unpaid suspensions of work, typically one week in four. A furlough is not a separation: the employment relationship continues, and most furloughed employees returned to work. The furloughs were suspended when the October 2024 reduction programme was announced, because permanent reductions replaced the temporary measure. This tracker excludes furloughs from the ledger by definition and records them in the 2024 round's section only because the programme's announcement referenced them. The same exclusion applied to workforce actions at Spirit AeroSystems during the strike, since Spirit was then a separate company and did not join Boeing until the acquisition closed in December 2025.

### Q: What severance do Boeing executives receive?

A separate and far larger benefit, documented in a filing rather than in reporting. The Boeing Executive Layoff Benefits Plan, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission as an exhibit to the company's annual report and amended effective January 1, 2025, provides an executive who experiences a layoff event with one year of base salary, plus the executive's annual target incentive amount calculated at the company's performance score for the year. Payment is made as a lump sum within a defined period after the event. Set against the reported standard of about one week of pay per year of service for non-represented staff below the executive level — capped, on the union-published schedules, at 26 weeks for an ordinary layoff — the executive plan is the most generous separation term in the company's structure, and it is the one term in this tracker sourced entirely to the company's own regulatory filing.

### Q: Has Boeing offered voluntary layoffs in these rounds?

Not in the rounds in this ledger — and in the newest round, explicitly not. Boeing's voluntary layoff instrument is historically important: in the 2020 pandemic programme, eligible employees in participating areas could volunteer for a lump sum of one week of pay per year of service, up to 26 weeks, with subsidized COBRA for a limited period, though the company could decline volunteers whose skills it needed. For the November 2026 Commercial Airplanes reduction, however, Boeing managers told SPEEA directly that there will be no voluntary layoffs or buy-outs; the round will be executed through involuntary layoffs and attrition only. The February 2023 and October 2024 actions likewise proceeded without a round-specific voluntary programme being announced. The tracker records the presence or absence of a voluntary offer in each round section, because it changes both who leaves and what the separations cost.

### Q: Which parts of Boeing are affected by the November 2026 round?

Only Boeing Commercial Airplanes — the division that builds the 737, 767, 777 and 787 — and within it, the targeting is by work statement rather than by site. Boeing managers told SPEEA that employment at all Commercial Airplanes sites is under review, that layoffs will not necessarily impact all sites, and that the cuts will focus on work statements that are ending or can be ended, rather than falling across the board. The defence and services divisions, both profitable in the third quarter of 2026, are outside the round. Staff in Wichita, Kansas and Tulsa, Oklahoma were told they are not being considered, according to trade-press summaries. Boeing has published no site or function distribution, and the December 9, 2026 notices and the WARN filings that follow them will provide the first documented distribution; this tracker will record them in the round's section as they appear.

### Q: How is this tracker updated when a new Boeing round is announced?

The new round is verified against the tracker's evidence standard first — a company announcement, a WARN filing, or two independent outlets reporting the same reduction of at least 100 roles — and the sources are recorded before anything is written. The round is then added at the top of the ledger as its own section, following the same template as every other round: announcement and effective dates, roles cut with unfilled roles stated separately, share of the workforce, divisions and geographies, the company's stated reason in its own framing, WARN filings, severance terms where reported, and a source line. The cumulative table gains a row and its totals are recomputed; the opening of the page is rewritten to reflect the new cumulative picture; any earlier statement the new round has made false is amended; and the date at the head of the page is updated. Nothing already in the ledger is removed, and a corrected figure is amended in place with the correction noted in its own section.
