## The Language of Time in Romeo and Juliet: The Verdict in Brief

The language of time in Romeo and Juliet is not decoration laid over a hurried plot. It is the mechanism that makes the plot feel hurried. Hours, minutes, weekdays, dawns, fortnights, summers, and feast days saturate the speech of every rank in Verona, from the Prince's proclamations to a nurse counting on her fingers, and the saturation is the argument of this article: the tragedy's famous tempo is produced at the level of the sentence before it is produced at the level of the event. Characters who face no deadline still count. A grandmotherly nurse dates a birth by an earthquake. Two old cousins at a feast dispute whether a shared memory is thirty years old or twenty-five. A girl waiting for news divides an hour into halves and finds each half an injury. The clock in this play is not an object on a wall. It is a habit of speech, and the habit runs in scenes where nothing is at stake, which is precisely why it is so frightening in the scenes where everything is.

The verdict argued through the pages that follow is therefore a claim about diction, not about plot mechanics. The compressed calendar of the action, five days from a Sunday brawl to a Thursday dawn, has been mapped often and well, and the haste that drives the characters from choice to catastrophe has its own full study elsewhere in this series. What has been less often isolated is the vocabulary itself: the specific words with which Verona names, divides, promises, and curses its hours. That vocabulary, catalogued scene by scene in the InsightCrunch time-words catalogue below, shows a pattern no timeline can show. Public time belongs to the day and is spoken in weekdays, bells, and cockcrows. Private time belongs to the night and is spoken in dawns awaited and dawns dreaded. Lovers count in hours; elders count in summers and decades; servants count in meals and curfews; and at the tomb, where counting can no longer change anything, the vocabulary inverts into its opposites, dateless, timeless, everlasting, as if the language itself gave up the count at the door.

Three consequences follow from the pattern, and each is defended in its own section. First, the play's smallest speeches carry its time-sense as reliably as its great set pieces: the Friar reads the hour off the sun's advance because his world owns no other instrument, and Juliet, three scenes later, is the only figure in the play who quotes a striking clock, which means the newest technology of time enters the tragedy in the mouth of its most impatient waiter. Second, the words for very soon, ere, anon, tomorrow, by and by, form a small vocabulary of promises, and the promises fail with a regularity that turns grammar into omen: nearly every appointment the play names by a word for soon is kept late, kept wrongly, or kept by a corpse. Third, the diction outruns the plot's need for it. A five-day action could be written with a fifth of this time-talk and lose nothing of its causality. The surplus is the meaning. Shakespeare did not merely set his story inside a short span; he built the shortness into the words, so that an audience hears the countdown even when the stage is showing a christening memory, a pastry order, or a joke about a clock's hand.

The critics who frame the argument are named where their claims are tested. Susan Snyder reads the catastrophe as mistiming generated by a comic structure, accident wearing the mask of fate. Emrys Jones reads the compression as deliberate scenic design, a formal architecture built by a dramatist in full control of his effects. M. M. Mahood, the indispensable critic of the play's diction, shows how single words split into double senses under pressure, and the time-words split widest of all. René Weis, whose Arden Third Series edition supplies every quotation in this article, cited by act, scene, and line, treats the play's internal calendar as a coherent system a reader can reconstruct day by day. The disagreement among these accounts, whether the time-scheme is inherited accident, designed architecture, or verbal behaviour, is adjudicated in its own section near the end, and the catalogue is the evidence the adjudication rests on. Quotation is kept brief throughout, because the subject is not any single line but the density of the whole verbal field.

One boundary is fixed at the outset and held throughout. This is a study of words and references, not of the haste theme in its causal form. Where haste is a mechanism, a deadline changes what a character does; where time-language is the subject, a word changes what an audience feels about a scene in which nothing may be decided at all. The distinction governs the choice of evidence. A speech earns its place in the catalogue by naming, counting, or figuring time, whether or not the naming moves the plot an inch. By that test the Nurse's earthquake arithmetic belongs beside the Friar's forty-two hours, and Capulet's three o'clock among the pastry orders belongs beside the Prologue's two hours of stage traffic. The play's tempo, the verdict runs, is the sum of such sentences. Verona does not merely run out of time. Verona talks, incessantly and in every register, as if it already had.

A word on method, since the verdict depends on it. The catalogue counts naming, not mentioning in passing: a passage is entered when a speaker performs an act of timekeeping, fixing a duration, an hour, a day, a season, or a deadline, or when a figure of speech takes its vehicle from the calendar or the clock. Casual scenery, a night that is merely dark, earns no row. The strictness matters because the saturation claim would be trivial if every dawn counted; dawns are the small change of Renaissance verse. What is not small change is the behaviour the strict count still records, a city in which a grandmother dates a birth by an earthquake before she will name an age, a father checks the weekday before he will name a bridegroom, and a bride counts a messenger's absence in fractions before she will name her fear. Under the strict rule the catalogue still fills twenty-one rows across all five acts, and the rows still cluster nowhere. That is the finding the rest of the article interprets, and its modesty is its strength: even counting only acts of timekeeping, the play cannot stop performing them.

## A Scale of Units: From Summers to a Lightning Flash

The first fact the catalogue establishes is the range of the scale. Romeo and Juliet names nearly every unit of duration its century possessed, and it distributes them by speaker with an consistency that amounts to a social grammar of time. At the top of the scale stand the units of the old: years, summers, decades. Capulet, refusing Paris in the first act, measures his daughter's readiness in harvests: "Let two more summers wither in their pride, / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride" (1.2.10-11). The unit is agricultural and patient, a summer being the smallest span in which a girl, like a crop, can visibly change. At the feast, the same generation counts in decades and quarrels over the count, Capulet insisting that the masque he and his cousin shared was "some five and twenty years" ago against his cousin's "thirty years" (1.5.34-41), the gap between the two figures itself a small essay on how the old experience duration, as a store of disputed anniversaries rather than a sequence of dates.

Below the year, the play's working unit is the day, and below the day, the hour, and it is in the descent from day to hour that the lovers live. The day belongs to the feud, to markets, brawls, weddings, and law; it is the unit in which appointments are made and sentences pronounced. The hour belongs to waiting, wooing, and dread. Benvolio's first question about his friend is a question about hours: "What sadness lengthens Romeo's hours?" and Romeo answers in the same coin, "Not having that, which, having, makes them short" (1.1.160-161). Before the lovers have met, the play has already established its emotional physics in a single exchange: love is the force that changes the length of an hour, and the whole tragedy will be an experiment in how short, and then how long, an hour can be made to feel. Romeo's first line in the play is a time complaint, "Ay me, sad hours seem long" (1.1.159), and his beloved's first extended speech about him will be a demand that the night hurry. Between those two poles, dilation and compression, the entire vocabulary oscillates.

The scale does not stop at the hour. It descends to the half hour, the span Juliet uses in Act 2, Scene 5 for the Nurse's promised return, and it descends below measurement altogether to the lightning flash, the unit Juliet chooses when she wants a figure for a joy that ends before it can be named: the contract is "too like the lightning, which doth cease to be / Ere one can say 'it lightens'" (2.2.119-121). The line is the play's most exact piece of time-diction, because it measures an event against the duration of a spoken sentence: the flash lasts less long than the words required to report it. A dramatist thinking in such units is not decorating a story about haste. He is building a scale on which the lovers' whole experience can be weighed, from the summers their fathers would have given them to the sub-verbal instant in which their joy, by Juliet's own figure, is spent.

Equally telling is the unit the scale lacks. The second, the modern atom of clock-time, has no place in the play's vocabulary; the reckoning of the 1590s had no use for it in ordinary speech, and Shakespeare's Verona divides its hours by halves and by the time a sentence takes, not by numbered instants. The absence matters because it defines the texture of the play's urgency. This is not the precision of a stopwatch. It is the precision of a world that tells time by bells, birds, light, and appetite, and that experiences a three-hour wait as three long hours, in Juliet's phrase, because no smaller denomination exists in which to file the pain. When the play does reach for exactness, it reaches for the hour and the weekday, the two units its public world can verify, and the tension between those verifiable units and the lovers' unverifiable instants, flashes, eternities, is the tension the whole diction keeps re-enacting.

### Which end of the time scale, the summer or the lightning flash, does the play trust more?

The play trusts neither, and distributes its trust by generation: summers are the unit of speakers who believe time is plentiful and will be obeyed, while the flash is the unit of speakers who have learned that time answers to no one, so the scale itself becomes a map of who still believes the calendar is on their side.

The distribution can be checked against any scene in the catalogue. Give a character a long unit, a summer, a fortnight, eleven years since an earthquake, and the character is old, settled, and speaking of the past. Give a character a short unit, an hour, a half hour, a flash, and the character is young, in love, or in danger, and speaking of a future that is already escaping. The Friar, the play's great border figure, is the exception that proves the grammar: he alone moves fluently up and down the whole scale, reading dawn off the sun like a countryman, prescribing a sleep of "two and forty hours" like an apothecary (4.1.105-106), and warning in proverbs that compress the scale into a single antithesis, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94). His fluency is his office. Everyone else in Verona is trapped at one end of the scale, and the tragedy can be retold as the story of what happens when the people of the hour are handed over to the people of the summer, and neither party can read the other's clock.

## The Counting of Hours: Act 2, Scene 5 and the Arithmetic of Waiting

The brief for this article names one passage as mandatory, and the passage earns the honour: the opening of Act 2, Scene 5, where Juliet, alone, performs the play's only sustained act of clock-counting. "The clock struck nine when I did send the Nurse; / In half an hour she promised to return" (2.5.1-2). Every element of the line deserves weight. The clock is a striking clock, a public instrument whose strokes can be heard across a neighbourhood, and it is the only such clock the play ever quotes. Juliet does not guess the hour from the light, as the Friar does, or from a bird, as she will at dawn in Act 3. She cites an instrument, and she cites it as evidence in an indictment: the promise was half an hour, the elapsed time is measurable, and the measurement is the charge. The scene that follows, in which the Nurse keeps Juliet waiting for news of her wedding while complaining of her bones, has its own full study in this series, [the scene study of the Nurse's delay and Juliet's torment of waiting](/2023/08/25/act-2-scene-5-nurse-delays-news/); the concern here is narrower, the arithmetic itself, the words in which a fourteen-year-old prosecutes an old woman's lateness.

The arithmetic escalates as the speech proceeds, and the escalation is the scene's real action. Juliet converts the wait into a proportion: "Now is the sun upon the highmost hill / Of this day's journey, and from nine till twelve / Is three long hours, yet she is not come" (2.5.10-11). Nine till twelve: the subtraction is a schoolroom exercise, and its very simplicity is the wound. A child can do this sum, and the sum says the promise has been broken six times over. Note the adjective the calculation earns: not three hours but "three long hours", the hour stretched by the waiting it contains, the same dilation Romeo complained of in his first scene, now given a numerator and a denominator. Then the speech divides the hour against itself. Love's messengers, Juliet argues, should not walk at all: "Love's heralds should be thoughts, / Which ten times faster glide than the sun's beams" (2.5.4-5). Ten times faster than light: the girl who has just counted three hours in strokes and sums is already pricing her desire in multiples of the sun's speed, and the two registers, the clerk's arithmetic and the poet's hyperbole, collide in a single speech because waiting has made both of them necessary. The clock tells her how long. Only the hyperbole tells her how it feels.

Set the passage against the play's other hour-counts and its singularity sharpens. Romeo in Mantua receives the news of Juliet's death and answers with a defiance addressed to the stars, not with any count of the days of his exile; the play never tells its audience, in his mouth, how long he has been gone. Capulet, setting the wedding, counts in weekdays, the coarse unit of a man arranging caterers. The Friar counts the potion's sleep in hours, but his count is a prescription, a professional's dosage, delivered in the level tone of a man reading a label. Juliet's count in Act 2, Scene 5 is the only one performed as suffering, arithmetic done aloud by the person the sum injures. That is why the passage anchors any study of the play's time-diction: it shows the vocabulary doing emotional work that no plot summary can reproduce, since a summary can report that the Nurse was late, but only the words can show a mind converting lateness into fractions, proportions, and multiples of sunlight.

### Why does Juliet measure the Nurse's absence in hours and half-hours instead of simply calling it long?

Because measurement is her only available action: she cannot go, send again, or hurry the messenger, so she counts, and the counting converts helplessness into evidence, giving her a case to prosecute the moment the Nurse appears, which is why the speech reads as an indictment being prepared rather than a complaint being vented.

The passage also fixes the play's clock in its historical moment, and the fixing is worth a paragraph of its own. A striking clock in a private speech was still a novelty of urban life in the 1590s; most of Shakespeare's first hearers told the hour by church bells and by the sun, and the word Juliet uses for the appointment she made the night before, "o'clock", was itself a recent contraction, of the clock worn smooth by use. The play thus gives its most modern instrument to its youngest speaker, and gives it to her indoors, in a chamber, in a speech about a promise. The elders of Verona conduct their time business in public units, weekdays proclaimed, feasts remembered, curfews rung. The new time, private, numerical, and merciless, belongs to the generation that will die by it. When the same Juliet, one act later, stands at a window arguing that the bird she heard was the nightingale and not the lark, she is fighting to return to the old time, the time told by creatures and by light, in which an hour more or less might still be negotiated. The clock of Act 2, Scene 5 does not negotiate. It struck nine. It will strike twelve. The diction has already told the audience which of the two times will win.

## Weekdays and Wedding Days: The Calendar Spoken Aloud

If the hour is the lovers' unit, the weekday is Verona's public one, and the play names its weekdays with a precision it grants nothing else in its calendar. No month is ever named for the action. No year is ever breathed. But the days of the week are spoken, checked, corrected, and bargained over in open court, and the sequence they form, Sunday's brawl to Thursday's dawn, is the skeleton every reconstruction of the action hangs on. The full reconstruction, with its famous cruxes and its editor-by-editor fortunes, belongs to [the study of the play's five-day timeline and how the days are distributed](/2022/01/28/romeo-and-juliet-timeline-five-days/). The concern here is the naming itself: what it means that this play, alone among the habits of its author's stage, keeps telling its audience what day of the week it is.

The naming reaches its climax in Act 3, Scene 4, the smallest great scene in the play, where Capulet settles his daughter's marriage in a handful of lines and the whole exchange is conducted in weekday diction. He opens with a confession phrased as a time shortage: "Things have fall'n out, sir, so unluckily, / That we have had no time to move our daughter" (3.4.1-2). Then comes the play's only direct question about time, and it is a question about the day, not the hour: "What day is this?" Paris answers, "Monday, my lord", and Capulet laughs the laugh of a man doing arithmetic he enjoys: "Monday! Ha, ha! Well, Wednesday is too soon. / A Thursday let it be" (3.4.19-21). Wednesday is rejected not as a date but as an interval, too soon being a judgement about the distance between Monday and the middle of the week, the time needed to prepare a feast and to soften a daughter. Thursday is chosen as a compromise between haste and decency, and Paris, the suitor whose desire has been kept at the level of a formal suit for three acts, answers with the lovers' own grammar breaking through the courtier's: "My lord, I would that Thursday were tomorrow" (3.4.29). For one line the weekday system and the desire system occupy the same sentence, and the audience hears how thin the wall between them is.

### Why does Capulet name the wedding day by its weekday rather than by its date?

Because Verona keeps its calendar socially rather than numerically: a weekday carries its cargo of custom with it, market days, fast days, the day the cooks can be hired and the day the kin can travel, so naming Thursday summons a whole timetable of obligations that a bare number on a month would leave blank.

The system's most revealing moment is a slip. Capulet, having set Thursday, sends Paris off with instructions and names the day wrong, Wednesday, then catches himself within the scene's closing lines and corrects the appointment back to Thursday. The early texts preserve the stumble, and editors from the eighteenth century onward have debated whether it is a character's error, an author's unrevised draft, or a deliberate dramatization of a mind outrunning its own arrangements. For a study of diction the debate has a simpler yield: the mistake is only possible, and only noticeable, because the play has trained its audience to keep the weekdays as carefully as the characters do. A slip in a calendar nobody was tracking would pass in silence. Shakespeare makes the slip audible, and in doing so confesses the design: the weekdays are load-bearing, and the audience is meant to feel the load shift when a tired old man, at past midnight, moves his daughter's wedding with the wrong name on his lips.

What the weekday system cannot do is as instructive as what it can. It can schedule a wedding; it cannot schedule a rescue. When the Friar takes over the calendar in Act 4, he speaks the system's language back to it with a professional's precision, telling Juliet, "Wednesday is tomorrow; / Tomorrow night look that thou lie alone" (4.1.89-90), and attaching to that weekday the play's most exact duration, the "two and forty hours" of the potion's sleep (4.1.105-106). The plan is a masterpiece of the public calendar: a named day, a named night, a numbered span, a waking timed to a Thursday morning when the bridegroom comes. Every unit in it is verifiable, and every unit in it fails, not because the arithmetic is wrong but because the arithmetic cannot govern the one errand that runs on no calendar at all, a letter carried by a man who can be stopped by a locked door and a suspicion of plague. The weekday system is the sound of a world that believes scheduling is control. The play lets that world speak its belief in its purest form, in names of days and counts of hours, the better to break the belief on a contingency no weekday can name.

There is a quieter weekday fact worth recording before the section closes. The lovers themselves almost never name a weekday. They meet on a Sunday night, marry on a Monday afternoon, part on a Tuesday dawn, and die in the small hours of a Thursday, yet in their own mouths the days go unlabelled; their calendar is made of nights, three of them, and of the dawns that end them. The naming of days is left to fathers, suitors, friars, and nurses, the officers of the public world. The distribution is a diction fact with an argument inside it: to name a weekday is to file an event in the city's ledger, and everything the lovers most value is precisely what cannot be filed. When Juliet finally does speak the public calendar, it is in the false confession to her father, promising obedience for a Thursday she intends to spend in a vault. The word Thursday in her mouth is a borrowed uniform, and the audience, trained by then to hear who owns which units, hears the borrowing.

## Telling Time Without Clocks: Dawns, Cocks, Bells, and the Friar's Garden

Against Juliet's striking clock the play sets an older instrumentation, the repertoire by which a pre-mechanical world reads the hour off the creation. The repertoire deserves a section of its own, because its vocabulary, dawns, cocks, bells, larks, curfews, is the medium in which most of the play's time is actually told, and because the tragedy can be heard as the story of this repertoire failing: sign after sign is given, and sign after sign is argued with, misread, or outpaced.

The repertoire's master is the Friar, and his entrance sets its terms. He comes on at dawn with a basket, and his first speech is a lesson in reading the sky as a dial: "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night, / Check'ring the eastern clouds with streaks of light" (2.3.1-2), and then, the practical point of the observation, "Now, ere the sun advance his burning eye, / The day to cheer and night's dank dew to dry, / I must up-fill this osier cage of ours / With baleful weeds and precious-juiced flowers" (2.3.5-8). The hour is told by a process, the sun advancing, the dew drying, and the telling is inseparable from the work it schedules: certain plants must be gathered while the virtue is in them, which is to say that the Friar's pharmacology runs on a solar clock whose units are changes in the world rather than strokes on a bell. His is the play's most accomplished natural timekeeping, serene, skilled, and entirely without numbers, and the serenity is the point. A man who reads time off the morning believes the world announces itself in good time to anyone attentive. The play will spend four acts refuting him with events that announce themselves too late, in Mantua, in a churchyard, in a tomb, to audiences of one.

### What tells the Friar the hour in his garden, if no clock speaks there?

The advancing sun and the state of the dew tell him, light doing the work of hands on a dial: his hour is a visible process rather than a numbered point, which is why his timekeeping carries a philosophy with it, the belief that a world readable at a glance is a world that gives warning before it strikes.

The household repertoire is cruder and louder. In Act 4, Scene 4, in the small hours of the wedding morning, Capulet bustles among the pastry and the keys and calls the time from the yard: "Come, stir! Stir! Stir! The second cock hath crowed, / The curfew bell hath rung, 'tis three o'clock" (4.4.3-4). Three separate instruments are stacked in two lines, cockcrow counted in sequence, bell rung at its appointed hour, and then, the newest note of all, the hour named in the clock's own idiom, three o'clock, in a kitchen, over an order for quinces. The comedy of the scene depends on bustle, but the diction is doing quiet historical work: the striking clock that Juliet quoted in her chamber has cousins in the street, and even a feast is now run by numbered hours. Yet the same scene preserves the older order inside the new, the cock and the bell flanking the numeral like bodyguards of an older regime. Verona in these lines is a city mid-conversion between two systems of time, and the play's catastrophe will be executed in the gap between them, too fast for the bells, too slow for the letter.

The repertoire's supreme scene is the dawn parting of Act 3, Scene 5, where two timekeepers are set against each other and the lovers argue over which one has sounded. Juliet's case is ornithological and desperate: "It is not yet near day. / It was the nightingale, and not the lark, / That pierced the fearful hollow of thine ear" (3.5.1-3). The nightingale sings by night; the lark sings at dawn; the entire question of whether Romeo lives or dies is thereby reduced to the identification of a bird, and both speakers know the identification is a fiction maintained by consent. Romeo concedes the fact and draws the sentence in a single antithesis: "I must be gone and live, or stay and die" (3.5.11). No scene in the play shows the old repertoire's dignity and its uselessness more exactly. Birdsong is a clock that can be argued with, and lovers will argue with it, and the argument itself consumes the very margin the clock was measuring. When the Nurse bursts in with the news that Lady Capulet approaches, the debate ends not because the lark has won but because the household's time, footsteps on a stair, has overridden the garden's. Verona keeps at least three times at once, natural, domestic, and mechanical, and its tragedy is among the first in English to be timed by all three and saved by none.

The repertoire has a civic branch as well, and its instruments are the curfew and the watch. The curfew bell that Capulet hears at three o'clock in Act 4, Scene 4 is a survival with its meaning inverted: the bell that once ordered fires covered and streets emptied at evening has become, in the wedding household's small hours, merely one more marker in a night of labour, rung and noted and worked through. The watch, the city's human clock, closes the play: it is the Watch that discovers the bodies, summons the Prince, and stands over the reckoning in Act 5, Scene 3, men whose office is to patrol the hours reporting at last on what the hours contained. Between the curfew and the watch the civic repertoire frames the action, bells at the beginning of the night's work and watchmen at the end of the night's crime, and its diction is the plainest in the catalogue, times named without figure or complaint. The plainness is a kind of verdict in itself. The city's instruments never fail to sound. What fails, every time, is the hearing: a bell rung over a household too busy to stop, a watch that arrives, punctually and uselessly, after the last appointment has been kept by the dead.

Arguments about density stand or fall on the record, so the record is set out here in full. The InsightCrunch time-words catalogue lists, scene by scene, the play's principal acts of time-naming: every passage in which a character counts, names, promises, or figures a duration, an hour, a weekday, or a season. It is a catalogue of diction, not of plot; an entry earns its row by its words, whether the words move the action or merely colour it. Readers assembling the catalogue into teaching materials will find that a structured deck renders the rows more legibly than a printed page, and the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) serves that purpose well when the table is carried into a seminar. What the assembled rows show, and what no single speech can show alone, is distribution: the time-words are not clustered in the famous scenes of haste but spread across all five acts and every social level of the city, thickest where the plot is calmest. The christening memories of Act 1, Scene 3 carry as many time references as the duel scene. The pastry orders of Act 4, Scene 4 carry a struck hour. Saturation, not clustering, is the phenomenon, and saturation is what the verdict of this article rests on: a dramatist who needed only a countdown would have saved his time-words for the countdown. This dramatist spent them everywhere.

Read down the table's middle column and a second pattern emerges beneath the distribution. The play's time-words migrate. In the first two acts they belong to courtship and household management, appointments, ages, memories. In the third act they are conscripted by the feud and the marriage market, weekdays and tomorrows spoken by men arranging other people's lives. In the fourth they become instruments of a plot, a potion's hours, a kitchen's o'clock. In the fifth they thin out almost to nothing, a defiance, a report with the numbers missing, and then flower once, terribly, into the language of no-time at all. The catalogue is therefore also a biography of the vocabulary: it is born in memory, works in appointments, is abused in scheduling, and dies into eternity. A timeline can record that the action takes five days. Only the diction can record that the words for time age, harden, and expire alongside the lovers.

## Earthquakes, Teeth, and Thirty Years: How the Old Count Time

Every student of the play meets the Nurse's chronology in Act 1, Scene 3, and most meet it as comedy: an old woman dating her charge's birth by an earthquake, a weaning, and the state of her own mouth. "I'll lay fourteen of my teeth, / And yet, to my teen be it spoken, I have but four, / She is not fourteen" (1.3.13-15). The joke is real, but the system underneath it is exact, and it deserves to be read as the play's fullest exhibit of how the old Verona keeps time. The Nurse's units are events in a body and a household. Juliet will be fourteen "come Lammas-eve at night" (1.3.23), a feast day, the first of August, when the first fruits are blessed: the birthday is filed not under a number in a month but under a celebration the whole parish keeps. The interval since the weaning is not a count of years but a memory with a date attached by disaster: "'Tis since the earthquake now eleven years; / And she was weaned, I never shall forget it, / Of all the days of the year, upon that day" (1.3.24-26). And the warrant for the whole computation is the speaker's own decaying instrument, the four teeth left to swear by. Time for the Nurse is not an abstraction that passes. It is a sequence of things that happened to her, and her chronology is accurate in the way of all oral calendars: the earthquake did happen, the weaning did follow it, and the sum, eleven years added to a weaned child's age, lands within a day of the truth.

Lady Capulet's contribution to the same scene is four words long and belongs to the opposite pole of the old world's practice: "A fortnight and odd days" (1.3.16). Where the Nurse expands, the mistress compresses; where the Nurse's unit is the parish feast, the mother's is the fortnight, the household's fortnightly accounting period, wages and rents and provisions all reckoned in such spans. Between the two women the scene assembles a complete pre-modern calendar in miniature: feast day, fortnight, earthquake year, the age of a child counted against the age of a servant's teeth. Nothing in the scene advances the plot by a single minute, since the marriage proposal that occasions it could be made without any of this arithmetic. The arithmetic is there because Shakespeare's Verona cannot open its mouth on the subject of a girl's marriage without first establishing, in four different systems, exactly where in time she stands. The saturation thesis rests on scenes like this one: time-diction lavished on exposition, at a density the action never requires.

The feast scene adds the masculine version of the same habit. Capulet and his cousin, watching the dancing, fall to disputing the date of their own last masque, thirty years against twenty-five, and Capulet settles the matter not by a year but by another wedding: "'Tis since the nuptial of Lucentio, / Come Pentecost as quickly as it will, / Some five and twenty years, and then we masqued" (1.5.38-40). A marriage and a church feast are the pins in his calendar too. The old men and the old nurse, for all their differences of rank and sex, keep time identically, by events in the community's shared memory, weddings, earthquakes, harvests, feasts. It is a calendar of high accuracy and low resolution: it can place an event within a season across thirty years, and it cannot tell anyone what o'clock anything is. The young, who need resolution, who must know whether the Nurse has been gone for half an hour or three, are already living in a different instrument's world, and the play's generational tragedy is inscribed in the difference. The old will set the young a wedding by the weekday and think the appointment precise. The young will count the strokes of nine and twelve and find the appointment a wound. Both are counting. Neither can read the other's figures, and no one in the play ever translates.

## Words for Very Soon: Ere, Anon, Tomorrow, and the Promises That Fail

A small family of words does the play's most dangerous work, the words that mean very soon. Ere, anon, by and by, presently in its period sense of at once, and above all tomorrow: these are the adverbs of appointment, the vocabulary in which Verona makes its promises to itself. A study of the play's diction eventually notices what a study of its plot notices faster, that the promises fail. But the diction study notices something the plot study cannot: the failures are grammatical before they are circumstantial. The words for soon in this play are systematically weaker than the words for now and the words for never, hedged, deferring, and borrowed, and the catastrophe can be retold as the history of a community that kept scheduling its salvation in adverbs.

Tomorrow is the family's head and its chief casualty. The lovers adopt it first, in the balcony scene, as the day of their appointment: "At what o'clock tomorrow / Shall I send to thee?" "By the hour of nine" (2.2.167-169). The exchange is the play's most businesslike, an hour named, a messenger promised, and it is kept, the one tomorrow in the play that arrives as billed, which may be why the audience is so unprepared for the word's later career. Mercutio takes it up in the duel and bends it into prophecy: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1.99-100), the appointment kept with terrible punctuality by a corpse. Paris wishes a Thursday were tomorrow and is answered by a calendar that gives him a Wednesday funeral instead. The Friar builds his rescue on the word, "Wednesday is tomorrow" (4.1.89), and the tomorrow he names does come, but it comes to a girl already laid in a vault and to a letter already sealed in a quarantined house. By the fifth act the word has been so thoroughly dishonoured that Romeo abandons the calendar of days altogether and addresses the stars, the older and colder keepers of appointment, only to defy them too. The trajectory of a single adverb, from appointment to irony to epitaph, is the tragedy in miniature, and it is a trajectory only a diction study can plot, since no single scene contains more than one station of it.

### What do the play's words for "very soon" actually promise, and how often is the promise kept?

They promise arrival inside a span too short to verify, an hour, a night, a day, and the play keeps the promise exactly once, at the balcony appointment, thereafter breaking it in ascending order of cost: a messenger three hours late, a suitor buried on his wedding morning, a rescue that reaches its sleeper minutes after her watcher has died.

The lesser members of the family work the same trade at smaller scales. Ere, the play's word for before, is the preposition of the threshold: Capulet uses it for his daughter's ripening, "ere we may think her ripe to be a bride" (1.2.11), Juliet for the lightning that ceases "ere one can say 'it lightens'" (2.2.120), the Friar for the sun's advance in his garden (2.3.5). In each case ere marks the narrow gate between a present state and its replacement, and in each case the gate is crossed faster than the speaker expects: the girl ripens into a wife within a day of the speech, the flash is gone before the sentence, the sun is up and the lovers are married before the herbs are gathered. Anon, Juliet's call across the balcony scene, "Anon, good nurse! Sweet Montague, be true" (2.2.141), promises an instant and delivers an interruption, the Nurse's voice from within, the first of the play's many demonstrations that in Verona the word for at once is the usual herald of a delay. Even the Friar's great proverb works by the family's logic in reverse: "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94) is the one piece of time-counsel in the play that proposes to defeat the adverbs by refusing them, and it is spoken to the young man who, within the scene, answers with the family's own counter-creed, "I stand on sudden haste" (2.3.93 in most numberings). The play's wisdom about soon is all on one side, and its practice is all on the other, and the distance between the proverb and the practice is measured, fittingly, in the hours of Act 2, Scene 5, where a promise of half an hour is redeemed at three.

One more member of the family deserves its line, because it is the family's traitor: the word late itself, which changes sides as the play proceeds. Early, late means no more than tardy, the Nurse is late, the hour is late. By the middle acts the word has acquired its second sense, recent, the late brawl, the late news. By the fifth it has arrived at its third and final sense, the sense in which English still files its dead: the late Juliet, the late Tybalt, persons spoken of in a past tense that no appointment can reach. Shakespeare does not announce the migration; no character remarks on it. But a reader who tracks the word down the catalogue watches the vocabulary of scheduling decay, inside five days of stage time, into the vocabulary of obituary, and the decay is the surest single proof that the play's tempo is a verbal achievement. Plots can be summarized in any words at all. This plot, summarized in its own words, tells the same story twice, once in events and once in the slow darkening of the language of soon.

## The Tomb's Anti-Time: Dateless, Timeless, Everlasting

Every system of words in the play meets its opposite somewhere, and the time-words meet theirs in the Capulet vault. The tomb scenes are saturated with duration language of a peculiar kind: words that do not measure time but abolish it, eternity-words, dateless, timeless, everlasting, for ever, planted in the mouths of the dying at exactly the point where the play's counting has run out of things to count. The inversion is too systematic to be accident. A play that has counted hours for four acts does not fall silent about duration at its climax; it changes sign, from arithmetic to its negation, and the change of sign is the sound of the countdown reaching zero.

Romeo's valediction is the exhibit. He enters the vault as the play's last appointment-keeper, a man who has ridden through the night on a timetable of his own making, and his speech sheds its numbers as it proceeds. What remains is a single coined negation addressed to death itself: the kiss is "a dateless bargain to engrossing death" (5.3.115). Dateless: without a date, outside the calendar that has governed every other contract in the play, the marriage contract, the Paris contract, the potion's contract of forty-two hours. Romeo, who made his wedding appointment by the hour of nine, seals his final bargain in a word that refuses all appointment, and the refusal is the tragedy's bleakest piece of diction, because it concedes what the whole vocabulary has been denying since the Prologue: that the calendar was never the lovers' instrument, only their persecutor, and that the one transaction it cannot touch is the one that ends them. Minutes earlier the same speaker had renounced the heavens' calendar outright, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), the stars being the oldest clock in his world, the keepers of the "fearful date" he had dreaded on the way to the feast (1.4.107). The defiance and the dateless bargain are one gesture spoken twice: a man resigning from every system that ever told him the time.

Juliet wakes into the same inverted vocabulary and completes it. Finding Romeo dead with the cup in his hand, her diagnosis of the scene is a time-word turned inside out: "Poison, I see, hath been his timeless end" (5.3.162). Timeless here carries its period weight, untimely, an end that came out of its season, and the line repays slow reading, because it makes the heroine the play's last and best reader of its own diction: what killed Romeo, in her phrasing, was not poison but a failure of timing, an end that arrived off the calendar. She then performs the only successful act of timekeeping in the fifth act, and it takes no time at all: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170). Against the Friar's forty-two hours, the Nurse's three, Capulet's weekdays, Romeo's night ride, the dagger's interval is the lightning flash of Act 2 made literal, an event that ceases to be ere one can say it lightens. The lovers' unit, announced in a figure of speech at their second meeting, is the unit in which the heroine dies. The diction had the ending in it from the start.

### Why does the language of time fall silent at the door of the tomb and return only as its opposite?

Because counting is the speech of people who still expect a future in which the count could matter: inside the vault no decision remains that a number could inform, so the vocabulary of measure collapses, and duration can be spoken of only in the words that deny it, dateless and timeless, until the Prince restores the calendar over the bodies.

The restoration, when it comes, is the play's final diction event and the coldest. The Prince closes the action not with an appointment but with a morning: "A glooming peace this morning with it brings; / The sun for sorrow will not show his head" (5.3.304-305). The sun, the Friar's dial, the lovers' enemy, the instrument by which every hour in the play has been told, is imagined refusing to rise, as if the natural clock itself had gone on strike at the sight of its own results. And the two fathers, who between them commanded the play's whole calendar of summers and weekdays, make their peace in a vocabulary from which time has been entirely withdrawn, the vocabulary of weight and metal: a statue in pure gold, a jointure answered in kind. Verona's last word on its children is not a date but a price. The catalogue that began with a Chorus promising two hours of traffic ends with gold, the one substance in the play that does not keep time because it does not decay, and the substitution is the verdict of the diction itself: a city that counted its hours so fluently, and spent them so badly, is left with nothing countable but its wealth.

## Time, Personified: The Witness Who Watched It Happen

One register of the vocabulary remains, the register in which time stops being a measure and becomes a person. The play personifies its other great abstractions freely, Death as a bridegroom, Night as a matron, Fortune as a fickle mistress, and the diction study of those figures belongs to a sibling article in this run. Time receives the same treatment more sparingly, and the sparingness is itself a datum: on the few occasions when Time walks on as a character, the speakers are at the ends of their resources, addressing the one witness who was present at everything and can be appealed to by no one.

The supreme instance is Lady Capulet's in the lament over Juliet's seeming corpse: "Most miserable hour that e'er time saw / In lasting labour of his pilgrimage!" (4.5.44-45). The figure deserves unpacking. Time is given eyes, a biography, and a journey: he has travelled long, in lasting labour, on a pilgrimage, and in all that travelling he has witnessed no hour as miserable as this one. The compliment to the grief is paid through the calendar: the hour is certified as a record, the worst entry in the longest ledger in existence. No other lament in the play attempts the figure, and its uniqueness is the measure of the moment, a mother reaching past every human comparison to the only observer whose experience is long enough to rank her loss. It also quietly completes the play's system. Hours in this play have been counted, cursed, promised, and wasted; here, for one line, an hour is entered into a chronicle kept by Time himself, and the chronicle-keeping is imagined as labour and pilgrimage, as if even Time found the five days heavy to carry.

Capulet, in the same scene's neighbourhood of the action, had already given the abstraction its other great line, and his is a creditor's version: the wedding arrangements open with his apology that events "have fall'n out, sir, so unluckily, / That we have had no time to move our daughter" (3.4.1-2). No time: the phrase is so familiar that its strangeness hides. A man with a palace, a household of servants, and a county's worth of connections pleads poverty in a single currency, hours, and the plea is the feud's whole economy confessed in four words. Verona's rich are time-poor, every one of them, and their poverty is not a condition of the plot's last week but a permanent feature of their speech, as available to Capulet at a christening negotiation as to Juliet in her chamber. The personifications and the idioms agree: time in this play is a substance that can be witnessed, spent, owed, and lacked, a character in the drama who never appears on the stage directions and is quoted by everyone.

That double status, measure and person, instrument and god, explains a feature of the diction that the catalogue records but does not by itself interpret: the play's time-words are so often sworn by, cursed, defied, and addressed. Stars are defied because they keep a calendar. Night is commanded because she keeps a household. The sun's horses are whipped because they keep a schedule. Apostrophe, the figure of addressing the absent, is the natural grammar of a world whose time is kept by persons rather than by machines, and the one striking clock in the play is never addressed at all: Juliet cites it, as evidence, the way a lawyer cites a document. The conversion traced across the whole vocabulary can now be stated in its final form. The play begins with a cosmos of persons keeping time, morn smiling, night frowning, steeds galloping, and ends with a numeral in a kitchen and a document in a girl's indictment, while the persons retire into metaphor and the metaphors into gold. The language of time in Romeo and Juliet is the record of a world learning to count, and discovering, in the same five days, everything that counting cannot hold.

## Critics at the Clock: Accident, Design, and the Word

The criticism of the play's time falls into three camps, and the camps disagree about the most basic question a diction study can ask: whether the saturation documented in the catalogue is an effect the play means, an effect it inherits, or an effect its words generate almost against its plot's intentions. The brief for this article requires the disagreement to be adjudicated, and the catalogue exists to make the adjudication possible.

Susan Snyder, in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies, gives the inheritance case its strongest form. On her account the play is built on a comic chassis: separated lovers, a benevolent friar, a bawdy go-between, a sequence of near-misses and crossed messages that in a comedy would resolve in the final act's marriages. What turns the machinery tragic is timing, accident arriving at the wrong hour with a regularity that begins to look like fate: a letter stopped by plague, a waking that misses its watcher by minutes, a suitor who reaches the tomb one scene too early. Snyder's catastrophe is thus an affair of mistiming administered by chance, and the time-scheme is on her reading less a designed pressure than the exposed wiring of the comic plot, the place where the audience can see how easily the happy ending was available and how narrowly it was missed. The reading explains the fifth act superbly. It explains less well why the first act, in which nothing can yet go wrong, is already dense with earthquakes, fortnights, and thirty-year-old masques.

Emrys Jones, in Scenic Form in Shakespeare, gives the design case. Jones reads the play as a structure in two great movements, its scenes arranged in patterns of anticipation and echo, its speed a function of an architecture the dramatist controls at the level of the scene rather than the sentence. On this account the compression of the source, in which the lovers' story sprawls across months, into a handful of days is a deliberate act of scenic engineering: the short span concentrates the action the way a lens concentrates light, and the sensation of countdown is the audience's registration of the design tightening. Where Snyder sees inherited machinery, Jones sees intention at exactly the point where the diction evidence bites hardest, because a dramatist compressing a calendar for structural reasons might be expected to mark the compression in his characters' mouths, and the catalogue shows the marking everywhere, in mouths and scenes the structure does not need.

M. M. Mahood, in Shakespeare's Wordplay, supplies the third term, and the adjudication turns on it. Mahood's subject is the word itself under pressure: the pun, the quibble, the single term that splits into two senses and forces a hearer to hold both. The time-words are among her richest territory in this play even where she does not isolate them as a class, because they split constantly: tomorrow, an appointment and a grave; late, tardiness decaying into death; an hour, a measure and a misery; a date, a day on a calendar and the fate the stars have entered against a name. On Mahood's account the tempo of the play is not only in its scheme or its scenes but in the experience of its language moment by moment, each time-word carrying a small double exposure, the appointment and the doom printed on the same plate. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, contributes the material evidence that steadies the whole dispute: the early texts transmit a calendar coherent enough to reconstruct day by day, and they transmit Capulet's Wednesday slip uncorrected, which on Weis's showing is best read as the dramatist writing a mind in real time, the calendar so present to the composition that its errors are the errors of a character and not of a copyist. Levenson's Oxford edition concurs in substance: the chronology is a system, and a system implies a systematist.

The adjudication, on the catalogue's evidence, runs as follows. Snyder wins the fifth act and loses the first. Her mistiming machinery is real, but it is the tragedy's payload, not its engine; a play that merely inherited a comic plot would distribute its time-talk where the machinery needs it, around the letter, the potion, and the waking, and this play distributes it with perverse evenness across christening memories and pastry orders. Jones wins the architecture and is confirmed by the diction at every point where the two can be compared: the compression is designed, and the design descends into the vocabulary, which is why scenes with no structural need for urgency are written in the language of countdown. Mahood wins the mechanism. Design explains that the time-words are there; only the splitting word explains how they feel, why a audience leaves the theatre with the sensation of having been hurried by sentences rather than by events. The verdict of this article therefore stands with Jones on intention and Mahood on effect, and assigns Snyder the true but partial province of the ending: the play's time is a designed verbal environment in which accident, when it comes, finds every word already ticking.

One further exhibit belongs in the record, because it is the exhibit editors handle with their hands. The play's calendar is reconstructible: the brawl falls on a Sunday, the marriage on the Monday, the duel and the parting across the Monday night and Tuesday dawn, the Paris wedding set for the Thursday and attempted on the Wednesday, the deaths in the small hours of the Thursday. Generations of editors, Weis and Levenson among them, have printed the scheme in their introductions as a settled feature of the text, and the settling matters for the adjudication, since an inherited muddle would not reconstruct. A dramatist adapting a source in which the lovers' story occupied months, and choosing to spend it in five days, had to keep the five days straight in his own head through composition, revision, and the copying of parts; the coherence of the result is the fingerprint of the intention Jones claims. But the same exhibit cuts the other way against any purely architectural reading, because the reconstruction is performed by readers, from words: the calendar exists nowhere in the play except in its diction, in a weekday named at midnight, a feast day remembered by a nurse, an hour struck offstage. The architecture is real, and it is built entirely of sentences. That is the ground on which the design case and the diction case meet, and on which this article's verdict finally rests: the play's tempo is an intention executed in vocabulary, and the vocabulary, once counted, is the intention's only surviving witness.

## The Body's Timetable: Dinners, School, Breath, and Sleep

Beneath the city's instruments and the lovers' nights runs a third timekeeping, the body's, and its vocabulary is the humblest in the catalogue: meals, school hours, breath, and sleep. Verona punctuates its days by eating. Romeo's first instinct on entering the play's opening brawl, a young man asking after a street fight in which his kinsmen's swords are still warm, is a question about dinner: "Where shall we dine? O me! What fray was here?" (1.1.104). The collision in a single breath, dinner and the fray, is the feud's domestic rhythm in miniature, violence fitted between meals as a household fits any errand. The feast that follows that night is timed by the same organ: an evening defined by courses, music, and dancing, at which a marriage market opens and a death sentence is, in effect, catered. By Act 4 the kitchen has become the play's truest clock-room, a household working through the night against a struck hour, spices and quinces ordered against a dawn, because a wedding, like a dinner, is an appointment the stomach keeps. When the bride is found dead, the feast's provisions are reclassified in a single sentence of Capulet's, the bridegroom become Death and the wedding meats become funeral baked meats in Hamlet's later echo of the same economy, and the reclassification is performed in the vocabulary of the table: nothing in the house has changed except the hour's meaning.

School keeps the second of the body's clocks, and the play reaches for it at the moment of purest joy. Romeo, leaving the balcony, measures love's motions against the one timetable every member of the first audience had served: "Love goes toward love as schoolboys from their books, / But love from love, toward school with heavy looks" (2.2.156-157). The simile is a small masterpiece of time-diction, because it assumes a shared phenomenology of duration: the hour before release flies, the hour before confinement crawls, and the same boy lives both hours in one day. No clock is mentioned and none is needed; the school bell is inside every hearer. That Shakespeare places the figure in Romeo's mouth at the summit of the balcony scene, rather than in any speech of complaint, confirms the article's running observation: the time-words are not reserved for the play's pain. They are the medium in which its pleasure is also reckoned, which is why their darkening across the acts registers as loss rather than as mere consistency of theme.

Breath keeps the third clock, and it is the one that fails. The Nurse returns from her errand to Romeo with the wedding news in her mouth and cannot deliver it, because her body is running on a slower instrument than her mistress's desire: "Do you not see that I am out of breath?" (2.5.26). The line is played for comedy, and the comedy is exact, since the scene has already established the rate at which the news ought to travel, thoughts ten times faster than the sun's beams, and now exhibits the rate at which it does travel, an old woman's lungs. Between those two rates, the sunbeam and the breath, the whole tragedy will be transmitted: every rescue in the play moves at the speed of a body, a friar walking, a servant riding, a letter carried in a man's hand through a plague town, while every desire moves at the speed of thought. The diction keeps the two rates grammatically distinct, assigning the vocabulary of light and flight to wanting, and the vocabulary of breath, bones, and aching joints to carrying, and the gap between the vocabularies is the space in which the catastrophe assembles itself.

Sleep keeps the last of the body's clocks, the only one that can be prescribed. The Friar's potion is a manufactured night, two and forty hours of borrowed death, and its horror in Juliet's soliloquy is precisely that it removes her from every instrument by which she has lived: no dawn will argue with her, no clock will strike for her, no breath will measure her, and her own appointment for waking is a figure in another man's notebook. Her farewell before she drinks is the play's one appointment made with no unit in it at all: "Farewell! God knows when we shall meet again" (4.3.14). After four acts of counting, the heroine steps out of the count entirely, and the play marks the step by giving her the vocabulary of pure uncertainty, a when with no number attached. Romeo in Mantua dreams his answering dream, his lady finding him dead and breathing him back to life, a sleep in which the body's clock runs backward and restores what the waking world has spent. Between her unitless farewell and his backward dream, the body's timetable completes its testimony: dinners, school bells, breath, and sleep are the instruments Verona actually lives by, and every one of them, in the tragedy's last two days, is interrupted, outrun, or drugged into silence.

## The Two Hours' Traffic: The Audience's Own Clock

The play's first act of time-naming is performed not by a character but by the Chorus, and it names not the fiction's time but the audience's: "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, 12). The phrase is usually glossed and passed over, a conventional apology for the performance's length, but in a play whose diction is elsewhere so exact about duration it deserves to be read as the catalogue's first and strangest entry. Before any Veronese has counted anything, the spectators have been told the size of their own commitment, two hours, and the metaphor chosen for those hours is a traffic metaphor, goods and persons moving through a space, with all the congestion the word implies. The play thus begins with two clocks running at once, the afternoon in the theatre and the five days in Verona, and the whole experience of the tragedy is the experience of those two clocks racing each other: the fiction must outrun the afternoon, four days and nights of life compressed into the span in which the audience will sit, stand, and go home to supper.

The compression ratio is the play's founding act of diction, because it is stated rather than merely performed. A dramatist who wanted only pace could have relied on pace; this one announces the figures, two hours against a story whose source gave it months, and lets the audience hold the ratio in mind through every subsequent count. When Juliet divides her three hours in Act 2, Scene 5, the house has already spent a measurable share of its own two, and the sympathy the scene generates is partly arithmetical fellowship: everyone present is counting something. When the second Chorus vanishes after Act 2 and no further report is made on either clock, the silence is itself a pacing device at the level of the frame, the management withdrawing its timekeeper and leaving the fiction to finish its days unannounced, at whatever speed dread dictates.

The frame also resolves, at the last, the question of which clock the play finally trusts. The Prince's closing speech makes no appointment and sets no term; its only duration is the story itself, "never was a story of more woe" (5.3.309), a quantity measured not in hours or days but in the telling, which is to say in the audience's two hours now completing. Verona's calendars have all failed or finished: the weekdays are spent, the potion's hours are spent, the stars are defied, the sun will not show his head. The one clock that has run true from the Prologue's promise to the Prince's epitaph is the performance's own, and the play's last act of time-diction is to hand that clock back to its owners, the spectators, who leave the theatre having spent exactly the span they were told they would spend, on a fiction about people who were promised spans of every size and kept none of them. A study of the play's time-language that omitted the frame would miss the entry that makes all the others legible: the audience is the play's first and last timekeeper, and the countdown it hears in Verona is tuned, from the twelfth line onward, to the afternoon it is sitting in.

## Diction, Not Haste: The Boundary of This Study

The brief for this article carries a complication, and the complication has been the discipline of every section: the haste theme owns the play's causality, and a study of time-diction must not re-derive it under a new name. The boundary can now be drawn with the whole vocabulary in view, and drawing it is the cleanest way to state what this article has and has not claimed. Haste, as the sibling study of the theme establishes, is a mechanism: it is what happens when decisions are taken inside spans too short for the information they need, and its evidence is the plot, the letter undelivered, the wedding advanced, the waking missed. [Time and Haste in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/01/12/time-and-haste-romeo-and-juliet/) argues that mechanism in full, and nothing in the present article should be mistaken for a second version of its case. The evidence here has been of a different order entirely: not what the characters decide, but what they say while deciding, and what they say when they are deciding nothing at all.

The test that separates the two studies can be applied to any passage in the catalogue. Remove the passage's time-words, and ask what is lost. In the haste scenes, something causal is lost: remove the forty-two hours and the Friar's plan has no shape. In the diction scenes, nothing causal is lost and everything atmospheric is: remove the earthquake and the teeth from the Nurse's speech and Juliet is still thirteen, the proposal still proceeds, but the scene's world, a Verona that keeps its past in its body, evaporates. The second kind of loss is this article's subject, and it is not a lesser kind. A play's tempo, as the companion study of the verse argues at the level of sound, [Verse Speed: How Sound Builds the Dread](/2025/07/11/verse-pacing-sound-dread-analysis/), is something an audience feels in the body before it understands it in the plot; diction is where that feeling is stocked. The countdown sensation that readers report from this play, the sense of a clock always running down, survives translation, survives prose summary, survives productions that cut the verse to ribbons, because it is carried in the nouns and adverbs, in nine and twelve and Thursday and tomorrow, and those are the last elements any adaptation dares to remove.

What, then, does the language of time finally do to the play's tempo? The verdict can be given in one sentence, and the catalogue is its proof. The plot supplies a short span; the diction supplies the sensation of the span, by making every character in Verona, at every level of society and in every register from proverb to pastry order, speak as a creature counting something, so that the audience inhabits a world in which time is never not being measured, promised, or mourned. Tempo in Romeo and Juliet is not the speed of the events. It is the density of the counting, and the counting never stops, which is why the play feels fast even in its stillest scenes, a christening party, a garden at dawn, a kitchen before a wedding, and why its final silence, two fathers pricing statues over the bodies, lands with the force of a clock stopped rather than a story ended. The haste theme explains how the lovers died. The language of time explains why an audience, four centuries of afternoons later, still feels the dying as a race it is losing alongside them.

## Three Nights and No Days: The Lovers' Private Calendar

The catalogue's distribution hides one further pattern that a section of its own can bring out: the lovers keep their accounts in a unit nobody else in Verona uses as a unit, the night. The public world counts days, and its ceremonies fall on them, brawls on Sunday, a wedding set for Thursday. The lovers' story, told in their own diction, is a story of three nights: the night of the feast and the balcony, the night of the marriage, and the night of the parting, with the tomb's night as the fourth that swallows the count. Their speeches return to the unit compulsively. Juliet's great invocation in Act 3, Scene 2 is an order placed with the night as with a merchant: "Come, night; come, Romeo; come, thou day in night, / For thou wilt lie upon the wings of night / Whiter than new snow on a raven's back" (3.2.17-19). Night is not when the meeting happens. Night is the meeting's substance, the thing summoned, loaded, and spent. Romeo's diction answers in kind from the balcony onward: the day is the medium of other people's business, of fairs and feuds and fathers, and his own affairs are transacted in a currency of darkness, "night's cloak" and borrowed wings, until the last night in the vault, where the unit and the man expire together.

The choice of unit has a precision that the imagery studies, concerned with light and dark as symbols, can afford to pass over, but a diction study cannot. A night, unlike an hour, cannot be subdivided by its owner. It has no strokes. It begins when the light fails and ends when the lark sings, and both boundaries are arguable, as Act 3, Scene 5 proves at the cost of Romeo's life. To count in nights is therefore to count in the largest unit that still belongs to private experience, and the lovers' preference for it is a preference for a calendar no authority can audit. Capulet can move a Thursday. He cannot move a night; he can only invade it, as the household does at dawn in Act 3, Scene 5, when the Nurse's warning and the mother's footsteps bring the day's calendar up the stairs and into the chamber where the night's calendar is being spent. The scene's famous debate about the birds is, in the diction's terms, a border dispute between two systems of account, and it is settled the way border disputes in this play are always settled, by the side that owns the house.

Set against the night-count, the lovers' rare uses of the public units stand out like borrowed clothes. Juliet can speak the clock's language when an errand requires it, the hour of nine, the half hour's promise, and she can speak the weekday's language when a deception requires it, promising her father a Thursday she means to spend in a vault. In each case the public unit is instrumental, a tool picked up for an audience of elders or servants. Left to themselves, in soliloquy and in the speeches they exchange, the lovers revert to their own denominations: nights, dawns, and the lightning flash, the units of creatures who experience time as weather rather than as schedule. The tragedy can be stated in these terms without remainder. Two incompatible calendars occupy one city. The public calendar is exact, verifiable, and armed with fathers, princes, and plague houses. The private calendar is felt, arguable, and armed with nothing but its own beauty. The play's five days are the record of the public calendar's victory, and its language of time is the record, kept in the lovers' own words, of everything the victory cost.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch time-words catalogue, and what does it count that a timeline cannot?

The InsightCrunch time-words catalogue is the scene-by-scene table in this article listing every principal passage in which a character names, counts, promises, or figures a duration, an hour, a weekday, or a season. A timeline counts events and the intervals between them; the catalogue counts words and the speakers who spend them, and the difference is the argument. The timeline of the play shows five days. The catalogue shows those five days being talked about incessantly, in christening memories, feast-day arithmetic, kitchen orders, and jokes, by speakers under no deadline at all. What the catalogue therefore measures, and a timeline cannot, is saturation: the density of time-diction in scenes where the plot requires none, which is the evidence that the play's countdown is a condition of its language rather than a by-product of its schedule.

### Q: Why is the hour, rather than the day, the lovers' natural unit of measurement?

The hour is the lovers' natural unit because the day belongs to the institutions that govern them, while the hour is the largest span their private experience can actually hold. Days in Verona are public property, named by weekday, filled with markets, brawls, weddings, and sentences, and counted aloud by fathers and princes. The lovers' meetings, by contrast, are stolen intervals inside other people's days: a dance, a balcony conversation, a wedding night ended by a bird. Their diction follows the fact. Romeo's first complaint is that sad hours seem long; Juliet sets her appointment by the hour of nine, divides the Nurse's absence into hours and half-hours, and measures her joy against a lightning flash. The day is the unit in which Verona arranges their lives. The hour, and below it the instant, is the unit in which they live them.

### Q: What is the smallest span of time the play ever names, and who names it?

Juliet names it, in the balcony scene, and it is smaller than any unit a clock of her century could register: the life of a lightning flash, which, in her figure, "doth cease to be / Ere one can say 'it lightens'" (2.2.119-121). The span is measured against the duration of a spoken sentence, so the play's smallest denomination of time is the time a sentence takes, a fitting atom for a tragedy conducted in speech. Below the flash, the scale's smallest mechanical entry is the half hour of the Nurse's promised return in Act 2, Scene 5, and the contrast between the two minima is the whole diction in miniature. The half hour is a public promise, made by a servant and broken. The flash is a private truth, spoken by a lover and kept with terrible fidelity at the tomb, where Juliet's death takes exactly as long as her figure said joy does.

### Q: Why does the play name the weekdays of its action but never name a month or a year?

Because Verona's calendar is social rather than archival: its inhabitants need to know where in the week an event falls, since the week governs markets, fasts, wages, and weddings, while the month and the year belong to records nobody in the play keeps. The season is fixed by other means, by Lammas and high summer and the heat Benvolio complains of on the day of the duel, so a month would add nothing the audience cannot already feel. A year would add less than nothing, since the feud's whole point is that it has outlasted dating; it is "ancient grudge", older than any speaker's memory can number. The weekday is therefore the exact size of the play's world: large enough to schedule a marriage, small enough to be carried in the head, and silent about everything before Sunday and after Thursday, which is precisely the silence the tragedy needs.

### Q: How does Romeo's time vocabulary change between the Rosaline scenes and the tomb?

It contracts, hardens, and finally resigns. In the Rosaline scenes Romeo's time-words are dilatory and decorative: sad hours seem long, love is a madness most discreet, and an afternoon of melancholy is a leisure he can afford to versify. The balcony gives him the appointment-maker's vocabulary, the hour of nine, the messenger, the kept tomorrow, a young man learning to schedule his joy. Banishment strips the ornament away; in Mantua his diction of time reduces to a single defiance addressed to the stars, the calendar of his first dread, and in the tomb it inverts altogether into the eternity-words, the dateless bargain, the refusal of every appointment ever made. The arc can be tracked as a falling scale of units, from hours idly stretched, to hours exactly kept, to no hours at all, and it is the surest index the play offers of what the five days have done to him.

### Q: Which characters speak of time in numbers, and which speak of it only in light, birds, and bells?

The numbered speakers are the young, the professional, and the desperate: Juliet, who counts strokes, fractions, and sums in Act 2, Scene 5; the Friar in his apothecary's capacity, prescribing two and forty hours; Capulet on the wedding morning, stacking cock, bell, and three o'clock in a single breath. The unnumbered speakers are those whose authority predates the clock: the Friar in his garden, reading the hour off the sun's advance and the drying dew; the Nurse, whose chronology runs on earthquakes, feast days, and teeth; the old Capulets at the feast, disputing decades by the weddings they contain. The division is not absolute, since the Friar crosses it in both directions, but it holds as a map of the play's two time cultures, and the tragedy happens where the cultures meet: a girl who counts in numbers, answered by a world that still counts in signs.

### Q: What did "anon" and "by and by" promise a first audience, and why does the modern sense of those words soften the play's urgency?

To a first audience, anon and by and by in their urgent uses promised immediacy: at once, in a moment, before the breath is out. Anon was the servant's answer to a summons and the lover's promise of an instant's absence, and Juliet's "Anon, good nurse!" from the balcony (2.2.141) asks for a pause shorter than a scene-change of the heart. The modern ear hears the same words as vagueness, by and by drifting toward someday, anon toward an archaism for later, and the drift reverses the play's pressure at exactly the points where the pressure matters most. Speeches written as promises of instant return are received as licences for delay. Recovering the period senses restores the diction's contract: in Verona, soon is a bond, and every broken soon in the play is a broken bond, legible as betrayal rather than as the weather of a slower age.

### Q: Why does "tomorrow" keep being promised in this play and never kept?

Because tomorrow is the word in which Verona files everything it cannot face in the present, and the play systematically forecloses on the filings. The lovers' first tomorrow is the honourable exception, the appointment by the hour of nine, kept to the stroke, and its very punctuality teaches the audience to trust the word. Thereafter the word is spent by speakers who do not control the future they are naming: Mercutio, promising a tomorrow that arrives as a grave; Paris, wishing a Thursday into a tomorrow and receiving a Wednesday burial; the Friar, building a rescue on a Wednesday that is tomorrow and watching the day arrive at a tomb instead of a bedchamber. The pattern is the diction's verdict on the city's deepest habit. Verona survives by deferral, feuds postponed, marriages deferred, letters sent tomorrow. The play's five days are what happens when tomorrow stops accepting deposits.

### Q: How do the play's eternity words, "dateless", "timeless", and "everlasting", differ from its clock words in the mouths that speak them?

The clock words are spoken by people still negotiating with the future; the eternity words are spoken by people who have stopped. Hours, weekdays, and appointments belong to the play's managers, its Juliets counting messengers, its Capulets setting Thursdays, its Friar prescribing spans, speakers who believe a number can still protect them. Dateless and timeless belong to the tomb, to Romeo sealing a bargain no calendar can govern and to Juliet diagnosing a death as an end that came out of its season. The distribution is exact enough to serve as a diagnostic: a character's position in the tragedy can be read off the sign of the duration words in the mouth, positive and countable while hope lasts, negative and infinite when it ends. Even the Prince's closing morning belongs to the second class, a dawn imagined not rising, the calendar's own instrument declining to keep time over the result.

### Q: Why does Friar John's report in Act 5 Scene 2 carry almost no time-words, when a lost day is its whole content?

Because the report is about a stoppage, and its diction performs the stoppage it describes. Friar John narrates doors sealed by searchers, a companion found, a letter that could not be sent and could not be fetched back by any messenger, and the account is strikingly bare of the play's usual instruments: no hour is named, no weekday checked, no interval measured. The one duration that matters, the length of the detention, is the one figure the speech never gives, as if the plague house stood outside the calendar altogether, in a pocket where Verona's fluency in counting simply does not run. The effect on an audience trained by four acts of saturation is visceral. Catastrophe in this play usually arrives over-articulated, counted and recounted. The fatal delay arrives uncounted, and the silence where the numbers should be is the sound of the mechanism breaking: a city that could tell anyone the time could not tell its rescuers how long they had lost.

### Q: Why does Romeo address the stars, the oldest calendar in his world, only after every human timetable has failed?

Because the stars are the calendar of last resort, the one system in the play that no father, friar, or plague house administers, and Romeo reaches for it only when the administered systems have all collapsed. He has lived under their entries since before the feast, dreading "some consequence yet hanging in the stars" that would "bitterly begin his fearful date" (1.4.106-107), and the word date does the quiet work: the stars keep appointments too, in a ledger older than Verona's. His defiance in Mantua, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), is therefore not a change of subject but the final entry in the play's long account with scheduled futures, a client repudiating the oldest bank. The gesture completes the diction's arc. A boy who feared the stars' calendar, a lover who kept the clock's, a bridegroom bound by the parish's weekdays, ends by resigning from every calendar at once, and seals the resignation in the tomb with a bargain he takes care to call dateless.

### Q: What does the Friar mean by "after-hours", and why does his time vocabulary treat the future as a creditor?

"After-hours" is the Friar's coinage at the wedding itself: "So smile the heavens upon this holy act, / That after-hours with sorrow chide us not" (2.6.1-2). The phrase imagines the future as a period that will present an account, hours yet to come arriving like a creditor after a loan, able to chide, to call in the debt of a hasty blessing with sorrow as the interest. No other character in the play speaks of time this way, as a party to a contract with a right of complaint, and the singularity suits the speaker. The Friar is the play's great arranger of other people's futures, marriages made, sleeps prescribed, wakings scheduled, and his vocabulary betrays the arranger's private knowledge: every schedule he sets creates a creditor he will have to answer to. The word is spoken as a hope, that the account may never be presented. The fifth act is the presentation, and the Friar's own closing narrative, an itemized confession before the Prince, is the sound of the after-hours being paid.

### Q: Why is the play's only direct question about time a question about the day rather than the hour?

Because the hour, in Verona, is a private concern, while the day is the unit in which public power transacts its business, and the play's only direct time-question is asked by public power at work. Capulet's "What day is this?" in Act 3, Scene 4 is not curiosity; it is the opening move of a marriage settlement, and Paris's answer, "Monday, my lord", supplies the fixed point from which Wednesday can be rejected and Thursday appointed. Nobody in the play ever asks what o'clock it is and receives an answer, although hours are constantly cited, struck, and counted, because an hour unasked is an hour owned by the person enduring it, Juliet's three long hours, the household's three o'clock. The distribution of the question is thus a small map of the city's constitution: days are common property, settled by asking; hours are suffered in private, and the suffering is the play's true chronology.

### Q: How does the word "late" change its meaning as the play proceeds, from tardy to recent to dead?

The word migrates through its three early modern senses in the order of the tragedy. In the early acts late means tardy, a failure of punctuality: messengers are late, hours run late, the Nurse's news arrives late. In the middle acts the recent sense takes over, the late brawl, the late encounter, events filed as news while their consequences are still arriving. By the fifth act the third sense, the sense English reserves for the dead, has silently become the word's centre of gravity, and speakers stand among persons who can only be described in the past tense. No character remarks the migration, and no single speech contains more than one stage of it, which is why it belongs to a diction study rather than to any scene analysis: the change is statistical, a darkening spread across dozens of small uses, and it converts the vocabulary of scheduling, inside five days of stage time, into the vocabulary of obituary without ever announcing the conversion.

### Q: How can an essay use the time-words catalogue to argue that the countdown is built into the play's sentences and not only into its plot?

By arguing from distribution, the one kind of evidence a plot summary cannot supply. The essay should first establish the baseline: time-diction appears at full density in scenes where the plot imposes no deadline, the christening arithmetic of Act 1, Scene 3, the disputed decades of Act 1, Scene 5, the pastry-hour of Act 4, Scene 4. It should then show the same diction performing the tragedy's emotional work at its hinges, Juliet's sums in Act 2, Scene 5 converting waiting into injury, the weekday bargaining of Act 3, Scene 4 converting a marriage into a countdown. The conclusion follows from the comparison: a dramatist who needed only a fast plot would have concentrated his time-words where his deadlines are, and a dramatist who spreads them evenly across exposition, comedy, and catastrophe is building the sensation of shortage into the language itself. The catalogue's rows, cited by scene, are the essay's exhibits.

### Q: Why does the Chorus measure the play in hours while the lovers measure it in nights?

Because the two parties keep different accounts of the same event, and the units expose what each account is for. The Chorus speaks for the performance, a public occasion with a contracted length, and its two hours' traffic (Prologue, 12) is a tradesman's figure, the span the audience has purchased and the company must fill. The lovers speak for an experience that public measures cannot file, and their unit, the night, is correspondingly unadministered: unstruck, unnumbered, beginning in a garden and ending at a bird's discretion. Between the two measures the whole tragedy is suspended. The audience watches five days it experiences as three nights, inside an afternoon it experiences as two hours, and the dissonance among the three clocks is not a puzzle to be solved but the sensation the play exists to produce, the feeling of lives being spent faster than any of the available instruments can record.

### Q: How should an actor speak the play's numbers, nine, twelve, three, and forty-two, so that an audience hears a countdown rather than information?

By treating each number as an event rather than a fact: the numbers in this play are things that happen to the speakers, and they land when the actor lets them cost something. Juliet's nine and twelve in Act 2, Scene 5 are not a report on the clock but the strokes of an indictment being assembled, and they need the weight of items entered in evidence, spaced so the arithmetic can be felt accumulating. Capulet's three o'clock in Act 4, Scene 4 is the opposite case, a number spent in bustle, proof that the same instrument can be played as comedy when the speaker is master of the hour it names. The Friar's forty-two should be level, a professional's dosage, its horror reserved for the audience's arithmetic rather than the speaker's emphasis. The rule that covers all three is the diction's own: a number in this play is a unit of somebody's remaining time, and it plays when the house can hear the subtraction.

### Q: What does the word "o'clock" itself reveal about the clock culture entering the play's language?

The word is a fossil of the transition the play dramatizes. O'clock is a contraction of "of the clock", worn down by frequent use in the decades just before the play was written, which means the phrase was new enough to be fashionable and common enough to be abbreviated, the linguistic signature of a technology passing from novelty to furniture. Shakespeare gives the word to exactly the speakers the catalogue would predict: to Juliet, making her appointment in the balcony scene, and to Capulet, bustling his kitchen at three o'clock on the wedding morning, the youngest lover and the oldest householder, the two poles of the play's world, both already speaking in the new idiom. Around them the older instruments persist, cocks, bells, larks, the sun's advance, so that the word sits in the text like a dated coin in a hoard of older currency: proof, at the level of a single syllable's erosion, that clock-time was entering English speech in the very years the play was entering the repertory.

### Q: How does the diction of the final scene replace counting with weighing, setting gold statues where hours used to be?

By completing the vocabulary's long retreat from measure. Through four acts Verona has priced everything in time: readiness in summers, news in hours, a wedding in weekdays, a rescue in a letter's delay. In the final scene the counting stops, not because time has been mastered but because nothing countable remains to the counters, and the fathers settle their account in the play's other great substance of value, metal. A statue in pure gold is promised for Juliet; Romeo's is pledged to lie beside it, "poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3.303). Gold is the anti-hour: it does not pass, strike, or expire, and a city that could not keep its children in time proposes to keep them in the one medium that ignores time altogether. The diction's last exchange is therefore a confession. What Verona could not do with all its clocks, it attempts with its treasury, and the substitution, hours for ounces, is the tragedy's final measurement of its own loss.

### Q: By what events, rather than by what numbers, does the Nurse date the past in Act 1 Scene 3?

By an earthquake, a weaning, a death, and her own teeth. The earthquake, eleven years before the scene, is her era, the public disaster by which private lives in her keeping are dated: Juliet was weaned on the day of it, "of all the days of the year, upon that day", and the coincidence fixes the child's age more firmly in her memory than any numeral could. Susan, the god-sister of an age with Juliet, is dead, and her death is another entry in the same calendar, as is the state of the Nurse's mouth, four teeth left of the fourteen she offers to wager on the computation. The system is complete and, within its terms, rigorous: public catastrophe, household milestone, bereavement, and the body of the reckoner herself. Its precision is the precision of oral cultures, in which a date is not a coordinate but a story, and the scene's comedy should not obscure its lesson for the diction study: Verona's oldest inhabitant keeps the play's most accurate calendar, and she keeps it without a single figure in it.
