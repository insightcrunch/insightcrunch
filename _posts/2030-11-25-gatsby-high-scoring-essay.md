## Why One Essay Can Carry a Whole Block

A block of instruction faces a structural problem that no single stage article can solve on its own. Each stage teaches its principle well: the thesis article teaches the contestable claim, the structure article teaches the developing line of reasoning, the evidence article teaches the embedded quotation, the analysis article teaches the close reading, the conclusion article teaches the genuine ending. But principles taught in isolation stay isolated in the student's mind. The thesis lives in one compartment, the evidence in another, and the examination essay demands all of them at once, in sequence, under time, with each paragraph doing several jobs simultaneously. The gap between knowing the parts and performing the whole is where most marks are lost. Students do not fail because they never learned what a thesis is. They fail because they never saw a thesis, a structure, evidence, and analysis operating together inside one continuous piece of prose, each supporting the others, the way an examiner expects to find them.

The worked example closes that gap by demonstration. Demonstration teaches differently from instruction, and the difference is worth naming precisely. Instruction tells the student what to do: make the thesis contestable, embed the quotation, read the language closely. Demonstration shows the doing: here is the thesis, contestable, in the second sentence of the introduction; here is the quotation, embedded in the grammar of the writer's sentence so that removing it would break the sentence; here is the close reading, spending three sentences on one word because that word carries the paragraph's claim. The margin notes then attach the examiner's reasoning to each moment: this move earns credit under the argument criterion because it takes a position the question can test; that move earns credit under the analysis criterion because it reads specific language rather than repeating it. Instruction gives the student the vocabulary of good writing. Demonstration gives the student the experience of watching good writing happen, with the examiner's valuation running alongside like a commentary track.

There is a second, quieter reason the capstone matters, and it concerns confidence. Many students approach the examination essay as a performance they have rehearsed in fragments, never once having seen the complete performance they are meant to give. That is an unnerving position, like an actor who has rehearsed every scene separately and never run the play. The annotated full essay is the first complete run-through. It shows the scale of the thing: how long the introduction actually is, how much analysis a single paragraph can carry, how the conclusion gathers rather than repeats. Students consistently misjudge these proportions from instruction alone, writing introductions three times too long and conclusions that merely stop. Seeing the proportions embodied corrects the misjudgment faster than any advice, because the eye learns proportion from examples the way the ear learns pitch from music. The margin notes reinforce the learning by naming what each proportion achieves, so the student absorbs not just the shape but the reason for the shape.

### Why does demonstration teach what instruction cannot?

Demonstration teaches what instruction cannot because it shows principles working under real conditions: the thesis setting terms, the paragraphs developing them, the evidence embedded where claims need proof. Instruction names the moves; demonstration reveals their cooperation, timing, and proportion, which is what the examination tests, since marks reward performing the moves rather than the vocabulary that names them.

The demonstration works because the essay is complete and the annotation is total. A partial example, an introduction here and a paragraph there, would show the parts without showing the whole, which is exactly the limitation the capstone exists to overcome. Here the reader watches the thesis set the terms in the opening, then watches each paragraph take up one of those terms and develop it, then watches the evidence arrive embedded in the writer's sentences rather than dropped in as decoration, then watches the analysis slow down over specific words at the moments where the argument needs proving, then watches the conclusion gather the strands without introducing new claims. The margin notes run beside all of it, naming the move and its mark value, so that at no point does the reader have to guess why a passage is strong. The block's separate lessons become one visible method.

The synthesis also runs in the other direction, from the example back to the stages. A student who has studied the components article on model paragraphs will recognize each paragraph here as an instance of the patterns taught there, and the recognition cements the pattern far more firmly than the instruction alone. A student who has read the marking article will hear the criteria behind every margin note, the argument criterion here, the analysis criterion there, and the abstract descriptors become concrete: this is what sustained analysis actually looks like across four paragraphs. The capstone therefore does double work. For the student who has done the block, it converts separate knowledge into integrated skill. For the student who arrives here first, it provides the destination, and the cross-links scattered through the later sections supply the route back to each stage for deeper study. Either way the essay carries the block, because the essay is the block, performed.

It is worth saying what synthesis is not, because the word invites a misreading that would waste the capstone. Synthesis is not summary. A summary of the block would restate each stage's lesson in compressed form: make the thesis contestable, embed the quotation, read the language closely, and so on. That restatement would add nothing to what the stage articles already teach, and a student who reads it would leave knowing the same things in fewer words. The synthesis this article offers is different in kind. It does not compress the lessons; it stages them, placing each principle inside a working essay where its function becomes visible. The difference matters because principles are understood through their function. A student can recite "embed the quotation" without knowing what embedding does to a sentence, but the student who watches the green-light sentence hold together only because the quotation is inside it understands embedding as a structural act. Summary teaches the vocabulary of the method. Staging teaches the method's mechanics, which is why the capstone needed a complete essay rather than a condensed handbook.

Synthesis is also not simplification. There is a temptation, when gathering a block into one example, to smooth away the difficulties, presenting an essay so clean it looks effortless. The sample resists that smoothing, and the annotation is honest about the effort inside it. The concession paragraphs show the argument being wounded and repaired; the word-level readings show analysis as slow labor, three sentences on a single verb; the second-reading section shows the essay surviving scrutiny rather than coasting past it. A student who expects the top band to feel easy will be unprepared for the actual work, and the capstone would mislead if it hid that work. The margin notes therefore name not only what each move earns but what it costs: the close reading costs patience, the concession costs the comfort of a simple position, the gathering conclusion costs the discipline of adding nothing new. The honest synthesis shows the price alongside the prize, which is what makes it usable. A method that looks effortless cannot be practiced; a method whose effort is visible can.

### What question does the sample essay answer?

The sample essay answers this question: to what extent is Gatsby's dream admirable? The prompt sits at the novel's center, resists a one-sided answer, and forces the judgment the top bands reward. The essay argues the dream is admirable in devotion and imaginative force but damning in object and cost, and every paragraph tests that mixed judgment against the text.

The choice of question shapes everything the essay does, which is itself part of the lesson. An evaluative prompt with a sliding scale, "to what extent," demands a thesis that takes a measured position rather than a side, and the essay's opening does exactly that, refusing both uncritical admiration and easy dismissal. A different question would produce a different essay, and the prompt-adaptation section later shows how the same moves transfer to one. For now, notice how the question's key terms, "dream" and "admirable," become the essay's load-bearing vocabulary, reappearing in the thesis, the topic sentences, and the conclusion, so the examiner never doubts the relevance of any paragraph. That continuous return to the question's language is one of the cheapest high-value moves in examination writing, and the annotation points it out wherever it happens.

## Reading the Margin Notes Like a Marker

The margin notes name the moves, but a student who wants to use them needs to understand why each move is priced the way it is, which means thinking like the marker for a while. The marking article describes the examiner's mindset in full: a trained reader measuring each script against published criteria, crediting only what the prose performs on the page. The notes in the table are that mindset compressed into labels. This section expands them back out, walking through the valuation behind the labels, so the student sees not just what the essay does but what the doing is worth and why. The aim is to make the examiner's reasoning feel like common sense rather than mystery, because once it feels like common sense the student can apply it to any draft, including drafts that look nothing like this sample.

Start with the thesis, because the thesis is where the examiner's provisional band forms. The note on the introduction calls the thesis contestable and pairs it with a roadmap, and both halves of that label carry specific value. Contestability matters because the argument criterion cannot credit a position that no one would dispute. An essay that opens by announcing that Gatsby's dream is important, or that the novel explores the American Dream, has given the examiner nothing to weigh, and the pencil stays still. The sample's thesis, that the dream is admirable in its devotion and force but damning in its object and cost, can be argued against: a reader could claim the cost outweighs the devotion entirely, or that the object excuses nothing, and the essay would then have to defend itself. That defensibility is what the criterion prices. The examiner is not looking for the right answer, since no mark scheme contains one. The examiner is looking for a position strong enough to be tested, and the test is the essay that follows.

The roadmap half of the label matters for a different reason, and students underestimate it. A roadmap sentence, the final sentence of the introduction mapping devotion, object, and cost, tells the examiner the essay's structure before the structure unfolds. The value is legibility. An examiner reading fast, with the criteria open, needs to see the line of reasoning to credit it, and a paragraph whose function is announced in advance is a paragraph the examiner reads with the right expectation. Without the roadmap, each paragraph must establish its own relevance from scratch, and some of the examiner's attention goes to figuring out where the essay is going instead of judging how well it gets there. The roadmap is therefore not decoration and not mere signposting for its own sake. It is a transfer of labor from the reader to the writer, and the writer is the one being marked, so the transfer pays.

### How does the essay's thesis do more than restate the question?

The essay's thesis does more than restate the question by taking a measured, disputable position the question invites but does not supply: admiration granted for devotion and force, withheld for object and cost. A restatement would merely repeat the prompt's terms; the thesis judges between them, giving the essay a claim to defend.

That judgment, between devotion and cost, is what makes the thesis do argumentative work from its first appearance. Consider the alternatives the essay refuses. It could have answered that the dream is entirely admirable, which would be a position but a thin one, vulnerable to the novel's own evidence about bootlegging and bodies in the road. It could have answered that the dream is not admirable at all, which would be defensible but would waste the novel's tenderness, the real force of Nick's closing tribute. The chosen thesis threads between those, and the threading is the thinking the criterion rewards. A marker reading the introduction can see immediately that this essay will have to balance competing evidence, which is a harder and more creditworthy task than prosecuting a single charge. The band impression forms high, not because the thesis is clever, but because it promises the kind of sustained judgment the top bands describe.

The thesis also does quiet structural work that the margin note only hints at. Its three terms, devotion and force, object, cost, become the organizing principle of the body, one term per paragraph in sequence, so the thesis is not just a claim but a plan. That unity of claim and plan is what examiners mean by a line of reasoning. Many student theses are claims without plans: they take a position and then the paragraphs wander, each making a true observation about the novel that never quite serves the position. The examiner then has a thesis to credit and paragraphs that do not develop it, and the argument mark stalls. Here the thesis generates the paragraphs, which means every paragraph inherits the thesis's relevance. The student learning from this should practice the test: read your thesis, then read each topic sentence, and ask whether the topic sentence is one of the thesis's terms developed. If a paragraph serves a term the thesis never named, either the thesis or the paragraph needs rewriting.

### How does embedded evidence differ from dropped quotation in the sample?

Embedded evidence is load-bearing: the sample folds each quotation into the grammar of its own sentence, so removing the quoted words would break the sentence, while a dropped quotation sits beside the point as decoration. Examiners credit the choosing and the placing, not the possessing, which is why the embedded phrase outscores the displayed passage.

The table's first body paragraph demonstrates the difference in miniature. The sentence about the green light does not announce the quotation and then present it; it makes the quotation the grammatical object of the writer's own clause, "Fitzgerald's most famous sentence about it does the theological work," followed by the line itself doing that work inside the sentence's structure. The analysis then continues in the same grammatical unit, reading "orgastic" and "recedes" as the sentence's own words. Contrast the dropped version, which the marking article anatomizes: the paragraph makes its point about the dream receding, then appends the quotation as a stamp of approval, then moves on. The examiner's eye, trained by thousands of scripts, goes straight to the sentence after the quotation to check whether analysis follows. In the sample, analysis always follows, because the quotation was placed there to be analyzed, not to decorate a point already made.

The practical consequence for drafting is a rule the student can apply mechanically. Write the analytical sentence first, the sentence that says what the language does, and then choose the shortest quotation that lets that sentence be true. The sample's reading of Daisy's voice works this way: the paragraph needs to prove that wealth is something Daisy contains rather than has, and the phrase "is full of money" proves exactly that through its preposition, so the quotation is short, precise, and placed where the claim needs it. Students who choose quotations first and analysis second end up with famous lines that prove everything and therefore nothing in particular, and their paragraphs read as displays rather than arguments. The margin note's phrase "load-bearing" is the test to carry into revision: cover the quotation with your thumb and ask whether the sentence still stands. If it stands, the quotation was decoration. If it collapses, the quotation was working, and the evidence criterion will pay for the work.

There is a further subtlety in the sample worth naming, because it separates strong essays from merely competent ones. The sample never quotes at length. Its longest quotation is a single sentence, and most are phrases of three to five words. Short quotation is a strategic choice, not a limitation of memory. A phrase can be read closely, word by word, which is what the analysis criterion prices; a passage can only be gestured at, and gesture is not analysis. The student who memorizes long passages for the examination is preparing to display rather than to read, and the display earns little. The student who memorizes short phrases, each chosen because its specific wording proves a specific claim, arrives with evidence that fits the argument the way a key fits a lock. The sample's quotations all fit this way, and the fit is visible in the table: each quoted phrase is followed by sentences that could not have been written about any other phrase. That specificity, the analysis that belongs to this quotation and no other, is the signature of evidence earning its marks.

## What the Second Reading Finds

The marking article describes an examiner's two passes over a script: the first forming a provisional band, the second testing it, with borderline scripts receiving the closest scrutiny. It is worth subjecting the sample essay to that imagined second reading, because the exercise reveals qualities that the first reading's admiration can miss and shows the student what durability looks like. A first reading rewards fluency, confidence, and the feeling of an argument going somewhere. A second reading asks harder questions: is this analysis or description wearing analytical vocabulary, does the evidence prove the specific claim or merely accompany it, would the paragraph survive if its best sentence were removed. The sample survives those questions, and watching how it survives teaches the standard more precisely than the first reading's enthusiasm.

Take the analysis-or-description test to the first body paragraph. A second-reading examiner, pencil hovering, asks whether the three sentences on "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us" genuinely read the language or merely restate it in fancier terms. Restatement would sound like this: the quotation shows that the future keeps moving away, which demonstrates the dream's impossibility. The sample does something else: it separates the adjective from the verb, assigns each a distinct job, intensity to "orgastic" and built-in failure to "recedes," and then draws the consequence that the grammar of desire contains its own defeat. That separation is the analysis, because it finds in the sentence a structure the paraphrase would flatten. The examiner's second pass is looking for exactly this, the moment where the writer's thinking does something the quotation alone does not do, and the paragraph supplies several such moments, which is why the provisional band holds.

### Where would an examiner pause in the sample essay?

An examiner would pause at the word-level readings, the grammar of "recedes," the preposition in "full of money," the plainness of "careless," because each shows the discriminating quality that decides a band: specific language read for its exact contribution to the claim. The pause marks where analysis, not description, is happening.

Apply the same test to the paragraph the first reading might have skimmed, the fourth, on Nick's double position. A suspicious second reading could charge that invoking "within and without" is a grand gesture covering thin thought: the famous phrase deployed, the novel's stance asserted, the paragraph coasting on the quotation's prestige. The paragraph's defense is structural. It does not merely quote the phrase; it reads the prepositions, "within" as intimacy and "without" as distance, and connects that doubleness to the essay's own method, the admiration-and-judgment thesis the introduction promised. Then it performs the subtlest move in the whole essay, returning the "receding" motif from the first paragraph in transformed shape, the private recession become the human condition. A second-reading examiner testing for coherence finds the essay's language rhyming with itself across four paragraphs, which is evidence of shaping rather than assembly. The pause here is longer, because the paragraph is doing two jobs at once, advancing the claim about narration and binding the essay together, and the double duty is what the top band's "sustained" really means.

The second reading also tests the essay's honesty, and this is where many strong first impressions fade. An examiner checking for evasion asks whether the essay confronted the hardest evidence against its thesis or arranged the argument to avoid it. The sample's thesis admires the dream's devotion, and the hardest evidence against that admiration is the human cost, Myrtle dead in the road, George destroyed, Gatsby shot. A weaker essay would have softened that ledger or hurried past it. The sample's third paragraph makes the cost its center, widening the frame deliberately, and lets the ledger do its damage to the admiration the first paragraph conceded. That willingness to let the argument be wounded and then to measure the wound precisely, "admirable to the extent that, and no further," is what the second reading rewards most. It is also the quality students find hardest to imitate, because it requires the writer to argue against their own opening, and the inability to do so is what keeps many fluent essays in the middle bands.

There is one more second-reading check worth performing, because it governs a decision students face in every examination: the handling of the famous. The sample uses famous lines, the green light sentence, the closing line, Daisy's voice, and a second reading asks whether any of them is there for prestige rather than proof. The test is removal: take out the famous line and ask whether the paragraph's claim still has its evidence. In each case the answer is no, because each famous line was chosen for a specific word doing a specific job, the grammar of recession, the preposition of containment, the plainness of the verdict. Fame is never the reason, which the margin notes state and the second reading confirms. Students preparing their own quotation banks should apply the removal test to every line they memorize: if the line's fame is doing more work than its wording, replace it with an unfamous line whose wording fits the claim exactly. The examiner rewards fit, and the second reading is where fit is verified.

## The Copying Trap: Why Imitation Fails

Every worked example carries a danger alongside its teaching, and honesty requires naming it before going further. The danger is that the student treats the sample as a template to copy: memorizing its sentences, its structure, its quotations, and reproducing them in the examination hall as if the marks belonged to the words rather than to the thinking. The temptation is understandable. The essay is strong, the annotation proves it is strong, and the examination rewards strength, so carrying the strong thing into the hall feels like the efficient move. It is not efficient. It is the single fastest way to turn a top-band performance into a middle-band script, and the reasons are structural, not moral. This section explains why imitation fails, so the later sections on extraction and adaptation have something to work against. The argument is not that students should avoid the sample. The argument is that they should learn from it the way its author learned the moves, by understanding, rather than the way a photocopier learns them, by reproduction.

The first reason imitation fails is the prompt. The sample answers one specific question, to what extent the dream is admirable, and every part of it is shaped to that question: the thesis judges admiration, the paragraphs test devotion, object, and cost, the conclusion measures the extent. An examination question will not be this question. It may ask about narration, about wealth, about the ending, about a character, and a memorized essay about admirability will answer none of them. The student who carries the sample into the hall must then either reproduce it whole, answering the wrong question, or adapt it on the spot, which requires exactly the understanding the memorization was meant to replace. The marking article is explicit about the cost: an essay that answers a different question than the one set forfeits the argument criterion's foundation no matter how strong the writing, because response to the task underlies every other mark. A brilliant irrelevance is still an irrelevance. The copier's best case, a question close to the sample's, still leaves the essay's key terms mismatched with the prompt's, and the examiner's first test, does this thesis answer the set question, starts with a deficit.

The second reason is subtler and concerns what examiners actually reward. The criteria price verbs, what the writer does with material on the page: argues, analyzes, selects, integrates. A memorized essay performs none of those verbs in the hall. The arguing was done by the sample's author at a desk; the analyzing was done in the close readings the student memorized; the selecting was done when the quotations were chosen. What the student performs in the examination is recall, and recall is not a criterion. The prose may be fluent and the quotations accurate, but the thinking is absent, and examiners are trained to feel the absence. The marking article describes the marker's instinct for the pause, the moment a paragraph shows genuine analysis and the examiner slows down. A memorized paragraph never triggers it, because the analysis in it is not being performed; it is being replayed. The difference is audible to a trained ear the way a memorized speech differs from a thought being formed, and markers hear thousands of scripts.

### How do examiners detect a memorized essay?

Examiners detect a memorized essay by its irrelevance to the set question and its generic fit: the thesis answers a different prompt, the paragraphs never touch the question's key terms, and the analysis feels replayed rather than performed. No criterion prices recall, so the script stalls in the middle bands however fluent its prose.

The detection is rarely a conscious accusation. Examiners do not sit with a plagiarism database open, hunting for memorized scripts; they simply read for the criteria, and the memorized script fails the criteria in a recognizable pattern. The thesis uses the wrong vocabulary, the vocabulary of the prepared question rather than the set one, so the response-to-task check falters in the first paragraph. The topic sentences advance claims the question never asked about, so the line of reasoning feels imposed rather than developed. The quotations, however well chosen for the original question, prove points the paragraphs do not need proved, so the evidence criterion finds selection without purpose. And the analysis, fluent as it is, never engages the specific wording of the set question, because it was written for different wording. The pattern is unmistakable in aggregate even when no single paragraph looks wrong, and the mark reflects the aggregate: a script that demonstrates knowledge about the novel but performs no thinking about the question.

There is also a practical consideration that students overlook, which is the behavior of memorized prose under pressure. A memorized essay is brittle. If the question matches the memorized one closely, the student reproduces fluently; if it diverges, the student must improvise transitions between memorized blocks, and the improvisation shows as seams, sudden shifts in register, paragraphs that begin confidently and then thin out. Examiners read the seams as evidence of lost control, and the expression criterion, which rewards sentences that carry complex thought without tangling, withholds its credit. The irony is sharp: the student memorized to guarantee control and ends up demonstrating its absence. The alternative the rest of this article teaches, extracting the moves and practicing their application to new prompts, produces prose that is less polished in rehearsal and far more controlled in the hall, because the control comes from understanding the task rather than from recalling a script.

The brittleness becomes visible in a concrete comparison, which is worth staging because students rarely believe it until they see it. Imagine the examination question is about wealth rather than admiration: "How does the novel present the moral effects of wealth?" The student who memorized the sample reaches for its strongest paragraph, the first body paragraph on the green light, and forces it onto the new question. The result reads like this.

*The devotion of wealth is the ground of whatever presentation it commands. Gatsby does not merely want Daisy; he organizes his entire existence around the wanting, and the novel renders that organization as a kind of secular faith. The green light at the end of Daisy's dock becomes its sacrament, and Fitzgerald's most famous sentence about it does the theological work: "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The moral effects of wealth are therefore shown through the dream's intensity.*

The seams show everywhere. The opening sentence keeps the sample's shape but its terms no longer connect: "the devotion of wealth" is a phrase that means nothing, devotion transferred from a dreamer to an abstraction because the student could not let the memorized cadence go. The green light paragraph then proceeds exactly as the sample wrote it, proving devotion, while the question asks about moral effects, so the paragraph's considerable analytical force is spent on the wrong target. The final sentence is the improvised bridge, and it collapses under the weight: "are therefore shown" claims a logical connection the paragraph never built, and the examiner reading it sees a student bolting a prepared answer onto an unprepared question. The prose is fluent. The thinking is absent. The mark stalls.

Now the same student, having extracted the moves instead of memorizing the sentences, writes fresh. The thesis judges between the question's terms: wealth in the novel enlarges its possessors' power while shrinking their moral attention, so its effects are corrupting but unevenly distributed. The paragraph takes one term, the shrinking of moral attention, as its mini-thesis: wealth narrows what its possessors can see. The embedded evidence is Daisy's voice, "full of money," read through the preposition the sample taught the student to notice: containment, wealth as something carried inside perception itself, so that Daisy cannot hear the world except through wealth. The analysis follows the quotation's wake, as the move requires: the moral effect is not greed but a trained deafness, and the paragraph proves it from a single small word. Not one sentence is shared with the sample. Every move is shared. The first version took an hour to memorize and scores in the middle band. The second took the same hour of move-practice and answers the question set. That is the whole argument against imitation in one comparison.

The honest position, then, is this. The sample is not a template. It is a demonstration of transferable principles, and the difference between a template and a demonstration is the difference between a script to recite and a method to learn. A template tells you what to say. A demonstration shows you how the saying is done, so you can do it about something else. Everything that follows is built on that distinction: the prompt-adaptation section shows the method applied to a different question, the extraction section shows how to pull the moves out of the sample deliberately, and the study section shows how to practice them until they are yours. The student who works through those sections will leave the sample behind, which is exactly what the sample is for.

## Prompt Adaptation: The Same Moves on a Different Question

The proof that the sample teaches principles rather than sentences is a transfer: take the moves the margin notes named and apply them to a question the sample never addressed, building a new essay from the same method. This section performs that transfer in full, so the student watches the method survive the change of subject. The new question is deliberately distant from the sample's: "Nick describes himself as 'within and without.' How far does his narration shape the reader's judgment of Gatsby?" Nothing in the sample essay answers this. Its thesis judged admiration; its paragraphs tested devotion, object, and cost. The new question asks about narration and judgment, which means every sentence of content must be invented fresh. What transfers is not the content but the moves: the contestable thesis, the roadmap, the mini-thesis topic sentences, the embedded quotation, the word-level reading, the conceded counter-reading, the integrated context, the gathering conclusion. Watch each move reappear in new clothing.

The adaptation begins where the sample began, with the prompt's demands. "How far" is a sliding-scale instruction like "to what extent": it asks for a measured judgment, not a yes or no. The key terms are "narration," "shape," and "judgment of Gatsby," so the thesis must take a position on how much of the reader's verdict on Gatsby belongs to Nick's telling rather than to Gatsby's doing. A contestable thesis: Nick's narration shapes the reader's judgment of Gatsby decisively but not completely; the enchantment is Nick's work, while the facts that limit the enchantment survive his telling, so the reader judges a Gatsby that Nick both creates and cannot fully control. That thesis can be argued against, a reader could claim Nick's control is total or minimal, which is what makes it a thesis rather than a restatement. The roadmap follows from its terms: first the shaping, how Nick's language enchants; then the limit, the facts that escape the enchantment; then the judgment, what the reader is left holding.

### How do you adapt the sample essay's moves to a different prompt?

You adapt the sample essay's moves by keeping the method and replacing the content: read the new prompt's key terms, build a contestable thesis from them, map one term per paragraph, and then write each paragraph with the same moves, embedded quotation, word-level reading, conceded objection. The moves transfer; the sentences do not.

Now the body, one term per paragraph, each paragraph performing the moves the table named. The first paragraph takes the shaping: Nick's narration enchants Gatsby before the reader meets him, and the enchantment is a deliberate construction. The topic sentence is a mini-thesis, disputable and serving the thesis: Nick manufactures Gatsby's glamour in advance, so the reader's first judgment is admiration by design. The evidence is embedded, not dropped: the early description of Gatsby's smile, which Nick says understood you "just as far as you wanted to be understood," arrives inside the writer's sentence about manufactured intimacy. The word-level reading slows over "as far as," a phrase that flatters the reader by pretending the limit of understanding is the reader's own desire, which is exactly how enchantment works: it makes the audience complicit. The paragraph's claim is that the judgment of Gatsby begins as Nick's artifact, and the quotation's specific wording proves it, because the smile's power is described as a function of the beholder's wanting.

The second paragraph concedes and complicates, the way the sample's second paragraph turned devotion against its object. The concession: the facts that limit the enchantment survive Nick's telling, which proves his control is incomplete. The topic sentence takes the position: Nick cannot narrate away the criminality, the carelessness of the Buchanans, or the bodies, and their persistence in his own narrative bounds his shaping power. The evidence here is the ledger the sample itself used, bootlegging, Wolfsheim, Myrtle in the road, but now deployed for a different claim: not that the dream is costly, but that the cost is visible despite the narrator's love. The word-level reading finds its purchase in Nick's own verdict, "They were careless people, Tom and Daisy," where the plainness of "careless" refuses the enchanted register entirely; the narrator who gilded Gatsby's smile will not gild this sentence, and the refusal is the limit of his shaping. The move is the same as the sample's, concession inside development, and the content is entirely new.

The third paragraph widens the frame, as the sample's third paragraph widened from dreamer to world. Here the widening goes from Nick to the novel's form: the narration shapes judgment not only through what Nick says but through what the structure withholds and reveals. The topic sentence: Fitzgerald gives Nick the double position, "within and without," so that the reader experiences enchantment and judgment simultaneously, and the simultaneity is the shaping. The embedded evidence is the phrase itself, read for its prepositions: "within" promises intimacy and "without" promises distance, and the novel delivers both at once, which is why the reader's judgment of Gatsby stays permanently mixed. The integrated context, arriving where it sharpens rather than as a freestanding paragraph, is the novel's narrative strategy: a participant narrator who loves his subject is the period's answer to the problem of how to render glamour without endorsing it, and naming that strategy explains why the shaping feels both sincere and suspect. No new facts about the novel's background are needed beyond that single functional point, because the criterion prices integration, not quantity.

The conclusion gathers, as the sample's conclusion gathered, returning the question's key terms with the essay's full weight behind them. Nick's narration shapes the reader's judgment decisively, the opening enchantment, the complicit smile, the double position are all Nick's manufacture, and no reader meets an unmediated Gatsby. But the shaping is not total, because the facts Nick faithfully records, the criminal money, the careless people, the receding future, bound what the enchantment can claim. The defensible answer to "how far" is therefore: far enough that the judgment is Nick's before it is the reader's, not so far that the reader cannot judge Nick's judgment. Nothing new appears; the three paragraphs' findings are gathered and the question answered in its own language.

Step back and compare the two essays, the sample and this adaptation, and the transfer becomes visible as structure rather than content. Both open with a contestable thesis built from the prompt's key terms. Both map one term per paragraph in a roadmap sentence. Both write topic sentences that are mini-theses, disputable and serving the whole. Both embed short quotations inside the writer's grammar and read single words where the claim needs proving. Both concede the strongest objection inside the development rather than ignoring it. Both integrate context at the point of use. Both conclude by gathering, with the question's language returning. Not one sentence is shared between them. That is what a transferable principle looks like: a method that survives the complete replacement of its material. The student who can perform this adaptation, taking the moves and building a new essay around a new question, has learned what the sample teaches. The student who can only reproduce the sample has learned its sentences, which the next section shows how to leave behind deliberately.

## From Margin Notes to Method

The prompt-adaptation section proved the moves transfer. This section shows how to extract them deliberately, turning the margin notes from labels on someone else's essay into a method for your own. Extraction is a skill with its own procedure, and it repays explicit teaching, because students left to their own devices extract the wrong things: they copy the quotations, memorize the thesis, admire the vocabulary. Those are the essay's possessions, not its moves. The moves are the operations the essay performs, and operations can be lifted out, named, and practiced on new material until they become habits. What follows is the extraction procedure worked through on the sample, move by move, so the student sees exactly what to take and what to leave.

The procedure has four steps, and each step is demonstrated on one margin note from the table. Step one is isolation: choose a single annotated part and state, in one clause, what the writer did that was not specific to this question. Take the introduction's margin note, "contestable thesis plus roadmap." The question-specific part is the content, admiration limited by object and cost. The transferable operation is the form: take a position the prompt invites but does not supply, make it disputable, and map its terms onto the paragraphs to come. Write that operation as an instruction to yourself: "My thesis will judge between the prompt's key terms, and its final sentence will name one term per paragraph." That instruction contains nothing about Gatsby's dream, which is precisely why it works for any prompt. The test of a successful isolation is that the resulting instruction could be followed by someone writing about a different novel entirely.

Step two is naming the criterion the move serves. The margin notes already do this, pairing each move with argument, analysis, evidence, context, or expression, but the student should reproduce the pairing from understanding rather than trust the label. Ask of the thesis move: which criterion pays for this, and why. The answer is argument, because the criterion prices a position taken on the question and defended, and a contestable thesis with a roadmap is the position in its most legible form. Naming the criterion matters because it tells the student when the move is mandatory and when it is optional. The thesis move is mandatory in every essay, since every prompt needs a position. The integrated-context move is conditional, needed only where background sharpens a passage. A student who knows which criterion each move serves can prioritize under time pressure, spending the scarce minutes on the moves the mark scheme prices highest for the question at hand.

### What does a study session with the annotated essay look like?

A study session with the annotated essay has three phases: read the essay straight through as an examiner would, then re-read it through the margin notes naming each move and its criterion, then close the essay and rebuild one paragraph from memory using only the move names. The final phase converts labels into habits.

Step three is the rebuilding, and it is the heart of the method. Close the sample, take one move, and perform it on new material. Choose the embedded-evidence move from the first body paragraph. The operation, isolated: fold the shortest proving phrase into your own sentence's grammar, then write the analysis the quotation was placed to support. Now perform it on a passage the sample never used. Take the shirts scene, where Daisy cries over the "beautiful shirts," and build the sentence: Daisy weeps over the shirts not from joy but from recognition, the "beautiful" fabric materializing the wealth she married for and the life she refused Gatsby for. The quotation is three words, embedded, load-bearing: remove it and the sentence's mechanism vanishes. The analysis follows in the quotation's wake, saying what "beautiful" does, turning an aesthetic word into an economic and moral one. That paragraph was not in the sample. Its move was. The student who can do this repeatedly, one move at a time, is no longer studying the essay. The student is practicing the method.

Work the same procedure on a harder move, the concession placed inside the development. The operation, isolated: grant the strongest objection to your paragraph's claim, then show the claim surviving the grant in narrowed form. Perform it on the novel's ending, material the sample used only in passing. Suppose the paragraph claims the closing pages redeem Gatsby by universalizing his longing. The objection: the universalizing, "so we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past," is beautiful but evasive, folding Gatsby's specific crimes and victims into a general human condition where no one is responsible. The concession grants it: the closing sentence does generalize, and the generalization does soften the ledger of Myrtle, George, and Gatsby himself. The survival: the softening is the point, because the novel's final judgment is that the longing outlasts its objects and its costs, which is a larger claim than exoneration and a sadder one. The paragraph now contains its own strongest critic and answers them, which is the move the top band prices under argument. Notice what transferred: nothing about admiration, the dream, or the green light. Only the operation, grant then narrow, applied to new material. Two moves rebuilt, two habits forming, and the sample's sentences nowhere in sight.

Step four is the assembly check, and it uses the table as a diagnostic. Take a practice paragraph of your own and run it down the margin notes as a checklist: does it open with a mini-thesis, is the quotation embedded, does analysis follow the quotation, does the paragraph advance the thesis's terms. Where the paragraph fails a check, the corresponding margin note tells you the repair, because each note names both the move and its value. Try it on the pastiche paragraph from the copying section, the forced green-light paragraph answering the wealth question. The checklist finds four failures in seconds: no mini-thesis, only a topic announcement; the quotation dropped, not embedded; the post-quotation sentence restating rather than reading; the final sentence claiming a connection the paragraph never built. Each failure maps to a margin note, and each note prescribes the exact repair, which is why the diagnostic saves the revision time students usually spend staring at a weak paragraph without knowing what is wrong with it. This is the table's second life, after the reading and before the examination: not a display to admire but an instrument to apply. Students who use it this way report a characteristic experience, which is worth describing because it marks the moment the method becomes theirs. The first few applications feel mechanical, even clumsy, as the moves are performed self-consciously. Then, after a handful of practice paragraphs, the moves start arriving unbidden, and the writing begins to feel like thinking rather than assembling. That transition, from deliberate to fluent, is the whole point of extraction, and it cannot be reached by memorization, which keeps the moves forever external, recited rather than owned.

A final caution about what not to extract, because the procedure has a failure mode. Do not extract the essay's content: its thesis about admiration, its quotations, its readings of specific lines. Content is bound to its question, and carrying it to a new prompt is the copying trap in a subtler form. Extract only operations: the way the thesis judges between terms, the way the quotation is embedded, the way the concession is placed inside the development, the way the conclusion gathers without adding. A useful test is the novel test: if the extracted instruction would still make sense applied to a different book, it is a move. If it mentions Gatsby's dream, Daisy's voice, or the green light, it is content wearing a move's clothes. The margin notes were written to make this separation easy, naming the operation first and the instance second, and the student who reads them with the separation in mind will find the table yields its method generously. The sample's generosity is its real value. It does not ask to be remembered. It asks to be dismantled, and then rebuilt as your own.

## Revising with the Table: A Before-and-After Demonstration

The table's second life as a diagnostic instrument deserves its own worked demonstration, because students often understand the margin notes and still cannot apply them to their own prose. The gap between recognizing a move and performing one is where revision technique lives. This section takes genuinely mediocre paragraphs, the kind that fill examination scripts, runs them through the table's checklist, and rebuilds them move by move, so the student watches diagnosis become revision. Two transformations are shown, a body paragraph and a conclusion, because those are the parts where the middle band most often settles.

The body paragraph to revise answers the sample's question, on the green light, and it is fluent, knowledgeable, and entirely middle-band.

*The green light is one of the most famous symbols in the novel. It sits at the end of Daisy's dock, and Gatsby reaches toward it across the water. The light represents his hopes and dreams for the future, especially his dream of being with Daisy. Fitzgerald calls it "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us," which shows that the dream keeps moving away from him. The green light is therefore an important symbol of the American Dream.*

Run the checklist. Thesis link: the paragraph has no mini-thesis, only a topic announcement, "one of the most famous symbols"; it never states what the paragraph claims about admiration. Quotation placement: the famous line is dropped in after the point is made, a stamp rather than a load-bearing member; covering it with a thumb leaves every sentence standing. Post-quotation analysis: the sentence after the quote, "which shows that the dream keeps moving away," restates the quotation's meaning instead of reading its language; no word is examined. Development: the final sentence reaches for "the American Dream," a grand label the paragraph never earns. Four failures, each named by a margin note, each prescribing its repair.

The rebuild performs the repairs in order. First the mini-thesis, disputable and serving the essay's terms: the green light earns admiration not as a symbol of hope but as the novel's most honest image of desire, because its language admits what its glow conceals. Second the embedded quotation, chosen short: the sentence now reads, "Fitzgerald's phrase for it, 'the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us,' makes the recession grammatical," folding the line into the writer's claim so the sentence needs it. Third the word-level reading that follows: "orgastic" gives the wanting a bodily urgency that admiration can attach to, while "recedes" builds the failure into the verb itself, so the image honors the intensity and pronounces the verdict in the same breath. Fourth the development: the paragraph ends not with a label but with a finding the next paragraph can take up, that the light's honesty about failure separates it from Daisy's voice, which conceals its corruption inside charm.

*The green light earns its admiration not as a symbol of hope but as the novel's most honest image of desire. Fitzgerald's phrase for it, "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us," makes the recession grammatical: "orgastic" gives the wanting a bodily urgency that admiration can attach to, while "recedes" builds the failure into the verb itself, so the image honors the intensity and pronounces the verdict in the same breath. That honesty separates the light from Daisy's voice, which conceals its corruption inside charm; the light admits, in its own language, what the dream costs.*

The comparison teaches what the checklist alone cannot: revision is not polishing sentences but installing moves. The original was not badly written; it was unperformed, knowledge displayed rather than thinking done. The rebuild keeps the student's knowledge and changes the operations, and the operations are what the criteria price. A student who practices this transformation, taking one flat paragraph per session and running it through the four repairs, builds the revision habit the examination rewards: hearing, in one's own draft, the places where the pencil would not move, and knowing exactly which move would move it.

The conclusion gets the same treatment, because conclusions are where the middle band most visibly settles. The weak version:

*To sum up, Gatsby's dream is admirable because he loves Daisy so much and never gives up. The green light, Daisy's voice, and the parties all show how strong his dream was. Although his dream had some problems, like the criminal activities and the deaths, his devotion was deeply inspiring. Gatsby will always be remembered as a great dreamer.*

The checklist is merciless. It introduces no synthesis, only restatement: "loves Daisy so much and never gives up" repeats the introduction's devotion without the essay's hard-won qualifications. It lists rather than gathers: "the green light, Daisy's voice, and the parties" is an inventory, not a judgment. It hedges where the essay earned precision: "some problems, like the criminal activities and the deaths" shrinks the cost the third paragraph proved into an aside. And it ends on sentiment, "deeply inspiring," abandoning the measured position for applause. An examiner's second reading finds nothing here that the essay did not already contain, stated less precisely.

The rebuild applies the conclusion's margin note, synthesis with no new argument, gathering each paragraph's finding and returning the question's language:

*Gatsby's dream, then, is admirable to the extent that devotion commands respect, and no further: the green light proved the devotion total, Daisy's voice proved its object unworthy, and the ledger of the dead proved its cost indefensible. The novel rescues the dreaming from the dream, honoring the refusal to accept the given world while condemning what Gatsby made of that refusal. Admiration without qualification misreads tenderness as approval; dismissal without remainder misses why the tenderness is there. The defensible measure holds both.*

Each clause gathers a paragraph; the question's "to what extent" is answered with a measured boundary; nothing new appears. Students should practice this transformation alongside the paragraph one, because the two together cover the essay's most failure-prone parts. The diagnostic habit generalizes: any draft can be run down the margin notes, any failing part has its named repair, and the repairs are the moves. That is the table's final lesson, and the most practical one in this article. The annotated essay is not only a performance to study but a toolkit for fixing the next performance, which is the one that gets marked.

## Verdict

The verdict this capstone reaches is a ranking, and it should be stated plainly. Of everything the essay block offers, the fully annotated worked essay is the highest-leverage study object, because it is the only one that shows the principles operating together under the conditions the examination imposes. Instruction teaches the moves. Demonstration teaches the performance. The performance is what gets marked. A student who has ten hours should spend the first six on the stage articles, learning each move in isolation, and the last four on this essay, watching the moves combine, extracting them, and rebuilding them on new prompts. That allocation reflects a judgment about where marks come from: not from possessing more principles, but from executing the known ones simultaneously, which only the worked example trains.

The verdict comes with its essential qualification, which the copying section argued at length and which bears repeating as the article's final position. The worked essay earns its place in those last four hours only if it is used as a method to dismantle, never as a script to memorize. Used as a method, it is the fastest route to the top band, because it converts abstract criteria into visible operations and then into habits. Used as a script, it is the fastest route to the middle band, because it substitutes recall for thinking and answers the wrong question fluently. The difference is not in the essay. It is in the student. The same table that teaches the moves enables the copying, and no annotation can prevent a reader determined to memorize. What the annotation can do, and what this article has tried to do around it, is make the better use so much more productive, and the worse use so clearly self-defeating, that the choice becomes obvious. Learn the moves. Leave the sentences.

That is also the position this block was built to support. Thirty-five articles of stage-by-stage instruction would be a library; with the capstone they become a curriculum, the stages supplying the vocabulary and the worked example supplying the performance the vocabulary describes. The series' standard was never that students know more about essays, but that they write better ones, and the difference between those two outcomes is exactly the difference between possessing the sample's sentences and performing its moves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does a high-scoring Gatsby essay look like?

A high-scoring Gatsby essay looks like argued thinking sustained across the whole response, not like a display of knowledge about the novel. It opens with a contestable thesis that answers the set question, develops that thesis through paragraphs that each advance the argument rather than restating it, embeds short quotations inside the writer's own sentences, reads specific words closely where the claim needs proving, concedes the strongest objection, integrates context only where it sharpens a passage, and concludes by gathering the findings into a final judgment. The annotated essay in this article's table is the concrete image: notice its proportions, the short positioned introduction, the substantial analytical paragraphs, the gathering conclusion, and notice that every paragraph performs a move the mark scheme prices. Examiners describe the top band in verbs, argues, analyzes, selects, integrates, and the high-scoring essay is the script in which those verbs are unmissable.

### Q: How does the worked essay show the components together?

The worked essay shows the components together by performing them in sequence inside one continuous response, so the reader watches the thesis set the terms, each paragraph take up one term and develop it, the evidence arrive embedded in the writer's grammar, the analysis slow over single words at the decisive moments, and the conclusion gather the strands without adding new claims. The margin notes label each part with its move and the criterion it serves, which turns the finished piece into a map of the cooperation: the thesis sets the terms the paragraphs develop, the paragraphs' quotations are chosen to prove the paragraphs' claims, the close readings serve the claims the quotations support. Studied alongside the stage articles, the essay converts separate lessons into one visible method, demonstrating the integration that instruction alone cannot quite deliver.

### Q: What does each annotated move earn?

Each annotated move earns credit under one of the five criteria examiners apply: argument, analysis, evidence, context, or expression. The contestable thesis earns argument credit for taking a defensible position; the roadmap earns structure credit for making the line of reasoning legible; the embedded quotation earns evidence credit for load-bearing placement; the word-level reading earns analysis credit for showing what specific language does; the integrated context earns context credit for sharpening a passage's meaning; the gathering conclusion earns argument credit for completing the thinking. The table's margin notes name the move and its criterion explicitly, so the student sees the valuation rather than guessing it. The discipline behind the notes is the real lesson: examiners credit only what the prose performs, and every mark the sample receives can be traced to a specific operation on the page.

### Q: What lifts the essay to the top?

What lifts the essay to the top is the combination of sustained judgment and directed analysis, neither of which can substitute for the other. The judgment is the measured thesis, admiration granted for devotion and force, withheld for object and cost, developed across paragraphs that complicate rather than repeat, so the argument deepens as it proceeds. The analysis is the close reading directed by that judgment: the grammar of "recedes," the preposition in "full of money," the plainness of "careless," each word read because the paragraph's claim needs exactly that word proved. Either half alone stalls in the middle bands, all skeleton or all muscle. Together they produce the density the top bands describe, every paragraph pulling its weight, every quotation load-bearing, every sentence advancing the case or reading the text. The lift is not a single trick but the absence of any coasting part.

### Q: Should you copy the sample essay?

You should not copy the sample essay, and the reasons are structural rather than moral. The sample answers one specific question, and your examination question will be a different one; a memorized essay about admirability cannot answer a prompt about narration or wealth, and an essay that answers the wrong question forfeits the argument criterion's foundation no matter how fluent its prose. Beyond the prompt mismatch, the criteria price verbs, arguing, analyzing, selecting, and a memorized script performs none of them in the hall; it performs recall, which no criterion rewards. Examiners detect the pattern through irrelevance: the thesis uses the wrong vocabulary, the paragraphs never touch the set question's key terms, the analysis feels replayed. Learn the sample's moves and leave its sentences, which is the distinction the adaptation and extraction sections of this article teach in full.

### Q: How do you learn transferable principles from it?

You learn transferable principles by extracting operations rather than content, using the four-step procedure this article demonstrates: isolate the move from its question-specific instance, name the criterion it serves, rebuild it on new material with the sample closed, and run your own drafts against the margin notes as a diagnostic. The isolation step is the crucial one: state in one clause what the writer did that was not specific to this prompt, and write it as an instruction that would still make sense applied to a different novel. The test of success is the prompt-adaptation exercise, taking the same moves to a genuinely different question and building a new essay from them. When you can perform that transfer, the principles are yours. Until then, keep the sample open and keep practicing the extraction, because the moves become habits only through deliberate repeated application.

### Q: How does a capstone article fit into your exam revision?

A capstone article fits into revision as the synthesis phase, after the stage-by-stage study and before the timed practice. Spend the early revision hours on the stage articles, learning each move in isolation: the thesis, the paragraph, the quotation, the close reading, the conclusion. Then turn to the capstone to watch those moves combine inside one complete performance, reading the essay straight through first and then through the margin notes. The synthesis corrects the most common revision failure, which is knowing the parts without being able to perform the whole. Finally, move to timed practice on new prompts, using the extracted moves rather than the sample's sentences. The capstone is therefore the bridge between knowing and doing, and revision that skips it often produces essays that read like checklists being ticked, all parts present and the whole never forming.

### Q: What should you read first in a fully worked essay demonstration?

Read the essay straight through first, the way an examiner reads, without stopping at the margin notes, to feel the argument gather force as a continuous piece of prose. The first reading builds the most important impression the demonstration offers: what a complete high-scoring response feels like from the inside, its pace, its proportions, its density. Only on the second reading should you slow down for the annotation, matching each margin note to its passage and asking why the move earns its credit. Reversing the order, notes first and essay second, turns the reading into a dissection before you have felt the whole, which weakens the synthesis the capstone exists to provide. A useful third pass is the structural skim: read only the introduction, the topic sentences, and the conclusion, to see the skeleton the analysis hangs on.

### Q: When should you stop consulting the worked example?

Stop consulting the worked example when you can reproduce its moves on a new prompt without looking, which is the operational definition of having learned them. The weaning has recognizable stages: first you need the table open beside every practice paragraph, then you need only the move names as a checklist, then you catch yourself performing a move unbidden, embedding a quotation or conceding an objection without planning to. At that point the example has done its work, and continued consulting risks the copying trap, keeping the moves external and recited rather than owned. A practical test is the blank-page paragraph: given a fresh question and ten minutes, can you produce a paragraph with a mini-thesis, an embedded quotation, and a word-level reading, with no reference material. When the answer is yes, close the sample and practice without it.

### Q: How do you write your own introduction after studying the sample?

Write your own introduction by performing the sample's moves on your prompt's terms, never by adapting its sentences. Read your question's key terms and decide what judgment they invite; take a measured, disputable position between those terms, the way the sample judged between devotion and cost; then map the position's terms onto your paragraphs in a final roadmap sentence. The introduction should be short, positioned, and structural, spending its words on the claim and the plan rather than on background or plot summary. Check it against the sample's introduction functionally: does it give the essay something to defend, and can the examiner see the line of reasoning before it unfolds. If either answer is no, revise the thesis before drafting further, because every paragraph downstream inherits the introduction's clarity or its confusion.

### Q: How do you check that your thesis answers your question and not the sample's?

Check by testing your thesis against your prompt's key terms directly: underline the question's operative words, then verify that each one appears in your thesis doing argumentative work, not merely mentioned. The sample's thesis is built from "dream" and "admirable," the terms of its question; yours must be built from yours, or the essay will drift toward the sample's territory no matter how original the paragraphs feel. A second test is the substitution test: replace your question with the sample's and ask whether your thesis still fits. If it fits both, it is too generic and needs sharpening toward your prompt's specific demand. The copying trap often enters here disguised as structure, the student keeping the sample's admirable-or-not frame for a question about something else entirely, so run the key-term check on every thesis before you commit to it.

### Q: What makes a practice question suitable for testing the transferred moves?

A suitable practice question is one the sample never addressed, forcing genuine transfer rather than disguised reproduction. Choose prompts from different territory: if the sample judged admiration, practice on narration, structure, character, or the ending, so no sentence of content can carry over and only the moves survive. The question should also be evaluative or analytical rather than descriptive, since the moves were built for judgment: prompts with "to what extent," "how far," or "discuss" test the thesis and concession moves, while purely descriptive prompts leave them idle. Finally, pick questions you have not already written about, because a rehearsed answer lets memory substitute for method. The adaptation section's narration question is a model of suitability: distant enough to require invention, demanding enough to require the full method.

### Q: How do you self-mark a practice essay using the margin notes as criteria?

Self-mark by reading your draft with the table's margin notes as a checklist, paragraph by paragraph, asking of each part the question the note asks: what move does this paragraph make, and which criterion prices it. Mark the thesis for contestability and roadmap, each topic sentence for mini-thesis quality, each quotation for embedded placement, each post-quotation sentence for genuine analysis, the conclusion for gathering without new claims. Where a paragraph fails a check, the corresponding note prescribes the repair, which turns self-marking from vague dissatisfaction into targeted revision. Be strictest about the two moves students most often fake: the word-level reading, which must belong to this quotation and no other, and the concession, which must genuinely complicate rather than perform humility. Score honestly against the criteria, not against your effort, since the examiner will do the same.

### Q: How do you rehearse the essay's structure without memorizing its sentences?

Rehearse the structure as a skeleton of moves, never as prose: thesis judging between the prompt's terms, one term per paragraph, each paragraph opening with a mini-thesis, evidence embedded, analysis following, concession placed inside the development, conclusion gathering. Practice by writing the skeleton for a new prompt in note form, five or six lines, then expanding one line into a full paragraph from understanding rather than memory. The skeleton is what transfers; sentences are what trap. A useful drill is the five-minute plan: given a fresh question, produce only the thesis and the paragraph map, then stop, and compare the map's logic against the sample's table without borrowing any of its content. Repeated skeleton practice builds the architectural habit the top bands describe, the shaped response, while leaving the sentences to be written fresh each time, which is exactly what the examination requires.

### Q: What does the worked example reveal about paragraph transitions?

The worked example reveals that strong transitions are logical rather than verbal: each paragraph begins where the previous paragraph's finding left off, so the movement feels inevitable without connective decoration. The sample's second paragraph opens by granting the first paragraph's point about devotion and then turning it against the object; the third opens by extending the limitation from object to cost; the fourth relocates the whole judgment into the narration. No paragraph announces its transition with a formula; the transition is the argument's own development, visible in the first sentence's relationship to the previous paragraph's last claim. The lesson for drafting is to end each paragraph with a finding that the next paragraph can take up, which makes transitions write themselves. Students who struggle with flow usually lack not connective phrases but this logical chaining, and the sample's table lets you trace the chain link by link.

### Q: How do you know when you have understood a move rather than memorized it?

You know by the transfer test: close the sample, take a new prompt, and perform the move on material the sample never touched. If you can embed a quotation, concede an objection, or build a roadmap for a question about narration having only studied the sample on admiration, the move is understood, because understanding is what survives the change of content. Memorization fails this test visibly: the student reaches for the sample's sentences, its thesis about the dream, its readings of specific lines, and stalls when they do not fit. A second indicator is fluency under time pressure: understood moves arrive unbidden in timed practice, while memorized ones require pausing to recall. The extraction section's novel test sharpens the distinction further: state the move as an instruction that would make sense for a different book entirely. If you can, it is yours.

### Q: How does the worked essay handle a counter-reading?

The worked essay handles its counter-reading by absorbing it into the development rather than quarantining it in a separate paragraph. The strongest objection to the thesis, that the dream's cost and corruption outweigh its devotion entirely, is conceded progressively: the second paragraph grants the devotion but faults the object, the third extends the fault to the cost, so the objection is answered by being incorporated. The thesis survives not by defeating the counter-reading but by including it, since the measured position, admiration limited by object and cost, already contains the objection's force. This is the move the top bands reward under argument: a line of reasoning that complicates itself honestly rather than prosecuting a single charge. Students who relegate counter-arguments to a token paragraph at the end signal that the objection was an afterthought; the sample shows it as the engine of the essay's development.

### Q: How do you turn the essay's thesis into your own contestable claim?

Turn it by keeping the operation and discarding the content: the sample's thesis judges between its prompt's key terms on a sliding scale, and yours must judge between yours. Identify your question's evaluative terms, decide where on the scale the evidence points, and state the position in a sentence a reasonable reader could dispute. Then give the thesis the sample's structural property, terms that map onto paragraphs, so the claim generates the essay's plan. The result will share no words with the sample's thesis and every one of its virtues: disputability, which gives the essay something to defend; measurability, which lets the conclusion answer "to what extent"; and generativity, which makes each paragraph inherit relevance. Practice the transformation explicitly: write the sample's thesis, label its operation, then write yours performing the same operation on different terms, until the operation feels like a habit rather than a borrowing.

### Q: What should you do after you can reproduce the sample's argument from memory?

After you can reproduce the sample's argument from memory, treat that fluency as a warning sign and change your practice immediately. Reproduction means the content has lodged where the moves should be, which is the copying trap in its most comfortable form: the essay feels learned, but what was learned was sentences. The corrective is to ban the sample's question from your practice entirely and work only on prompts it never addressed, performing the moves on fresh material until the memory of the sample's content fades behind the habit of its method. A further step is to teach the moves aloud, explaining to an imaginary student why the thesis is contestable or why the quotation is embedded, because explanation forces operational understanding that recitation never reaches. The goal was never to know this essay. The goal is to write your own at its level, and that work begins where reproduction ends.
