Luigi da Porto, a wounded cavalry officer from Vicenza, wrote the story the world now calls Romeo and Juliet before Shakespeare was born, and the novella he left behind, Giulietta e Romeo, fixed nearly everything the later fame rests on. He placed the tale in Verona. He named the feuding houses Montecchi and Cappelletti. He called the lovers Romeo and Giulietta, gave them a ball for a meeting place, a friar for a confessor, a sleeping draught for an escape, and a tomb for an ending. The verdict of this article is plain at the outset: Shakespeare supplied the poetry, the speed, and half the cast, but the architecture of the story, its city, its surnames, its machinery of secret marriage and failed message, is da Porto's construction, and source scholarship that begins anywhere else begins too late.

That verdict needs its evidence, because the da Porto novella is also a contested document. Its date is uncertain, its debts are disputed, its author wrapped the fiction in a claim that the events had really happened, and the line of transmission between an Italian prose tale printed at Venice and a London stage play of the 1590s runs through several intermediaries. Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare remains the standard map of the play's ancestry, prints da Porto at the head of the line for good reason: every later version, Bandello's expansion, Boaistuau's French retelling, Painter's prose, Brooke's poem, and the play itself, descends from the form da Porto gave the tale. Olin H. Moore, the most determined scholar of the legend's origins, pushed the pedigree further back to Masuccio Salernitano, and the disagreement between those two emphases, pedigree against construction, is the argument this article adjudicates as it goes.

Restoring da Porto to the head of the story changes how the play itself is read. Details that look like a dramatist's free inventions, the friar's hope that a marriage might end a feud, the hero racing a false report against a true letter, the heroine waking in a vault beside the man the letter was meant to save, turn out to be inherited engineering, chosen by a writer working two generations earlier in another language and another form. What Shakespeare added then stands out in its true proportions: not the plot, which he took nearly whole, but the pressure of verse, the crowded street life of Verona, and a set of secondary figures raised from names or nothing into people. Source study of this kind is not ancestor worship. It is the only reliable way to distribute credit for the most retold love story in European literature between the man who built it and the man who made it sing.

The method of what follows matches that distribution of credit. Each element of the familiar story is traced to the first text that contains it in working form; each scholarly quarrel, over the date, over Masuccio, over Lucina, is stated with its authorities named and then adjudicated rather than merely reported; and the play is kept in view throughout as the tale's greatest reader, the standard against which the value of da Porto's inventions is most easily seen. Where the evidence runs out, the article says so, since a source study that invents certainty about an undated, posthumous, second-hand narrative would betray the very standards the novella's own soldier-author, with his dispatch-writer's respect for verifiable fact, brings to his fiction.

## Who Was Luigi da Porto, the Soldier Who Wrote Giulietta e Romeo?

Luigi da Porto was born at Vicenza in 1485, into a respectable family of that city, and his first career was not literature but war. He served the Venetian Republic as a captain of light cavalry in Friuli during the grinding Italian wars that followed the League of Cambrai, campaigning along the republic's exposed eastern frontier. His historical letters, written from the field, record sieges, raids, and the miserable logistics of that frontier war with a soldier's impatience for ornament. In 1511 a severe wound, received in the fighting in Friuli, left him partly paralysed and ended his active service while he was still in his twenties. The retired officer withdrew to his villa at Montorso, in the hills west of Vicenza, and turned to the writing that the wound had made his remaining profession.

The novella belongs to that retirement. Da Porto wrote verse, letters, and prose in the cultivated Italian of the Bembo circle, and Giulietta e Romeo is framed as a gift: the dedicatory letter addresses Lucina Savorgnan, a noblewoman of one of Friuli's great families, and presents the tale as a true history of two Veronese lovers, offered to a lady whose own circumstances gave unhappy love a local address. The framing matters for everything that follows, because da Porto does not present the story as an invention to be admired but as a record to be believed, "novellamente ritrovata," newly recovered, in the wording of its first printed title. He died in 1529, before the tale reached print, and so never saw the small book that would carry his name further than his letters or his soldiering ever did.

His credentials as a witness deserve a cold eye. He was a Vicentine, not a Veronese; Verona was a neighbouring city of the Venetian mainland state, familiar ground to a cavalry officer of the region without being home ground. He claims the story at second hand, from a Veronese archer named Peregrino who served in his company and told it during the campaigns. That chain of custody, a tale carried by a common soldier from the city where it is set, is exactly the kind of provenance a sixteenth-century novella liked to wear, and exactly the kind a modern reader should weigh rather than swallow. What is not in doubt is the fact of authorship: the novella is his, the form it gives the legend is his, and no earlier surviving text contains the story in anything like the shape he gave it.

The retirement that produced the tale deserves a fuller picture, because the novella's qualities are a retired soldier's qualities. Da Porto's historical letters from the Friulian war are prized by historians precisely for their unliterary virtues: exact observation, impatience with official lying, and a habit of judging great lords by the suffering their quarrels cost ordinary soldiers. The same habits govern the novella. Its feud is never romanticized into pageantry; it is a standing civic injury that interrupts dances, fills streets with armed young men, and finally fills a tomb, and the narrative voice counts the cost the way the letters count casualties. Even the lover's errancy is observed with a campaigner's eye for logistics: vigils kept in a hostile street, messages entrusted to friars because friars may travel, a ride from Mantua timed against a waking. Scholars of the Italian sources have long noted that da Porto writes love the way his generation of officer-humanists wrote war, as a system of risks managed by intelligence and defeated by institutions.

His literary formation was equally specific. Vicenza and the Venetian mainland in the 1510s and 1520s were saturated with the influence of Pietro Bembo, whose Prose della volgar lingua of 1525 fixed the rules of cultivated Italian prose on fourteenth-century models. Da Porto moved in that orbit, and the novella shows it: the diction is elevated and Tuscanizing, the sentences periodic, the whole framed as a document fit to be laid before a great lady. That a provincial officer's prose exercise, undertaken in enforced leisure at Montorso, should have outlasted the state papers of the war he fought is the kind of irony the letters themselves would have appreciated. He wrote the tale as a man of letters; he had become a man of letters because a wound had unmade him as a man of war.

His other writings fill out the portrait and matter to the tale's interpretation. The Rime, his poems, work the Petrarchan conventions of the period with a soldier's impatience breaking through the polish; the historical letters remain the most respected part of his output among historians of the Italian wars, cited for their ground-level testimony about the Venetian defence of Friuli. Put the three bodies of work together and a consistent mind appears: observant, unsentimental, preoccupied with the gap between what institutions proclaim and what they do, and gifted at letting small facts carry large judgements. The novella's famous restraint, its refusal to weep over its own catastrophe, is the letters' manner applied to fiction. Readers who meet da Porto first as a name in a source list miss the point that his contemporaries could have supplied: the tale was written by one of the most clear-eyed prose witnesses of his generation, and its clarity is a professional habit, not an accident of transmission.

## When Did Luigi da Porto Write the Novella, and Why Do Scholars Dispute the Date?

The honest answer is that the novella carries two dates and neither is certain. Composition is conventionally placed around 1524, in the last working years of da Porto's retirement, and the shorthand "circa 1524" used by Bullough and the reference tradition rests on internal and biographical inference rather than on any dated manuscript. Publication came after his death: the tale was printed at Venice around 1530 or 1531, anonymously at first, in a collection of his prose and verse issued by others' hands. Between the writing and the printing there is no surviving autograph to interrogate, and the gap is one reason the dating stays soft.

The uncertainty is not pedantry, because the date governs the debts. If the novella was complete by the middle of the 1520s, da Porto wrote before Bandello's generation had reshaped the Italian novella, and his tale stands as a product of the war years and the Bembo-era prose reforms. If composition slid later, the sequence of influence among the Italian collections tightens. Scholars have also worried at the dedication: the letter to Lucina Savorgnan is integral to the tale's meaning, since it binds the fiction to a named living reader, yet dedications are precisely the parts of a posthumous book that editors and friends could adjust. The cautious handling the question deserves is to hold three things separately: the tale was written in the 1520s, most plausibly around 1524; it was printed posthumously at Venice about 1530 or 1531; and every stronger claim than that is inference wearing a date's clothing.

One further caution belongs here. The novella's own internal date, the era of Bartolomeo della Scala, lord of Verona in the first years of the fourteenth century, is a setting, not evidence. Da Porto deliberately archaized his story, placing it some two centuries before his own time in the Verona of the Scaliger signoria, and that chosen distance is part of the fiction's design. Confusing the tale's internal chronology with its composition date is the oldest error in the scholarship of the legend, and the source studies that keep the two apart, Bullough's among them, are the ones to trust.

The print history adds a further layer of caution. The tale circulated in the collections of da Porto's prose and verse through the middle decades of the sixteenth century, and it was in that form, an Italian novella among others in a dead officer's gathered works, that the next generation of storytellers found it. Matteo Bandello, preparing his own vast collection of novelle, took the Veronese tale up within a generation of its printing, which establishes the one firm chronological point in the whole inquiry: whatever the exact year of composition, da Porto's text was in print and in other writers' hands in time to be wholly reworked by 1554. The interval between the anonymous Venetian printing and Bandello's expansion is the tale's first transmission, and it happened inside Italy, between two writers of the same novella culture, before any translator touched it. Arguments about the dating that ignore that fixed point, and several older ones did, build on sand.

The anonymity of the first printing deserves its own sentence in the record, because it shaped the tale's strange fame. A story published without a living author's name to defend it, and framed as a recovered history rather than as a fiction, entered the European bloodstream as something between chronicle and folklore: Bandello could rewrite it without deference, Boaistuau could moralize it without apology, and Verona could eventually adopt it as municipal fact. The recovery of da Porto's authorship by later scholarship is therefore not a footnote but a restoration, the return of a designed work to its designer. Modern editions print the tale under his name as a matter of course, and the source tradition represented by Bullough treats the attribution as settled; what remains genuinely open is only the chronology of composition and the exact weight of the Masuccio debt, the two cautions this article has kept in force throughout.

## The Novella as Literature: Prose Form and the Boccaccio Inheritance

A source study owes its subject one question that plot summaries never ask: what kind of book is this? The novella da Porto wrote belongs to the form Boccaccio's Decameron had made the prestige vehicle of Italian prose, the short tale of love and fortune framed by an occasion and addressed to refined readers, and da Porto works the inheritance knowingly. Where Boccaccio frames with a company of narrators, da Porto frames with a letter: the dedicatory epistle to Lucina does the work of the Decameron's proem, establishing the teller, the hearer, and the moral weather in which the tale is to be received. The device lets him be at once inside the story, as the officer who heard it from his archer, and above it, as the gentleman presenting it to a lady whose judgement he courts.

Inside the frame, his manner is the manner of the early sixteenth-century novella at its most disciplined. Long stretches of the action are carried in narrative summary rather than scene, journeys and vigils and negotiations compressed into a sentence each, while the moments that matter, the ball, the window colloquies, the appeal to the friar, the waking in the tomb, are slowed into direct speech and circumstantial detail. The economy is worth naming because it explains the tale's fitness for the stage: da Porto had already performed the dramatist's first task, deciding which ten minutes of a months-long affair deserved to be scenes. Brooke's poem and the play inherit not only his plot but his proportions, swelling exactly the episodes he had swollen and summarizing where he had summarized. Editors who print the novella beside the play, as Bullough's source volumes do in summary and translation, make the filiation visible at a glance.

His prose also carries one quality the translations tend to lose: a soldierly dryness at the moments of highest feeling. Where Bandello will later amplify the tomb scene into pages of rhetoric, da Porto reports the double death with a restraint that reads as reportage, and the restraint is a large part of why his truth claim persuades. The style is the frame's ally. A tale told like a dispatch asks to be filed as fact, and generations of readers filed it so, to the point that Verona itself eventually obliged with a tomb and a balcony for the tourists. The afterlife of those civic fictions belongs to the study of the play's setting; here it is enough to register that the novella's manner, not merely its matter, taught Europe to believe in Romeo and Giulietta.

## Why Did Da Porto Set the Story in Verona Under Bartolomeo della Scala?

The Prologue of the play still announces the address he chose: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, lines 1 to 2). Before da Porto, the closest ancestor tale, Masuccio's Mariotto and Ganozza, unfolds in Siena. The move to Verona is da Porto's single most consequential decision, because Verona brought with it a ready-made civic mythology of faction: a city ruled in the Scala era by a signoria balanced over feuding magnate houses, close enough to da Porto's Friuli campaigns to be vivid, distant enough in time to be safe. He names the presiding lord precisely, Bartolomeo della Scala, and hangs the tale on that reign as a chronicle hangs on a regnal year.

The choice also solved a narrative problem. A secret marriage between children of enemy houses requires a city where the enmity is public, hereditary, and enforced by the street, and where a friar's hope of reconciling the houses through a wedding makes strategic sense. Verona under the Scala gave da Porto all three conditions in one name. Later writers kept the city without exception: Bandello, Boaistuau, Painter, Brooke, and Shakespeare all stage the story in da Porto's Verona, and the play's Mantua, the place of exile a short ride away, is likewise his geography, fixed in the novella and never moved again.

Verona under the Scala was also, for an Italian reader of the 1520s, a byword with a specific charge. The Scaliger signoria stood in the cultural memory as the last great age of the independent Italian city lordships, splendid, violent, and extinct, a setting in which private faction could still decide public outcomes because the state was a household writ large. Dante himself had been sheltered at Verona by the Scala, and the city's name carried literary consecration as well as political colour. By lodging his feud in that Verona, da Porto bought his tale a double prestige: the glamour of a lost civic world and the authority of the Dantean tradition that had already fixed the Montecchi in the city's story. The choice looks inevitable in retrospect only because it worked; Siena had served Masuccio perfectly well, and nothing in the machinery required Verona at all. The machinery's portability is the measure of the choice's importance. Da Porto did not find the story in Verona. He put it there, and it has never lived anywhere else.

### Why does da Porto present the tale as true history rather than invention?

Because belief is the novella's instrument: by naming a reigning lord, citing a Veronese archer as his informant, and calling the tale a recovered history, da Porto borrows chronicle authority for a love story, making the lovers' deaths register as civic fact rather than as a fiction-maker's arrangement, and binding his dedicatee to judge real conduct, not ornament.

That truth claim should be read as craft, not as testimony. No Veronese record corroborates a Romeo Montecchi or a Giulietta Cappelletti, and the archives of the Scala period, thin as they are, offer the feud's surnames without the lovers. The claim functions the way the frame of Boccaccio's tales functions, as a contract with the reader: treat these events as the kind of thing that happened in that city, and let the moral weight fall where chronicle weight falls. Da Porto, who had written real campaign letters full of verifiable names, knew exactly how a document earns belief, and he spent that knowledge on a fiction.

## The Feud as Da Porto Wrote It: Households, Honour, and the Street

Because the play's feud arrives with a Prince, an edict, and a body count announced in advance, readers often import that machinery into the novella, where it does not exist. Da Porto's feud is quieter and, in its way, more frightening: a standing enmity between two great households, inherited rather than declared, enforced by the young men of each house as a matter of honour and habit, and nowhere dignified with a stated cause. The play's Prince remembers a history of ignition, "Three civil brawls, bred of an airy word, / By thee, old Capulet, and Montague" (1.1.87 to 88), but in the novella no authority catalogues the quarrel at all. The signoria of Bartolomeo stands over Verona as a frame and a date; the feud operates beneath it, in the space where household honour outranks civic peace, and nobody in the tale expects the state to end it. That is precisely why the friar's marriage plot is the tale's only peace process: where no public instrument exists, reconciliation must be smuggled in through kinship.

Mantua completes the feud's geography, and its choice is as deliberate as Verona's. Mantua lay outside the Veronese orbit, a Gonzaga city beyond the reach of Cappelletti influence and of the signoria's household politics, close enough for a night's desperate ride and far enough for an exile to be severed from every protector. Da Porto stations his Romeo there in a condition the tale renders with documentary patience: maintained by his family, starved of reliable news, dependent on servants and friars for every fact about the city that has cast him out. The exile's information famine is the tragedy's enabling condition, since a Romeo resident in Verona could not have been deceived by a funeral, and the geography that creates the famine, Verona near and Mantua far, is part of the original design, preserved by every successor without alteration.

The feud's sociology shapes every device da Porto builds. The ball is penetrable because carnival suspends the feud's border controls for a night. The window courtship is necessary because no neutral ground exists on which a Montecchi and a Cappelletti might be seen speaking. The secret marriage is inevitable because a public suit would be read as an act of war by other means, a Montecchi attempt on the Cappelletti succession. Tebaldo's death in a street brawl is the feud's ordinary tax, collected at random. Even the tomb is feud territory, a family vault into which the enemy husband must break like a raider. Strip the feud out and no incident in the novella survives; it is not the story's background but its physics. Later writers intensified the physics, Shakespeare most of all, by giving the feud speeches, servants, and a nemesis in the Prince, yet the system they intensified is da Porto's: two households, one city, no arbiter, and love as the only smuggled good.

One consequence deserves emphasis, because it separates the novella from the folklore it is sometimes mistaken for. Da Porto's feud has no origin myth. No ancient insult, no stolen bride, no boundary stone explains it; the tale begins, as the play's Chorus begins, with the enmity as a given of the world, "from ancient grudge" in the Prologue's phrase. The refusal to explain is a literary decision of the first order. A feud with a cause might be argued out of existence; a feud without one can only be outlasted, outwitted, or paid for, and the tale tries the three in order. Da Porto, who had spent his youth in the Friulian wars watching factions whose origins nobody could state and whose costs everybody could count, wrote the feud he knew: hereditary, causeless in practice, and fatal chiefly to the young.

The feud's manpower deserves a note, because da Porto is precise about it. The enmity is not prosecuted by the old men, who appear in the tale as fathers disposing of marriages, but by the young men of the two houses and their companies, for whom the quarrel is a code of the street: to be seen, to be accompanied, to answer a slight before witnesses. Tebaldo is the code's purest product in the tale, a champion whose whole identity is the house's honour at sword-point, and Romeo's killing of him is therefore not a private act but a payment extracted by the system from its newest debtor. The play multiplies the street's population and gives the code its theorists and its clowns, yet the demography, old men arranging, young men bleeding, is da Porto's exact sociology, drawn from the factional Italy he had campaigned across.

## Where Did the Names Montecchi and Cappelletti Come From?

Here the verdict must be given against da Porto's originality, because the feud's surnames are older than his novella and he took them from the most famous Italian poem of all. Dante, in the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, surveying the factions that had ruined Italy, couples the two names in a single line of reproach: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti," come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, houses already ruined or ruining themselves by faction. The Montecchi were a real Veronese family of the Ghibelline party; the Cappelletti were historically a family of Cremona, and Dante's line grieves over both as exhibits of civic self-destruction rather than as enemies of each other in one city. The fuller account of that Dantean background belongs to [Dante's Montecchi and Cappelletti and the feud's literary ancestry](/2025/11/28/dante-montecchi-cappelletti-feud/), which traces what the poet actually wrote about the two houses.

Da Porto's act was editorial and decisive at once. He took Dante's coupled surnames, settled both houses in Verona, set them at each other's throats in the Scala era, and made their enmity the engine of a love story. Nothing in Dante suggests lovers, a feud over a marriage, or Verona as the stage for the pair together; all of that is da Porto's construction on borrowed stone. The distinction matters for the map this article builds: the names are inherited, the feud as the story's mechanism is invented, and the difference between those two statements is the difference between a pedigree and an achievement. Bullough's summary of the sources keeps the Dante debt in its place, as the explanation of the surnames and of nothing else.

The Dante passage repays a closer look, because it shows what kind of material da Porto was working with. In Purgatorio 6, the poet meets the troubadour Sordello and breaks into the great invective against Italy, a catalogue of cities and houses devouring themselves in faction while the emperor neglects the garden of the empire. Montecchi and Cappelletti appear in that catalogue as families already ruined, "already come to grief," exhibits in an argument about misgovernment, not as principals in any story. There is no feud between them in Dante, no Verona stage shared by the pair, no lovers, no friar, no tomb. The names lie in the canto like two spent cartridges from Italy's civil wars. What da Porto did was to pick them up, reload them, and fire them through a plot: assigning one to the hero and one to the heroine, planting both in the Montecchi's own city, and converting Dante's emblem of civic decay into the machinery of a private catastrophe. Literary history is full of borrowed names; very few borrowings re-found a legend. The distinction between Dante's line and da Porto's use of it is the cleanest illustration in the whole source question of the difference between a pedigree and an act of authorship.

The anglicized forms show the transmission at work. Montecchi becomes Montague and Cappelletti becomes Capulet as the tale passes through French and English hands, the Italian plurals worn down into the surnames an English audience could hold. What never changes, at any stage, is the pairing itself and the city beneath it, because da Porto had welded names, houses, and place into a single unit that later writers received as one indivisible fact.

## How Does Da Porto Portray Romeo and Giulietta Themselves?

His Romeo is a young Veronese gentleman of the Montecchi, already suffering when the tale opens, not for Giulietta but for another lady, a hard-hearted beauty who repays his devotion with coldness. The prior, hopeless attachment is da Porto's device and one of his most durable bequests: it sends Romeo to the Cappelletti ball under friendly pressure to look at other women, and it sets the pattern, renewed at Mantua and in the tomb, of a lover whose feeling runs ahead of his information. Shakespeare keeps the structure and gives the cold lady a name, Rosaline, and a voice in other mouths; the nameless cruelty of da Porto's original becomes, in the play, the explicit foil against which the real attachment is measured.

His Giulietta is eighteen years old, the only child of the head of the Cappelletti, and she is drawn with more initiative than the tradition that followed sometimes allowed her. At the ball it is her hand that lingers and her wit that engages; in the night courtship she tests the stranger's identity and intentions before she yields anything; facing the imposed marriage, she carries her refusal to the friar and accepts the desperate remedy on her own decision. Da Porto's narrative voice treats her with a respect the moralizing versions later in the century would qualify: Brooke's English poem would scold the lovers in its preface, but the Italian original lets Giulietta's conduct argue for itself. Her age is worth pausing on, because the play famously makes its heroine not yet fourteen while Brooke had made her sixteen; da Porto's eighteen-year-old is the eldest of the line, an adult by the standards of her city, and the pathos of her choices changes accordingly. Where the play's terror is partly a child's courage, the novella's is a woman's deliberation, and the difference starts here, in his text.

Together, the pair as he writes them are less lyrical and more social than their stage descendants. Their speeches in the novella are reported in prose summary as often as they are rendered directly, their passion measured in journeys, vigils, and negotiations conducted through windows and friars. What da Porto gives them is not a voice, that gift waited for the dramatist, but a situation so well jointed that every later writer found it easier to re-voice than to rebuild.

The distribution of speech in the novella confirms the portrait. Da Porto renders the lovers directly at the moments of decision and summarizes them at the moments of transport: the ball's handclasp dialogue, the window's negotiated confession, and the appeal to Lorenzo are scenes, while the raptures between them are reported in a clause. The effect, unusual in a love story, is to make the pair most vivid when they are most deliberate, and it fixes the tradition's centre of gravity. Shakespeare's lovers are remembered for soliloquy, for feeling overheard; da Porto's are remembered, by the scholars who read him closely, for negotiation, for feeling transacted under risk. Both are true to their forms, the stage soliloquizes and the novella transacts, but the transactional heroine came first, and the play's Juliet, who sets the terms of the balcony scene like a contracting party, descends from her as surely as from any lyric source.

Two further features of his character-drawing deserve emphasis. The first is Romeo's melancholy, which da Porto presents not as a pose but as a condition with a clinical edge: the young man's hopeless service to the cold lady has thinned him, withdrawn him from company, and made his friends fear for him, and the ball is prescribed almost as medicine. The Petrarchan lover of the Italian lyric tradition, pale, sleepless, and sighing, stands behind the portrait, and Shakespeare's first-act Romeo, sighing in sycamore groves and shutting himself in darkness, is the same patient in verse. The second feature is the novella's treatment of Giulietta's household position. She is the only child of the Cappelletti's head, which makes her marriage a matter of dynastic consequence and her secret disposal of herself an act of real rebellion against the house's whole strategy. Da Porto keeps that stake in view: the parents' choice of the Count of Lodrone is not tyranny for its own sake but the ordinary exercise of a great family's rights, which is why the tale's sympathy for the daughter never curdles into caricature of the father. The tragedy in his telling is structural before it is personal, a well-built world producing an unbearable result, and that tonal balance, too, descends to the play.

## The Birth of 'Giulietta': A Name and a Heroine Made New

The article's title speaks of a birth, and the claim should be made precise, because Giulietta herself is da Porto's most complete invention. Earlier tales in the line have heroines, Masuccio's Ganozza among them, but none of them is Giulietta: the name, the age, the household position, the wit at the ball, the self-possession at the window, and the will that ends the tale are a single act of characterization performed in the 1520s. Even the name is a small poem. Giulietta is the diminutive of Giulia, a girl's name made tender by its suffix, and setting it against the block capitals of the feud's surnames, Montecchi and Cappelletti, da Porto builds the story's whole tension into its title page: the smallest name in the tale belongs to the person the great names destroy. The tradition agreed with the choice instinctively. Bandello keeps her, Boaistuau and Painter transmit her, Brooke anglicizes her toward Juliet, and the play's heroine, for all her added poetry, walks in the outline da Porto drew, a daughter of the house, courted at a window, married by a friar, and killed by the collision of obedience and love.

Romeo's name carries a quieter freight. In Italian usage a "romeo" could denote a pilgrim bound for Rome, and scholars of the sources have long noted the aptness of the exile's name, the wanderer who ends his pilgrimage at a tomb in his own city. Whether da Porto chose the name for that resonance or took it as he found it in the Veronese air cannot be proved, and the cautious verdict is to register the fitness without building on it. What is certain is the pairing: before da Porto, no Romeo loves any Giulietta in any surviving text, and after him, no other pairing is imaginable. Names in narrative are not labels but destinies, and the destiny the world remembers was assigned in a villa at Montorso by a writer who may have supposed he was recording, rather than creating, the lovers of Verona.

The heroine's afterlife inside the tale's own transmission confirms the birth. Each reteller enlarges Giulietta's part, Bandello with rhetoric, Brooke with speeches, the play with soliloquies that turn her into the drama's second consciousness, and the direction of enlargement never reverses, because da Porto had built her with room to grow: decisive where the plot needs decisions, constant where it needs constancy, and articulate at exactly the joints, ball, window, friar's cell, tomb, where later writers would need her to speak. A heroine so placed is not an inheritance. She is a foundation.

## What Happens in Da Porto's Novella? The Plot, Beat by Beat

The tale opens in carnival season. Romeo Montecchi, wasting in his hopeless suit to the cold lady, is persuaded by friends to attend, masked, a great ball at the house of the Cappelletti, where the city's beauties will be on display and where a Montecchi has no business being. There he sees Giulietta, dances with her, and holds her hand through the figure of the dance; she, remarking on the cold hand of a fellow guest, the courtier Marcuccio, whose hands were famously always cold, presses Romeo's warm one and asks, in effect, whose hand this heat belongs to. The exchange fixes both: he learns her name and her house, she learns his, and neither withdraws. The ball, the mask, the dance, and the handclasp are all da Porto's staging, and the play's first act preserves the whole design, down to the handclasp that Shakespeare expands into the shared sonnet of Act 1 Scene 5, the exchange that ends with Juliet's benchmark for the whole tradition: "You kiss by th' book" stays unspoken in the novella, but the courtship by hands, hers diagnosing and his pleading, is already there.

Courtship follows at her window. On successive nights Romeo stations himself in the street beneath the Cappelletti windows, and Giulietta, having discovered his vigils, speaks with him from above, reproaches the danger, tests his honour, and at last confesses a returned love on condition of marriage. The play's orchard scene, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2 to 3), descends from these exchanges with the geometry unchanged: the lover below in the dark street, the lady above at the lighted window, the house's danger pressing on both. Since marriage between the houses cannot be asked openly, the lovers carry their case to Giulietta's confessor, Frate Lorenzo, a Franciscan of learning and wide acquaintance, who marries them in secret, calculating that the union, once revealed, might reconcile the feuding houses and bind him to both. The friar's reconciling motive, so central to the play's Laurence, is da Porto's invention entire.

Then the feud collects its debt. In a street brawl between the houses' young men, Tebaldo, Giulietta's cousin and the Cappelletti champion, is killed by Romeo, and Romeo, condemned for the killing, is banished to Mantua. The lovers share a brief married life in stolen meetings before the exile separates them, and in Mantua Romeo waits on news while in Verona Giulietta's parents, ignorant of the marriage, settle her future: she is to wed a Count of Lodrone, a match of standing that the family presses with increasing force. She refuses, is threatened, contemplates death, and goes to Lorenzo, who gives her the remedy that defines the tale: a powder which, swallowed, will lay her in a deathlike sleep for three days, so that she will be mourned and laid in the Cappelletti tomb, from which the friar will release her and send her secretly to Mantua. Lorenzo writes to Romeo explaining everything, and the letter never arrives, its friar-bearer prevented, on the usual scholarly summary of the passage, amid the plague precautions of the road. Romeo instead receives, from his own servant fresh from Verona, the flat report that Giulietta is dead and entombed.

The ending da Porto built has never been improved, only re-voiced. Romeo buys poison, rides to Verona by night, forces the tomb of the Cappelletti, and drinks beside the body of his wife. Giulietta wakes from the three days' sleep to find him dead or in his last moments beside her. Lorenzo, arriving too late, offers to hide her in a convent; she refuses, and dies by her own act of will, holding her breath until life leaves her, stretched upon Romeo's body. The friar's account to the authorities, the lovers found in one tomb, ends the feud as Lorenzo had hoped a wedding would: the Montecchi and the Cappelletti are reconciled over the bodies, and the two are buried together with public honour. Every station of that itinerary, ball, window, cell, street, exile, imposed suitor, powder, failed letter, tomb, is present in the play, in the same order, doing the same work.

Two beats of the plot deserve stress because later fame has blurred them. The first is the married interval. Da Porto's lovers are not snatched apart on their wedding night: after the secret ceremony they meet repeatedly in stolen visits, and the marriage exists as a lived fact before the exile annuls it in practice. That interval changes the moral arithmetic of everything after, since Giulietta's resistance to the Lodrone match is not a girl's preference but a wife's refusal of bigamy, and the friar's powder is not an adventure but the last lawful expedient of a marriage defending itself. The play compresses the interval to a single night and a dawn parting, gaining intensity and surrendering some of the novella's juridical clarity. The second beat is the tomb's architecture. The Cappelletti vault in da Porto is the family's monumental ark, the stone chest in the burial ground where the house's dead lie together, and Giulietta is carried there in funeral state before the whole city. The publicity matters: her counterfeit corpse is displayed to Verona, mourned by the parents who drove her to the draught, and entombed under the family's name, so that Romeo's night entry is an intrusion into the enemy house's most guarded interior, the feud's geography completed in a grave.

The exile's texture is the third beat worth rescuing from summary. In Mantua, Romeo lives on intelligence: servants ride the road, rumours arrive unpriced and unverifiable, and the exile's whole existence narrows to the interpretation of reports, which is why the servant's flat announcement of the funeral strikes with the force it does. In Verona, meanwhile, Giulietta's refusal of the Lodrone match is conducted as a siege in reverse: entreaties, threats, and the family's mounting incomprehension drive her first to a stated resolve to die rather than submit, and only then to Lorenzo, whose powder is offered to a woman who has already chosen death in earnest and must be argued into its counterfeit. The scene matters for the tale's moral bookkeeping. The potion plot is not a friar's trick played on events; it is a rescue improvised at the demand of a principal who would otherwise be dead by her own hand within the week, and every later version that keeps her desperation, including the play's great soliloquy before the vial, is spending da Porto's capital.

### How much time passes inside da Porto's novella from the ball to the tomb?

Months, not days: the courtship runs through a carnival season into repeated night meetings, the secret marriage is followed by stolen married life before the exile, and the Mantuan separation stretches across the negotiations for the Lodrone match, giving da Porto's lovers a settled, seasonal attachment where the play compresses the same itinerary into a few days of crisis.

That leisurely span is one of the few structural features the tradition refused to keep. Brooke's poem also allows the affair months, and it is Shakespeare who crushes the calendar, making the love a matter of days and the tragedy a function of speed. Reading da Porto restores the alternative: a version in which the lovers are not snatched from each other by haste but worn down by a feud that has all the time in the world, and in which the catastrophe arrives not because events move too fast but because one letter moves too slowly.

## Frate Lorenzo's Wager: The Friar Who Marries Enemies

One figure in the novella rewards a section of his own, because he is da Porto's most original contribution to the story's personnel and the hinge on which its whole mechanism swings. Frate Lorenzo is introduced as Giulietta's confessor, a Franciscan of reputation for learning, and the lovers' appeal to him is the tale's first mature decision: what cannot be asked of the fathers might be accomplished by the Church. Lorenzo's consent is the novella's boldest stroke. He does not marry the pair in a transport of sympathy. He calculates. A marriage accomplished in secret and revealed at the chosen moment might do what sermons and signorial edicts had failed to do, bind the Montecchi and the Cappelletti in kinship, end the feud, and make the friar who engineered the peace a power in Verona. The play preserves the calculation in Laurence's own words of hope that the alliance may turn the households' rancour to pure love, and the line descends from da Porto's Lorenzo with its motive intact.

The wager explains the friar's later conduct with an economist's neatness. Every subsequent act of Lorenzo's is the defence of an investment: the powder protects the marriage from the Lodrone match that would expose and void it; the letter protects the powder from Romeo's despair; the plan to free Giulietta from the tomb and hide her until she can join her husband protects the whole structure from the scandal of discovery. When the wager fails, it fails at its weakest joint, not in design but in communication, and Lorenzo's final appearance, narrating the entire scheme to the authorities, is the reckoning of an honest gambler producing his accounts.

The powder deserves a sentence inside Lorenzo's portrait, because it characterizes his learning as da Porto's age understood learning. The friar's remedy is neither magic nor poisoner's craft but the period's respectable arcana, the soporific simples and compounded powders that learned men, and friars with their infirmary gardens and dispensaries, were licensed to handle. Da Porto gives the device a pharmacist's plausibility, a measured draught with a stated term, three days, and a known outcome, and that plausibility is load-bearing: the plot requires every adult in Verona, parents, physicians, and watchers, to read the sleeping girl as a corpse, and only a remedy inside the period's medicine could carry that conviction. The play will later give its friar a garden, a basket, and a philosophy of herbs to justify the same vial; the novella, more austerely, gives its friar a reputation, and lets the reputation do the work. Da Porto draws him without the play's later accretions, no herb garden philosophy, no moral panic, but the essentials are all present at the creation: the learning, the daring, the political hope, and the fatal reliance on a letter. Source scholars who call Lorenzo the first character in the tale, in the sense of the first figure whose decisions generate the plot rather than suffer it, have the text on their side. Romeo and Giulietta supply the passion; Lorenzo supplies the causality; and both supplies are da Porto's.

## The Ball, the Window and the Potion: Scenes Da Porto Created

Three of the map's rows deserve a closer look, because each is a scene-type the tale bequeathed to the stage. The ball is the first. Courtly narrative before da Porto generally begins such loves by sight at church, at a window, or by report; the masked festivity, where the hero enters the enemy's house in disguise and the lovers meet inside the rituals of dance, is his chosen machine, and it does several kinds of work at once. It motivates the meeting without violating the feud's logic, since carnival license covers the intrusion; it gives the lovers a sanctioned touch, the dance's handclasp, inside a public room; and it plants the enmity inside the festivity, because the Cappelletti household is celebrating while a Montecchi burns in its midst. The play inherits all of it and adds only Tybalt's recognition and Capulet's restraint, the feud entering the scene as drama rather than as premise.

The carnival frame does quieter work that the scene-by-scene accounts often miss. A masked ball in the carnival season is the one occasion in the tale's social calendar when a young man may cross an enemy threshold without a challenge being issued on the spot, when hands may meet in the dance under the licence of the figure, and when a lady may speak with a stranger inside the forms of courtesy. Da Porto sets the whole catastrophe in motion inside that licensed interval, which gives the lovers' later outlawry its exact measure: everything they do afterward, the vigils, the secret marriage, the counterfeit death, is an attempt to prolong by conspiracy what carnival had granted them by custom for a single evening. The tradition kept the mechanism without always noticing it, since Brooke and the play preserve the feast while thinning the carnival logic that made the feast a door. Restoring the logic is one of the small services a return to da Porto performs for readers of the play.

The window courtship is the second. Da Porto's night scenes, Romeo in the street and Giulietta at the window above, create the vertical stage picture that the tradition never abandoned: desire speaking upward, caution speaking down, the lady's chamber imagined as a lighted height and the lover as a petitioner in the dark. The details he establishes persist with remarkable fidelity, her fear for his safety if he is found, his willingness to be found, the movement from reproach to confession to the demand for marriage as the price of further meetings. When the play's Juliet says the orchard is death to him if any kinsman finds him there, the sentence is a dramatist's line built on a novella-writer's floor plan.

The wedding itself is the second scene-type, quieter than the window and easily missed. Da Porto stages the marriage in Lorenzo's own place, the friar's cell and church, with the sacrament administered in secret and the couple departing as husband and wife into a city that must never know. The scene's power lies in its legality: within the tale's Catholic world the marriage is real, indissoluble, and prior to every claim the Cappelletti later assert, so that the Lodrone match is not a rival courtship but an attempted bigamy arranged in ignorance. The play inherits the cell, the secrecy, and the juridical fact entire, and builds its crisis on the same foundation: everything after Act 2 is the collision between a marriage that exists and a world that does not know it. That foundation is not Masuccio's alone, for the Siena tale also marries its lovers in secret; but da Porto sharpens the instrument by giving his friar the reconciling design, making the sacrament a peace treaty in disguise and its concealment a strategic necessity rather than a lovers' convenience.

The potion is the third, and the most consequential single device in the tale. Da Porto's Lorenzo supplies a powder, to be taken in drink, that produces a sleep indistinguishable from death and lasting three days; the play converts the powder to a vial and the span to a measured term: "Take thou this vial, being then in bed, / And this distilling liquor drink thou off" (4.1.93 to 94), with the sleep set at "two and forty hours" (4.1.105). The device is the tale's hinge because it converts a social problem, an imposed marriage, into a metaphysical gamble, a counterfeit death, and every later version preserves it exactly where da Porto put it, after the refusal and before the letter. Its genealogy is not spotless, since a feigned-death draught also figures in the Masuccio line of descent, and the honest credit is divided: the device as an inherited mechanism, its placement and its three-day term as da Porto fixed them, are his.

A fourth scene-type should be added to the three, because the tradition kept it as faithfully as any: the letter. Da Porto builds his catastrophe not on a battle or a betrayal but on a document, Lorenzo's explanatory letter, whose failure converts a rescue plan into a death sentence without any character willing the result. The novella handles the mechanism with a bureaucrat's precision: the letter is written, entrusted to a travelling friar, stopped by the plague precautions that governed movement between Italian cities, and never delivered, while the false report travels the same road unimpeded because bad news needs no courier's credentials. Shakespeare, who may never have read a line of da Porto, preserves the asymmetry exactly, giving Friar John a stopped journey and Balthasar an open road. The letter as a tragic device, the true message slower than the false one, is among the most modern things in the novella, and it is wholly da Porto's arrangement of the inherited parts.

The waking in the tomb is the fifth and last of the great scene-types, and the one where da Porto's restraint earns its highest return. His Giulietta wakes to a completed fact: no dialogue with a dying husband, no exchange of last words, only the body, the friar's arriving comfort refused, and the willed extinction of her own breath. Later versions could not bear the silence and filled it, Bandello with lamentation, the play with the dagger's brisk transaction, but the original's austerity has its own authority, and modern readers repeatedly single the passage out as the novella's summit. The scene also completes the tale's geometry of knowledge: she who was courted through a window in ignorance of what the feud would do wakes in a tomb in full knowledge of what it has done, and the whole education the story exists to record is contained in the interval between those two awakenings of understanding.

## What Did Da Porto Borrow from Masuccio's Mariotto and Ganozza?

Half a century before da Porto wrote, Masuccio Salernitano of Salerno included in his Novellino, printed in 1476, the tale of Mariotto Mignanelli and Ganozza of Siena, and Olin H. Moore's great argument was that the Romeo and Juliet legend begins there. The kinship is real and structural. Masuccio's lovers marry in secret before a friar; the hero kills a man in a brawl and flees into exile at Alexandria; the heroine, pressed toward another marriage, escapes by a friar's device into a counterfeit death and a tomb; word travels badly across the distance; the hero returns to the tomb and is destroyed, executed by the state after he is taken breaking into it; the heroine dies of grief. The Siena tale's tone differs from its descendant's as sharply as its ending: Masuccio writes as a moralist of appetite punished, his lovers' secrecy a disorder the world's order corrects by executioner and by sorrow, while da Porto writes as a student of institutions, his lovers destroyed not by correction but by the ordinary working of feud, law, and distance. That tonal migration, from exemplum to tragedy, is itself one of da Porto's transformations, and it is why his version, and not Masuccio's, could become the property of the stage. Set beside da Porto's plot, the machinery is recognizably the same engine: secret marriage, killing, exile, feigned death, failed communication, tomb. The separate study of [Masuccio Salernitano's Mariotto and Ganozza and the Siena ancestor tale](/2025/10/17/masuccio-salernitano-mariotto-ganozza/) follows that novella on its own terms.

### Did Luigi da Porto invent the story of Romeo and Giulietta from nothing?

No: the engine of secret marriage, exile, counterfeit death, and tomb descends from Masuccio's Siena tale and older story patterns behind it, but the story as the world knows it, Verona, the two Dantean surnames, the lovers' names, the friar's reconciling design, and the double death inside the tomb, is da Porto's assembly, and no earlier text contains it.

The adjudication between Moore and the da Porto tradition turns on what the word "origin" is being asked to mean. Moore wins on pedigree: the plot's moving parts were not minted in 1524, and any account that calls da Porto the inventor of the whole tale collapses at the first sight of Masuccio. But Bullough's arrangement wins on substance, because the question readers actually ask, where does Romeo and Juliet come from, is answered not by the engine but by the car built around it. Masuccio's ending is a judicial execution and a death of grief, separated in place and manner; da Porto's is the mutual catastrophe in the tomb, poison and held breath over one body, which is the ending the world remembers. Masuccio's city is Siena and his families are no one's feud; da Porto's Verona and its two houses are the story's permanent address. The balanced verdict is that da Porto stands to Masuccio as an architect to a supplier of parts: the debt is genuine, the building is his.

A refinement of Moore deserves its due in the same breath. Moore also argued that da Porto worked from more than Masuccio, that the tale absorbed the pattern of Pyramus and Thisbe, the classical lovers divided by a wall and united in a tomb, and the judgement of the scholarship since has generally let both claims stand: a novella source for the machinery, a classical pattern for the tomb's emotional geometry. Neither claim touches the assembly, which remains da Porto's.

Moore's method is worth a word, since the adjudication leans on it. Working in the early twentieth century, when the legend was still often treated as a piece of floating folklore, Moore demonstrated descent the way a philologist demonstrates it, by the accumulation of sequential correspondences too specific to be parallel invention: not merely a secret marriage or a feigned death in isolation, but the same joints in the same order, marriage, killing, exile, imposed second match, counterfeit death, tomb. That standard of proof is why his Masuccio thesis has survived a century of checking, and why the corrections later scholarship applied were corrections of emphasis rather than of fact. What Moore could not show, and did not pretend to show, was any route by which the Siena tale's lovers acquired Veronese names, a civic feud, or an ending in mutual tomb-death; those transformations occur between Masuccio's page and da Porto's, in one writer's workshop, and they are the transformations that made the story exportable. A tale of Siena, ending in an execution, stayed in Siena. Da Porto's version went around the world.

## What Is Missing from Da Porto's Version of the Story?

The surest way to see Shakespeare's hand is to list what da Porto's novella does not contain. There is no Mercutio. The nearest approach is the ball's Marcuccio, a courtly guest whose perpetually cold hands Giulietta jokes about as she takes Romeo's warm one, a name and a temperature and nothing more: no wit, no Queen Mab, no death in the square. There is no Nurse, no confidante of Giulietta's household carrying messages and memories; the lovers' go-between is the friar alone. There is no Benvolio, no chorus, no Prince delivering edicts and a closing judgement, no apothecary scene staged as a temptation, and no duel at the tomb, since the Count of Lodrone never comes near it. The play's most famous additions are precisely the novella's absences, which is why claims that Shakespeare merely versified an Italian tale fail on an inventory check.

### What does Marcuccio's cold hand have to do with Mercutio?

Possibly a name and a spark, no more: Marcuccio is a guest at da Porto's ball whose hands were proverbially always cold, and Giulietta's joke over the contrast with Romeo's warm hand gives later writers a Marcuccio in the right room at the right moment, but the wit, the friendship, and the death that define Mercutio are Shakespeare's creation entire.

The absences run to tone as well as personnel. Da Porto's feud has no street theatre of servants and no civic proclamation hanging over it; his Verona is a city of households and a watchful signoria, not of brawling factions under a death edict. His Romeo does not fight a Paris at the tomb door, and his Giulietta does not die by a blade: the dagger that the play puts in her hand at "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath" (5.3.169 to 170) belongs to the later line of the tale, to Bandello's retelling and the versions that follow it, not to da Porto, whose heroine dies by the held breath of an act of will. Even the play's velocity is an absence in the original, where the affair breathes across months. The novella is complete without all of this, which is the point: it is a finished work, not a sketch for one, and the tradition treated it as a quarry of plot rather than of life.

The inventory can be pressed further, into matters of emphasis that audiences now take for the story's essence. The novella has no dawn parting, no lark and nightingale, because its lovers part by arrangement and messenger rather than by a single stolen night. It has no balcony speech as the world quotes one, no gallery of aphorisms, no dying Mercutio cursing both houses, no Paris laying flowers and challenging the intruder, no Prince closing the action with a ledger of loss. Its feud produces exactly one street killing before the tomb, where the play produces a cascade. Even its reconciliation is quieter, a settlement narrated rather than a judgement pronounced. None of this is deficiency. The novella is a prose tale of some thirty pages in modern printing, and its ratios are a novella's ratios; the play is a two hours' traffic built to fill a stage. But the absences fix the boundary of da Porto's authorship with documentary precision, and any account of the story's origins that blurs the boundary, crediting the Italian with the play's wit or the Englishman with the novella's plot, fails the first test of source scholarship, which is to say what is actually in the texts.

## How Did Da Porto's Story Reach Shakespeare?

The route is documented and indirect, and no link in it requires Shakespeare to have opened da Porto's Italian. Da Porto's novella was taken up and expanded by Matteo Bandello, whose Novelle of 1554 retold the tale at greater length and with richer rhetoric; the separate study of [Matteo Bandello's novella and its expansion of the tale](/2025/10/10/matteo-bandello-novella-1554/) covers that retelling in its own right. From Bandello the story passed into French in Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires Tragiques of 1559, and from Boaistuau into English twice over: as prose in William Painter's Palace of Pleasure of 1566 and, decisively for the stage, as Arthur Brooke's long poem The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet of 1562, the play's immediate source.

Bandello's retelling, which the linked study treats at length, can be instanced here in three strokes, because the strokes mark the exact boundary of da Porto's text. Bandello names the imposed suitor Paris and gives him a lineage; Bandello arms his Giulietta with Romeo's dagger for her death, replacing the held breath; and Bandello thickens the household around the heroine, moving the tale toward the populated world the stage would complete. Each change is an improvement in theatrical capital and a small falsification of the original's design, and the three together show why the line needed both writers: da Porto to build the story, Bandello to furnish it. The dagger alone proves the point. It is the version of the death the whole world now pictures, and it is not in da Porto at all. The whole line, from Masuccio's Siena through Vicenza, Milan, Paris, and London, is mapped in [the sources of Romeo and Juliet set out as a family tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), and the map's lesson is that the story survived by being rewritten at every border it crossed.

### Could Shakespeare have read da Porto's Italian for himself?

Almost certainly the question never arose: the play's verbal debts run to Brooke's English poem, whose details, errors, and expansions the dramatist follows, so the working line of descent is da Porto's plot at three removes, and any Italian Shakespeare commanded reached him filtered through the French and English intermediaries who had already remade the tale.

That indirection explains the play's curious fidelity. Shakespeare is faithful to incidents he never read in their first form, the ball, the friar's motive, the powder become a vial, because Brooke had preserved da Porto's architecture while translating its surfaces into English moralizing verse. Editors of the play, René Weis in the Arden Third Series among them, accordingly treat Brooke as the source the dramatist worked from and da Porto as the source of the source, the head of the line whose design survives every translation laid over it. The distinction is the one this article has maintained throughout: da Porto's is the foundational role, not the immediate one, and confusing the two has muddled more accounts of the play's origins than any other single error.

The intermediaries each left fingerprints that prove the route. Boaistuau, Englished by nobody and read by everybody, moralized the tale into a warning against disobedient love and gave the French version the tragic emphasis its title advertises. Painter Englished Boaistuau in prose, in the Palace of Pleasure, and so made the story available to any English reader of the 1560s in a form close to the French. Brooke versified the same French source into some three thousand lines of poulter's measure, expanding the lovers' speeches, inventing small connective tissue, and prefaceing the whole with a scolding of the lovers' disobedience that sits oddly against the sympathy of his own verse. It is Brooke's poem, with its expansions and its accidents, that the play tracks scene by scene, sometimes line by line; and behind Brooke stands Boaistuau, and behind Boaistuau stands Bandello, and behind Bandello stands a posthumous Venetian printing of a Vicentine officer's tale. Four rewritings, three languages, one architecture. The chain is the strongest evidence the foundational verdict has: plots that survive four translations are not surviving by accident, and the credit for a structure that no intermediary could improve belongs to the structure's designer.

England's acquaintance with the tale before the play is the chain's last link and worth a sentence of its own. Between Painter's prose of 1566 and Brooke's poem, in print from 1562, the story of Romeo and Juliet was available to English readers for a full generation before any dramatist touched it, in the two forms, prose for the library and verse for the parlour, that fixed its incidents in the culture's memory. When the play reached the stage in the 1590s, its first audiences were not meeting a novelty but a favourite, and the dramatist's liberties, the added Mercutio, the compressed days, were taken with a plot the house already owned. That familiarity is da Porto's posthumous doing as surely as the plot is: his architecture was the reason the tale survived its four rewritings intact enough to be recognized, applauded, and demanded again.

## Was the Novella About Da Porto Himself? The Lucina Question

The dedication to Lucina Savorgnan invited the reading, and Moore's generation took the invitation seriously: the wounded officer, retired from the wars and loving a lady of a great Friulian house whose family politics kept the lovers apart, had written his own case into the Veronese tale, himself as Romeo and Lucina as Giulietta. The parallels are suggestive rather than documented. Da Porto's letters place Lucina at the centre of his retirement years; the novella's frame, a soldier telling a lady a story of love blocked by houses and healed only in death, fits the dedication's posture of offering and warning; and the Savorgnan were themselves a faction-torn family, divided between branches whose feuds bloodied Friuli in precisely da Porto's campaigning years.

### Why is the novella dedicated to Lucina Savorgnan?

Because the tale is offered as a gift with an argument inside it: da Porto presents Lucina, the lady of his own thwarted attachment, with a recovered history of lovers destroyed by their houses' enmity, letting the dedication turn a Veronese fiction into a personal address about fortune, family power, and the price of obedience, whoever finally judges its sincerity.

The skeptical counterweight is equally solid, and the verdict here follows it in part. Dedications were currency in the period's literary economy, the tale's materials demonstrably predate any plausible autobiographical design, and a writer ransacking Masuccio and Dante for a story is engaged in literature before he is engaged in confession. The adjudicated position is a narrow one: the autobiographical reading explains the dedication, the tone of intimate address, and the choice to publish a soldier's love-tale under a lady's name, but it explains nothing about the plot, which is inherited and assembled. Where the romantics of the older scholarship saw a roman à clef, the defensible residue is a frame with a pulse: a real attachment lending urgency to borrowed events.

The Savorgnan background strengthens the frame's pulse without rescuing the full autobiographical claim. Lucina's family was one of the great houses of Friuli, and its internal feud, between the branches centred on Udine and on the family's rural lordships, had filled da Porto's own campaigning ground with private war during his years of service; he had watched a house devour itself at close quarters, in the same seasons in which he received the wound that retired him. A tale about two houses whose enmity destroys their children, offered to a daughter of the Savorgnan by a soldier of the Friulian wars, cannot have been a neutral gift whatever its plot's provenance. The adjudication therefore stands where the evidence leaves it: the dedication is autobiographical in address and occasion, the novella is not autobiographical in substance, and criticism does best when it keeps the two judgements in separate columns, as the map keeps its columns, rather than melting them into a single romantic certainty.

## Bullough Against Moore: How the Scholars Fought Over the Origin

The disagreement this article has been adjudicating deserves a section in its own right, because the da Porto question is a small, perfect model of how source scholarship works and quarrels. Geoffrey Bullough, assembling the first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare in the 1950s, arranged the play's ancestry as a line of texts and placed da Porto's novella at its head among the story's effective forms: before him, analogues; after him, descendants. His method was documentary and comparative, printing summaries and translations, aligning incidents, and letting the alignment argue. On that evidence his placement has never been seriously disturbed: every account of the play's sources written since, including the introductions of the standard editions, begins the story's real history where Bullough began it.

Olin H. Moore's challenge came from an earlier generation and a different instinct. Moore was a hunter of origins, and his studies of the legend in the 1930s and 1940s refused to let the line start at da Porto when the machinery demonstrably ran earlier. By aligning Masuccio's Mariotto and Ganozza joint by joint with the Veronese tale, he showed a descent that Bullough's own volume acknowledges by printing the Siena tale among the sources. Moore further argued the autobiographical case for the dedication, reading the novella as its author's veiled memoir, and he speculated boldly about intermediate forms and lost versions through which the material might have passed. Later scholarship kept the Masuccio descent, disciplined the speculations, and cooled the autobiography into a possibility about the frame rather than a key to the plot.

René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, represents the working settlement the quarrel produced. His introduction treats Brooke's poem as the dramatist's certain source, Bandello and the French line as its ancestry, and da Porto as the head of the whole descent, the point at which the legend becomes the story of Romeo and Juliet in form as well as in embryo. That is, in substance, this article's verdict, and the adjudication can be stated as a rule of evidence: Moore is to be followed wherever the question is ancestry, Bullough wherever the question is form, and the editor wherever the question is what the dramatist actually used. The three authorities do not finally contradict one another; they answer different questions, and the appearance of a fight dissolves once the questions are kept apart. What survives the dissolution is the fact all three establish between them, that the story has a recoverable beginning, and that the beginning has a name, a city, a wound, and a date within a few years: Luigi da Porto, Vicenza, the wars of Friuli, the 1520s.

## The Verdict: How Much of Romeo and Juliet Belongs to Luigi da Porto?

Gather the accounts. To Dante he owes two surnames and nothing else. To Masuccio he owes the engine: secret marriage, a killing, an exile, a counterfeit death, a message gone astray, a tomb. To the older story patterns behind both he owes the tomb's classical geometry. Everything else in the familiar story is his: Verona and its Scala frame, the feud converted from two surnames into the plot's governing machine, the lovers' names, the ball and the handclasp, the window courtship, the confessor-friar with his reconciling design, Tebaldo's death, the Mantuan exile, the imposed Count of Lodrone, the three-day powder, the poison at the tomb, the heroine's death by held breath, and the reconciliation of the houses over two bodies in a single grave. That is not a source's share; it is an author's.

The series thesis that this article was written under can now be stated in its corrected form. The cliché imagines Shakespeare inventing Verona, a genius dreaming a city, two families, and a plot out of the air of the Globe. The record shows something better worth teaching: a maimed cavalry captain in a Vicentine villa, working from a Salernitan tale and a line of Dante, assembled the story so durably that four rewritings in three languages could not break its frame, and the dramatist's genius, which no page of this article disputes, spent itself where genius earns most, on voice, pace, and the people the novella had left uninvented. Bullough's placement of da Porto at the head of the sources is therefore upheld, Moore's pedigree is accepted and bounded, and the foundational verdict stands: Romeo and Juliet began, as a story, with Luigi da Porto.

For readers and teachers the practical consequence is a change of starting point. The play is usually introduced as a bolt of original genius and its Italian ancestry mentioned, if at all, as a curiosity for a footnote. The record assembled here supports the opposite order of presentation: begin with the novella and the story exists; begin with the play and the story must be explained. A student who has walked the da Porto map knows which parts of the evening in the theatre are five centuries old and which are the dramatist's morning work, and that knowledge sharpens rather than dulls the wonder, since it separates the two miracles, the building of the story and the building of the play, and gives each its author.

One closing distinction protects the verdict from its own exaggeration. To call da Porto foundational is not to call him the play's author in any sense an audience would recognize: strip his novella from the record and the play could not exist in its shape, but strip the play from the record and the novella remains a minor jewel of the Italian sixteenth century, read by specialists and unknown to the millions who can quote its balcony. Fame flowed backward along the line of descent, from the stage to the source, and da Porto's current readership is itself one of Shakespeare's creations. Source study that forgets that flow becomes mere priority-mongering. The balanced sentence, and the one this article ends its argument on, is reciprocal: Shakespeare made da Porto immortal by taking his story, and da Porto made Shakespeare's most famous plot possible by building it. Between those two debts, the older and quieter one belongs to the soldier of Vicenza, and it has waited long enough to be entered in the record at its full value. The da Porto map stands as the entry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch da Porto map, and which elements does it credit to da Porto?

The InsightCrunch da Porto map is the table in this article that registers, element by element, which parts of the familiar story enter the record in Luigi da Porto's novella. Its pattern is concentration: the setting, the feud's surnames, the lovers' names, the ball, the window courtship, the secret marriage, the potion, the failed letter, and the tomb ending are not scattered across centuries of folklore but fixed in one text of the 1520s, written by one identifiable author. What later versions add, the map's right-hand column shows, is chiefly personnel, pace, and voice, layered onto an architecture that da Porto had already completed. The map's discipline is its honesty: a row is claimed for da Porto only where his text contains the element in working form, and the rows where the credit is shared, the draught among them, are marked as shared rather than silently annexed.

### Q: When was Giulietta e Romeo written, and when was it published?

Composition is conventionally dated to about 1524, during da Porto's retirement at Montorso after his wound ended his military career, though the dating rests on inference rather than on a dated manuscript. Publication followed his death in 1529: the novella was printed at Venice around 1530 or 1531, first anonymously, in a collection of his prose and verse prepared by others. The distinction between the two dates matters to the scholarship, because the composition date governs which Italian contemporaries could have influenced him, while the print date governs how the tale began its journey toward Bandello, Boaistuau, Painter, Brooke, and the English stage. Students of the tale should therefore cite it the way the scholarship does, as written about 1524 and printed about 1530, holding both dates under the same circa and treating any more precise claim as a hypothesis about evidence that has not survived.

### Q: Was Luigi da Porto a Veronese, like his lovers?

No. Da Porto was born at Vicenza in 1485 and spent his career in Venetian service in Friuli, far east of Verona. Verona was a neighbouring city of the Venetian mainland dominion, well known to an officer of his region, and he is careful to source his tale to a Veronese informant rather than to personal knowledge. The distinction is part of the novella's design: Verona in the story is a chosen, bookish setting, assembled from Dante's surnames and the city's Scala-era reputation for faction, not a home town remembered. His own ground was the Vicentine hills and the Friulian frontier where his wound was received. The near-miss in his biography is part of the tale's fascination: the story of Verona's lovers was written by a man who knew Verona as a neighbour, a soldier, and a reader of Dante, and who built its streets out of those three knowledges rather than out of residence.

### Q: Who was Peregrino, the archer da Porto credits with the tale?

Peregrino, also spelled Pellegrino in some accounts, is the Veronese archer in da Porto's company whom the frame names as the storyteller: during the Friulian campaigns, this common soldier from Verona related the history of the two lovers to his officer, who later wrote it down. Whether Peregrino existed as described cannot be verified, and source scholars treat the attribution with the caution owed to all novella frames, since Boccaccio and his heirs had made the found tale and the cited witness into conventions of the form. The attribution still does real work in the text, anchoring the fiction's claim to be recovered history and giving its Veronese colour a named human conduit. The detail also flatters the fiction's design, since a soldier's tale told on campaign explains both the story's Verona colour and its narrator's distance from the events, the two things da Porto most needed his frame to establish at once.

### Q: What part does Bartolomeo della Scala play in the novella's frame?

Bartolomeo della Scala, lord of Verona in the first years of the fourteenth century, is the reign under which da Porto sets the events, and his name functions as the tale's certificate of date and place. By attaching the lovers to a specific signoria two centuries before his own time, da Porto gives the feud a political roof: a ruling house under which great families might war in the streets, a friar might scheme for civic reconciliation, and an unrecorded private catastrophe might plausibly vanish from chronicle. Bartolomeo himself never enters the action. His role is chronological and atmospheric, the borrowed authority of a real reign spent on an unverifiable story. The device also quietly advertises the tale's learning, since Bartolomeo's Verona was Dante's Verona too, the city of the Scala court where the poet had been sheltered, and da Porto's frame claims that consecrated past for his lovers in a single proper name.

### Q: Which cousin does Romeo kill in da Porto's novella, and how does the killing happen?

Romeo kills Tebaldo, Giulietta's cousin and the fighting head of the young Cappelletti, in a street brawl between the two houses' parties. The killing is not a formal duel but the explosion of the feud's street warfare, and its consequence is immediate and legal: Romeo is condemned and banished to Mantua, and the secret marriage is severed by geography at the moment it can least afford exposure. Shakespeare keeps the victim, the killer, and the sentence, while inserting Mercutio's death before Tebaldo's fall and so converting a brawl's casualty into the play's pivot of revenge. In da Porto the mechanism is simpler and colder: the feud kills by process, through the law's exile. Tebaldo also matters as a measure of the tradition's growth: da Porto gives him function without a face, the house's sword arm, while the play builds him into the prince of cats, a duellist whose style and temper make his fall a catastrophe of character and of plot alike.

### Q: How does Giulietta die in da Porto's version?

Giulietta wakes in the Cappelletti tomb from the three days' sleep to find Romeo dead by poison beside her, refuses the friar Lorenzo's offer to conceal her in a convent, and dies by holding her breath until her life leaves her, stretched upon her husband's body. The death is an act of pure will, without weapon or wound, and it marks one of the sharpest differences between the novella and the play. Shakespeare's heroine dies by Romeo's dagger, a change the tradition took from Bandello's retelling; da Porto's original ending is quieter and, in its way, more absolute, since nothing external kills her at all. Editors and source scholars regularly single the held breath out as the novella's most haunting invention, precisely because it cannot be staged or filmed without translation into gesture: literature's most famous double death begins, in its first form, as an act of will invisible to any watcher.

### Q: Why does Lorenzo's letter never reach Romeo in Mantua?

Lorenzo writes to Mantua explaining the powder, the counterfeit death, and the plan to free Giulietta from the tomb, and entrusts the letter to a friar travelling that road; on the usual scholarly summary of the passage, the bearer is prevented amid the plague precautions of the journey, detained and turned back where infection is feared, and the explanation dies on the road. Romeo receives instead his own servant's unmediated report that Giulietta is dead and buried, which contains every fact except the one that matters. The failed letter is da Porto's bleakest mechanism, preserved by every successor: the catastrophe is not caused by malice or fate but by a message that almost arrived.

### Q: Who is Frate Lorenzo in da Porto's novella?

Frate Lorenzo is a Franciscan friar, Giulietta's confessor, a man of learning, wide acquaintance, and practical resource, and he is da Porto's invention in the role the play inherits. He marries the lovers in secret, and his motive is stated policy rather than mere kindness: he hopes the accomplished union, revealed at the right moment, will reconcile the Montecchi and the Cappelletti and attach both houses to him. When the Lodrone marriage is imposed, it is Lorenzo who devises the counterfeit death and writes the letter, and Lorenzo who must finally narrate the catastrophe to the authorities. The play's Friar Laurence, gardener, moralist, and plotter, is this figure given a stage and a herb garden. Lorenzo is also the tale's narrator within the story, since his final confession to the authorities is the instrument by which Verona, and the reader, learn the whole design, a structural duty da Porto assigns him and the play preserves in the Friar's closing relation before the Prince.

### Q: What earlier love troubles Romeo before he meets Giulietta?

Da Porto's Romeo opens the tale already in service to a lady who returns his devotion with disdain, an unnamed, hard-hearted beauty whose coldness has worn him to the melancholy his friends hope to cure. It is to shake that hopeless suit that they persuade him, masked, to the Cappelletti ball, where the beauties of Verona will give his eyes other work. The device is da Porto's and the tradition keeps it faithfully: Brooke and Shakespeare inherit the prior attachment as the foil of the true one, and Shakespeare names the cold lady Rosaline and keeps her famously offstage. Structurally, the first love exists so that the second can be measured as its opposite, sudden warmth answering settled frost.

### Q: What happens at the Cappelletti ball where the lovers meet?

The ball is a carnival festivity in the Cappelletti palace, and Romeo enters it masked among companions, a Montecchi hidden inside his enemies' celebration. Dancing with Giulietta, he holds her hand through the figure of the dance; she, noticing the chill of Marcuccio's famously cold hand in the same company, remarks on the warmth of the hand she holds and draws from him the confession of whose it is. Names and houses are exchanged inside the courtesy of the dance, and neither lover withdraws from the knowledge. Da Porto stages the meeting as public ritual with a private current running through it, the exact design the play's feast scene preserves and enlarges.

### Q: How is the courtship conducted in the nights after the ball?

Romeo takes up a vigil in the street beneath the Cappelletti windows, and Giulietta, discovering him there, opens a courtship conducted vertically, her voice from the window above, his from the dark street below. Across successive nights she reproaches the danger of his presence, interrogates his identity and intentions, and moves by stages from caution to confession, granting her love on the explicit condition of honourable marriage. The geometry is da Porto's bequest to the stage: the lighted height of the lady, the lover petitioning upward, the hostile house wrapped around both. Shakespeare's orchard scene rebuilds the same architecture with a garden wall and an orchard's trees added to the street. The courtship's patience is its signature: night succeeds night, terms are stated, tested, and only then accepted, so that the marriage, when it comes, reads as the conclusion of a negotiation between equals rather than as a seizure of feeling, and Giulietta's caution does as much to create the love as Romeo's daring.

### Q: What form does the sleeping draught take in da Porto, and how long does the sleep last?

Lorenzo gives Giulietta a powder, to be swallowed in drink, which produces a sleep so like death that physicians and family alike will read it as a corpse's stillness, and the sleep is set to last three days. She takes it on the eve of the imposed Lodrone wedding, is mourned as dead, and is carried to the Cappelletti tomb, where the friar plans to release her on waking and send her secretly to Mantua. The play refines the device into a measured vial and a term of two and forty hours, but the mechanism, its three-beat rhythm of draught, tomb, and intended rescue, is da Porto's, assembled where the tale most needed an engine of error. The three days deserve their own remark: the span is long enough for a funeral, an entombment, and a report to cross to Mantua, and short enough for the rescue to remain thinkable, a calibration the play tightens to forty-two hours precisely because its action has no months to spare.

### Q: Who is the Count of Lodrone in da Porto's plot?

The Count of Lodrone is the bridegroom Giulietta's parents settle on her after Romeo's exile, a match of rank and advantage negotiated over her refusal and never dignified in the novella with the lovers' kind of interior life. He exists in the plot as pressure: the approaching wedding is the deadline that drives Giulietta to Lorenzo and the powder, and his suit measures how completely her family disposes of her without knowledge of the marriage that already binds her. He never meets Romeo and never appears at the tomb. Bandello's retelling gives the suitor the name Paris, and under that name he grows, in the play, into a character who can plead, mourn, and die. In da Porto the Count's anonymity is itself the meaning: he is not a rival but a transaction, the marriage market's price for Giulietta quoted by her parents, and her refusal is therefore aimed not at a man but at the system that supposes her disposable.

### Q: How does da Porto's novella end for the two houses?

The feud ends over the bodies. Lorenzo's full account to the authorities establishes how the secret marriage, the exile, and the counterfeit death produced the double death in the tomb, and the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, confronted with the cost of their enmity in its most concentrated form, are reconciled. The lovers are buried together with public honour, their shared tomb standing as the city's monument to the price of faction. Shakespeare preserves the settlement's shape while darkening its accounting, giving the reconciliation to the Prince's judgement and the fathers' rivalry in grief; da Porto's ending is simpler and civic, the signoria's peace restored by a friar's confession and a common grave.

### Q: Why do scholars link the novella to Masuccio's Mariotto and Ganozza?

Olin H. Moore established the link by setting the plots side by side: Masuccio's Siena tale of 1476 already contains a secret marriage before a friar, a killing and an exile, a heroine escaping an imposed marriage by a counterfeit death, a tomb, and a catastrophe brewed from distance and bad news. The correspondences are too many and too sequential to be coincidence, and the scholarly consensus since Moore accepts the descent while disputing its weight. What divides the critics is the residue: da Porto's Verona, his Dantean surnames, his lovers' names, his reconciling friar, and his double death inside the tomb appear in no earlier text, so the Masuccio link explains the tale's machinery without explaining the tale. The link is best stated in the form this article's adjudication gives it: Masuccio supplied an engine, da Porto built the car, and scholarship goes wrong only when it asks one of those facts to do the other's work.

### Q: In what form did da Porto write and publish the tale?

Da Porto wrote the tale as an Italian prose novella, framed by a dedicatory letter to Lucina Savorgnan and presented under a title claiming the history was newly recovered. He published nothing of it in his lifetime: the novella reached print posthumously at Venice, around 1530 or 1531, in a collection of his prose and verse assembled by others, and at first without his name on the tale itself. The form matters to the story's afterlife, because the novella, brief, plain, and built on reported action as much as on rendered speech, offered later writers a complete plot with its voices still unwritten, an open invitation that Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Shakespeare each accepted in turn. The posthumous, anonymous birth of the text also explains a persistent feature of its fame: for centuries the tale circulated as a found history rather than as a named author's work, which let each reteller, and eventually Verona itself, treat the lovers as common property awaiting a claimant.

### Q: How does Romeo obtain his poison and reach the tomb in da Porto's ending?

On receiving his servant's report that Giulietta lies dead in the Cappelletti tomb, Romeo procures poison in Mantua, rides to Verona by night, and lets himself into the family vault where her body has been laid, in some accounts forcing the tomb's closure to reach her. There, beside the supposed corpse, he drinks the poison and dies, so that Giulietta wakes from the powder's three days' sleep to the completed catastrophe. The play stages the same itinerary with additions, the apothecary's poverty argued aloud, Paris waiting at the tomb door, but the journey itself, Mantua to Verona, poison to vault, grief to act, is da Porto's closing design unaltered.

### Q: Which parts of Shakespeare's play have no basis in da Porto at all?

The absences are a roll of the play's glories. Mercutio exists only as the ball's cold-handed Marcuccio, a name without a character; the Nurse, the Chorus, Benvolio, and the Prince's edicts are absent entire. The dawn parting, the Queen Mab speech, the killing of Paris at the tomb, and the heroine's death by the dagger all belong to later hands, the dagger to Bandello's line and the rest to the dramatist. Even the play's speed is an addition, its few days crushed out of the novella's months. Da Porto supplied the story's architecture complete, and the inventory of what he did not supply is the fairest measure of what Shakespeare, working from Brooke's copy of that architecture, actually invented. The inventory cuts the other way as well, and an honest essay keeps both edges sharp: everything on the absence list is a reason the play is a greater work than its source, and everything on the map is a reason the greater work is not an original story.

### Q: How should an essay use the da Porto map to argue where the familiar story began?

An essay should build its case in the map's own order: establish the ancestry honestly, Dante for the surnames and Masuccio for the machinery, then show, row by row, that everything a reader means by the story of Romeo and Juliet, Verona, the two houses at war, the named lovers, the ball, the window, the friar's design, the powder, the failed letter, and the tomb, enters the record in a single novella of the 1520s. The conclusion the evidence supports is precise rather than greedy: da Porto did not invent ex nihilo, and Shakespeare did not invent the plot at all. Between those two negations stands the accurate sentence, that Luigi da Porto assembled the story the world remembers, and the essay that proves the assembly owns the argument.
