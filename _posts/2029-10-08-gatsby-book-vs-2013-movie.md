Book vs movie: the 2013 Gatsby compared against its source produces a result that surprises most people who expect one answer or the other. Baz Luhrmann's adaptation has a reputation for taking liberties, and the reputation is half right. The liberties are real, but they are concentrated in a small number of places, and the plot itself survives with a fidelity that would satisfy a checklist. What changes is not what happens. What changes is who is telling it, why, and how it reaches you.

![Book vs movie: the 2013 Gatsby compared](/assets/images/blog/blog-75.webp)

That distribution is the finding, and it is worth stating before any detail. A viewer arriving from the novel will recognize almost every event in sequence. The same viewer will find the experience of the story transformed, because Luhrmann and his co-writer Craig Pearce added a frame that reassigns the narration a motive, converted the novel's symbols from described images into visible objects, and staged the parties as an assault on the senses. Each of those is a decision about interpretation rather than about plot, and together they add up to a different reading of the same events.

This article maps the changes precisely, item by item, and then argues about which ones actually matter. The general question of how the novel measures against its films is treated in the [comparative account of page against screen](/2028/11/27/gatsby-novel-vs-films/), and the picture as a work in its own right is examined in the [analysis of the 2013 Luhrmann adaptation](/2029/09/10/gatsby-2013-luhrmann-film/). What follows is the fidelity comparison specifically: the scorecard, and what the scorecard is good for.

## Book vs Movie: The 2013 Gatsby Compared on Plot

Take the plot first, because clearing it lets everything else come into focus.

Nick Carraway arrives on Long Island and rents a small house next to an enormous one. He crosses the bay to dine with his cousin Daisy and her husband Tom, meets Jordan Baker, learns of Tom's mistress, and returns home to glimpse his neighbor reaching toward a light across the water. Tom takes him to the garage in the ash country and then to an apartment party in the city. An invitation arrives; Nick attends the party and meets his host. Gatsby drives him to lunch and produces a business associate. Jordan relays the Louisville history. Nick arranges the tea. Gatsby and Daisy reunite; the mansion tour follows; the shirts fly. The affair proceeds through the summer. The party Daisy attends goes badly. The confrontation at the Plaza breaks it open. Driving home, the car kills Myrtle Wilson. Gatsby waits outside the Buchanan house through the night. Wilson, directed toward the mansion, shoots him in the pool and then himself. Nobody attends the funeral. Nick leaves the East.

Every element of that sequence appears in the picture, in that order. There is no invented subplot, no new character of consequence, no reordering of major events. Measured as plot fidelity, this adaptation scores extremely high, and higher than the 1949 version by a wide margin.

The compressions are real but ordinary. The party sequences consolidate. Some minor figures thin out or disappear. Nick and Jordan's relationship is reduced. Wolfsheim's role shortens. These are the standard economies of converting a novel into a two-hour picture and they change nothing structural.

### Do the events arrive in Fitzgerald's order?

Substantially, yes. Every major event appears in the novel's order, with no invented subplot, no significant new character, and no restructuring of the sequence. The compressions are ordinary: consolidated party scenes, a reduced Nick and Jordan thread, and a shortened role for Gatsby's business associate.

## The Frame: Nick in the Sanitarium

The largest departure is also the first thing the audience sees, and it deserves the most careful treatment because everything else follows from it.

The picture opens not on the novel's first paragraph but at a place called the Perkins Sanitarium, some years after the summer, where an older Nick Carraway is being treated. The diagnosis given is morbid alcoholism, and the doctor, played by Jack Thompson, prescribes writing as therapy. Nick then narrates the story, first speaking and then writing, and at the end assembles a manuscript, titles it Gatsby, and adds the words The Great by hand.

The name is a deliberate signal. Maxwell Perkins was Fitzgerald's editor at Scribner's, and the sanitarium carries his name as an acknowledgment. Luhrmann has described the reasoning openly. The problem the filmmakers faced was that they wanted to use Fitzgerald's actual language, and language in a film has to come from somewhere; a voice needs a speaker in a specific place with a specific reason. They considered showing Fitzgerald working with Perkins directly and concluded the editor would become too large a presence. The doctor character was modeled on a real psychiatrist, and Luhrmann shot footage of that psychiatrist working with Tobey Maguire in character as preparation.

Is the device unwarranted by the text? Partly. Fitzgerald's Nick does refer to Gatsby as the man who gives his name to this book, which establishes that the novel is a written object composed by Nick. That much is in the text. The rest is invention: the alcoholism, the institution, the therapeutic motive, the doctor.

The invention is also pointed rather than arbitrary. Nick claims in the novel that he has been drunk just twice in his life, and the picture quietly plays against that claim, suggesting a narrator less reliable about his own habits than he presents himself as being. That is a real reading of the text, and a defensible one, since Nick's self-description as an honest and reserved observer has been questioned by critics for decades.

### Where does the picture begin instead?

The picture opens at the Perkins Sanitarium, named for Fitzgerald's editor Maxwell Perkins, where an older Nick is being treated for what a doctor calls morbid alcoholism. Writing is prescribed as therapy, and he composes the story as a manuscript, finally titling it and adding the word great by hand.

## What the Frame Costs and What It Buys

The frame is the change most worth arguing about, so it is worth working through both sides properly.

What it buys is substantial. It licenses the voiceover, which is how the picture keeps large quantities of Fitzgerald's prose, including sentences that would otherwise have no route onto the screen. It supplies an explanation for the retrospective tense that the novel leaves vague. It makes visible the fact that Nick is constructing a narrative rather than reporting one, which is a genuinely sophisticated point about the book and one that most readers miss. And it gives Maguire something to play in the framing scenes beyond neutral observation.

What it costs is also substantial, and the costs are less obvious.

The first cost is uncertainty. In the novel we never learn when Nick is writing, what has become of him, or how much time has passed. That vagueness keeps his account unsettled and keeps the reader working. Fixing him in a sanitarium with a diagnosis and a doctor converts an open question into a resolved one, and the resolution is reductive: his narration now has a psychological cause. Everything he says can be attributed to a man in treatment.

The second cost is more technical. The frame appears to bind the picture to Nick's viewpoint, but the picture repeatedly breaks that binding, cutting to scenes Nick could not possibly have witnessed. The novel is disciplined about this in ways readers rarely notice; where Nick reports something he did not see, he tells you who told him. A film that establishes a subjective frame and then abandons it has the appearance of point of view without the constraint, which is the worst of both arrangements.

The third cost is tonal. Therapy is a modern explanatory frame, and installing it inside a nineteen-twenties story imports a psychology the novel does not use. Fitzgerald's Nick is not processing trauma. He is making a moral judgment about a class of people, and the difference between working something out emotionally and reaching a verdict is not small. Anyone examining [how the narration operates in the opening chapter](/2022/07/04/great-gatsby-chapter-1-narration/) will notice how much of that opening is argument rather than feeling.

Reaction to the device has been divided among people with standing. Fitzgerald's granddaughter has said publicly that it was her least favorite element and that she would have preferred the openness of the novel. Others involved with the family's legacy defended it.

### Why does the sanitarium frame matter more than other changes?

Because it changes the narration's status rather than its content. The novel leaves Nick's distance and condition unspecified, which keeps his judgments provisional. Assigning him a diagnosis, a doctor, and a therapeutic motive supplies a psychological cause for everything he says, converting an open question into a settled one.

Two genuine additions, both parts of the sanitarium frame that opens and closes the picture. ## Nick's Vocation and the Writer Problem

One modification deserves separate treatment because it is more consequential than its size suggests.

Fitzgerald's Nick describes himself as having been rather literary in college and mentions writing editorials, and he sets that aside for the bond business. It is a small detail, mentioned once, doing modest work: it tells you he has some verbal facility and some pretension, which makes his narration plausible.

The picture expands this considerably. Nick is shown as a frustrated writer who has taken up finance, and the film stages the substitution, showing him setting aside literature in favor of books about bonds. By the end he is a writer again, and the manuscript he produces is the novel we are watching.

The expansion has a real justification in the text, since the novel does say Nick wrote, and since it does present itself as a book Nick composed. It also produces two effects the novel avoids.

The first is that Nick becomes the story's protagonist in a way Fitzgerald resists. The novel's Nick is a witness whose own life is deliberately underwritten; we learn very little about his ambitions, and the vagueness keeps attention on Gatsby. Give him a vocation, a crisis, and a recovery, and he acquires an arc. Arcs attract sympathy, and sympathy moves the story's center.

The second is that his narration acquires literary intent. In the novel we are not meant to think about Nick's prose style, because thinking about it would break the illusion that we are receiving an account rather than reading a composition. Once we watch him typing, every beautiful sentence is a sentence he chose, which is a fascinating idea and a different book.

### Does Nick's vocation expand on screen?

He remains a bond trader, but the picture expands the novel's brief mention of his literary background into a full substitution, showing him abandoning literature for finance and returning to writing afterward. The manuscript he produces in the frame is presented as the novel itself.

## The Father's Advice and the Paraphrase Question

A smaller change that students often notice and rarely analyze well.

The novel opens with Nick recalling advice from his father: that whenever he felt like criticizing anyone, he should remember that not everyone has had his advantages. The picture keeps the moment but softens the phrasing in places, moving toward a simpler formulation about looking for the best in people.

The usual complaint is that this dumbs the material down. That complaint is not wrong, but it stops too early. The two versions say different things, and the difference is worth naming.

Fitzgerald's version is about class. Advantages means material and social advantages, and the advice is a warning that moral judgment is entangled with privilege. It is also, crucially, advice Nick does not follow. He spends the entire book judging people, and the novel's opening sets up that failure deliberately, with Nick claiming to reserve judgment in one sentence and delivering verdicts for two hundred pages afterward.

A version about looking for the best in people is about temperament rather than class. It makes Nick generous instead of compromised, and it removes the irony that the original phrasing sets running from the first page.

That is a genuine loss and a small one. It matters because it is representative: the picture consistently prefers emotional clarity to ironic complication, and this single substitution shows the preference operating on the novel's first substantive sentence.

### Is the opening paternal counsel reworded?

It keeps the moment and softens the wording in places, moving from Fitzgerald's phrasing about unequal advantages toward a simpler idea about seeing the best in people. The shift converts a warning about class and privilege into a statement about temperament, and it dissolves the irony of a narrator who claims to withhold judgment.

## The Green Light and the Visualized Symbol

Symbols pose the adaptation's most interesting technical problem, and the green light is the test case.

In the novel the light appears three times and means something different at each appearance. In the first chapter it is an unexplained object at the end of a dock, and Gatsby is reaching toward it in a gesture Nick does not understand. In the fifth chapter, with Daisy standing beside him, Gatsby remarks that the light is sometimes obscured by mist, and Nick observes that his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one, because the symbol has been exchanged for the thing itself. In the final pages it becomes general: Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that recedes before us, and the passage widens from one man's dock to a continent's history.

The meaning is produced by the sequence and by the gaps between appearances. The [dedicated analysis of the green light passage](/2029/01/29/gatsby-green-light-quote/) works through the mechanism in detail.

The picture renders the light vividly, repeatedly, and beautifully. It glows across the water in a way no reader ever quite pictured. Luhrmann treats it as the visual signature of the whole production.

And here is the problem: a symbol that is continuously visible cannot accumulate. The novel's light works partly by disappearing between chapters, so that each return reframes the last. When the image is always present and always emphasized, every appearance carries the same charge, and the three-stage shift flattens into a single sustained mood. The picture keeps the second-chapter observation about diminished enchantment, so the idea survives as dialogue. The structure that made the idea land does not.

This is a general principle rather than a criticism of one production. Prose symbols work by intermittence. Film symbols work by presence. Converting one to the other preserves the object and alters the operation.

### What does the picture do with the dock light?

Vividly and continuously, as the production's central visual signature, glowing across the bay and returning at every emotionally significant moment. The novel's three appearances produce meaning through separation and reframing; constant visibility gives each appearance the same weight, so the shift in meaning flattens into a sustained mood.

## The Words on Screen

One choice is unique to this adaptation and deserves notice, because it makes the fidelity question unusually explicit.

At several points, most prominently during the closing meditation, Fitzgerald's sentences appear on screen as text, animated over the images. The words are the novel's own, presented as words.

This is a strange and revealing gesture. It is the maximum possible fidelity, quotation without any mediation at all, and it simultaneously admits that the images cannot do what the sentences do. If the picture could render the closing pages visually, it would not need to print them.

Read charitably, the device acknowledges that this book's greatness is verbal and declines to pretend otherwise. Read less charitably, it is an adaptation putting up a sign that says the good part is over here.

Either way, it makes an argument this whole series has been advancing, and makes it inside the picture itself: the novel's value is in the prose, and a film that wants to deliver that value has to either quote it, print it, or accept losing it. The 2013 version does all three at different moments, which is more honest than pretending the transfer is complete.

### Is Fitzgerald's prose printed as text?

Yes. Fitzgerald's sentences appear as animated text at several points, most prominently over the closing meditation. The gesture is the maximum possible fidelity and simultaneously an admission that the images cannot reproduce what the prose does, since a film that could would not need to print the words.

## Where the Adaptation Is Faithful and Nobody Notices

The reputation for liberty obscures how closely several difficult scenes are handled, and giving credit here sharpens the argument about where the real departures sit.

The reunion tea is nearly exact. The rain, the excruciating awkwardness, Gatsby's near-flight, the clock he knocks and catches: all present. This is one of the hardest sequences in the novel to stage because its comedy and its pain arrive together, and the picture manages both.

The shirts follow immediately and are handled with restraint that is surprising in a production this loud. Daisy weeps, the line about the shirts is spoken, and the moment is allowed its inadequacy.

The Plaza confrontation is close to the text, including the demand that breaks it. Luhrmann stages it hot and crowded, but the words and the outcome are Fitzgerald's, and the crucial failure, Daisy's inability to say she never loved Tom, is preserved rather than softened.

The funeral is faithful and, if anything, intensified. Attendance is cut further than the novel's already brutal reckoning, and the emptiness reads.

And the closing lines are kept entire.

That list matters for judging the whole. A production that wanted to rewrite the novel would have fixed the Plaza scene, which is emotionally frustrating, and would have given the funeral a mourner or two. It did neither.

### Which scenes does the 2013 version get right?

The reunion tea with the fallen clock, the shirts, the Plaza confrontation including Daisy's crucial inability to disown her marriage, the funeral's emptiness, and the closing meditation kept whole. These are the hardest sequences to stage and the ones a production seeking easy emotional satisfaction would have altered.

## The Compressions and the Casualties

Fairness requires the other column, and some losses are worth naming precisely.

Jordan Baker suffers most. In the novel she is a substantial figure: a professional golfer with a cheating scandal attached, a woman of deliberate incuriosity, and Nick's partner in a relationship that develops across the summer and ends in a cold conversation where she accuses him of misrepresenting himself. She is the novel's other unreliable narrator, and her account of Louisville is the source of everything we know about Gatsby and Daisy's past. The picture keeps her presence and thins her function, converting a co-narrator into a supporting character.

Wolfsheim's reduction removes the novel's clearest statement about the source of Gatsby's money and about the machinery under the parties. The character carries an ugly inheritance from Fitzgerald's period stereotyping, which every adaptation has handled awkwardly, and reducing him sidesteps the difficulty at the cost of the information.

Nick's own moral compromise thins. The novel's Nick facilitates an adultery, lies by omission repeatedly, and admits to enjoying his access to other people's secrets. A version where he is a sympathetic writer in recovery is a version where his complicity is softer.

The party guest catalogue disappears entirely, and with it a piece of social satire that operates through accumulation. Fitzgerald lists names for a full page, and the joke is both that the list is absurd and that Gatsby knew none of them.

### Which material disappears in compression?

Jordan Baker's substantial role as a second unreliable narrator and Nick's romantic partner, most of Wolfsheim's function as evidence of criminal money, the catalogue of party guests that operates as accumulated satire, and much of Nick's own moral compromise, which softens under a sympathetic framing.

## The Parties: Amplification as Comparison

The party sequences are where the two versions look least alike, and working out why is more useful than registering the obvious difference.

Fitzgerald's descriptions are famously double-edged. Guests came and went like moths among the whisperings and the champagne and the stars, an image that makes them lovely and trivial in the same breath. The orchestra plays, the laughter spills, the cars arrive in a five-deep line, and the language never settles into either enjoyment or contempt. The catalogue of guest names that follows a few chapters later is absurd on its face and functions as evidence that the host knew none of them.

The picture stages these as overwhelming spectacle: the camera racing, the color saturated, the editing rapid, contemporary music driving the sequences. The stated logic, developed at length in the [account of how the soundtrack reframes the novel](/2029/10/15/gatsby-luhrmann-soundtrack/), is that period jazz now sounds quaint and therefore fails to convey the shock the music carried in 1922. Modern music restores the newness.

Assess this as a comparison problem rather than a taste question and something specific emerges. The novel's parties are seductive and slightly disgusting simultaneously. Film struggles with simultaneity, so a production must either find a visual equivalent or split the two feelings across time. Luhrmann splits them: the first half of the picture is intoxication, the second half is consequence and desolation.

That is a legitimate structural solution and it is not what the book does. Fitzgerald's disgust is present at the height of the party, embedded in the same sentence as the pleasure, which is why the parties never feel safe to enjoy. Sequential separation makes the enjoyment clean and the reckoning clean, and cleanliness is the one quality the novel's social scenes never have. The dedicated treatment of [the parties as symbol and spectacle](/2025/05/26/gatsby-parties-symbol/) develops what the doubleness is doing.

### How do the film's parties differ from the novel's?

The novel makes the parties seductive and faintly repellent within the same sentences, so pleasure is never clean. The picture separates the two responses across time, delivering intoxication in its first movement and desolation in its second, which is a workable structural solution to an effect film cannot render simultaneously.

## The Valley of Ashes and the Amplification Problem

A second case, and it complicates any simple claim that amplification distorts.

Fitzgerald's ash country is described in language that is deliberately excessive: a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat, forming ridges and hills and grotesque gardens, where ash men move dimly and crumble through the powdery air. This is not realism. It is a landscape converted into a moral proposition, and the prose is nearly hallucinatory.

Here the picture's maximalism is arguably an asset rather than a liability. A restrained, naturalistic wasteland, which is what the 1974 version produces, is less faithful to the passage than an oversized and slightly unreal one. Luhrmann's ash country is enormous, stylized, and dominated by the billboard, and the register is closer to the prose than sober period accuracy manages.

That is worth stating plainly because it cuts against the usual verdict. The criticism that this adaptation exaggerates assumes the novel is restrained. It is not. Fitzgerald's descriptive prose is frequently extravagant, piling adjectives, reaching for the mythic, and treating physical scenes as arguments. A production that matches that extravagance is doing something defensible.

The distinction that survives is between extravagance in service of an image and extravagance in service of momentum. The ash country works because the excess is doing what the novel's excess does. The party editing works less well because speed erases the doubleness that the novel's excess preserves. Amplification is neither faithful nor unfaithful in itself; it depends on which quality is being amplified.

### Does amplification always distort the novel?

No. Fitzgerald's prose is often extravagant rather than restrained, particularly in the valley of ashes, where a hallucinatory register converts landscape into moral argument. A stylized and oversized rendering matches that better than sober naturalism. The test is whether the amplification serves the same quality the prose amplifies.

## Casting as Interpretation

Comparative essays routinely ignore casting, and this adaptation makes a strong case for treating it as a textual decision.

Fitzgerald gives Gatsby very little physical description. What he gives is a smile: an expression of eternal reassurance that faced the whole eternal world for an instant, concentrated on you with an irresistible prejudice in your favour, and understood you just as far as you wanted to be understood. That passage is the character's introduction, and it describes an effect rather than an appearance.

Casting a globally famous star supplies an appearance the novel withholds and, more consequentially, supplies prior belief. The audience already accepts that this man is extraordinary before he does anything. Some of the character's work is therefore done in advance, and some of it becomes impossible, since the novel's Gatsby has to earn his effect against a background of rumor and suspicion.

Daisy's casting poses a related problem in reverse. The novel builds her almost entirely from voice and from Nick's report of what she does to people, culminating in the observation that her voice is full of money. Casting requires an actual timbre, and no timbre sounds like money. The picture handles this by leaning on the reaction shots, showing what she does to Gatsby rather than trying to render the quality directly, which is a sensible workaround.

Tom's casting is the least discussed and arguably the most successful. Fitzgerald's Tom is physically imposing, intellectually shallow, and dangerous in a way that has nothing to do with cleverness. That combination is straightforwardly castable, which is why every screen version gets Tom approximately right and struggles with the other two.

### Why does casting count as a textual decision?

Because the novel withholds Gatsby's appearance and describes only the effect of his smile, so any casting supplies information Fitzgerald deliberately omitted. A famous star also supplies prior belief in the character's exceptionality, doing part of the work in advance and making the earned version of that effect unavailable.

## Two Endings Compared

The final movement deserves close attention, because it is where fidelity and transformation sit most tightly together.

The novel ends in stages. Gatsby is shot; the holocaust is complete; Nick handles the aftermath and the funeral; he has the cold conversation with Jordan; he encounters Tom on Fifth Avenue and shakes his hand anyway, which is his own moral failure recorded without comment; he walks the beach; and then the closing meditation moves outward from the house to the island to the continent to a general claim about human wanting.

The picture keeps most of this and adds one thing: Nick, back in the frame, completing the manuscript and titling it. That addition converts the ending from a meditation into an accomplishment. The novel's last pages are a man failing to resolve something and saying so beautifully. The picture's last moments are a man finishing a book.

The difference is not enormous and it is directional. A completed manuscript is a redemption. Nick has made something out of the summer, which means the summer produced value. Fitzgerald's ending offers no such consolation; Nick goes home to the Midwest with a set of judgments and no product, and the final image is of motion without progress, boats beaten back.

Notice that the picture keeps the boats. The words are all there, spoken and printed. What has changed is the context in which they land, and that is the whole pattern of this adaptation in a single instance: Fitzgerald's language retained, Fitzgerald's situation adjusted, and the adjustment altering what the retained language means.

### How does the film's ending differ from the novel's?

The picture keeps Nick's closing meditation entire, in voiceover and printed on screen, and adds a return to the frame where he completes and titles the manuscript. That addition converts an unresolved reckoning into an accomplishment, since a finished book implies the summer produced something, which the novel pointedly declines to suggest.

## A Method for Scene-Level Comparison

Because the ledger is the article's tool, here is how to use it on a scene the table does not cover, so the approach transfers.

Pick any sequence. Read the novel's version with a pen. Mark three things: what happens, what the narrator says about what happens, and what the prose does at the level of sentence, meaning any image, rhythm, or word choice that carries weight independent of the information.

Then watch the corresponding sequence and mark the same three categories. Events almost always transfer. Narratorial comment transfers if the voiceover carries it and vanishes if not. Sentence-level work transfers almost never, because it has no equivalent channel.

Now compare the two sets. The events column will match, which tells you nothing. The second column will partially match, which tells you what the production considered essential commentary. The third column will be nearly empty on the film side, and what fills that emptiness is the interesting question: what did the picture substitute? Music, usually. Or color. Or camera movement. Those substitutions are the adaptation's actual creative work, and describing them precisely is what a strong comparative essay contains.

Applied to the shirts, the method yields this. Events: shirts thrown, Daisy weeps, line spoken, all transferred. Comment: Nick's observation survives partially. Sentence work: the novel's catalogue of colors, apple green and lavender and faint orange with monograms of indian blue, is a list whose specificity does the work, and the film substitutes visible fabric and a musical cue. The substitution is competent and it changes the mechanism from enumeration to sensation.

### How do you compare a scene properly?

Mark three things in the novel's version: what happens, what the narrator says about it, and what the sentences do independently of the information. Track the same three in the film. Events transfer, commentary partly transfers, sentence work almost never does. What the production substitutes for that third category is the real subject.

## The Namable Claim: Faithful in Plot, Transformed in Texture

Here is the argument, stated for citation.

Call it faithful in plot, transformed in texture. Comparing the novel to the 2013 picture scene by scene produces a fidelity map with a distinctive shape: events transfer almost completely, dialogue transfers heavily, and the two genuine additions are both parts of a frame that changes not what is told but who is telling and why. The transformation is real and it operates at the level of delivery rather than content, which means the common description of this adaptation as unfaithful is imprecise. It is faithful to the story and unfaithful to the manner, and the manner is where the novel lives.

The claim's usefulness is that it makes the comparison specific. Instead of arguing about whether the picture respects the book, an essay can locate exactly where fidelity holds and exactly where it breaks, and can then argue about whether the breaks serve a defensible reading. That is an arguable thesis rather than a preference.

### Is resemblance the right measure here?

No. Fidelity measures resemblance, not quality, and this picture demonstrates the gap: its plot is highly faithful while its effect is transformed. The useful question is whether each departure serves a defensible reading of the novel, which requires evaluating the interpretation rather than counting the differences.

## Which Departures Actually Matter

Not all changes are equal, and ranking them is the analytical work that separates a good comparative essay from a list.

The frame matters most, for the reasons already given: it reassigns the narration a cause and closes a question the novel keeps open. Everything else in the picture is downstream of it.

The symbol handling matters second. Converting intermittent prose symbols into continuously visible film objects preserves the images and dissolves the accumulation that gave them meaning. This affects the green light most and the eyes of the billboard nearly as much.

The tonal amplification matters third. The novel holds seduction and disgust in the same sentences; the picture separates them, delivering seduction in the first half and disgust in the second. That is a legitimate structural solution to an impossible simultaneity, and it is also a real change to what the book does.

Jordan's reduction matters fourth, because it removes a second unreliable voice and simplifies the novel's epistemology.

The paraphrasing of the father's advice matters least of the significant changes, though it is representative of a consistent preference for clarity over irony.

What does not matter much: the consolidated parties, the shortened running time of individual scenes, the reduced minor characters. These are the ordinary arithmetic of adaptation and no essay should spend space on them.

### How should the changes be ranked?

The sanitarium frame, because it supplies a psychological cause for narration the novel leaves unexplained. Then the conversion of intermittent prose symbols into continuously visible objects, which dissolves their accumulation. Then the separation of the novel's simultaneous seduction and disgust into sequential halves. Jordan's reduction follows.

## Using the Comparison in an Essay

The practical section, because most readers here have a comparative assignment.

The failing version of this essay lists differences and rates the film against the book. It contains no claim and earns low marks everywhere. Difference is data, not argument.

The working version treats each departure as evidence of an interpretation and evaluates the interpretation. Structure it in three moves: name the change precisely, state what reading of the novel it implies, then test that reading against quoted text.

Applied to the frame, that produces something like this. The picture assigns Nick a diagnosis and a therapeutic motive, which implies a reading in which his narration is symptomatic. Test it: the novel's Nick claims to have been drunk just twice in his life and claims to reserve judgment, and both claims are contradicted by his own account, so a reading that treats him as an unreliable self-reporter has textual support. The frame is therefore a defensible interpretation delivered through an undefendable mechanism, since the specific diagnosis is invented while the underlying suspicion is earned. That is a real argument with a real complication in it.

Applied to the green light, it produces a different shape. The picture makes the symbol continuously visible, which implies a reading in which it is a constant object of longing. Test it: the novel makes the enchantment diminish once Daisy is present, which requires the light to be intermittent, so the picture's handling contradicts a specific textual movement. That is a departure that fails its own test, and saying so is stronger criticism than any general complaint about excess.

Three of these worked examples make an essay. Quote the novel in every one, because a comparative essay that never quotes the source is an essay about a movie. No, and the attempt is usually detectable. ## How the Picture Reads the Novel

Setting the ledger aside, it is worth stating what interpretation the whole apparatus adds up to, since that is what an adaptation ultimately delivers.

The 2013 version reads The Great Gatsby as a tragedy of sincere feeling destroyed by a careless world. Gatsby is romantic and admirable; his hope is presented as genuinely beautiful; Daisy is weak rather than culpable; Tom is straightforwardly the villain; and Nick is a decent man damaged by proximity to all of it. The spectacle is there to make the seduction convincing, so that the collapse costs something.

That reading is available in the text and is not the only one. Fitzgerald's judgment on Gatsby is much less settled. Nick calls him worth the whole crowd and also finds something contemptible in what preyed on him. The dream is described as an extraordinary gift for hope and also as a colossal vitality of illusion, which is not a compliment. The novel keeps admiration and diagnosis in tension permanently.

The picture resolves the tension toward admiration, and it does so consistently: through the casting, the music, the framing, and the visualized symbols. That is a coherent interpretive position rather than a failure to understand, and it should be argued with rather than dismissed.

### What interpretation does the 2013 version advance?

That the novel is a tragedy of sincere feeling wrecked by careless wealth, with Gatsby's hope presented as genuinely admirable and Nick as a decent man damaged by proximity. The novel keeps admiration and diagnosis in permanent tension; the picture resolves that tension consistently toward admiration.

## The Fidelity Reputation and Why It Formed

A closing observation about reception, because the gap between this adaptation's reputation and its actual fidelity record is itself worth explaining.

The picture is widely described as taking large liberties. The ledger says otherwise about plot. So where does the reputation come from?

Three sources. The first is the soundtrack, which is the most audible departure from period and the one that generated the loudest early argument. Music is immediate and unmissable, and an anachronism in the score reads as an anachronism in the whole production even when the script is following the book closely.

The second is visual style. A production that races, saturates, and stages everything at maximum intensity feels unfaithful to a novel most readers experience as elegant and controlled. That impression is partly a misremembering of the prose, which is often extravagant, but the impression is strong and it colors judgment of everything else.

The third is the frame, which is genuinely an invention and which occupies the first and last minutes of the picture, the positions viewers remember most clearly.

Add those together and the reputation makes sense without being accurate. Viewers register the departures they can hear and see, and register the fidelity nowhere, because faithful adaptation is invisible by nature. Nobody notices that the Plaza scene is close to the text; they notice the music underneath it.

That asymmetry is worth carrying into any comparative writing. Perceived infidelity tracks salience rather than magnitude, and a careful comparison will frequently discover that the changes an audience objects to are smaller than the ones it accepted without noticing.

### Why is the 2013 version considered unfaithful?

Because its departures are the salient kind. The anachronistic soundtrack is unmissable, the visual intensity contradicts how most readers remember the prose, and the invented frame occupies the opening and closing minutes. Faithful adaptation registers with nobody, so the picture's substantial plot fidelity goes unnoticed while its audible choices define its reputation.

## Closing Verdict

Book against movie, the 2013 Gatsby compares far more closely than its reputation suggests and diverges in a smaller number of deeper places than the usual complaints identify. The plot is nearly intact. The major scenes are staged with real fidelity, including the difficult ones a less confident production would have softened. Fitzgerald's sentences survive in quantity, delivered in voiceover and at moments printed directly on the screen.

The departures cluster around one decision and its consequences. Installing Nick in a sanitarium and giving his narration a diagnosis and a therapeutic purpose closes a question the novel deliberately leaves open, and everything else follows: the sympathy, the resolution of the book's tensions toward romance, the treatment of symbols as continuous rather than intermittent. Those changes serve an interpretation, and the interpretation is defensible even where it is reductive.

The useful verdict is therefore not a grade. It is a map. Use the change ledger to locate precisely where the picture holds and where it lets go, argue about whether each release was earned, and quote the novel while doing it. That produces analysis. Complaining that a film is not a book produces nothing, and the 2013 version is a better instrument than that complaint deserves, because it is faithful enough to make its infidelities legible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does the 2013 film compare to the book?

Closely on plot and loosely on manner. Every major event appears in the novel's order, from Nick's arrival through the funeral and departure, with no invented subplot and no significant new character. Large quantities of Fitzgerald's dialogue and narration survive, some of it printed on screen. What changes is delivery: a framing device installs Nick in a sanitarium writing as therapy, the parties are staged as maximal spectacle with contemporary music, and the novel's symbols become continuously visible objects rather than intermittent described images. The result is faithful to the story and transformed in texture, which is why summary comparisons in either direction mislead.

### Q: What specific changes does the 2013 film make?

Only two outright additions, both belonging to the frame: the opening at the Perkins Sanitarium with a doctor prescribing writing, and the closing sequence in which Nick completes and titles the manuscript. Everything else is modification of emphasis. Jordan's Louisville account becomes a dramatized flashback rather than reported speech. Nick's brief literary background expands into a full vocational arc. His father's advice is partly paraphrased. Nick and Jordan's relationship shrinks and its ending changes. Wolfsheim is reduced and recast. The parties, the green light, and the valley of ashes are rendered with heightened visual intensity rather than altered in substance.

### Q: What framing device does the film add for Nick?

The picture opens at a facility called the Perkins Sanitarium, named in acknowledgment of Fitzgerald's editor Maxwell Perkins, where an older Nick Carraway is being treated for what a doctor describes as morbid alcoholism. The doctor, based on a real psychiatrist, prescribes writing as therapy, and Nick recounts the story first in speech and then on paper, eventually assembling a manuscript, titling it, and adding the word great by hand. Luhrmann has explained the reasoning directly: the filmmakers wanted to use Fitzgerald's language, and a voice in a film requires a speaker with a place and a motive.

### Q: How does the film visualize the green light?

As the production's central visual signature, glowing across the bay and returning at emotionally significant moments throughout. It is beautiful and continuously legible in a way no reader ever quite pictured. That continuity is also the difficulty. In the novel the light appears three times with substantial gaps between, and its meaning shifts across those gaps, from an unexplained gesture, to an object whose enchantment has diminished now that Daisy stands beside it, to an emblem of a general human condition. Constant visibility gives every appearance the same charge, so the three-stage development flattens into one sustained mood.

### Q: Should the film be judged only by fidelity?

No. Fidelity measures resemblance to a source, not quality, and this picture demonstrates the gap sharply: it is highly faithful at the level of plot while transforming the reading experience completely. Judging by fidelity alone would rate it high and explain nothing about why viewers who love the novel react so variously to it. The productive question is whether each departure serves a defensible reading of the text. That requires evaluating interpretations rather than counting differences, and it produces arguments that can actually be disputed, which is what analytical writing needs.

### Q: Which departures from the book matter most?

The sanitarium frame ranks first, because it supplies a psychological cause for narration the novel deliberately leaves unexplained, closing a question that keeps Nick's judgments provisional. Second is the treatment of symbols: converting intermittent prose images into continuously visible film objects preserves the images and dissolves the accumulation that produced their meaning. Third is the separation of Fitzgerald's simultaneous seduction and disgust into sequential halves, seduction in the first movement and disgust in the second. Fourth is Jordan Baker's reduction, which removes a second unreliable voice. Consolidated parties and trimmed minor characters matter very little.

### Q: Does the 2013 version keep the novel's plot intact?

To a degree that surprises most viewers who expect otherwise. Nick's arrival, the East Egg dinner, the apartment party, the first Gatsby party, the lunch with Wolfsheim, Jordan's Louisville account, the reunion tea, the mansion tour, the shirts, the party Daisy attends, the Plaza confrontation, Myrtle's death, Gatsby's night of waiting, the shooting, the empty funeral, and Nick's departure all appear in the novel's order. There is no invented subplot and no significant new character. The compressions are the ordinary economies of adaptation rather than structural interference with the story.

### Q: What happens to Nick's job in the 2013 version?

He remains a bond trader, as in the novel, but the picture expands Fitzgerald's single mention of his literary background into a full arc. Nick is shown abandoning literature for finance in a staged substitution, and he returns to writing in the frame, producing the manuscript that becomes the novel. The expansion has textual warrant, since Fitzgerald's Nick describes himself as having been rather literary in college and refers to Gatsby as the man who gives his name to this book. Its cost is that Nick acquires an arc, which draws sympathy toward him and away from Gatsby.

### Q: Does the 2013 version change Nick's father's advice?

It keeps the moment and softens the phrasing in places. Fitzgerald's version warns against criticizing anyone by recalling that not everyone has had the same advantages, which is a statement about class and about the entanglement of moral judgment with privilege. The picture moves toward a simpler formulation about looking for the best in people, which is a statement about temperament. The substitution also dissolves the novel's opening irony, since Fitzgerald's Nick claims to reserve judgment and then delivers verdicts for the entire book. A generous narrator is a different narrator from a compromised one.

### Q: How does the 2013 version handle Jordan Baker?

By keeping her present and reducing her function considerably. Fitzgerald's Jordan is a professional golfer with a cheating scandal attached, a woman of cultivated incuriosity, the source of everything the reader learns about Louisville, and Nick's romantic partner across the summer in a relationship that ends in a cold exchange where she accuses him of dishonesty. She is effectively the novel's second unreliable narrator. The picture converts her into a supporting figure, dramatizes her Louisville account as flashback rather than reported speech, and substantially shortens the relationship, which simplifies the book's handling of how anyone knows anything.

### Q: Does the 2013 version keep the closing lines?

Yes, entire. Nick's final meditation is delivered as voiceover, and the words also appear on screen as animated text, which is the maximum possible fidelity to a passage this famous. The movement from Gatsby's belief in the light, to the future that recedes before us, to the boats borne back into the past, arrives complete. What changes is function. On the page the passage is an argument that expands in stages, each reframing the last. Over imagery and music it becomes elegiac mood. The words survive; the escalating logic softens into feeling.

### Q: What does the 2013 version cut from the novel?

Jordan Baker's substantial role as second narrator and romantic partner is the largest loss. Wolfsheim's function as evidence of where the money came from is heavily reduced. The catalogue of party guests, a full page of absurd names operating as accumulated satire, disappears entirely, since lists resist filming. Much of Nick's own moral compromise thins under a sympathetic framing, so his complicity in the affair and his enjoyment of privileged access register less sharply. Various minor figures, including the McKees and much of Owl Eyes, shrink to brief appearances or vanish.

### Q: How does the 2013 version treat the eyes of Doctor T J Eckleburg?

Prominently and legibly, with substantial visual emphasis at key moments. The billboard is realized as an imposing object dominating the ash country, and its recurrences are staged for impact. The difficulty is the same one that affects the green light. In the novel the eyes appear intermittently across separated chapters, accumulating association until Wilson finally mistakes the advertisement for God, and that mistake lands because the reader has been quietly building the connection for two hundred pages. A film that emphasizes the image every time it appears delivers the symbol without the slow construction that made its final use devastating.

### Q: Does the 2013 version change the Plaza Hotel confrontation?

Not in substance. The staging is hotter and more crowded than a restrained production would choose, but the words, the escalation, and the outcome are Fitzgerald's. Crucially, the picture preserves the moment that matters most: Gatsby demands that Daisy say she never loved Tom, and she cannot say it. That failure is the hinge of the novel, since it establishes that the dream required an absolute no living person could supply. A production seeking emotional satisfaction would have softened it or given Daisy a stronger reason. This one did not, which is a real mark in its favor.

### Q: How does the 2013 version handle Gatsby's funeral?

Faithfully, and if anything with the emptiness intensified. Fitzgerald's account is already brutal: Nick works the telephones and finds that nobody will come, and the eventual attendance is minimal. The picture cuts attendance further still, which strengthens rather than dilutes the point. This is one of the sequences where film has a genuine advantage over prose, because the physical scale of an unfilled space can be shown rather than described, and an enormous house with almost nobody in it registers immediately. The novel tells you nobody came; the camera lets you see how much room there was for them.

### Q: Is the 2013 version's dialogue taken from the novel?

Much of it, and more than viewers usually notice. The major exchanges survive close to the text, including the Plaza confrontation, the shirts, and the reunion. Nick's narration quotes Fitzgerald extensively in voiceover. There are three qualifications. Some passages are paraphrased toward simpler formulations, the father's advice being the clearest case. Material from other Fitzgerald writing is occasionally interpolated into the dialogue. And Luhrmann mixes original lines with the novel's, generally without drawing attention to the seams. The overall proportion of authentic text is high by adaptation standards.

### Q: Can a student write an essay from the 2013 version alone?

No, and examiners generally spot the attempt. The picture supplies plot, atmosphere, and a set of memorable images while omitting exactly what essays are marked on: the narrator's self-contradictions, the accumulation of symbolic meaning across separated appearances, the ironies that exist only in phrasing, and the specific wording that close analysis requires. An essay written from the film describes events and calls that analysis. Watched after reading, the picture becomes genuinely useful, since comparing a staged scene with its source isolates what the prose was doing that the staging cannot.

### Q: Does the 2013 version show the novel's words on screen?

Yes, at several points, most prominently over the closing meditation, where Fitzgerald's sentences appear as animated text across the images. The gesture is worth analyzing rather than merely noting. It represents maximum fidelity, quotation with no mediation whatsoever, and it simultaneously concedes that the visuals cannot reproduce what the prose achieves, since a picture capable of rendering those pages would not need to print them. Read generously, it is an honest acknowledgment that this novel's power is verbal. Read less generously, it is a signpost indicating where the good material is located.
