## A Charge on the Bill and a Fund Behind It

The Universal Service Fund collects a percentage of the interstate revenues earned by telecommunications carriers and pays that money out to support service in places and for institutions where the market price alone would leave service unaffordable or unavailable. Carriers report the revenues that form the base, a contribution factor converts that base into a quarterly obligation, and the pooled money flows back out as support for networks, low income subscribers, schools, libraries, and rural health care providers. The charge on a telephone bill and the payment to a rural carrier are two ends of one machine.

The fund converts a subsidy that once hid inside monopoly telephone rates into an explicit levy and payment system, and every legal challenge attacks that conversion point. Under the older arrangement, regulators tolerated high prices on some services and routes so that other services and routes could be priced below cost, and the transfer stayed invisible inside a single rate structure. The fund separates the transfer from the rate. Money is collected under a stated formula, held in a dedicated fund administered for the Federal Communications Commission, and disbursed under program rules that name the recipient, the purpose, and the amount.

![Universal Service Fund contribution and support flow - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Four programs carry the disbursements. The High Cost program supports carriers serving rural, insular, and high cost areas where the cost of building and maintaining a network exceeds what local subscribers can reasonably pay. The Lifeline program lowers the monthly price of telephone or broadband service for qualifying low income households. The Schools and Libraries program, called E-Rate, discounts the telecommunications and internet services bought by eligible schools and libraries. The Rural Health Care program discounts the connectivity bought by eligible rural health care providers, including support for broadband networks that connect multiple providers.

The administrator is the Universal Service Administrative Company, a nonprofit corporation acting under the direction and oversight of the Federal Communications Commission. The administrator collects the contributor data, projects program demand, invoices carriers, receives the contributions, and pays support to recipients that have satisfied program requirements. The commission sets policy, adopts the rules, decides appeals, and fixes the contribution factor each quarter. Disbursements run at about 9 billion dollars a year, a scale that explains why the design of the collection mechanism attracts as much legal attention as the design of the payments themselves.

Because the transfer is explicit, every element of the machine is contestable in a way the old internal subsidy was not. A challenger can ask who has authority to impose the levy, which revenues belong in the base, which services count as supported, which carriers qualify to receive payment, and whether a private administrator exercises power that belongs to a government agency. Each of those questions attaches to a specific statutory element, and the statute that supplies the elements is section 254 of the Communications Act, as added by the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The sections that follow take the machine apart element by element, beginning with the problem the statute inherited.

## The Problem Universal Service Was Invented to Solve

Telephone service has a cost structure that punishes distance and rewards density. A mile of wire, a pole line, a switch, and a maintenance crew cost roughly comparable sums whether the line serves fifty households in a city block or five households spread along a rural road. The urban network spreads fixed costs across a large base of subscribers paying monthly bills. The rural network spreads comparable fixed costs across a small base, so the cost per subscriber rises sharply even when the quality of the underlying equipment is identical. Left to price alone, rural service either costs far more than urban service or does not get built at all.

Affordability poses the second half of the problem. A network that exists but is priced beyond the reach of low income households fails the purpose that justified building the network, because the value of a communications system grows with the number of people who can be reached through the system. A household cut off from telephone service is cut off from emergency calls, from employers, from schools, and from public services that assume a working connection. Universal service policy treats connection itself as the goal, and treats price and geography as the two obstacles that policy has to overcome without dismantling the commercial structure that operates the networks.

The phrase entered American telephone politics in the early twentieth century, during the era when Theodore Vail led the American Telephone and Telegraph Company and argued in substance for one integrated system, operating under one coherent policy, offering service to all. The slogan in that campaign described a unified network more than a subsidy program, and the distinction matters. Vail sought consolidation and interconnection under a dominant carrier, with regulation supplying the public oversight that competition supplied in other industries. Affordability and rural extension were presented as products of system unity, achieved through internal pricing decisions rather than through a separate fund.

The Kingsbury Commitment of 1913 gave that vision a settlement with the federal government. In a letter from company vice president Nathan Kingsbury to the Attorney General, the Bell System agreed to interconnect its long distance lines with independent telephone companies, to stop acquiring independent competitors in ways that suppressed rivalry, and to divest its interest in Western Union. The commitment traded acceptance of a regulated, interconnected monopoly for restraint on expansion. Inside such a monopoly, cross subsidy was administratively simple. Profits and high margins on long distance and urban business services could carry local and rural service at prices below standalone cost, and no separate accounting exposed the transfer.

Congress wrote the aspiration into statute in the Communications Act of 1934, whose opening provision states the purpose of making a rapid, efficient, nationwide wire and radio communication service with adequate facilities available at reasonable charges to all the people of the United States. The sentence created a regulatory mission rather than a funding mechanism, and for decades the mission was pursued through rate design inside a monopoly and, after divestiture, through a patchwork of access charges and state programs. The federal and state division of labor grew out of the technology itself. Interstate service fell to federal regulation, intrastate service remained with state commissions, and universal service sat across the boundary, since a rural subscriber needed both a local network and a connection to the national one.

## The First Element: A Statutory Duty to Contribute

Section 254(d) supplies the collection side of the machine in direct language. Every telecommunications carrier that provides interstate telecommunications services shall contribute to the mechanism that preserves and advances universal service, and the contribution must rest on an equitable and nondiscriminatory basis. The sentence does three kinds of work at once. It identifies the class of contributors by function rather than by company name, so the duty follows the activity of providing interstate service. It makes contribution mandatory rather than voluntary, so no carrier can free ride on a network of universal availability financed by competitors. It also imposes a fairness standard on the design of the obligation itself.

The equitable and nondiscriminatory standard governs both the base and the rate. A contribution system that singled out one class of carrier for a heavier burden, without a reason grounded in the statute, would collide with the standard. A system that measured one carrier by gross revenues and a competitor by a narrower measure would raise the same problem. In practice the standard pushes the commission toward a uniform percentage applied to a consistently defined revenue base, with adjustments explained by differences in the character of the service rather than by the identity of the provider. Litigation over contributions returns repeatedly to the standard because it is the statutory test a challenger can apply to any formula the commission adopts.

The same paragraph reaches beyond the mandatory class. The commission holds permissive authority to require contribution from any other provider of interstate telecommunications if the public interest requires contribution to preserve and advance universal service. That authority matters when technology shifts the market. A service that functions as a substitute for traditional telephone service can drain the revenue base if subscribers migrate toward the substitute while the substitute contributes nothing. Permissive authority lets the commission follow the migration, bringing functionally comparable providers into the contributor class through rulemaking rather than waiting for Congress to amend the statute for each new technology.

Exemptions and exclusions mark the boundary of the duty. The statute permits the commission to exempt a carrier or class of carriers when a contribution would not serve the public interest, and the administrative system excuses very small contributors under the de minimis rule addressed in the discussion of the contribution factor. Government entities, certain nonprofit providers, and providers whose interstate revenues fall below the threshold occupy different positions under the rules, and each position has to be justified against the mandatory language. The pattern is deliberate. Congress wrote a broad duty, a fairness standard for administering the duty, a discretionary power to extend the duty, and a narrower discretionary power to excuse it, so that the contributor class could adapt without losing its statutory anchor.

A duty without a defined object would be an empty command, and section 254 answers that problem by pairing the duty to contribute with definitions of what contributions support. The amount a carrier owes has no independent moral or economic logic. The amount derives from the cost of the supported services, delivered by eligible carriers, at a level the statute describes as sufficient. Contribution is therefore the dependent element of the system, calculated backward from commitments made elsewhere in the section. That dependence explains the structure of the quarterly calculation, in which projected demand from the four programs drives the percentage that contributors pay, rather than the percentage being set first and the programs spending whatever the percentage happens to raise.

## The Second Element: Designated Services That Evolve

Section 254(c) defines universal service itself as an evolving level of telecommunications services that the commission establishes periodically, taking account of advances in telecommunications and information technologies and services. The word evolving carries the weight of the definition. Congress declined to freeze the content of universal service in the technology of 1996, and declined equally to leave the content open ended. The definition is a standard with criteria, administered through periodic administrative judgment, so that the set of supported services can move from basic voice telephony toward more capable services as networks and public expectations change around the statute.

The statute lists the factors that guide each periodic determination. The commission considers whether a service is essential for education, public health, or public safety, whether a substantial majority of residential customers subscribe to the service, whether public telecommunications networks have deployed the service, and whether inclusion of the service serves the public interest, convenience, and necessity. Each factor disciplines the others. Essential character without widespread subscription describes an aspiration rather than a universal service. Widespread subscription without essential character describes a popular product rather than a public obligation. Deployment matters because support for a service that networks cannot deliver would purchase nothing.

Voice telephony stood at the center of the original designation, surrounded by the features that made a basic connection usable, such as single party service, tone dialing, access to emergency services, access to operator services, and access to directory assistance and long distance carriers. The designation described a functioning connection to the public network rather than a bare physical line. Over successive reviews the center of gravity moved toward broadband capable networks, reflecting the migration of education, health care, public safety, and ordinary commerce onto internet access. Support mechanisms followed the designation, so that High Cost support increasingly rewarded deployment and operation of networks capable of delivering broadband alongside voice.

Periodic review is the procedural guarantee that keeps the evolving definition honest. The Federal-State Joint Board recommends, the commission decides, and the record in each proceeding has to confront evidence about subscription, deployment, price, and technology. A determination that added a costly service without evidence on the statutory factors would be vulnerable on review, and a refusal to consider a service that plainly satisfied the factors would present the mirror image problem. The criteria also protect the contribution system. Contributors fund a defined set of services rather than an unlimited conception of connectivity, and the discipline of the list keeps the levy tethered to purposes Congress actually specified.

The evolving definition creates a productive tension with the contribution base. Designated services have moved toward broadband, while the statutory duty to contribute rests on providers of interstate telecommunications services, a category drawn in the vocabulary of an earlier network. Successive commissions have worked inside that tension through classification decisions and through the permissive contribution authority, and challengers have worked inside the same tension by arguing that the mismatch shows the mechanism exceeding its statutory design. The tension is structural rather than accidental. An evolving benefit definition paired with a comparatively fixed funding definition guarantees recurring legal conflict at exactly the point where the two definitions meet.

## The Third Element: Eligible Carriers Only

Payment under section 254(e) flows through a gate. Only a carrier designated as an eligible telecommunications carrier may receive universal service support for the provision of supported services, and the supported services must be offered throughout a designated service area. The gate serves an accountability purpose. Support is public money raised through a compulsory levy, and the designation process lets the designating authority confirm before payment that a carrier actually offers the supported services, advertises their availability, and accepts the service obligations that accompany the money. A carrier unwilling to serve an entire area at the supported level cannot collect support for serving only the profitable portion of that area.

### Which carriers are allowed to receive universal service support, and who designates them?

Support goes only to carriers designated as eligible telecommunications carriers. State commissions designate carriers for areas under state jurisdiction, and the Federal Communications Commission designates carriers where a state lacks authority or declines to act. Designation requires offering the supported services throughout the designated service area on common carrier terms.

Designation normally begins with the state commission, which holds primary authority for areas within the state. The commission route reflects the federal structure of the statute, since state regulators hold the detailed knowledge of local service areas, local costs, and the carriers operating in each community. Where a state commission lacks jurisdiction over a carrier or a type of service, or where the state declines to act, the federal commission designates directly. Common carrier status stands behind the whole inquiry, because designation attaches to carriers that hold themselves out to serve the public indiscriminately, and the supported services must be provided using the carrier facilities or a combination of carrier facilities and resale.

The service area concept does quiet but consequential work. Historically, service areas tracked the service territories of incumbent rural telephone companies, called study areas, and a competitive carrier seeking designation in a rural area generally had to accept the entire study area rather than selecting the densest or wealthiest exchanges. The rule against selective entry, often described as a rule against cream skimming, protected the economics of universal service. If a new carrier could take support for the cheapest customers while the incumbent retained the obligation and the cost of the most expensive customers, the support arithmetic for the incumbent would collapse. For nonrural areas, service areas follow the wire center or other boundaries set under state and federal rules.

Designation also carries continuing obligations after the initial grant. An eligible carrier must advertise the availability of supported services and their prices through media of general distribution, must comply with reporting and certification requirements that verify support is used for the purposes for which the support was provided, and must satisfy build out and performance commitments attached to particular support mechanisms. A carrier that ceases to qualify can lose designation, and a carrier that misuses support faces repayment and enforcement. The gate therefore operates in both directions across time. Designation controls entry into the payment system, and the obligations attached to designation give regulators a continuing hold over conduct inside the system, without which the levy on contributors would finance payments that no authority effectively supervised.

## The Fourth Element: Specific, Predictable and Sufficient Support

Section 254(b) states the principles that govern every universal service policy the commission adopts, and three phrases from the principles do most of the legal work. Quality services should be available at just, reasonable, and affordable rates. Consumers in all regions, including low income consumers and consumers in rural, insular, and high cost areas, should have access to telecommunications and information services reasonably comparable to the services provided in urban areas and at rates reasonably comparable to urban rates. Federal and state mechanisms for preserving and advancing universal service should be specific, predictable, and sufficient. The principles read as aspirations, and administrators ignore the text at their peril, because courts and litigants treat each phrase as a benchmark against which a rule can be measured.

The comparability principle disciplines the High Cost program. Support that left rural rates far above urban rates, or rural service quality far below urban quality, would fail the stated principle even if money changed hands. Rulemaking records therefore build comparisons into the design, benchmarking supported service against urban rates and measuring deployment against urban availability. The principle also explains why support follows cost rather than following carrier revenue. A mechanism keyed to revenue would pay the most to carriers with the largest sales, while a mechanism keyed to the gap between cost and a comparable rate pays where the statutory problem actually sits. Challenges to High Cost reforms repeatedly argue that a new model breaks comparability, and defenses repeatedly answer from the same sentence.

The affordability principle disciplines the demand side programs. Lifeline exists because availability at a comparable rate still leaves households for whom any standard rate is unaffordable, and the principle names low income consumers expressly rather than treating poverty as an afterthought. In rulemaking, the principle supports eligibility rules, discount levels, and enrollment procedures aimed at participation by qualifying households. In litigation, the principle cuts in two directions. Program defenders invoke affordability against reforms that would narrow eligibility or raise effective prices, while program critics invoke administrative discipline against designs that spend support on households outside the statutory concern. The text supplies both sides with vocabulary, which is the mark of a principle that genuinely governs rather than decorates.

Specific, predictable, and sufficient operates as the design specification for the entire mechanism. Specific means the support must be identified and directed rather than diffused through general rate relief. Predictable means carriers must be able to plan investment against support that follows stated rules rather than discretionary grants. Sufficient means the total collection must actually cover the cost of the commitments the statute makes, which converts the contribution factor into a dependent variable. A factor set too low starves the programs and violates sufficiency. A factor set without a disciplined demand projection invites the charge that collection exceeds the statutory purpose. Nearly every argument about the size of the fund is, at bottom, an argument about the meaning of sufficient.

Contribution equity completes the set. The principles state that every telecommunications carrier providing interstate services should contribute on an equitable and nondiscriminatory basis, and that the commission should base policies on additional principles that serve the public interest where appropriate. The equity principle links the payment side back to the collection side, preventing a design that pursues comparability for recipients through a base that arbitrarily burdens one class of contributor. Taken together, the principles function less as a mission statement than as a checklist for judicial review. A rule that cannot be narrated through the principles has a legal problem, and a rule that can be narrated through the principles begins litigation with the statute itself as part of the defense.

## The Joint Board and the Federal State Division of Labor

Section 254(a) institutionalizes federalism inside the statute. The commission must convene a Federal-State Joint Board on Universal Service, composed of commissioners from the federal commission, commissioners from state utility commissions, and a representative of state consumer advocates, and the board must recommend decisions on the definition of supported services and related questions. The commission must issue rules within a statutory period after receiving the recommended decision, and departures from board recommendations require explanation. The structure gives states a formal voice inside the federal mechanism without giving any single state a veto over national policy.

The Joint Board delivered its recommended decision in 1996, in the months after the Telecommunications Act became law, and the recommendations shaped the first federal universal service rules adopted in 1997. The board addressed the definition of supported services, the structure of support for rural and nonrural carriers, the design of discounts for schools, libraries, and rural health care providers, and the treatment of low income support. The recommended decision mattered beyond its individual conclusions. A recommendation produced jointly by federal and state regulators carried political and evidentiary weight in the rulemaking that followed, and the commission order adopted much of the framework while explaining points of departure, establishing a template for later proceedings on broadband era reform.

States run a parallel half of the system with independent legal foundations. State commissions designate eligible carriers for areas under state jurisdiction, administer state universal service funds financed under state law, set intrastate requirements, and supervise the local service obligations that federal support presupposes. State funds vary widely in size and design, reflecting differences in geography, in the structure of rural telephone provision, and in state policy choices about affordability. The parallel structure means a rural carrier commonly answers to two universal service regimes at once, receiving federal support under federal rules for interstate purposes and state support under state rules for intrastate purposes, with cost allocation rules policing the boundary between the two.

The division of labor produces friction at predictable points. A state designation decision can determine access to federal money, so a federal payment system depends in part on state administrative capacity and state policy judgment. Federal reforms that change service area rules or impose national performance standards can override arrangements a state built around local conditions. Cost allocation between interstate and intrastate jurisdictions affects both the contribution base and the size of support, so jurisdictional accounting disputes carry financial consequences disproportionate to their technical appearance. The Joint Board exists partly to manage that friction inside a standing institution rather than leaving every dispute to litigation between levels of government.

The federal state structure also shapes the legal challenges. A challenger attacking designation attacks a decision that may belong to a state commission, while a challenger attacking the contribution factor attacks a federal order, and the two attacks proceed in different forums under different standards. Defenders of the system present the division as evidence of care, showing that Congress built consultation and state participation into the statute rather than concentrating the whole mechanism in one agency. Critics present the same division as diffusion of accountability, in which each level of government attributes unpopular charges to decisions taken at the other level. Both descriptions fit the structure, and the structure persists because the network itself remains neither purely national nor purely local.

## How the Contribution Factor Is Calculated

The contribution factor is a fraction, recalculated every quarter, and the arithmetic is transparent even when the inputs are contested. The numerator is projected program demand for the quarter, meaning the support the four programs expect to disburse plus administrative expenses and applicable reserves. The denominator is projected interstate end user telecommunications revenues for the quarter, meaning the revenue base reported by contributors. Dividing demand by the base produces the percentage that each contributor owes on the interstate portion of revenues. The factor for the fourth quarter of 2026 is 42 percent, a figure that reflects both the scale of program demand and the long contraction of the traditional interstate revenue base against which demand is charged.

Revenue reporting supplies the denominator. Contributors file FCC Form 499-A annually, reporting revenues for the completed year and registering the information that establishes contributor identity and revenue categories, and FCC Form 499-Q quarterly, reporting revenues from the prior quarter and projecting revenues for the coming quarter. The administrator aggregates the projections, the commission reviews the demand and revenue estimates, and the factor takes effect for the quarter unless the commission acts to modify the proposal. Errors and revisions feed into later quarters through true up adjustments, so a projection that overstated revenues produces a higher factor in a subsequent quarter and a projection that understated demand produces a compensating correction in the same direction.

Allocating revenue between interstate and intrastate jurisdiction is the hardest measurement problem in the system, because many services blend the two. Wireless carriers may use a safe harbor under which 37.1 percent of revenues are treated as interstate, sparing the carrier the burden of tracking the jurisdiction of individual calls and data sessions. Interconnected VoIP providers may use a safe harbor of 64.9 percent of revenues treated as interstate. A provider may instead conduct a traffic study that measures actual jurisdictional use and supports a different allocation, accepting the audit burden that accompanies a study. Safe harbors trade accuracy for administrability, and the trade is contested whenever a harbor percentage drifts away from measured use, because every point of understatement in the base raises the factor charged to contributors whose revenues are fully counted.

The de minimis rule removes the smallest contributors from the system. A provider whose calculated annual contribution falls below the de minimis threshold of 10,000 dollars is excused from direct contribution for that year, on the reasoning that the administrative cost of billing and collecting trivial sums exceeds the value of the sums to the fund. The exemption has a boundary effect. Providers near the threshold structure reporting and monitor revenue classification with the threshold in mind, and the administrator must police the boundary against artificial fragmentation of a larger business into nominally separate small providers. The threshold also interacts with pass through practices, since customers of an exempt provider may still bear fund costs embedded in the wholesale prices the provider pays to contributing carriers.

Quarterly true ups keep the machine honest across time. Demand projections miss because application cycles, deployment schedules, and program participation vary, and revenue projections miss because the telecommunications market moves during the quarter being measured. The true up mechanism carries the difference forward rather than reopening settled quarters, preserving predictability for carriers that billed customers on the basis of the announced factor. The result is a factor that behaves less like a price and more like a balancing account rate, rising when the base shrinks or demand grows and falling only when one of those pressures reverses. Critics of the fund focus on the rising factor as evidence of structural failure, while defenders answer that the arithmetic faithfully reports a statutory mismatch between expanding commitments and a narrowing base, and that the remedy for the mismatch belongs to Congress or to classification decisions rather than to the division itself.

## Who Writes the Check and Who Bears the Cost

Legal liability and economic burden part company inside the fund. The carrier writes the check. Section 254(d) places the duty on the carrier, the administrator invoices the carrier, and enforcement runs against the carrier for any shortfall. A carrier that fails to contribute faces penalties, interest, and referral for collection, and no customer of the carrier stands in the legal chain of obligation. The statutory design therefore presents the contribution as a cost of doing interstate business, comparable in form to other regulatory charges that attach to the provider rather than to the purchaser. Presentation, however, is not incidence, and the distance between the party that pays the administrator and the party that finally bears the cost is where the economics of the fund begins.

Carriers recover the cost through prices, and federal rules permit a carrier to pass the contribution through to customers as a line item on the monthly bill, commonly labeled as a universal service charge or federal universal service fee. The line item may not exceed the contribution attributable to the service billed, and the label may not misrepresent the charge as a tax or a government mandated fee imposed directly on the customer. In practice the charge appears as a percentage applied to the interstate portion of the bill, moving each quarter with the contribution factor. A customer comparing bills across quarters can therefore watch the fund rate change in miniature, which is precisely the visibility that distinguishes the explicit system from the hidden cross subsidy that preceded the fund.

Incidence analysis asks who can pass the cost onward and who ends up absorbing a charge when passing stops. Robert W. Crandall and Leonard Waverman, in Who Pays for Universal Service? published by Brookings Institution Press in 2000, examined the distribution of the burden and concluded that the cost falls mainly on consumers of interstate telecommunications services, including low income households that the demand side programs exist to assist. The Crandall and Waverman conclusion, as presented in Who Pays for Universal Service? from Brookings Institution Press in 2000, treats the contribution as a burden borne disproportionately by users of interstate services relative to the benefits many of those users receive, because demand for basic telephone service responds weakly to price and carriers therefore shift the charge forward rather than absorbing the charge against profit.

Comparison with research on [the distribution of costs and benefits in farm subsidy programs](/2021/01/15/farm-subsidy-policy-impact/) sharpens the point that the party named in a statute and the party bearing the final burden are rarely identical. A subsidy financed by a narrow levy on one set of transactions distributes costs differently from a subsidy financed through general taxation, even when the programs deliver identical benefits to identical recipients. The fund made a financing choice with distributional consequences. Interstate service users, a group that includes small businesses, rural customers buying long distance or wireless service, and low income households paying the line item on a basic bill, supply the revenue that pays support back to a partially overlapping group of recipients. Overlap softens the transfer for some households and reverses the transfer for others.

The regressivity question follows directly. A percentage charge on a necessity service takes a larger share of income from a poor household than from a wealthy household, and the Lifeline discount of 9.25 dollars a month, or 34.25 dollars on Tribal lands, offsets the burden only for households that qualify for Lifeline and successfully enroll. E-Rate discounts from 20 percent to 90 percent illustrate the contrasting design inside the same fund, where the subsidy rate varies explicitly with need and rurality rather than applying uniformly. Defenders of the financing structure answer that every funding source distributes costs somehow, that general revenues carry burdens of a different shape, and that the visibility of the line item at least forces the distribution into public argument. Critics answer that visibility without reform merely documents the burden. The argument persists because the underlying fact is stable. Carriers transmit the cost, consumers bear the cost, and the statute assigns the duty to the party in the middle.

## The Administrator: Why the Universal Service Administrative Company Exists

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 created obligations, contribution duties, and support purposes, but a statute by itself collects no revenue, reviews no application, and pays no invoice. An institution had to sit between the text of section 254 and the flow of money that reached carriers, schools, libraries, clinics, and low income households. In 1997 the Federal Communications Commission filled that gap by creating the Universal Service Administrative Company, called USAC, as a subsidiary of the National Exchange Carrier Association. In 1998 USAC became an independent nonprofit corporation, separate from the association that had incubated it, and it has administered the fund under commission direction since that reorganization.

The choice of a separate administrator reflected a practical division between judgment and execution. Setting policy involves discretionary choices about eligibility, support formulas, discount levels, and the size of the burden placed on contributors, and those choices belong to an accountable public agency answerable through administrative procedure and judicial review. Execution involves a different kind of work entirely: maintaining databases of carriers and beneficiaries, processing thousands of forms on fixed calendars, checking arithmetic, verifying eligibility records, issuing commitments, and moving money on schedule. A commission organized around rulemaking, adjudication, and enforcement is poorly shaped for high volume transaction processing, while a specialized administrator can build systems, staff, and routines around that single repeated task.

Separation also offered a measure of insulation and clarity. Because USAC administers programs created by the commission, carriers and beneficiaries deal with one office for filings and disbursements rather than approaching separate bureaus for each program. Because USAC does not set the policy it administers, a disappointed applicant can distinguish between an administrative error in applying a rule and disagreement with the rule itself, and each kind of complaint has a different route for resolution. The administrator route also allowed the commission to draw on industry familiarity with billing systems and settlement practices without handing policy authority to an industry body.

Governance was designed to keep the administrator close to the communities that fund and receive support without giving any one group control. The USAC board of directors has been drawn from telecommunications carriers that contribute to and receive support from the fund and from beneficiary communities that include schools, libraries, rural health care providers, low income consumers, and state regulatory officials. That composition gives the board direct familiarity with billing records, application burdens, network costs, and the practical effect of deadlines and documentation demands. It also creates a standing tension that the structure expects the commission to police, since directors drawn from interested sectors administer money in which their sectors have a stake.

The limits on USAC matter as much as its powers. USAC collects contribution data, administers the programs, and disburses support, but the Federal Communications Commission sets policy, approves program rules, reviews USAC budgets, and reviews its decisions through the appeal process. USAC can recommend, project demand, and flag administrative problems, yet changes to eligibility, discount matrices, caps, and support mechanisms require commission action. Its operating budget itself comes from the fund and receives commission scrutiny, so the administrator spends support money only under oversight from the agency that created it. That arrangement keeps democratic accountability with the commission while placing routine administration in an institution built for repetition, accuracy, and scale across disbursements of about 9 billion dollars a year.

## What the Administrator Does Across a Funding Year

A funding year at the Universal Service Administrative Company follows a repeating cycle that begins with revenue and ends with audited disbursement. The cycle opens with data collection. Carriers file revenue information on prescribed forms, reporting interstate and international end user telecommunications revenues that form the contribution base. USAC receives those filings, checks them for completeness and consistency, and aggregates them into the revenue figures that the Federal Communications Commission uses when the contribution factor is set for each quarter.

From the aggregated data, USAC prepares projections of program demand and administrative expense and supplies the commission with the information behind its factor recommendation. The commission, rather than the administrator, adopts the factor, and USAC then translates it into invoices. Carriers receive bills reflecting reported revenues multiplied by the factor, and USAC tracks payment, pursues delinquency, and deposits collections into the fund accounts from which support is later paid.

The middle of the cycle belongs to applications. Each program runs application windows during which schools, libraries, health care providers, carriers, and eligible households submit requests through program specific systems. USAC reviews applications for eligibility, checks requests against program rules, verifies discount levels and calculations, and resolves inconsistencies through documented inquiries. Where review succeeds, USAC issues commitment letters stating the support approved for a funding year and the conditions attached. A commitment is a reservation of fund money against a described purchase or service, and it becomes a disbursement only after services are delivered, invoices are submitted, and certifications confirm that the rules were followed.

Disbursement closes the cycle and opens the audit trail. Beneficiaries and service providers submit invoices against commitments, USAC checks amounts against approved requests, and payments flow to the party entitled to receive them under each program rule. Records remain available for later audit and appeal. The work is carried out through four operating divisions aligned with the four programs, alongside shared functions for contributions, finance, and information systems. | E-Rate (schools and libraries) | Schools and libraries, paid through their service providers | Discounted telecommunications, internet access, and internal connections | A discount percentage scaled by poverty and rural status, within an annual ceiling | Schools and Libraries Division |
| Rural Health Care | Rural health care providers and consortia that include them | Telecommunications and broadband connections linking clinics and hospitals | Support based on the difference between urban and rural rates or a set discount, within an annual cap | Rural Health Care Division |

Read against the table, the funding year has a single logic repeated four times with different beneficiaries and formulas. Revenue enters through contribution invoices, demand enters through application windows, commitments convert eligible demand into reserved amounts, and disbursements follow delivery and certification. A late commitment compresses the time available for purchasing and invoicing, and a documentation defect can hold a payment long after a service reached the beneficiary.

## The High Cost Program and the Price of Rural Networks

The High Cost program rests on a simple cost fact that geography makes unavoidable. A telephone or broadband network carries large fixed costs that change little with the number of subscribers served: poles, conduit, fiber or copper loops, switching and transport equipment, power, maintenance crews, and repair after storms. In a dense city block, hundreds of subscribers share the cost of a short run of plant. On a rural route, a handful of households may be spread along miles of road, so the same plant is divided among far fewer bills. Cost per subscriber therefore rises as density falls, and terrain, distance, and severe weather push the figure higher still.

Universal service policy answers that arithmetic with support paid to eligible carriers serving high cost areas. Rather than requiring rural subscribers to pay the full local cost of their loops, or allowing carriers to abandon territory where revenue cannot cover cost, the fund narrows the gap between what subscribers can reasonably pay and what service costs to provide. Support flows to the carrier, conditioned on serving the designated area and meeting service obligations, and the amount reflects the characteristics of the territory. Eligibility rules and certification duties tie the money to a continuing duty to serve.

The program began in the voice era, when support mechanisms were built around the cost of telephone loops and the expenses of incumbent local carriers. Its center of gravity later shifted toward broadband, as the service that universal service was expected to sustain changed from dial tone alone to high capacity internet access. Funds associated with the Connect America Fund and its successors illustrate the shift in mechanism rather than merely in name. Support that once reimbursed a share of recorded voice network costs was redirected toward defined broadband deployment obligations, with specified speeds, latency standards, and locations to be served within set periods. Described generically, those successor mechanisms pay carriers to build and operate broadband in places where the business case fails, and they attach the payment to measurable deployment rather than to general corporate revenue.

Two methods for setting that support stand in instructive contrast. Model based support begins with an engineering and economic model that estimates the cost of serving each location in an area, given terrain, density, and plant requirements, and then offers support calculated from the model in exchange for accepting deployment duties. The model approach gives predictability and national consistency, and it avoids rewarding a carrier for recording high costs that a more efficient operator might not incur. Its weakness lies in the model itself, since any estimate can misstate local conditions and either overpay for easy territory or underpay for difficult ground. Auction based support takes the opposite route. Eligible providers bid for the support they require to serve an area, and bidding reveals the subsidy each bidder needs rather than the subsidy a model assumes. Auctions can lower total cost, but they require careful design on default rules and penalties so that a low bid does not become an unbuilt network.

Both methods try to solve the same enduring problem more honestly than cost reimbursement did. Paying recorded costs dulls the incentive to control them, paying modeled costs risks paying for a map rather than a territory, and paying winning bids risks trusting a promise that later proves optimistic. High Cost support therefore pairs each payment method with location reporting, performance testing, and recovery provisions, so that the subsidy buys a functioning network in expensive country rather than an annual transfer detached from service.

## Lifeline and the Affordability Problem

The High Cost program addresses the cost of building networks where people live far apart. Lifeline addresses a different barrier that persists even where networks already pass the door: a household may be unable to afford the monthly price of service that neighbors purchase without difficulty. The Federal Communications Commission created Lifeline in 1985, in the period after divestiture raised concern that rising local rates would push low income households off the telephone network altogether. The program gives a discount of 9.25 dollars a month on eligible telephone or broadband service, and the discount rises to 34.25 dollars on Tribal lands, where poverty rates, geographic isolation, and gaps in network availability combine to make connection harder to obtain and sustain.

The discount reaches the household through the carrier rather than through a direct cash payment. A participating carrier enrolls the eligible subscriber, applies the discount to the monthly bill, and claims reimbursement from the fund for the amount given. That structure keeps the benefit tied to an actual subscription, so support follows a working connection instead of becoming general income assistance detached from service. It also places the carrier inside the eligibility process, which creates both convenience for the subscriber and a compliance risk for the program, since a carrier paid per enrolled subscriber has a financial interest in enrollment volume. Program rules, audits, and verification systems exist in large part to counter that incentive.

### Why does Lifeline pay the carrier rather than sending money to the household?

Payment through the carrier ties support to a live subscription, lets the discount appear directly on the bill the household must pay, avoids a separate payment system for millions of small monthly amounts, and gives the fund a single accountable claimant whose enrollment and reimbursement records can be audited against subscriber eligibility data.

Eligibility turns on household income and participation in specified assistance programs, and the program allows only one discount per household. The one per household rule sounds simple and proves difficult in practice, because households share addresses in apartment buildings, shelters, and multigenerational homes, and because a discount attached to a person rather than a dwelling requires records capable of distinguishing separate households at one address. Duplicate support wastes fund money and exposes carriers and subscribers to recovery, so address checks and household worksheets form part of the enrollment record.

Verification has been centralized to reduce inconsistent carrier by carrier judgments. The National Verifier, run by the Universal Service Administrative Company, checks applicant eligibility against available databases and documented proof, and carriers rely on its determinations for enrollment and periodic recertification. Centralized verification gives one standard for evidence, one record of the determination, and a clearer separation between the party that profits from enrollment and the party that decides eligibility. It does not remove every barrier. Database matches fail for people with thin administrative records, names and addresses appear in inconsistent forms across agencies, and recertification deadlines can end discounts for households that remain eligible but miss a notice. Lifeline therefore illustrates a permanent trade inside affordability policy. A discount small enough to administer at national scale still requires proof, renewal, and error correction, and each safeguard added against improper payment also adds friction for the households the discount exists to serve.

## E-Rate: Discounts Instead of Construction

Section 254(h) of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 extended universal service beyond households and carriers to the institutions where a community gathers to learn. It directed that elementary and secondary schools and libraries receive services at discounted rates, and the Federal Communications Commission built the E-Rate program on that instruction. The program, formally the schools and libraries universal service support mechanism, differs from the High Cost program in a fundamental respect. It does not pay to construct networks or reimburse a carrier for serving expensive territory. It discounts the price that an eligible school or library pays for services and connections purchased from a provider, leaving the institution as the buyer and the market transaction at the center of the arrangement.

The discount scale carries the policy judgment. Discounts range from 20 percent to 90 percent on eligible purchases, scaled by poverty measured through school lunch eligibility and by rural status. A wealthy suburban district pays most of its own bill and receives a modest reduction, while a poor rural system pays a small fraction of the price for the same category of service. Poverty supplies the main progression in the matrix because the program aims its deepest help at institutions whose local tax and funding bases are weakest, and the rural adjustment recognizes that distance raises the underlying price before any discount is applied. Applicants calculate the discount from enrollment and lunch eligibility data for the relevant buildings, and errors in that data translate directly into overpayment or underpayment, which is why the figures attract close review.

Process disciplines the purchase. An applicant begins competitive bidding by filing Form 470, which describes the services sought and opens a window during which providers may bid. The applicant evaluates bids under rules that require price to be the primary factor, documents the choice, and then files Form 471, the funding request that identifies the provider selected, the services ordered, the discount claimed, and the support requested. The Universal Service Administrative Company reviews the request and issues a funding commitment decision stating what the fund will support. Only after services are delivered and invoicing steps are completed does money move, generally as a credit through the provider or as reimbursement against paid bills.

The program operates within an annual ceiling. The commission set the ceiling at 2.25 billion dollars in 1997, when the program began, and raised it to 3.9 billion dollars in 2014, after demand, eligible broadband needs, and years of inflation had pressed against the original figure. The ceiling matters because eligible demand can exceed the money available in a funding year, so the program uses funding priorities that favor connectivity services over less central categories when requests outrun the cap. Internal connections, meaning the wiring, routers, switches, and wireless equipment that distribute connectivity inside a school or library building, compete within that structure alongside the services that bring connectivity to the building wall.

That distinction between service to the building and distribution inside it shaped much of the program design. A fast connection delivered to a demarcation point has limited value if classrooms lack the internal network to carry it, while internal equipment has no value without an external connection of adequate capacity. E-Rate support therefore reaches both sides of the wall, under category rules and per applicant budgets for internal connections. Across the whole mechanism, the discount design keeps institutions purchasing and choosing, while the fund absorbs a poverty weighted share of the price.

## Rural Health Care and Institutional Demand

The Rural Health Care program applies universal service policy to a setting where distance carries clinical consequences. A rural clinic may need to send imaging to a distant specialist, hold a consultation with a hospital physician, exchange records with a referral center, or sustain the ordinary administrative traffic of modern care, and each function depends on telecommunications and broadband connections whose rural price reflects the same density problem that shapes residential network economics. Section 254 directed support for health care providers in rural areas, and the program pays toward the cost of eligible telecommunications and broadband service for those providers rather than toward buildings, staff, or medical equipment.

Support has commonly been measured against the gap between rural and urban rates for comparable service. Where a rural provider pays far more than an urban counterpart for the same capacity because the connection crosses long, sparsely served routes, the fund narrows that difference, so that geography rather than clinical need does not decide whether a facility can connect at a workable price. Later program structures added a discount based mechanism for broadband alongside the rate difference approach, giving providers and consortia more than one route to support depending on the service purchased. Eligibility rules define which providers count, favoring public and nonprofit institutions such as hospitals, clinics, community health centers, health departments, and educational institutions that train health care personnel.

Consortia form a distinctive feature of the program. Rural providers may apply together, and a consortium may include nonrural providers alongside rural ones, provided the rural membership satisfies program requirements. A hospital hub in a small city can therefore share an application and a network with the rural clinics that refer patients to it, which matches the actual topology of care. Shared applications also spread administrative cost and support the dedicated networks that link facilities into a single system for records, imaging, and consultation.

The program operates under an annual cap. The cap stood at 400 million dollars for many years and was raised to 571 million dollars in 2018, when demand from expanding broadband based care pressed against the older figure. A cap forces prioritization when eligible requests exceed available money, and program rules have used funding windows and proration mechanisms to allocate limited support among applicants.

Clinics matter to rural network economics beyond their own bills, which explains why the fund treats them as anchor demand. A health care provider purchases reliable, higher capacity service on a continuing institutional budget, and that predictable revenue improves the business case for building or upgrading the route that serves the clinic. Once a route exists to reach the clinic, households and businesses along it become cheaper to connect, because part of the fixed cost of the path has a committed customer behind it. Institutional demand therefore does double work in thin markets. It meets an immediate need for care delivered across distance, and it steadies the revenue base on which broader rural networks depend, turning a single clinic connection into a piece of shared local infrastructure.

## Audits, Appeals and the Recovery of Improper Payments

A fund that disburses about 9 billion dollars a year through thousands of carriers, schools, libraries, clinics, and service providers cannot rely on application review alone. Money moves after certifications and invoices, and some share of payments in any large program will rest on mistaken calculations, ineligible purchases, inadequate documentation, or deliberate misstatement. Oversight exists to find those payments, recover them, and deter the next error. Audits form the first layer. The Universal Service Administrative Company conducts audits and reviews of carriers and beneficiaries, examining whether revenues were reported correctly, discounts were calculated from accurate data, services billed were the services delivered, and support claimed matched the rules of the relevant program and funding year.

Audit method follows the money through documents. Auditors sample transactions, trace invoices back to commitments and contracts, compare claimed discount levels against eligibility records, and test whether services supported by the fund were delivered for eligible purposes. Contributors face parallel scrutiny, since a carrier that underreports interstate revenue pays less than the rules require and shifts its share onto other contributors. Findings can produce repayment demands, adjustments to later payments, or referral for enforcement where the evidence suggests deliberate misconduct rather than clerical failure.

A second layer sits inside the Federal Communications Commission itself. The commission Office of Inspector General investigates fraud, waste, and abuse touching commission programs, including universal service, and its work can run alongside or beyond administrator audits. Inspector general investigations carry tools and consequences beyond routine program review, and cases involving deliberate deception can reach civil or criminal enforcement through the appropriate authorities. The distinction matters for how participants experience oversight. An audit dispute concerns whether a rule was followed and a payment was proper, while an investigation concerns whether someone intended to deceive the fund, and the two tracks follow different procedures even when they begin from the same transaction.

Recovery is the financial consequence that gives the rules weight. Payments made in violation of program rules are subject to recovery from the party responsible, whether the error originated with a beneficiary that claimed an ineligible expense or a carrier that reported revenue incorrectly. Recovery can reach back across funding years, which is why record keeping duties extend well beyond the date of payment. Beneficiaries and carriers must retain the documents behind applications, bids, invoices, eligibility determinations, and service delivery for the periods the rules prescribe, and an inability to produce records can itself defeat a claim that a payment was proper. The burden falls on the recipient to prove entitlement, not on the fund to prove the negative from missing paper.

Disagreement has a defined path. A party aggrieved by an administrator decision appeals first to the Universal Service Administrative Company, which reviews its own determination on the record presented, and then to the Federal Communications Commission, which can affirm, reverse, or modify the outcome under program rules and precedent. Commission decisions on recurring questions build guidance that later applicants and auditors apply, clarifying how rules treat shared buildings, discount data, bidding defects, and documentation gaps.

## The Money Before the Fund: Cross Subsidies Inside a Monopoly

Long before an explicit fund existed, American telephone policy pursued universal service through prices rather than appropriations or contributions. During the Bell System era, the dominant arrangement placed a single integrated monopoly in charge of local exchanges and long distance service across most of the country, and regulators used that integration to hold some prices below cost by setting others above it. Long distance rates, business rates, and urban rates stood above the cost of providing those services, and the surplus they generated subsidized residential and rural local service priced below its cost. A household paid less for basic local connection than the loop and exchange cost to provide, while a business customer or a frequent long distance caller paid more than the incremental cost of those calls. The subsidy never appeared as a tax or a line item. It lived inside the rate structure itself.

Several mechanisms kept the internal transfer working. Separations procedures divided the cost of plant used jointly for local and long distance service between state and interstate jurisdictions, and the share assigned to the interstate side grew over time, which moved more cost recovery onto long distance prices regulated at the federal level. Settlements then distributed long distance revenues among the companies whose local networks originated and terminated the calls, channeling money toward high cost local operations. Because one system dominated both ends of the arrangement, the transfer needed no separate collection agency. Regulators set the prices, the monopoly collected the surplus where it was generated, and the same corporate structure applied it where service lost money.

Access charges became the first explicit piece of the arrangement after divestiture approached and took effect. When competing carriers needed to connect their long distance customers to local subscribers, they paid local companies charges for originating and terminating access, and those charges were set at levels that continued to carry part of the cost of the local loop. A charge that had been an internal accounting matter inside one company became a visible price between companies, and with visibility came dispute about how much of the charge paid for actual access and how much carried the inherited subsidy for local service.

Historians of the system disagree about what the original goal even was, and the disagreement clarifies what the monopoly era actually pursued. Milton Mueller, in Universal Service, published by MIT Press in 1997, is held consistently as arguing that universal service originally meant network interconnection and uniformity rather than household affordability. In that account, the early drive behind the phrase concerned one connected system with compatible standards and a single policy for interconnection, a goal that served the dominant carrier as much as it served the public, and the later association of the term with subsidized household service was a subsequent layer added onto an older idea about network unity. That reading recasts the Bell era subsidies as instruments of system building rather than as a mature welfare policy for telephone subscribers.

Richard R. John, in Network Nation, published by Harvard University Press in 2010, approaches the same history from the political side, tracing how American communications networks were political constructions shaped by public decisions about charters, rates, access, and obligation rather than natural products of technology or markets. Read together, the two accounts resist a simple story in which a benevolent monopoly quietly delivered affordable service. The cross subsidies were real, but they rested on deliberate choices about who would overpay, who would underpay, and which institution would be trusted to hold the arrangement together.

## Divestiture and the Unraveling of the Hidden System

The hidden subsidy system depended on monopoly for its arithmetic. A transfer collected from long distance, business, and urban customers can support residential and rural service only while those customers lack another source for the overpriced services. The 1984 breakup of the Bell System destroyed that condition. Under the consent decree that ended the government antitrust case, the Bell System was divided, with the long distance and equipment arms separated from the regional companies that kept the local exchanges. The local companies retained the high cost loops and the obligation to serve, while the services that had generated the surplus became competitive markets open to new entrants.

Competition attacked the subsidy at its source. Rival long distance carriers such as MCI and Sprint, which had fought for entry before divestiture, could target the most profitable traffic first: high volume business customers, dense intercity routes, and customers whose rates sat farthest above cost. That pattern, often called cream skimming, is not a moral failing of entrants but the predictable logic of entry, since a new carrier survives by serving customers whose prices offer margin. Every customer who left the incumbent arrangements for a cheaper rival took away part of the surplus that had held local residential rates down. The more successful competition became in the markets that funded the transfer, the less transfer remained to fund it.

Pressure then moved to the prices that had been sheltered. Local rates faced upward pressure as regulators confronted the gap between what residential service cost and what subscribers paid, a gap that competition in long distance could no longer quietly fill. Raising local rates carried political cost, because subscribers experienced the increase as a new charge for an existing service rather than as the removal of a hidden discount, and many households on low incomes faced genuine difficulty paying cost based local prices. The concern that produced Lifeline in 1985 grew directly from that squeeze, as policymakers looked for a targeted way to keep basic service affordable for the households least able to absorb higher rates without recreating the entire monopoly rate structure.

Regulators tried intermediate repairs. Access charges kept part of the transfer alive by requiring long distance carriers to pay local companies for connection to subscribers, and separations and settlements continued to allocate costs and revenues in ways that favored local service. Each repair, however, reproduced the original vulnerability in a more contested form. A competing carrier paying inflated access charges had both the incentive and the legal standing to challenge them, arguing that the charges distorted the competitive market and taxed its customers to support a rival network. Business customers learned to bypass arrangements that overcharged them, through private networks and alternative routing, and large users pressed regulators for prices closer to cost. The transfer survived, but as a disputed levy between companies rather than as an unnoticed internal allocation.

The lesson of the unraveling reaches beyond telephony. A hidden transfer can survive only while the institution carrying it controls the transactions from which the surplus is drawn. Competition separates those transactions, prices move toward cost in the contested markets, and the subsidy loses its funding base precisely because the policy succeeded in opening the market. Policymakers who still want the transfer must then choose between abandoning the goal, raising the sheltered prices to cost, or rebuilding the subsidy in explicit form with a collection mechanism that competition cannot erode. By the middle of the nineteen nineties, American telephone policy had effectively exhausted the first two options, and the third became the work of the next statute.

## Rebuilding the Subsidy in the Open

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 chose the third option. Section 254 converted the hidden transfer of the monopoly era into explicit contributions and disbursements, collected under public rules, administered by a designated administrator, and paid out through defined programs for high cost areas, low income households, schools and libraries, and rural health care. The conversion changed the politics of the subsidy as much as its mechanics. A transfer buried in relative prices had been nearly invisible to the customers who funded it. A contribution system with a calculated factor, quarterly adjustments, and carrier recovery of the charge through customer bills made the cost of universal service a stated figure that contributors could dispute, legislators could cite, and beneficiaries could be required to justify.

The Federal Communications Commission implemented the statutory design in the Universal Service Order of 1997, cited as Order 97-157, which established the contribution methodology, created the administrative structure that produced the Universal Service Administrative Company, and set the initial rules for the expanded programs, including the new support for schools, libraries, and rural health care. The order had to resolve questions the statute left open: which revenues would bear contributions, which carriers and services qualified for support, how discounts would be calculated, and how the expanded commitments would fit within a contribution burden that the economy would sustain. Each answer created winners and losers in identifiable amounts, which guaranteed that implementation would be contested in a way the internal settlements of a monopoly never were.

The funding fights followed predictably from the visibility. Carriers disputed the revenue base and the treatment of particular services, beneficiaries pressed for higher caps and broader eligibility, and contributors objected when the contribution factor rose with program demand. Because the factor appeared in public orders and, through carrier billing practices, on the bills customers received, universal service acquired a price tag that opponents could campaign against and supporters had to defend on its merits. A reader tracing the whole statutory design from principles to programs can follow the full survey of the statute in [a guide to the Telecommunications Act of 1996](/2017/07/01/telecommunications-act-1996-guide/), which places section 254 among the competition and licensing provisions it was written to coexist with.

Visibility also brought discipline that the hidden system lacked. Explicit disbursements can be audited, capped, appealed, and recovered when improperly paid, and explicit contributions can be measured against the revenue base that bears them. Program performance can be debated with figures in the open: how much support reached high cost areas, what discounts accomplished for school connectivity, whether Lifeline enrollment matched the eligible population. The older system asked subscribers to trust that internal prices served the public interest. The rebuilt system publishes its transfers and accepts the argument that follows.

The settlement reached in 1996 and 1997 has therefore endured as a structure even while every element inside it remains contested. The contribution factor still translates program demand into a charge on a defined revenue base, the administrator still collects and disburses under commission oversight, and the four programs still express the same judgment that monopoly era prices expressed silently: connection to the common network is treated as a public obligation, and the cost of extending it to expensive places, poor households, and anchor institutions is shared across the system that benefits from universal reach. The difference is that the sharing is written down, billed, and argued about in public, which is the condition competition made unavoidable and the statute made permanent.

## The Tax Question at the Center of Every Challenge

Every legal challenge to the Universal Service Fund eventually reaches the same threshold question, and the question is about a label rather than about a dollar amount. The charge that carriers pay into the fund, calculated each quarter through the contribution factor, either counts as a fee imposed under a regulatory program or counts as a tax levied by the government. The distinction sounds semantic, and in many areas of law a label chosen by Congress receives deference, but in the universal service context the label decides which body of constitutional doctrine governs the dispute, which institution holds the burden of justification, and which precedents supply the test. A dispute framed as a fee dispute becomes a dispute about agency authority under a statute. A dispute framed as a tax dispute becomes a dispute about the legislative power itself, because the Constitution assigns the taxing power to Congress in Article I, and any transfer of that power demands a separate and stricter justification.

The fee side of the line rests on a familiar structure. A fee, in the classic formulation, funds a specific benefit or a specific regulatory program, bears a reasonable relationship to the cost of that program, and falls on the class of parties connected to the activity being regulated. The universal service contribution fits that description on its face. Section 254 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 directs carriers to contribute on an equitable and nondiscriminatory basis to the preservation and advancement of universal service, the money flows into defined programs rather than into general revenue, and the payors are the providers whose networks the programs support and extend. Under that reading, the Federal Communications Commission acts as an administrator filling in a statutory scheme, and the ordinary rules of administrative law apply, including the rule that an agency needs an intelligible principle to guide its discretion rather than a blank instruction to raise money.

The tax side of the line rests on the destination and the compulsion of the payment. A tax raises revenue for public purposes under legal compulsion, and the universal service contribution also fits parts of that description. Payment is mandatory rather than voluntary, carriers pass the cost through to subscribers as a line item on monthly bills, and the funded purposes, connectivity for rural households, schools, libraries and rural health care facilities, serve the general public rather than only the carriers who pay. The amount collected is large, with fund disbursements of about 9 billion dollars a year, a scale that resembles a revenue measure more than a filing charge or a licence payment. Critics therefore argue that calling the payment a contribution does not change its economic character, and that a court should classify the exaction by its operation rather than by its statutory name.

### Is the universal service contribution a tax, and why does the label matter?

The contribution functions as a regulatory fee when it funds a specific program under congressional direction and as a tax when it raises general revenue without such direction. The label matters because a tax triggers rules about who may impose it, while a fee follows delegation rules for agencies.

The classification then controls the delegation analysis that follows from it. If the charge is a fee, the question is whether Congress gave the Commission enough guidance to administer a program, a test that agencies satisfy in most litigated cases. If the charge is a tax, the question hardens, because several judges and commentators treat the taxing power as a core legislative function that Congress may share only within narrow limits, if at all. The same dollars, the same quarterly factor and the same administrator therefore produce two different constitutional cases depending on the first classification step. Litigants understand the leverage in that step, and briefing in the universal service cases devoted substantial attention to the label before reaching any question about the Universal Service Administrative Company or about the adequacy of section 254 as guidance.

The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc in 2024, adopted the tax characterization and described the contribution in paraphrase as a levy raised from the public without the discipline that attends an appropriation or a statute setting a rate. That characterization did not by itself invalidate the fund, but the characterization selected the doctrinal path, and along that path the court found the delegation defective. Other circuits, addressing the same challenge from the same organization, treated the charge as a fee tied to a defined program and reached the opposite result under the more permissive administrative framework. The disagreement among the circuits was therefore not only about how much guidance section 254 contains. The disagreement began one step earlier, at classification, and the classification step explains why courts reading identical statutory text arrived at incompatible judgments about a single national program. The Supreme Court granted review against that divided background, and the tax question remained embedded inside the delegation question throughout the argument and the decision that followed in 2025.

## Delegation to an Agency: The Consumers Research Challenge

The nondelegation doctrine holds that Congress, as the branch vested with legislative power, may not hand that power to another institution without supplying a standard that confines the recipient. The doctrine has deep roots in the constitutional structure and in early republic practice, yet the Supreme Court has invalidated statutes on nondelegation grounds in only a small number of cases, concentrated in 1935. The governing test for the modern era asks whether Congress supplied an intelligible principle to guide the delegee. A statute satisfies the test when a court can tell whether the agency acted within the direction Congress gave, even when the direction is broad. Statutes phrased in terms of public interest, fair administration or necessary protection have survived under the test, because the Court has treated the surrounding statutory context, the history of the program and the defined subject matter as part of the principle itself.

Section 254 presents the test in a concrete form. The statute names the services that count as universal service through evolving principles, directs that rates in rural and high cost areas remain reasonably comparable to rates in urban areas, requires specific, predictable and sufficient federal and state mechanisms to preserve and advance universal service, and instructs that every telecommunications carrier providing interstate service contribute on an equitable and nondiscriminatory basis. Supporters of the fund read those provisions together as a principle with boundaries: the Commission may support defined services, for defined places and institutions, through contributions drawn from a defined class. Opponents read the same provisions as aspirations without a ceiling, because the statute sets no maximum rate for the contribution factor, no maximum size for the fund and no fixed formula for balancing the principles against each other. The litigation therefore turned on how much specificity the intelligible principle standard demands when the delegated decision involves raising money.

Consumers Research, an organization that challenges regulatory programs on structural constitutional grounds, carried the nondelegation argument into several courts of appeals. Petitions in the Fifth Circuit, the Sixth Circuit and the Eleventh Circuit pressed materially similar claims, and the circuits divided. The Sixth Circuit and the Eleventh Circuit upheld the fund, reasoning that section 254 supplies guidance comparable to guidance sustained in earlier delegation cases. The Fifth Circuit moved in stages, first through a panel and then through the full court. The Fifth Circuit en banc ruling of July 24, 2024 held the scheme unconstitutional, combining the tax characterization with a demanding view of delegation and with a separate objection to the role of a private administrator. The split among the circuits made Supreme Court review the ordinary next step, and the Court took the case under the name FCC v. Consumers Research.

The Supreme Court decision in FCC v. Consumers Research, decided June 27, 2025, by 6 to 3, upheld the fund against the nondelegation challenge. Justice Elena Kagan wrote for the majority and held that section 254 supplies an intelligible principle, because the statute identifies the beneficiaries, the services and the qualities that supported service must have, and because the contribution duty falls within boundaries that a reviewing court can police. Justice Elena Kagan treated the amount question through the same lens, reasoning that a principle need not fix a numerical cap when the statute ties support to the sufficiency required for defined services at reasonably comparable rates. Justice Neil Gorsuch dissented, joined by two colleagues, on the ground that the scheme transfers the taxing power to an agency and then in practice to a private company, a transfer that Justice Neil Gorsuch regarded as the kind of legislative surrender the nondelegation doctrine exists to prevent. Justice Neil Gorsuch would have demanded a far more specific congressional decision about how much money universal service may take and from whom.

The division between the majority and the dissent was therefore a division about the function of the intelligible principle standard itself. For Justice Elena Kagan, the standard asks whether Congress made the fundamental policy choices and left the agency to fill in details within a bounded field. For Justice Neil Gorsuch, the standard fails its purpose when the details left to the agency include the effective rate of a nationwide levy. Both positions claimed support in precedent, and both acknowledged that the Court had not struck down a statute on this ground for ninety years. The holding settled the immediate question for universal service: delegation from Congress to the Commission, on the terms section 254 provides, is constitutionally permissible. The holding left other questions about the administrator untouched, because a ruling that Congress may delegate says nothing about whether the particular administrative vehicle Congress and the Commission used was itself created in the manner the law requires.

## Delegation to a Private Company: The Subdelegation Argument

A second delegation objection operates one level below the first. Even if Congress may delegate authority to the Federal Communications Commission, the argument runs, the Commission may not pass that authority onward to a private party, and the Universal Service Administrative Company is a private nonprofit corporation rather than a government agency. The objection matters because USAC performs functions that sit close to the center of the funding mechanism. USAC collects revenue data from carriers, projects program demand, calculates the quarterly numbers from which the contribution factor emerges and administers disbursements under the four programs. If USAC in substance sets the factor, then a private entity exercises a power that the Constitution reserves to accountable public institutions, and the defect would persist even after a court sustained the delegation from Congress to the Commission. Lawyers call that defect private nondelegation or subdelegation, and the doctrine draws support from cases invalidating arrangements in which private parties wrote binding rules for competitors.

The factual dispute concerned the real relationship between projection and decision. The contribution factor follows arithmetically from two inputs: the amount the programs require and the base of assessable revenues. USAC assembles both inputs, and the Commission formally approves the factor each quarter. Challengers characterized the approval as routine acceptance of a number built elsewhere, pointing to the volume of the filings and to the expertise asymmetry between a specialized administrator and a generalist agency. Defenders characterized the same sequence as ordinary administrative assistance, pointing to the authority the Commission retains to revise projections, to direct changes in program administration and to replace the administrator altogether. The legal question was therefore functional rather than formal: subordination in documents counts for little if subordination in practice has dissolved, and independence in practice counts for little if the governing documents reserve final decision to the agency.

The Supreme Court answered the subdelegation argument in 2025 as part of FCC v. Consumers Research. The Court held that USAC remains subordinate to the Commission, which approves the projections and the factor, reviews the budgets and retains final authority over every element that determines how much carriers pay and how much beneficiaries receive. On that understanding, USAC performs administrative and advisory work under supervision, comparable to a contractor that gathers data and prepares recommendations, while the legally binding decisions remain the decisions of the Commission. The majority treated the retention of final approval as decisive rather than incidental, because subordination doctrine asks who holds the last word, and the record showed the Commission exercising review rather than surrendering the function. The dissent treated the same record as evidence that formal approval can mask practical transfer, a disagreement about institutional reality rather than about the governing rule.

The older precedent that structures the analysis is Sunshine Anthracite Coal Company v. Adkins, decided in 1940. In that case the Court sustained an arrangement in which private coal boards proposed prices and a public commission held authority to approve, disapprove or modify the proposals before the proposals acquired legal force. The mechanism is the enduring part of the precedent. Private participation survives constitutional scrutiny when a public authority with power to change the outcome examines the private recommendation and makes the operative decision, and private participation fails when the private recommendation binds regulated parties without that intervening public judgment. Carter v. Carter Coal Company, decided in 1936, marks the opposite pole, where a statute let one set of private producers impose terms on another set without adequate public control. The universal service arrangement was tested against the Adkins mechanism in 2025 and was placed on the Adkins side of the line.

The subdelegation holding has a practical consequence beyond the fund itself. Modern administration relies heavily on specialized entities that process applications, manage data and model costs, in fields from health payment to spectrum management. A ruling that functional assistance becomes unconstitutional whenever the assisting entity assembles the numbers would unsettle arrangements across the government, while a ruling that any formal approval suffices would invite agencies to hollow out their own responsibility. The 2025 decision chose a middle position tied to retained and exercised authority. That position closed the private delegation attack on the fund as the fund stood in 2025. The position did not address, and could not have addressed, a different kind of objection raised the following year, an objection not about who exercises power but about whether the corporate vehicle through which the power runs was ever lawfully brought into existence.

## The Government Corporation Problem

A government corporation occupies an unusual place in federal law. The form offers businesslike flexibility, separate accounts, a board and the capacity to contract and hire outside some ordinary agency constraints, and the form also creates risks that Congress moved to control after the expansion of corporate instrumentalities during the first half of the twentieth century. The Government Corporation Control Act of 1945, codified at 31 U.S.C. sections 9101 to 9110, supplies the controlling discipline. The Act lists the corporations subject to its regime, imposes budget, audit and reporting requirements on government corporations, and provides that an agency may establish or acquire a corporation only under specific congressional authorization, either through a law that names the corporation or through a law that authorizes the specific type of corporation involved. The premise is straightforward: an entity that spends public money in corporate form should exist because Congress decided the entity should exist, not because an agency found the corporate form convenient.

The Universal Service Administrative Company was created through a different route. The Federal Communications Commission, acting under the general direction of section 254 and its rulemaking authority, directed the National Exchange Carrier Association to establish an independent subsidiary, and USAC emerged from that regulatory process in the late nineteen nineties as a private nonprofit corporation organized under Delaware law. The Commission designated USAC as the administrator of the universal service support mechanisms, first on an interim basis and then on a permanent basis, and USAC built the staff, systems and procedures through which the four programs operate. No statute names USAC. No statute, according to the challenge that matured in 2026, specifically authorizes the Commission to establish a corporation to administer universal service. For supporters of the arrangement, the absence of a naming statute is unremarkable, because agencies routinely use contractors and nonprofit administrators under general statutory authority. For challengers, the absence is the entire case, because USAC is not an ordinary contractor hired for a defined task but a permanent corporation created at agency direction to run a program disbursing about 9 billion dollars a year.

A panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit addressed the question in a ruling of October 15, 2026. Judge Edith Brown Clement wrote for the panel and held that Congress never specifically authorized the Federal Communications Commission to establish USAC, as the Government Corporation Control Act requires. Judge Edith Brown Clement treated USAC as a government corporation for purposes of the Act, emphasizing the circumstances of creation, the governmental function the corporation performs and the continuing control the Commission exercises over the corporation. The order, by its terms, does not take effect for at least two months from the date of the ruling. The Federal Communications Commission is considering its options in response to the ruling. No further step by the Commission is stated here, and the legal position during the interval is governed by the terms of the order itself.

The analytical distinction between the 2026 holding and the litigation that preceded the holding deserves emphasis, because the two lines of attack are easy to conflate and the conflation misstates what each decision decided. The nondelegation and subdelegation cases asked who may wield power that Congress conferred: whether Congress gave enough guidance to the Commission, and whether the Commission kept enough control over USAC to satisfy subordination doctrine. The Supreme Court answered both questions in 2025 in a manner that sustained the fund. The Government Corporation Control Act question asks a prior and separate question: whether the administrator itself was lawfully created. A corporation can be subordinate to an agency in every operational respect and still lack the statutory authorization that the 1945 Act demands for its existence. Conversely, a corporation authorized in the manner the Act requires would still need to operate under constitutional delegation limits. The 2025 decision settled who may wield delegated power under section 254. The 2025 decision did not settle whether the administrator was lawfully created under a statute enacted fifty years before section 254, and the 2026 panel ruling addresses that separate defect on separate statutory ground, with separate remedial questions about administration during any transition.

The remedial dimension explains the delayed effect written into the order. Universal service disbursements flow on monthly and quarterly cycles to carriers, schools, libraries and health care providers that structure budgets around expected support. An order displacing the administrator without an interval for substitution or cure would interrupt those flows regardless of the merits of the legal holding. A delayed effective date gives the Commission, Congress and any successor administrator a window in which a lawful structure could be put in place, whether through legislation that supplies the missing authorization, through reassignment of functions inside the Commission or through designation of a different administrative vehicle. The existence of the window is a fact stated in the order. The use made of the window belongs to institutions whose decisions are not recorded here, and no outcome for that process is asserted.

## The Complication: A Funding Base That Shrinks as the Mission Grows

The strongest argument against the thesis that explicitness strengthened the fund begins with arithmetic. Making the transfer visible also made the transfer depend on a defined revenue base, and the defined base has contracted for structural reasons that have little connection to the size of the mission. Contributions rest on interstate and international telecommunications service revenues reported by carriers. Households and businesses, meanwhile, have shifted spending toward broadband connections, bundled packages and applications that ride over connections, categories that sit outside the assessable base or enter the base only in part. Each dollar of communications spending that migrates from assessable telephone revenue to non-assessable categories leaves the remaining base smaller. The quarterly contribution factor, which divides program requirements by the assessable base, therefore climbs even in periods when program budgets hold flat. A factor of 42 percent for the fourth quarter of 2026 expresses that relationship in a single number: the rate looks extreme in part because the denominator has shrunk, not only because the numerator has grown.

The spread of obligations that the shrinking base must carry appears in the four programs table from section 11, where High Cost support for networks, Lifeline support for households, E-Rate support for schools and libraries and Rural Health Care support for clinics draw on one contribution stream. A base tied to one generation of service is financing commitments that expand with the next generation of service, because supported networks are increasingly broadband networks and supported institutions connect at broadband speeds. The mismatch is architectural rather than managerial. Careful administration, rigorous audits and honest accounting cannot cure a denominator problem, because the denominator is fixed by statutory and regulatory classification choices made for other purposes. The fund can be perfectly administered and still grow more expensive per assessed dollar with every year of migration from telephone service toward broadband service, and the visibility that once counted as a virtue makes the rising percentage impossible to miss on the bills that subscribers receive.

Economic criticism of the structure has a long pedigree. Jerry A. Hausman, in Taxation by Telecommunications Regulation, published in 1998, argued that the levy works as a distortionary excise tax on telecommunications services, raising the price of the taxed service, suppressing demand for the taxed service and shifting consumption in ways that impose efficiency losses beyond the revenue raised. Jerry A. Hausman treated the contribution as a tax in economic substance regardless of the legal label, and Jerry A. Hausman connected the design to a broader pattern in which regulators accomplish through surcharges what legislatures would face resistance accomplishing through explicit taxation. The argument gains force as the factor rises, because a small surcharge on a large base distorts modestly, while a rate above two fifths applied to a narrow base distorts heavily at the margin, rewarding substitution toward untaxed alternatives and thereby shrinking the base further in a self-reinforcing cycle.

A second objection concerns distribution. Robert W. Crandall and Leonard Waverman, in Who Pays for Universal Service?, published by Brookings Institution Press in 2000, examined the incidence of universal service charges and argued that a levy on telephone bills falls regressively, because communications service absorbs a larger share of income for low income households than for high income households. The objection has an uncomfortable edge for a program family that includes Lifeline, a benefit aimed at affordability for low income subscribers. One part of the system taxes a bill that low income households struggle to pay in order to finance, among other purposes, a discount on a bill for qualifying households, with administrative cost and leakage along the path. General taxation, drawn from an income tax base with progressive rates, would distribute the burden differently, and the comparison sharpens the question of why a redistributive program rests on a narrow consumption levy at all. The contrast with [direct support paid to households from general revenue](/2026/10/03/how-government-cash-payments-work/) makes the design choice plain: universal service chose carrier-side collection and carrier-side subsidy, with the strengths and the distortions that follow from that choice.

The thesis survives the complication in a narrowed form. Visibility did produce auditability, because a defined contribution, a defined administrator and defined programs generate records that inspectors general, the Government Accountability Office and reviewing courts can examine, and the litigation history demonstrates that reviewability in action. Visibility also produced a legislative record and a judicial record that hidden rate averaging never generated. Where the thesis narrows is on sustainability. Explicitness solved a legitimacy problem and a measurement problem, and explicitness did not solve a base problem, because the base was defined by a service classification that technology was already overtaking. The fund is more lawful, more examinable and more honestly priced than the system that preceded the fund, and the fund rests on financing ground that contracts beneath the structure. Both propositions hold at once, and any reform discussion that denies either proposition misdescribes the position.

## Broadband and the Boundary of the Contributing Base

The boundary of the contribution base follows a classification line drawn for regulatory purposes. Under the statutory framework, telecommunications service means the offering of telecommunications for a fee directly to the public, while an information service means an offering for the retrieval, processing or use of information via telecommunications. The Federal Communications Commission classified broadband internet access service as an information service rather than a telecommunications service, a classification sustained through litigation and retained through successive administrations with variations in the accompanying regulatory regime. The classification carries many consequences, and the consequence for universal service finance is direct: revenues from a service classified as an information service sit outside the base of interstate telecommunications revenues from which contributions are drawn. Broadband, the connectivity that universal service money increasingly funds, therefore does not pay into the fund through the mechanism that telephone service uses.

The resulting asymmetry shapes carrier economics and consumer bills alike. A provider selling legacy telephone service contributes on the interstate portion of that revenue and commonly passes the charge through as a visible line item. A provider selling broadband access, often the same provider selling over the same physical network to the same household, collects broadband revenue outside the contribution system. Bundled offerings force allocation judgments between assessable and non-assessable components, and the judgments invite dispute, audit and strategic pricing of bundle elements. The line also distorts competition between technologies that deliver functionally similar connectivity, because the regulatory classification of the offering, rather than the use the subscriber makes of the connection, determines whether the revenue carries a contribution burden above two fifths at the margin. Economists across the policy spectrum have criticized the asymmetry on efficiency grounds, and defenders of the boundary have answered on statutory grounds, arguing that the Commission lacks authority to assess a service that Congress placed outside the telecommunications category or that reclassification would carry regulatory consequences far beyond fund finance.

The Commission examined the boundary in the Report on the Future of the Universal Service Fund, issued in 2022 after Congress directed a study of the fund in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act. The report canvassed base broadening among the principal reform options, describing paths that would assess broadband revenues, paths that would assess other categories of providers and paths that would move support toward direct appropriations. The report laid out advantages and disadvantages for each path without settling the policy, and the report has since served as the common reference point for legislative proposals, because the report assembles the revenue estimates, the legal constraints and the administrative questions in one place. The report also documented the trajectory that motivates reform discussion: a continuing decline in assessable revenues paired with support obligations oriented toward broadband deployment and adoption.

The appropriated alternative is not hypothetical, because Congress has financed broadband construction at large scale through appropriations in the same period. The Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment program, known as BEAD, carries an appropriation of 42.45 billion dollars for grants to states and territories for broadband deployment and related purposes. BEAD operates through the National Telecommunications and Information Administration rather than through USAC, disburses grant money appropriated in advance rather than contributions collected quarterly, and answers to appropriations oversight rather than to the contribution factor. The coexistence of BEAD and the fund demonstrates that two financing philosophies can run side by side toward overlapping goals: a standing, carrier-financed, formula-driven fund for recurring support, and a time-limited, appropriation-financed grant program for capital construction. The comparison clarifies the trade at the heart of base reform. Broadening the contribution base preserves the standing fund model and spreads the cost across broadband bills. Relying on appropriations preserves broadband bills from the charge and subjects support to the annual and supplemental decisions of Congress. The classification boundary is therefore not a technical footnote to the fund. The boundary decides which households and which services pay for the national commitment to comparable connectivity, and every reform design in the next section is, at bottom, a proposal to move that boundary or to abandon the boundary as the financing instrument.

## Reform Designs and What Each One Trades Away

Reform discussion organizes around four families of designs, and each family trades one defect of the present structure for a different defect that the present structure avoids. The first family broadens the contribution base to include broadband revenues. The arithmetic appeal is immediate, because the base would include the dominant and growing category of communications spending rather than only the declining category. The legal path is contested, because assessment requires either a reclassification of broadband service or a congressional amendment supplying authority to assess information service revenues. The distributional effect is also double edged. Spreading the cost across broadband bills lowers the rate on telephone bills and aligns payment with the service being supported, while placing a new charge on the very service whose adoption universal service policy seeks to encourage, including for the low income households that adoption programs target.

### How would adding broadband revenue to the contribution base change the arithmetic?

Adding broadband revenue would enlarge the denominator against which quarterly needs are divided, so the same program total would produce a lower percentage rate. Providers of broadband service would enter the contribution system, and the cost would spread across a broader set of bills instead of resting only on telephone revenues.

The second family assesses edge providers, the large platforms and application companies whose traffic and business models depend on universal connectivity. Supporters argue that the beneficiaries of a universally connected public include the firms that sell services to that public at scale, and that a contribution drawn from digital advertising or platform revenues would rest on a broad and growing base. Opponents answer that the connection between an edge provider and the cost of rural networks is attenuated, that defining the assessable class invites boundary disputes as severe as the broadband boundary dispute, that pass-through would reach consumers through prices in any event, and that a charge aimed at a small number of very large firms functions as a targeted tax designed outside the appropriations process. Administration would also require a new reporting apparatus for companies that have never filed carrier revenue forms, and the statutory authority for assessing non-carriers under section 254 is doubtful without amendment, because the contribution duty in the statute attaches to carriers providing interstate telecommunications services and, permissively, to other providers of interstate telecommunications.

The third family funds universal service from general appropriations. The design has clarity in its favor: Congress would decide the size of the commitment in the open, through the budget process, paid from the general tax base, with annual oversight and no distortionary surcharge on a single service category. The design also carries the risk that defines appropriations, and the Affordable Connectivity Program supplies the standing example. That program, funded at 14.2 billion dollars, paid a monthly broadband discount for eligible households and ended on June 1, 2024 when the appropriation ran out without renewal. Enrollment had reached a scale larger than Lifeline enrollment, providers had built offerings around the benefit, households had organized budgets around the discount, and the benefit still terminated on the exhaustion of a fixed sum. A universal service system converted to appropriations would gain budgetary honesty and lose the continuity that a standing contribution mechanism provides across election cycles and budget disputes. Carriers deciding whether to build networks with twenty year payback periods in high cost territory price that continuity risk into investment decisions.

The fourth family restructures the programs while leaving finance largely intact: consolidating overlapping benefits, retargeting High Cost support as BEAD-funded construction completes networks, converting portions of support into direct household benefits, or tightening eligibility and accountability rules across the four programs. Restructuring trades comprehensiveness for efficiency, and restructuring alone cannot answer the denominator problem, because even a leaner set of programs divided by a shrinking base produces a rising factor over time. The realistic legislative packages therefore combine families, pairing base broadening with program changes or pairing appropriations for new construction with contribution finance for recurring operation. Every combination redistributes cost among telephone subscribers, broadband subscribers, platform customers and general taxpayers, and the choice among combinations is a distributive and institutional choice dressed as a technical one. The fund at about 9 billion dollars a year is small beside the federal budget and large beside the bills of the subscribers who finance the fund directly, and that dual scale explains why reform is simultaneously easy to postpone and hard to ignore.

## What Is Settled and What Remains Open

Settlement in a litigated system is always provisional, and still the universal service disputes have produced a clear division between questions that carry authoritative answers and questions that remain open for Congress, the Commission or further litigation. On the settled side, the delegation questions stand answered after 2025. FCC v. Consumers Research established that section 254 supplies an intelligible principle for the delegation to the Federal Communications Commission, and the same decision established that the Universal Service Administrative Company, subordinate to the Commission and subject to Commission approval of projections and of the contribution factor, does not exercise unlawfully delegated private power. Those holdings rest on a majority of the Supreme Court, bind lower courts and govern the fund unless Congress changes the statute or a later Court revisits the doctrine across administrative law generally. Litigants may continue to test the edges of the holdings, and the holdings themselves are part of the settled structure.

The statutory elements also stand settled as a matter of text and long administration. Section 254 defines the principles of universal service, names quality services at just, reasonable and affordable rates as the statutory goal, requires reasonably comparable service and rates between rural and urban areas, assigns contribution duties to carriers on an equitable and nondiscriminatory basis and creates the Federal-State Joint Board mechanism through which the Commission receives recommendations on definitional questions. The program structures built on that text are likewise settled in operational terms. High Cost distributes support to eligible carriers serving expensive territory, Lifeline discounts service for qualifying low income households, E-Rate discounts connectivity for schools and libraries according to a matrix tied to poverty and location, and Rural Health Care supports connectivity for eligible providers. Rules within each program change through ordinary rulemaking, and the existence and basic architecture of the four programs have survived administrations of both parties and decades of audit criticism.

The open side of the ledger begins with the administrator. The Fifth Circuit panel ruling of October 15, 2026 held that Congress never specifically authorized the Commission to establish USAC under the Government Corporation Control Act, and the order does not take effect for at least two months from that date. The corporate status of the administrator, the validity of past administration under an unauthorized vehicle and the lawful path to a compliant administrative structure are open questions governed by the order, by any further appellate proceedings and by any legislative response. The contribution base question also remains open. No authoritative decision has brought broadband revenues into the base, no statute has substituted appropriations for contributions as the general financing method, and the factor of 42 percent for the fourth quarter of 2026 illustrates the arithmetic that keeps the question active. The evolving definition of supported services remains open in a different sense, because section 254 builds evolution into the statute through periodic Joint Board review, and the line between a supported telecommunications service and an unsupported application continues to move with technology and with Commission judgment exercised under the statute.

The division between settled and open questions maps onto a division between judicial and legislative competence. Courts settled the constitutional delegation questions, because those questions concern the validity of a statute and an administrative structure under higher law. Courts are poorly placed to choose a contribution base, because base selection requires revenue estimates, distributive judgments and transition rules that belong to a legislature or to an agency acting under fresh legislative direction. The Government Corporation Control Act question sits between the two domains, arising under a statute and therefore fit for judicial decision, yet curable by a short statute if Congress prefers the administrator Congress never specifically authorized. A reader surveying the field in the aftermath of the 2026 ruling therefore faces a fund whose constitutional foundation has been sustained by the highest court, whose programs operate under settled rules, and whose administrator and financing base await decisions from institutions that act on political rather than judicial calendars. No timetable for those decisions is offered here, and no outcome for those decisions is asserted.

## Why a Levy on Telephone Bills Still Carries a National Network

A hidden transfer became a public machine. The sentence restates, in compressed form, the entire history that the preceding sections traced from monopoly rate averaging through the statute of 1996 to the litigation of the present decade. Under the older order, support for rural service and for affordable rates moved invisibly inside a rate structure that a regulated monopolist administered and that subscribers could not decompose into parts. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 and the administrative structure built under the Act converted that invisible redistribution into named programs, a named administrator, a quarterly percentage published for public inspection and a body of rules and audits and court decisions that any citizen can examine. The conversion did not make the transfer smaller, and the conversion did not make the transfer painless, as a contribution factor of 42 percent for the fourth quarter of 2026 attests. The conversion made the transfer legible, and legibility changed the politics, the economics and the law of universal service at once.

Legibility carries a price in the literal sense and in the institutional sense. The literal price appears as a line item that subscribers can resent in a way that subscribers never resented an averaged rate, and resentment fuels the recurring political pressure to cap, shrink or reassign the charge. The institutional price appears as exposure: a public machine can be sued over the guidance in its statute, over the private character of its administrator and over the corporate authorization for that administrator, and the fund has been sued on each ground in turn. Hidden systems enjoy a kind of protection through obscurity, because a transfer that no observer can isolate presents no target for a doctrinal challenge. The fund surrendered that protection in exchange for accountability, and the exchange looks correct even after a decade of litigation, because the accountability mechanisms worked as accountability mechanisms are supposed to work. Audits found waste and audits forced repayment and reform. Courts tested the structure and courts sustained the constitutional core of the structure while identifying, in the most recent ruling, a statutory defect in the creation of the administrator that Congress has power to cure.

The one point worth carrying away from the whole account concerns the relationship between visibility and durability in public programs. A subsidy that citizens can see can be attacked, and a subsidy that citizens can see can also be defended on evidence, measured against stated principles and repaired when a court or an auditor finds a flaw. The universal service principles in section 254, reasonably comparable rates, specific and predictable and sufficient support, equitable and nondiscriminatory contribution, function as public criteria against which the machine is judged, and the criteria exist because Congress wrote the machine into law instead of leaving redistribution inside a tariff. The fund disbursing about 9 billion dollars a year is therefore best understood not as an anomaly of telephone regulation but as an early and unusually complete example of a general form: an explicit, administered, judicially reviewable transfer, financed by a visible levy on the industry being supported.

The structural implication reaches beyond telecommunications. Every mature network industry confronts the same distributive problem, because networks cost far more per user in sparse territory than in dense territory, while the social case for comparable access strengthens as the network becomes essential. Legislatures answering that problem choose, in effect, among three instruments: hidden averaging inside regulated prices, an explicit fund financed by a levy on the industry, or direct payment from general revenue subject to appropriations. The United States tried the first instrument for most of the twentieth century, built the second instrument in 1996 and has tested the third instrument at the edges through programs such as BEAD at 42.45 billion dollars and the Affordable Connectivity Program at 14.2 billion dollars. The levy on telephone bills still carries a national network because the second instrument, for all the strain on the base beneath the levy, continues to offer the combination that the alternatives struggle to match: money that arrives with the regularity of a utility mechanism, directed by principles written in a statute, through an administrator whose acts generate a record that courts and auditors can test. Whether the base widens, the administrator changes form or Congress rewrites the financing chapter, the underlying commitment that the levy expresses, that access to the defining network of an era should not depend entirely on the cost of the ground between a household and the rest of the country, remains the commitment that each successive structure has been built to serve.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Universal Service Fund?

The Universal Service Fund is a federal support system created under section 254 of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 to keep communications service available and affordable across the United States. It collects contributions from telecommunications carriers, calculated as a percentage of their interstate revenues, and distributes the money through four programs that support rural networks, low income households, schools and libraries, and rural health care providers. A nonprofit administrator, the Universal Service Administrative Company, runs the collection and payment machinery under the direction of the Federal Communications Commission. The fund disburses about 9 billion dollars a year, and its purpose is to close the gap between what service costs to provide in expensive or poor areas and what subscribers there can reasonably be charged.

### Q: Where does the money in the Universal Service Fund come from?

The money comes from contributions owed by telecommunications carriers that provide interstate service, including long distance companies, wireless providers and interconnected voice over internet protocol providers. Each carrier's payment is its reported interstate end user revenue multiplied by a contribution factor that is recalculated every quarter. Carriers generally recover the cost from their customers, which is why a universal service charge appears as a line item on telephone bills even though the legal duty to contribute rests on the carrier. No annual appropriation supplies the fund, and the money does not pass through the general Treasury accounts that finance ordinary government operations. The system's financing is therefore a dedicated transfer inside the communications sector, from the broad base of subscribers toward the places and institutions the statute names.

### Q: What is the contribution factor and how is it set each quarter?

The contribution factor is the percentage rate that converts the fund's needs into carrier obligations for one quarter. The administrator projects how much the four programs will require, adds administrative costs and adjustments, and divides the total by the projected interstate end user revenues of contributing carriers. The Federal Communications Commission reviews and announces the resulting percentage, and each carrier then owes that share of its own reported interstate revenue. For the fourth quarter of 2026 the factor is 42 percent. The rate moves from quarter to quarter because both sides of the division move: program demand changes with applications and obligations, and the revenue base changes with the size of the interstate telecommunications market that carriers report on their quarterly and annual worksheets.

### Q: What is USAC and what does it do?

USAC is the Universal Service Administrative Company, an independent nonprofit corporation that the Federal Communications Commission created in 1997 to administer the Universal Service Fund. USAC collects carrier revenue data, prepares the projections behind the quarterly contribution factor, invoices contributors, and operates the application, commitment and payment processes for the four support programs through dedicated divisions. It also runs eligibility verification systems, conducts audits of carriers and beneficiaries, and recovers payments that violated program rules. USAC does not set policy. The Commission writes the rules, approves the factor, decides appeals from USAC decisions and oversees the administrator's budget and performance, an allocation of roles that became central when courts examined whether the administrator exercises power that belongs to the government.

### Q: What are the four universal service programs?

The fund operates four programs, each aimed at a different gap. The High Cost program supports carriers that build and operate networks in rural and remote areas where subscriber revenue cannot cover the cost of service. Lifeline provides a monthly discount that keeps telephone or broadband service affordable for qualifying low income households. The schools and libraries program, known as E-Rate, discounts telecommunications services, internet access and internal connections for eligible schools and libraries, with deeper discounts where poverty is greater. The Rural Health Care program supports the connections that rural clinics and hospital consortia use for their communications and health information exchange. Together the four divide the universal service mission by beneficiary: places, households, learning institutions and health providers.

### Q: Why do telephone bills show a universal service charge?

The charge appears because most carriers choose to recover their contribution cost directly from subscribers, and billing rules permit them to identify that recovery as a separate line item. The carrier owes the contribution whether or not it recovers the money, and the line item is the carrier's pricing decision rather than a government bill sent to the household. Presenting the cost separately made the universal service transfer visible to the public for the first time, since the earlier monopoly system had buried the same subsidy inside general telephone rates where no subscriber could see it. The amount varies with the interstate portion of a bill and with the quarterly contribution factor, which is why the charge changes even when a household's calling pattern stays the same.

### Q: What is the difference between the High Cost program and Lifeline?

The two programs attack different sides of the affordability problem. High Cost support goes to carriers for building and operating networks in areas where terrain, distance and sparse settlement make service expensive to supply, and it addresses the availability of a network at a comparable price. Lifeline goes, through carriers, to households whose incomes make even an ordinary price hard to pay, and it addresses the affordability of subscribing to a network that already exists. A rural household can benefit from both at once, since High Cost support may sustain the network reaching the household while a Lifeline discount of 9.25 dollars a month reduces the household's own bill. One program subsidizes the cost of supply; the other subsidizes the income of demand.

### Q: How does E-Rate decide how large a discount a school receives?

E-Rate sets each applicant's discount on a matrix that combines the poverty of the student population with the school's rural or urban character. Poverty is measured through eligibility for the national school lunch program, and the discount rises with that share, running from 20 percent for the least needy applicants to 90 percent for the poorest and for many rural applicants. The discount applies to eligible telecommunications services, internet access and internal connections, within an annual program ceiling that stood at 2.25 billion dollars when the program began and was raised to 3.9 billion dollars in 2014. Applicants request discounts through a competitive bidding process, file funding requests for chosen services, and receive commitments that convert to payments after services are delivered and invoiced.

### Q: Why is rural telephone and broadband service more expensive to provide?

Rural service costs more because the fixed cost of a network is spread across far fewer subscribers. A mile of cable, a tower or an exchange building costs roughly the same whether it serves five hundred households or five, and rural routes add distance, difficult terrain and long repair journeys to every element of construction and maintenance. Revenue per mile falls with settlement density while cost per mile does not, so the arithmetic that makes urban networks profitable inverts in the countryside. Harsh weather exposure and the need for backup power and redundant routes across empty country add further cost. Without support, a carrier facing that arithmetic must either charge rural subscribers several times the urban price, decline to build, or let an existing network decay.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about the fund in 2025?

In FCC v. Consumers' Research, decided on June 27, 2025, by 6 to 3, the Supreme Court upheld the fund's financing structure against a delegation challenge. Justice Elena Kagan, writing for the majority, held that section 254 gives the Federal Communications Commission an intelligible principle to guide the contribution system, so Congress had not handed away its legislative power. The Court also rejected the argument that the administrator, USAC, wields unlawfully delegated private power, reasoning that USAC works under the Commission's supervision, with the Commission approving the projections and the contribution factor. Justice Neil Gorsuch dissented, arguing that the scheme transfers the taxing power in substance. The decision left the fund's collection machinery intact while leaving other legal theories against it available for later cases.

### Q: What did the Fifth Circuit decide about USAC in 2026?

On October 15, 2026, a panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit held that the Federal Communications Commission had violated the Government Corporation Control Act when it created the Universal Service Administrative Company, because Congress never specifically authorized the Commission to establish the corporation. Judge Edith Brown Clement wrote the decision. The holding attacks a different point from the delegation cases the Supreme Court resolved in 2025: not whether the agency received or shared too much power, but whether the administrative corporation itself was lawfully brought into existence under a statute from 1945 that reserves the creation of government corporations to Congress. The order by its terms does not take effect for at least two months, and the Commission is considering its options.

### Q: Is the Universal Service Fund part of the federal budget?

The fund sits outside the ordinary budget in its financing, though it remains a federal program in law and oversight. Its money arrives from mandatory carrier contributions rather than from appropriations, and its disbursements do not compete in the annual budget process that funds departments and agencies. Congress therefore does not vote each year on how much universal service money exists; the quarterly contribution factor adjusts collections to program demand under the statute. The arrangement is deliberate, designed to give carriers and beneficiaries the predictability that multi-year network investment requires. It also explains the recurring political argument about the fund, since a spending stream of about 9 billion dollars a year operates with less annual congressional control than an appropriated program of the same size would face.

### Q: Why did universal service used to be hidden inside telephone rates?

Under the Bell System monopoly, universal service was financed by internal cross subsidy rather than by any separate fund. Regulators allowed long distance calls, business services and urban lines to be priced above their cost, and the surplus sustained residential and rural local service priced below cost. Because one company operated the whole system, the transfer could live inside its rate structure without any bill, account or public calculation identifying it. Subscribers paid for the subsidy without seeing it, and the size of the transfer was known to no one with precision. Competition destroyed the arrangement, since rival long distance carriers could undercut the inflated prices that carried the subsidy, draining the surplus. The 1996 Act replaced the hidden transfer with explicit contributions and payments.

### Q: What is an eligible telecommunications carrier?

An eligible telecommunications carrier is a carrier that a state commission, or the Federal Communications Commission where a state lacks authority, has designated to receive universal service support for a defined service area. Designation carries duties: the carrier must offer the supported services throughout its area, using its own facilities alone or combined with resale, must advertise the availability and charges of those services, and must use support only for the provision, maintenance and upgrading of the facilities and services the support targets. Only designated carriers may receive High Cost or Lifeline support, so designation is the gate between the fund and the companies that deliver service. A carrier that wishes to stop serving an area must seek permission to relinquish the designation rather than simply withdrawing.

### Q: What stops E-Rate money from being wasted?

E-Rate surrounds its discounts with procedural controls at every stage. Applicants must seek services through competitive bidding, document their selection of the most cost effective bids, certify compliance with program rules, and retain records for audit. The administrator reviews funding requests before committing money, checks invoices against commitments before paying, and conducts audits of beneficiaries and service providers, with payments recovered where rules were broken and appeals available to the Commission. The Federal Communications Commission's Office of Inspector General investigates fraud in the program, and enforcement actions have barred violators from participating. No control system removes waste entirely from a program distributing billions of dollars across tens of thousands of schools and libraries, and the program's history includes documented abuse; the controls exist to make abuse detectable, recoverable and punishable rather than invisible.

### Q: Do other countries run universal service funds?

Many countries operate universal service funds or obligations built on the same recognition that network economics leave rural and poor populations unserved at commercial prices. The designs differ in financing: some levy contributions on operators' revenues in the manner of the United States system, some fund service from general taxation, and some impose build-out obligations as conditions of licenses or spectrum awards without a central fund at all. International bodies including the International Telecommunication Union have documented recurring weaknesses in levy-based funds, including money collected faster than administrators can disburse it. The comparative record suggests the hard problem is shared across systems: defining the service that counts as universal, measuring the true cost of providing it in expensive territory, and disbursing support without rewarding inefficiency.

### Q: Why is the base of revenues that pay for the fund shrinking?

The contribution base consists of interstate telecommunications service revenues, a category written into law when voice telephony dominated the industry. Consumer and business spending has since moved toward broadband connections, bundled packages, messaging and applications, much of which is classified as information service revenue rather than telecommunications revenue and therefore sits outside the assessable base. Voice revenues themselves decline as flat rate and bundled plans replace per-minute interstate calling. The fund's obligations, meanwhile, increasingly concern broadband networks. Program needs divided by a shrinking base produce a rising contribution factor even in periods of flat program budgets, which is how the factor reached 42 percent for the fourth quarter of 2026. The mismatch between a twentieth century revenue category and a twenty-first century mission is structural rather than temporary.

### Q: What is the Federal-State Joint Board on Universal Service?

The Federal-State Joint Board on Universal Service is a body of federal and state regulators that section 254(a) of the Communications Act requires the Federal Communications Commission to convene. It includes Commission members and state utility commissioners, with representation for consumer advocates, and it issues recommended decisions on the definition of supported services and the design of the support mechanisms. Its 1996 recommended decision supplied the architecture that the Commission's 1997 implementing order largely adopted. The board exists because communications jurisdiction in the United States is divided: interstate service is federal, intrastate service belongs to the states, and a national support system built on only one side of that line would misprice the other. The board gives the states a formal voice inside federal universal service policy.

### Q: How does the fund treat broadband differently from telephone service?

Broadband occupies two different positions in the system depending on which side of the ledger is examined. On the spending side, broadband is central: the supported services have evolved toward broadband capability, and the High Cost and other programs direct money toward building and operating broadband networks in expensive areas. On the collection side, broadband revenue is largely absent, because broadband internet access has been classified as an information service rather than a telecommunications service, and the contribution duty in section 254 attaches to telecommunications carriers on their telecommunications revenues. The fund therefore pays for the broadband era out of the telephone era's revenue base. That asymmetry drives the rising contribution factor and most reform proposals, which differ mainly in how they would bring the collection side into line with the spending side.

### Q: What would happen if the fund were paid for from general taxation instead?

Financing universal service from general taxation would move the cost from telephone subscribers to the broader tax base, spreading it across income and other taxes rather than concentrating it on one sector's bills. Supporters of the change argue that a broad base would lower the rate needed, reduce the distortion that a heavy levy places on telecommunications prices, and put the program's size before Congress in the annual budget process. Critics answer that appropriated programs live at the mercy of budget politics, pointing to the Affordable Connectivity Program, funded at 14.2 billion dollars, which ended on June 1, 2024 when its money ran out. A contribution system trades that political exposure for stability, at the price of a narrower and more distortionary base, and the choice between the two is a judgment about which risk matters more.
