## Did the First Audiences Pity or Condemn the Suicides?

The suicides at the end of Romeo and Juliet pose a problem that the play's first audiences could not dodge. Every man and woman in the Theatre in the middle 1590s lived under a church that taught self-killing as a sin against God and under a law that treated the self-killer as a felon, confiscated the dead person's goods, and denied the corpse the burial rites of the parish church. Yet the play those audiences watched ends with two young people killing themselves on stage, in full view, in speeches of great beauty, and no voice in the fiction sentences them for it. The fathers who might have prosecuted their disobedience instead promise statues of gold. The Prince who might have opened the law's inquiry instead closes the play with a couplet about woe. Between the doctrine the audience carried through the door and the sympathy the play engineered inside it, a genuine historical question opens, and scholars have argued it from both sides for generations.

![The tomb in Verona where the suicides of the lovers close the tragedy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

The verdict defended across this article is that the first audiences pitied the lovers, and pitied them as the governing response, while the framework of condemnation remained present in the theatre as knowledge rather than as feeling. Condemnation was the official sentence, available in every pulpit and every coroner's court in England. Pity was the response the play was built to extract, and the period's own practice, in its inquest juries, its neighbourhoods, its schoolrooms and its playhouses, had already made room for that response long before Shakespeare exploited the room. The two responses were not evenly matched inside the playhouse. Doctrine supplied the words in which the deaths could be condemned; the play declined to use a single one of them, and it surrounded the bodies with gold, grief and reconciliation instead. An audience can hold a doctrine and still weep against it, and the argument here is that the evidence, weighed whole, shows an audience positioned to weep.

Three bodies of evidence decide the question. The first is the official record: what the church taught, what the law did, and what a coroner's jury was sworn to find. The second is the record of practice: what juries, neighbours and families actually did when a self-killing occurred in a parish, which often diverged sharply from the official script. The third is the play itself, read as a machine for producing a response: its Chorus, its dropped moralising, its final scene's conspicuous refusal to stage any of the verdicts that church and law had ready. Each body of evidence has its champion among scholars. Roland M. Frye, in Shakespeare and Christian Doctrine (1963), argued that the plays address audiences formed by Christian teaching and are properly judged inside that framework. Michael MacDonald and Terence R. Murphy, in Sleepless Souls: Suicide in Early Modern England (1990), rebuilt the question from the inquest records and found practice consistently softer than doctrine. Jill L. Levenson, editing the play for Oxford (2000), read the ending as a melancholy coda that qualifies the lovers' achievement without sentencing them. The disagreement among those positions is real, and it will be adjudicated, not merely surveyed, before the verdict is given.

The question stayed open for so long partly because its two sides were never arguing from the same archive. The doctrine is quotable: it sits in printed books, in catechisms and homilies and summae, and a scholar can cite it chapter and verse. The practice slept in county archives and coroners' rolls, in Latin verdicts and fiscal accounts, largely unread until the historical scholarship of the late twentieth century, above all MacDonald and Murphy's Sleepless Souls in 1990, reconstructed it in bulk. Criticism formed on the printed archive naturally concluded that the period condemned; the historians who opened the manuscript archive found a period busily declining to condemn. Much of the modern disagreement is that archival accident wearing the dress of interpretation, and one service an adjudication can perform is to say so. The play itself, the third archive, was never in dispute; only its weight in the scales was. The sections that follow give each archive its full hearing in turn, the doctrine at its most formidable, the practice at its most documented, the play at its most closely read, and then weigh them in that order, because that is the order in which they pressed upon a spectator: the teaching learned from childhood, the practice known from the parish, the fiction experienced for two hours in the yard.

One preliminary fact sharpens everything that follows. The first audiences had no word "suicide". The noun enters English only in the 1640s, devised by Sir Thomas Browne and printed in his Religio Medici of 1643, two generations after the play. The period's words were verdicts in themselves: self-murder, self-homicide, and the law Latin felo de se, a felon against himself. To ask what an Elizabethan thought of the lovers' deaths is therefore to ask what a culture thought when its only names for the act were accusations. And yet that same culture filled the yard of the Theatre to watch the act performed as the climax of a love story, and the earliest title page of the play boasts that it had been played "with great applause". The applause and the accusation coexisted. The accusation deserves one more moment's attention, because its vocabulary is the doctrine-first case in concentrated form. Self-murder names an agent and a crime in a single breath; felo de se names a verdict; even the period's rarer compounds keep the law or the butcher's yard inside the word. On the strictest version of the doctrine-first logic, a culture that could not name the act without convicting it should have produced a uniformly convicting audience, and the applause on the title page stands as the anomaly that logic cannot digest. The anomaly is resolved by the practice record, which shows a culture convicting the act in language and acquitting the person in behaviour with a consistency that amounts to a second doctrine, unwritten and everywhere. Explaining how they could coexist is the work of the sections that follow.

A word about what an answer to the question can look like. The first audience was not a single mind. The yard held apprentices and citizens, the galleries held merchants, lawyers, gentry and the women of the city, and any reconstruction that assigns one feeling to all of them falsifies the gathering. The question worth asking is the narrower one the sources can bear: which response did the occasion, the culture and the play conspire to make governing, the response a spectator would carry out of the theatre as the settled account of what had been watched? Individuals no doubt dissented in both directions, then as in any audience since. But a playhouse response has a centre of gravity, set by the training the spectators shared, the frameworks their institutions had given them, and the engineering of the particular fiction in front of them. It is that centre of gravity the debate among scholars concerns, and it is recoverable, because each of its three determinants left records. The doctrine left sermons, catechisms, statutes and rubrics. The practice left inquest rolls, verdicts and the complaints of officials who found the practice too soft. The engineering is the play, which survives entire. The method of this article is to keep the three records separate until each has said its piece, and then to let them confront one another in the adjudication, where the disagreements among Frye, MacDonald and Murphy, and Levenson can be settled against evidence rather than temperament.

## What Did the Church Teach About Self-Murder?

The teaching was unambiguous, and it reached ordinary churchgoers through every channel the period possessed. The sixth commandment, "Thou shalt not kill", was expounded in catechising as a prohibition that covered the killing of the self: the prohibition protected the neighbour's life and the parishioner's own with the same words, since both lives belonged to God and neither was the holder's to surrender. Behind the catechism stood a thousand years of settled doctrine. Augustine, in the first book of The City of God, had confronted the honoured self-killings of pagan Rome and ruled that no private person held authority to kill, even the self, even to escape sin or suffering. Thomas Aquinas, in the Summa Theologiae, gave the prohibition its durable scholastic form, arguing the act against nature's self-love, against the community that loses a member, and against God, to whom alone the passage between life and death belongs. By the 1590s the teaching required no treatise to reach a playgoer. It was in the commandments read in church, in the sermons of the parish clergy, and in the common stock of proverb and prayer.

The sharpest point of the doctrine was not the killing but the despair that was presumed to drive it. Period theology treated despair, the settled conviction that God's mercy did not extend to the despairing soul, as the most dangerous of spiritual states, because it closed the door that repentance needed. Judas Iscariot stood in the pulpits as the type of the self-murderer: a man whose remorse, preachers insisted, was genuine but misdirected, who confessed his sin to the priests and then, instead of seeking mercy, hanged himself, and whose end therefore proved that sorrow without hope damns where sorrow with hope saves. Peter, who had also betrayed his master and wept and lived, was held up as the counter-type. A self-killing, read through this taxonomy, was a death in the very act of an unrepented mortal sin, committed at the one moment when repentance had become impossible, and the inference that the soul was lost followed with a logic that few parishioners would have needed spelled out. The self-murderer was presumed to have died as Judas died.

### What did Augustine argue against the honour of Lucretia's death?

Augustine argued that Lucretia's self-killing was either the punishment of an innocent woman by her own hand, which is murder, or the confession of a guilty conscience, which destroys the innocence the deed was meant to prove. Honour cannot be established by a crime, and Roman admiration mistook a further injury for a remedy.

The teaching reached the laity through channels more pervasive than the pulpit. The catechisms from which children learned their faith expounded the commandment as a protection laid over every human life, the neighbour's and the learner's own, the life being God's property held in stewardship rather than a possession to be surrendered at will, and the schoolroom drilled the exposition at the age of rote. Sermons on the commandments, a staple genre of the period's preaching, returned to self-murder as the extreme case that proved the commandment's reach, and the literature of dying well taught the deathbed as a battleground on which despair was the enemy's final weapon, to be fought with prayer and company rather than yielded to alone. The manner of a death, moreover, was common talk in every parish, and the categories available for discussing it were the catechism's: a sudden death was read for its signs, hopeful or despairing, the way weather was read for harvest. The formation was thorough, repeated and early. What the adjudication will have to weigh is not its existence, which no serious reading denies, but its competition, since the same minds were being furnished, by school texts and stage plays and parish practice, with a second and older set of responses that the doctrine never succeeded in evicting.

The church's discipline gave the doctrine a visible, local form. Christian burial was the community's final act of fellowship with a dead member, and the self-murderer was excluded from it. The exclusion was ancient canon law long before it was print: the burial office, the prayers at the grave, the tolling bell and the churchyard grave were all withheld from those who had laid violent hands on themselves, a refusal the later prayer-book rubric would codify in the words that the office "is not to be used for any that die unbaptized, or excommunicate, or have laid violent hands upon themselves". Every parishioner knew what an unhallowed burial looked like, because the alternative was staged at the parish boundary: the body carried out without clergy, without mourners, and laid in a highway or at a crossroads, in ground that belonged to no congregation. Doctrine, in other words, was not an abstraction for the play's first audiences. It had a geography, and the geography stood at the edge of every parish they lived in.

One further feature of the teaching belongs in the doctrine's own ledger, because the condemnation case is routinely overstated at exactly this point. The church pronounced on the act with total confidence and on the individual soul with a reserve its preachers themselves maintained. Sermons against self-murder thundered against the sin and then, characteristically, left the particular dead person to the secret judgment of God, since no observer could know what passed in a final moment, whether terror had given way to a repentance too late for witnesses but not for mercy. The reserve was not softness imported from outside the doctrine; it was the doctrine's own acknowledgment that judgment of souls was not the church's office. Its pastoral consequence was large. A parishioner formed by such preaching learned to abhor the category and to suspend judgment on the corpse, which is, to the letter, the posture the play's final scene occupies: the act uncondemned in words because uncommented, the persons mourned without reserve. The distance between the pulpit's full position and the play's position is considerably smaller than the distance between the pulpit's slogans and the play.

The teaching, however, contained a pastoral counterweight that the same churchgoers also absorbed, and any account that stops at the sentence misrepresents the doctrine's lived shape. The official Homily of Repentance, appointed to be read aloud in churches, warned hearers against despair precisely by treating it as a temptation to be resisted with comfort: the despairing parishioner was to be answered with the promises of mercy, not abandoned to the fear. William Perkins, the most widely read English casuist of the generation, built a large part of his cases of conscience around the troubled mind that believed itself damned, and his pastoral direction ran consistently toward reassurance: the very anguish of a conscience that feared reprobation was, in the casuists' handling, compatible with grace and often its companion. The divines who supplied the period's sternest doctrine of self-murder simultaneously maintained a ministry of comfort to the living who were tempted toward it, and the two teachings were heard by the same ears on successive Sundays. The result was a double formation. A churchgoer learned that the completed act was a horror and that the despairing person was an object of compassion and care. Nothing in that formation prevented the two responses from attaching to the same corpse at a play's end: horror at the category, compassion for the persons. The play, as the final-scene analysis will show, is built to separate exactly those two objects, condemning nothing while exhibiting the persons at maximum claim on compassion.

The Prayer Book itself drilled the double response at the bedside. The Order for the Visitation of the Sick, the office every parishioner heard read over the seriously ill, exhorted the sufferer to patience and warned against the despair of God's mercy as the sickness's spiritual danger, directing the minister to comfort the afflicted conscience with the promises of scripture. Despair, in the church's own liturgy, was something that happened to a person the community gathered around, a temptation of the sickbed to be answered with presence, prayer and reassurance. The parishioner who had sat through such a visitation, as most adults had, carried an embodied memory of the correct communal posture toward a despairing soul, and the posture was attendance, not abandonment. The playhouse asked for the same posture over two despairing deaths, sustained for the length of a scene, and the audience had been rehearsed in providing it by the most solemn domestic ritual the church possessed.

Against this teaching stood a counter-current that the same churchgoers met in the grammar school and the bookshop, and its presence complicates any simple claim that the audience's framework was single. Educated Elizabethans had been raised on Roman exempla in which self-killing could be the last act of honour: Cato falling on his sword at Utica rather than outlive the republic, Brutus and Cassius on the field at Philippi, Lucretia avenging her violation with a knife and a demand for justice. Seneca's tragedies, Englished piecemeal from Jasper Heywood's Troas of 1559, with his Thyestes and Hercules Furens following within two years, John Studley's versions later in the decade, and the whole gathered in Thomas Newton's Seneca His Tenne Tragedies of 1581, made violent Roman deaths a schoolroom property, and Seneca's prose praised the wise man's right to leave life as a guest leaves a banquet. The church condemned the act; the classics, which the church's own schools taught, dignified some of its instances. An Elizabethan playgoer did not need to resolve that contradiction in the abstract, because the culture had not resolved it either. What matters for the argument here is that both frameworks were present in the yard, and the play could count on each part of its audience finding one of them ready to hand.

## What Did the Law Do to a Felo de Se?

The law spoke the same sentence as the church in a different register. Self-killing by a person of sound mind was a felony at common law, felo de se, and it was among the oldest felonies on the books, treated as such since the medieval centuries when Bracton's treatise described the forfeitures that followed it. The dead felon could not be tried, so the law punished everything the felon left behind. A coroner's inquest, held over the body with a jury of neighbours, determined first whether the death was self-killing at all, and then the only question that carried consequences: whether the dead person had been of sound mind, a felo de se, or out of his wits, non compos mentis, at the moment of the act. On that single distinction everything turned, since the law's penalties attached only to the deliberate act of a sane person.

### Why were the goods forfeited while the land passed to the heir?

The goods were forfeited because the common law treated the sane self-killer as a felon whose chattels, money, plate, stock and even debts owed to him fell to the crown. The land descended to the heir because self-murder, uniquely among felonies, worked no corruption of blood and no escheat of the freehold.

Forfeiture of the goods and chattels was the first penalty, and it fell not on the corpse but on the widow and children, who could lose the tools, stock and savings of a household at the moment of its bereavement. The second penalty was the burial. The body of a felo de se was denied the churchyard and interred ignominiously, customarily in a public highway or at a crossroads, often at night, in some districts with a stake driven through the body, without clergy, without prayers, without any of the rites that marked a Christian's passage. The custom was not statute; it was something older and in some ways stronger, a folk-legal practice recorded in coroners' rolls across centuries, and it was designed to be seen. A crossroads burial was a public text. It announced to every passer-by that the person beneath the road had been judged, and it fixed the family's shame at the busiest point of the parish map. The stake, where it was used, belonged to the same folk logic as the crossroads themselves: both were meant to confuse and confine a restless dead.

The law's treatment of the living who attempted the act and survived completes the picture, and it too is gentler in operation than in text. An attempted self-killing was punishable as a misdemeanour, and the period's courts did fine or imprison a handful of the most public cases; but prosecutions were rare, and the instinct of neighbours and magistrates alike ran toward treating the survivor as an object of care and watchfulness rather than of exemplary punishment. The pattern repeats the inquest pattern at one remove: the text of the law condemns without qualification, the administration of the law qualifies without embarrassment, and the community's behaviour, not the text, is what a playgoer had watched all his life. It is worth insisting on the cumulative shape of these findings. Doctrine, forfeiture, burial, prosecution: at every joint of the official framework, the record shows the same flex, the same gap between the sentence as written and the sentence as lived, widening exactly where the human cost of the full sentence came into view. A play that wished to move its audience past the doctrine toward pity was not fighting the culture. It was recruiting the culture's own practised habit, the habit of the jury box and the parish, and staging it in gold leaf.

The applause and the accusation coexisted nowhere more plainly than in that habit.

The law's severity had a structural weakness, however, and the weakness is the hinge of the whole debate. The penalties could only be imposed on a finding of felo de se, and the finding belonged to a jury of the dead person's neighbours, men who knew the widow, owed the dead man money or were owed by him, and would pass the family in church every Sunday for the rest of their lives. The coroner presided, but the verdict was the neighbourhood's. Everything in the record of practice flows from that arrangement, and it is why the official sentence and the lived sentence diverged so persistently. A London playgoer had very likely never attended an inquest, but the playgoer knew the institution the way every parishioner knew it: as the neighbours' court, where the law's harshest words met the community's knowledge of the dead, and where the outcome was not foreordained.

The procedure itself deserves a moment's attention, because its texture explains the verdicts it produced. An inquest began with the coroner summoned to view the body where it lay, and the viewing was not a formality: the jurors were sworn over the corpse, neighbours empanelled from the surrounding townships, men who had known the dead person in life and would answer for the finding to a community that knew the circumstances as well as they did. The indictment that recorded a felo de se finding spoke in set formulas of the dead person having feloniously and voluntarily killed and murdered himself against the peace of the crown, a fiction of prosecution aimed at a defendant beyond reach. The finding set two machines in motion. The fiscal machine seized the goods, and the ritual machine excluded the body. Even the fiscal machine, though, had a human governor: forfeited goods were the crown's to grant away, and the records show them granted, on petition, to officials, to creditors, and not seldom in effect back toward the impoverished household, the crown's almoner standing in the process as a reminder that the seizure was patronage as much as punishment. Every stage of the law's severity, in other words, passed through the hands of people who could see the family it would fall on. The law wrote a sentence of exemplary harshness and then arranged for the sentence to be administered, mitigated or evaded by neighbours. That arrangement is not hypocrisy in the record; it is the record, and an audience drawn from the same parishes brought its knowledge of the arrangement into the playhouse with it.

The officer at the arrangement's centre had his own reasons to handle it gently. The coroner was not a distant crown bureaucrat. He was a local man, elected by the county's freeholders, paid by fees for the inquests he held, and dependent for his information, his juries and his quiet life on the goodwill of the very neighbourhoods his verdicts could ruin. A coroner who developed a reputation for severity would find deaths reported to him later, described to him worse, and circumvented by the hundred small cooperations on which his office ran. The fiscal tightening that Stevenson's rising verdicts record was real, and it came from above, from a crown and a council pressing for the forfeitures the law promised. The resistance to it was equally real, and it came from below, from an officer and a jury whose incentives all pointed toward the merciful construction. The system, in other words, was not a severe law unfortunately staffed by soft men. It was a severe law deliberately staffed, at every point of contact with a corpse, by the people with the strongest imaginable motives to soften it, and left that way by a state that collected what the softness left behind. Playgoers understood institutions the way they understood their parish: not from the statute book but from the men who worked them. Their expectation of what an inquest would do to a pitiable corpse was formed by that understanding, and it was an expectation of mercy more often than the law's text would suggest.

## What Did Coroners' Juries Actually Do With the Law?

MacDonald and Murphy's Sleepless Souls changed the study of this question by moving it from the pulpit to the archive. Working through coroners' inquests preserved among the records of the King's Bench, the two historians counted verdicts rather than sermons, and the picture that emerged was of a system under strain between its doctrine and its operators. In the decades around the play, the overwhelming majority of inquest verdicts on self-killings were findings of felo de se; verdicts of non compos mentis, the merciful finding that the dead person had been out of his wits and so no felon, were a small fraction of the whole, a few in every hundred. On that evidence the machinery of condemnation was not a dead letter in Shakespeare's lifetime. It ran, and it usually ran against the dead.

But the same archive shows the machinery being quietly resisted at every point where resistance was possible. A verdict of felo de se required that the death reach an inquest as a self-killing in the first place, and deaths could be, and were, returned as accidents, as misadventures, as the work of unknown hands. Sara M. Butler's study of medieval suicide verdicts documented juries across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries describing plain self-killings as accidents or concealing them altogether, and she identified the motive in the record itself: sympathy for the damage a felony verdict did to the reputations and fortunes of the survivors compelled jurors to find means of avoiding such verdicts. MacDonald and Murphy found the same pressures at work in the Tudor and Stuart records. The neighbours who staffed the jury bore the cost of the law's sentence in their own parish, and they softened it where they could, by mercy disguised as fact-finding. The insanity verdict, rare in the 1590s, would grow across the following century into the standard verdict precisely because it formalised the mercy that juries were already practising by other means.

The favourite field of the mercy was the water. A body found drowned presented a jury with the period's most elastic facts: drowning might befall a bather, a drunk crossing a bridge, a traveller who missed a ford in the dark, and the same body in the same water might be a self-killing that no witness had seen begin. The accident findings in the archive gather disproportionately around deaths by water, and the pattern is intelligible without any conspiracy. The jurors chose, among the stories the evidence permitted, the story that left the family whole. Hanging allowed less elasticity, and verdicts on the hanged run harsher in the records, which is itself instructive: the mercy tracked the plausibility of mercy's story, and where the facts forbade the kind story, juries did not invent it wholesale. The practice was not lawlessness. It was a community applying, inside the forms of the law, a standard for a felony finding that the law's own text did not supply, the standard of what neighbours could bear to swear over a corpse they had known in life.

### Why did reported suicide verdicts rise across the south-east between 1530 and 1590?

S. J. Stevenson's study of south-eastern inquests found verdicts multiplying because enforcement tightened: crown officials pursued the forfeitures a felo de se verdict generated, coroners were pressed to hold inquests parishes had quietly avoided, and deaths once buried without record entered the king's courts. The rise measures fiscal reach, not hardened hearts.

The medical language of melancholy gave the merciful impulse a learned vocabulary within the play's own decade. Timothy Bright's A Treatise of Melancholie, published in 1586, the first English book devoted to the condition, argued at length that the terrors of the melancholy mind were the effects of a bodily humour and not the verdicts of God on a damned soul, and that the melancholic's conviction of personal damnation was a symptom to be treated rather than a truth to be believed. Bright wrote against the despairing conscience directly: the sufferer who believed himself reprobate was, on the physician's account, a patient misreading his disease. A decade later, a playgoer who had absorbed even the outline of that argument possessed a second framework for any death by despair, one in which the dead person was sick rather than felonious. The framework was not yet dominant, and MacDonald and Murphy are clear that the medicalisation of self-killing belongs mainly to the century after the play. Its existence in print by 1586 is nonetheless part of the period's evidence: the pitying framework was not a later invention projected backward. It was available, in English, in the play's own lifetime, in a book addressed to a sufferer identified only as M.

The honest summary of the practice evidence is therefore double. The official machinery condemned, and in the 1590s it condemned more efficiently than it had a generation earlier, as Stevenson's rising verdicts show. At the same time, the machinery was operated by neighbours whose documented instinct was to protect the living from its consequences, and the culture's medical and classical literatures each supplied a language in which a self-killing could be understood as sickness or as honour rather than as felony. An Elizabethan audience did not bring a single verdict into the playhouse. It brought a contested one, and a play that wanted to govern the response had materials to work with on both sides of the contest.

The protection, moreover, was unevenly distributed in ways the audience would have recognised from life. MacDonald and Murphy's archive shows the machinery of shame working at full strength most reliably against the poor and the friendless, whose deaths were reported, inquested and sentenced with the fewest obstacles, while the deaths of the respectable were the deaths most often hushed, medicalised in the telling, or buried with a quietness that amounted to collusion between family, minister and neighbourhood. Clergy were not immune to the collusion: the period's church courts and visitations periodically complained of irregular burials and of ministers who had allowed doubtful corpses into consecrated ground, complaints that testify to a practice running ahead of the rule. The pattern has a direct bearing on the audience question. The lovers of the play are not the poor and friendless. They are the heirs of the two richest houses in Verona, children whose deaths their city buries with statues and a prince's attendance. Even judged by the practice of the audience's own parishes, rather than by the play's design, deaths of persons of that standing were the deaths the system handled with the most ceremony and the least stigma. An apprentice in the yard might know that a servant found hanged in a garret would be carried to the crossroads; he also knew that a great household's calamity would be managed with gloves. The play's golden statues are an exaggeration of the period's practice, not a contradiction of it.

One structural fact underlies the whole practice record and is worth stating plainly before the argument moves to the play. The institutions that operated the official sentence were not staffed by a separate caste of officials. Coroners were elected neighbours; jurors were neighbours; churchwardens, constables and ministers were drawn from the same streets as the families their decisions touched; and the men who filled those offices in the parishes filled the playhouse yard in the afternoons. The audience and the apparatus were the same people in different rooms. That identity dissolves the debate's crudest form, in which a stern official culture is imagined confronting a tender audience across a divide. There was no divide. The tenderness documented in the inquest rolls and the applause documented on the title page are two readings of one population, taken in two of its rooms, and they agree. The doctrine belonged to that population too, sincerely held and universally known. What the population did with its doctrine, in room after room where the doctrine met a particular corpse, is the most consistent story the archives tell, and the playhouse was simply the room where the stakes were lowest and the habit could run freest.

## What Did Brooke's Preface Order Readers to Feel?

The strongest documentary evidence for the condemnation case is not a sermon or a statute. It is the play's own source, prefaced with instructions for how to judge it. Arthur Brooke's The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet, published in 1562 and reprinted in 1587, within a decade of the play's first performances, carried an address "To the Reader" that sentenced the lovers before their story began. Brooke describes his subject, in the preface's most quoted sentence, as "a couple of unfortunate lovers, thralling themselves to unhonest desire; neglecting the authority and advice of parents and friends; conferring their principal counsels with drunken gossips and superstitious friars (the naturally fit instruments of unchastity); attempting all adventures of peril for th'attaining of their wished lust". The sentence continues through the abuse of marriage's honourable name "to cloak the shame of stolen contracts" to its conclusion, "finally by all means of unhonest life hasting to most unhappy death". Every element of the period's condemnation is present: the desire is dishonest, the counsellors are corrupt, the friar is an instrument of unchastity, the marriage is a cloak for shame. Brooke then tells the reader what the spectacle is for, comparing the lovers to the drunken slaves whom Spartan parents displayed to their children to teach a loathing of drunkenness. The lovers are a warning exhibit. Pity, in the preface's design, is a failure of reading.

Brooke also supplies, inside his own preface, a small fact that cuts against the use usually made of him. He records that the story had already been set forth on the London stage before his poem appeared, and that the stage version had been received, in his phrase, "with more commendation" than he could look for as a poet. The theatrical life of the tale thus precedes its most famous printed sentence. Whatever the lost play of the early 1560s made of the lovers cannot be recovered, but Brooke's aside establishes that audiences had applauded the story in a playhouse a generation before Shakespeare's version, and that a moralist preparing the tale for print felt the stage's popularity as the fact his preface had to answer. The preface reads, in that light, less like the period's spontaneous response to the lovers and more like a corrective addressed to a response already known to be warm. Correctives are evidence for the thing they correct.

Brooke was not an isolated scold. William Painter's version of the tale in The Palace of Pleasure (1566) likewise framed the story with moral reflection on disobedience and lust, and the tale's continental transmitters had handled it as an exemplum. The framing had travelled with the story at every stage of its transmission. Luigi da Porto's Venetian novella reached print already shadowed by the catastrophe; Matteo Bandello's retelling heightened its pathos; Pierre Boaistuau's French version of 1559, in the Histoires Tragiques through which the tale passed into English hands, clothed it in the apparatus of the tragic history, a genre whose contract with its reader was instruction through calamity. Brooke, working from Boaistuau, sharpened the inherited moralising into the preface's sentence, and Painter's frame kept the same contract for the tale's prose readers. By the time the story reached Shakespeare, it had been judged in three languages, always in the same direction. The printed story available to English readers arrived, so to speak, pre-judged. This matters to the debate because it establishes what the default literary response to the lovers was in the decades before the play: a reader meeting Romeus and Juliet on the page met them under sentence, and the sentence was part of the book's furniture. The full anatomy of that preface, its charges item by item and the poem's own resistance to them, belongs to the comparison of [Brooke's Moralising Preface vs Shakespeare](/2026/02/27/brooke-moralising-preface-vs-shakespeare/), which owns the subject; the point needed here is narrower. When Shakespeare took up the story, the condemnation reading was not a possibility he had to invent or a risk he ran. It was the reading his source had already published, and he had to decide, line by line, what to do with it.

What he did was remove it. The play has no preface and no moralising narrator. Its Chorus is a reporter, not a judge: the Prologue sonnet calls the lovers "star-cross'd" and their love "death-mark'd", describes the deaths as the means by which the parents' rage is buried, and asks the audience for patience and attention, "What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue, 14). Fate language does the work that blame language did in Brooke. Where Brooke's reader is told the lovers thralled themselves, Shakespeare's audience is told the stars crossed them and their parents' strife doomed them, and the difference is the whole argument in miniature. A star-crossed death solicits pity by definition, since pity is the response owed to suffering that exceeds desert. The Prologue does not argue the lovers' innocence; it does something more effective, assigning their end to fortune and to the feud before any question of their deserving can be raised, so that the audience's judgment is occupied by the machinery of the catastrophe rather than by the morality of the couple at its centre. The Prologue even supplies its own adjective for the spectacle it is selling: the subject of the scene, it promises, is "the fearful passage of their death-mark'd love, / And the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove, / Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage", and the lovers' story has already been named a few lines earlier by its "piteous overthrows". Piteous is the play's first emotional instruction, issued before a single actor in character has spoken. Brooke's preface had also issued a first instruction, and it was loathing. The two frames, set side by side, are the whole distance between the tale as the period's print sold it and the tale as its greatest playhouse remade it, and the reprint history shows the contest running in real time: Brooke's moralised poem was reprinted in 1587, within the decade that produced the play, the two verdicts competing in the same bookstalls and the same yard for the same story.

The Act 2 Chorus confirms that the neutrality is structural rather than a prologue's accident. Returning after the lovers' meeting, the second Chorus describes the new love in the language of appetite, inheritance and siege: old desire dying, young affection gaping to be its heir, Romeo's love answering where his enemy's daughter holds the field. The vocabulary is worldly, even wry, and it analyses the passion as a natural event with social consequences. What it never does is grade the passion morally. Across both of the play's framing speeches, the only judgments passed are on the feud, whose rage is the Prologue's one condemned thing, "their parents' rage" being the force the children's end must bury. Shakespeare had two choruses, the period's most convenient seats for an authorial verdict, and stationed reporters in both.

The removal was systematic, not local. Brooke's poem lingers over the lovers' faults and returns at its close to the lesson; the play distributes its closing attention to grief, reconciliation and the fathers' vows. Brooke's friar is one of unchastity's natural instruments; the play's Friar is rebuked for his part in the plan, by the audience's knowledge and by his own conscience, but he is addressed by Verona's prince as a holy man and his account is accepted as the tragedy's explanation. Brooke's lovers die as the wages of unhonest life; the play's lovers die inside speeches that the culture has since treated as the standard of erotic devotion, and their deaths are answered by golden statues. A dramatist who wanted his audience to condemn the suicides had every facility for saying so: a chorus, a friar, a prince and two fathers all speak after the deaths, and any one of them could have carried the sentence. The sentence is the one thing none of them says.

## How Does the Final Scene Refuse Every Official Verdict?

Act 5, scene 3 is the test case, and it repays slow reading, because the scene is built like a legal proceeding and then declines to deliver the law's conclusions. The tomb is discovered bloody; the Watch secures the ground; suspects are detained; the Prince arrives in the capacity of judge and announces an inquiry: "Seal up the mouth of outrage for a while, / Till we can clear these ambiguities" (5.3.216-217). Witnesses are examined. The Friar gives a full narrative of the marriage, the potion and the failed letter. Balthasar produces Romeo's letter to his father, which corroborates the Friar's account and records the purchase of the poison. The forms of an inquest are all present, assembled on stage in front of the audience, nearly all of whose members knew exactly what an inquest over self-killed bodies was for. What the inquest in the play never does is the one thing a real inquest existed to do. No one asks whether the dead were of sound mind. No one names a felony. No verdict on the manner of the deaths is returned, and no consequence for the bodies or the families is so much as mentioned. The proceeding that the audience's world reserved for judgment of the self-killer is staged in full and then spent entirely on the feud.

### How long does the audience sit with the bodies before authority arrives?

The audience sits with the bodies through the scene's long first movement: Paris's death, Romeo's farewell over Juliet, her waking, the Friar's failed rescue and her death all unfold before the Watch secures the churchyard. More than half of the tragedy's longest scene is spent inside the tomb before any officer of judgment arrives.

The withholding begins earlier, inside the deaths themselves. Romeo's farewell is the speech of a bridegroom, not a felon: "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace! and, lips, O you / The doors of breath, seal with a righteous kiss / A dateless bargain to engrossing Death!" (5.3.112-115). The kiss is called righteous. His last words are an act of marital fidelity, "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3.120), and the poison is figured as a "cordial", a medicine, in the speech's closing exchange with his own body. Juliet wakes to a scene the Friar interprets for her in the language of providence, not sin: "A greater power than we can contradict / Hath thwarted our intents" (5.3.153-154). Her death follows in a handful of lines, "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170), and the speed matters: the play gives condemnation no time to form. Between her waking and her death there is no speech of counsel, no voice of the church, no pause in which the official framework could be applied. The audience is carried past the doctrinal moment on the momentum of grief, which is precisely how the scene is engineered.

The scene also instructs the audience in how to see the tomb, and the instruction comes from Romeo himself. His speeches over the body do not describe a crime scene; they describe a theft. Death is personified as a rival lover: "Shall I believe / That unsubstantial Death is amorous, / And that the lean abhorrèd monster keeps / Thee here in dark to be his paramour?" (5.3.102-104). The vault becomes a bride-chamber guarded by a jealous keeper, the corpse a bride detained, and Romeo's entry a rescue performed too late. An audience that has spent ten minutes inside that metaphor has been re-educated about the category of what it is watching. The period's official vocabulary for the tomb was felony, despair and pollution; the vocabulary the play builds inside the tomb is courtship, rivalry and loss. By the time the Watch arrives with its prose and its lanterns, the audience has been sitting inside Romeo's re-description long enough for the official one to sound, when it fails to arrive at all, like no loss. The scene's most consequential omission is thus prepared by a positive act of reframing. Condemnation is not merely withheld; the imaginative furniture that would have supported it has been carried out of the room and replaced.

When judgment does arrive, it lands everywhere except on the lovers. The Prince sentences the feud: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love! / And I for winking at your discords too / Have lost a brace of kinsmen: all are punish'd" (5.3.296-299). Heaven, in the Prince's theology of the event, has used the deaths as an instrument against the hate; the lovers are the means of the punishment, not its objects. The Friar, the one living person who might have been prosecuted, offers himself to "the rigour of severest law" and is answered with a character reference from the bench: "We still have known thee for a holy man" (5.3.270). Romeo's killing of Paris, a homicide discovered in the same hour and confessed by the evidence of the scene, is never charged; the inquiry passes over it in silence, and Paris is mourned rather than avenged. The fathers compete in commemoration, Montague vowing a statue of Juliet "in pure gold", Capulet answering with Romeo's, and Capulet naming the dead pair in the play's own verdict on their status: "Poor sacrifices of our enmity!" (5.3.303). Sacrifices, in the period's religious vocabulary, are offerings, victims whose deaths purchase a communal good. No speaker in Verona calls the lovers felons, sinners, or examples. The Prince's closing distribution, "Some shall be pardon'd, and some punished" (5.3.308), leaves its referents unnamed, and the final couplet fixes the event's category for the audience on its way out of the theatre: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). Woe is the vocabulary of pity. Warning would have required a different word, and the play had Brooke's whole lexicon of warning to borrow from and borrowed none of it.

Jill L. Levenson's reading of this ending, in her Oxford edition, is the most exact modern statement of its doubleness. Levenson writes that in the coda to their deaths the play "qualifies their achievement", since the fathers celebrate the marriage with gold statues while Capulet calls the lovers "poor sacrifices of our enmity", and she concludes that the tragedy brings "a glooming peace" but "no signs of lasting change". The qualification is real, and it keeps the ending from sentimentality; but it is a qualification of the reconciliation's worth, not a sentence on the dead. Even the ending's sharpest modern editor finds the coda melancholy rather than condemnatory, a spectacle of waste rather than a judgment of sin. The fuller weighing of what the deaths settle, and whether the close tilts toward hope or desolation, belongs to the separate question of [Is the Ending Hopeful or Hopeless?](/2031/03/28/is-the-ending-hopeful-or-hopeless/); the narrower point here is that on the specific question the period's law and church would have asked, the identity of the guilty, the play's final scene returns no answer against the lovers at all.

Three smaller features of the scene's accounting deserve notice, because each of them quietly declines a resource the period offered for condemnation. The first is the Prince's inclusion of himself in the sentence. "All are punish'd" is spoken by the one figure in the play with the legal standing to punish, and its grammar begins at home: the judge has lost a brace of kinsmen for winking at the discords, and counts his own loss within the punishment rather than above it. A judge who enters himself among the punished does not proceed to further sentences; the scene's justice is complete the moment it is pronounced, and nothing is left over to be visited on the dead. The second feature is the scene's evidentiary method. The truth of the tomb is established by narrative and document, the Friar's connected story confirmed by Balthasar and by Romeo's letter, which the Prince reads and accepts: "This letter doth make good the Friar's words". An inquest exists to return a verdict; the scene substitutes a reconstruction, and by the time the reconstruction is complete the gathering's business has become mourning and reconciliation. The letter, the period's most intimate and least official of instruments, is allowed to close the case that no court ever opens. The third feature is Lady Montague. Her death, from grief at her son's exile, is reported by her husband in two lines and folded into the scene's ledger without taxonomy of any kind: "Grief of my son's exile hath stopp'd her breath" (5.3.210-211). A death by sorrow stands in the record a few yards from two deaths by the hand, and the play draws no line between them, as though the scene's implicit anthropology of grief had dissolved the category the law and the pulpit policed. Grief kills in the scene's accounting, by hand and by sorrow alike, and the accounting pities all three dead the same way.

The scene declines one further prosecution that the period's law held ready, and the omission deserves emphasis because it concerns the adults rather than the dead. At common law, a person who aided, counselled or supplied the means of a self-killing could be treated as a murderer, the felony of the dead being imputed to the living hand that had equipped it. The tomb scene contains a whole chain of such hands. The Friar devised the potion and the plot; an apothecary, against the law of his own city as the play states it, sold the poison; Balthasar carried the news that set the machinery moving. An inquiry animated by the period's categories would have found defendants enough. The play's inquiry finds none. The Friar's offer to accept "the rigour of severest law" hangs in the air unanswered, and the proceedings close instead in reconciliation and commissions for statues. Verona, in short, declines to prosecute anybody for anything arising out of the deaths, at the exact point where an English audience's legal imagination was best stocked with charges. The completeness of the refusal, extending from the corpses to their aiders, is what elevates the omission from a gap in the plotting to a policy of the fiction.

The statues repay one more look through the period's eyes. The fathers vow figures in pure gold, raised where the city will see them, as the monuments of their children. Civic commemoration of that kind belonged, in the culture the audience knew from its classics, to the honoured dead: to founders, patriots and the Romans of the schoolroom, Cato and Lucretia among them. A felo de se received a crossroads; the lovers receive what the classical honoured dead received, the city's gold and the city's memory. The classical frame does not merely soften the Christian frame at the play's close; it replaces it, awarding the dead the one afterlife the play can stage, fame in the mouths of the living, which is exactly the afterlife the closing couplet performs by retelling their story under its "woe". The audience is sent home as part of that machinery of commemoration, carriers of the tale. Pity, at the play's end, is not a private feeling the spectator happens to have. It is the office the fiction assigns.

The scene's light makes the same point in the period's own symbolic language. The tragedy has unfolded in darkness, torchlight and the false dawns of the parting scenes, and its last movement is staged at daybreak, the hour the culture associated with judgment and with resurrection morning alike. The Prince's dawn arrives deliberately dimmed: "A glooming peace this morning with it brings; / The sun for sorrow will not show his head" (5.3.305-306). Judgment morning comes, and what it delivers is not a verdict but a peace, with nature itself put into mourning dress. The borrowing is exact and the substitution is the play's signature: the eschatological furniture of the last judgment, the opened graves, the assembled city, the presiding judge, the morning, is all present, and the sentence the furniture exists to deliver is replaced by reconciliation and a sad story. An audience formed by sermons on the last judgment could not miss the shape of the scene, and could not miss what the shape had been emptied of. The play stages doomsday over its suicides and returns, in place of damnation, "glooming peace". That is the boldest single stroke in the whole construction, and it is executed in the period's most public religious vocabulary, where every spectator could be trusted to read it.

## Why Did Juliet's Death Ask for a Different Verdict Than Romeo's?

The two deaths were not equally exposed to the period's sentence, and the play knows it. Romeo reaches the tomb through a homicide and an illegal purchase of poison, speaks of himself, accurately, as a desperate man, and kills in despair at a loss that the audience knows is not real. His is the death that most nearly matches the period's dossier: the frenzied, self-ruining act of a mind unseated, and the strongest case a doctrine-first reading can make is built from his half of the scene. Juliet's death answers a different dossier altogether. She wakes to find the marriage bed become a grave, is offered flight to a nunnery by the one adviser she has, refuses it in a speech of decision rather than frenzy, and joins her husband with words of welcome spoken over the dagger: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170). In the period's own scripts, a wife who would not survive her husband stood in an honoured line, the line of the faithful widow whose constancy the culture praised in exempla even while its divines fenced the act itself about with prohibitions. The play gives Juliet's death the shape of a vow kept rather than a prohibition broken, and it gives her the tomb scene's clearest head: she reads the scene she wakes into, in seconds, more accurately than any living character manages that night.

Romeo, too, arrived in the tomb pre-read by scripts his audience knew, and the strongest of them was not the felon's. The distracted lover was a standing figure of the period's stage and page, a young man unseated by passion whose ravings drew the house's compassion rather than its censure, and Romeo has been performing that figure since the orchard scene: sleepless, sighing, forgetful of his name and his feud, legible to any playgoer as love's melancholic. Bright's treatise had given the same figure a medical address. On the physician's account, the lover's fixed idea, his conviction that life without the beloved was a sentence, was the melancholy imagination at work, a humour to be treated by counsel and company. When Romeo names himself desperate to Paris at the tomb's mouth, an audience furnished with Bright's framework heard a diagnosis where the pulpit heard a confession, and the two hearings could coexist in the same spectator without cancelling: the doctrine supplied the sin, the medicine supplied the sickness, and pity is the response that sickness commands. The play never argues the medical case in terms. It does not need to. It stages a young man whose behaviour matches, symptom by symptom, the casebook picture its decade had just printed.

The asymmetry matters to the debate because the scholarly argument about the first audience is usually conducted as though the deaths formed a single object of judgment. The period's habits of judgment were finer-grained. Its casuists distinguished the despairing from the constant, its jurors the mad from the malicious, its balladeers the wronged maiden from the self-murderer, and its schoolrooms the Roman wife from the raving tyrant. An audience formed by those habits had every equipment needed to pity Juliet unreservedly while harbouring a residue of the doctrine for Romeo, and the play's design exploits the equipment: Romeo's death is staged for pity through its error, the boy killed by a false report, and Juliet's through her fidelity. By the scene's end the two are buried in a single couplet and a single phrase, "the fearful passage of their death-marked love", the play having first secured each death under the strongest exculpatory script its culture kept. The verdict on the pair is the sum of two separately acquitted cases.

## What Else Had Playgoers Been Taught to Feel?

A playhouse audience's responses are trained by more than doctrine, and the training available in the London theatres of the early 1590s deserves its own reckoning, because it cuts against the pulpit with surprising force. The period's theory of tragedy, in the most influential English statement of it, assigned pity as tragedy's proper product. Sidney's Defence of Poesy, printed in 1595, the play's own moment, praised high tragedy for "stirring the affects of admiration and commiseration" and for teaching "the uncertainty of this world" through the falls of the great. Commiseration, in Sidney's vocabulary, is pity with the sufferer, not judgment on him. Behind Sidney stood the recovered Poetics of Aristotle and its great Italian commentaries, Robortello's of 1548 and Castelvetro's Poetica of 1570, which had made the mechanics of tragic pity a technical subject across Europe. Aristotle's condition for pity is precise: it is felt for suffering that is undeserved, or that exceeds desert, befalling someone enough like the spectator for the suffering to be imaginable as his own. The Prologue's framing of the lovers, star-crossed, death-marked, destroyed by a feud they did not make, is a near-perfect construction of Aristotle's conditions. Their suffering is certified in advance as exceeding anything their faults could have earned, which is the technical definition of the pitiable. A playgoer need not have read a word of the commentaries for the engineering to work on him, since the engineering operates through the framing, not through theory. But the theory existed, in the play's own decade, in print, in the same city, and it described exactly the response the play was built to harvest. A spectator formed by that poetics came to a tragedy expecting to be moved to fellow-feeling, and the expectation was a critical commonplace, not a private softness. The Chorus of the play itself asks for exactly that contract: patience, attention, and the loan of the audience's feelings for two hours.

The culture's other theatre of death trained a cognate discrimination. Public executions were the period's most attended spectacles of dying, and the literature they generated, the printed accounts of last speeches, dying confessions and scaffold behaviour sold in the streets, taught its readers to judge a death by its manner. A condemned felon who died composed, penitent and courageous was admired in the very teeth of his felony, and the pamphlets lingered over the composure as the death's redeeming feature; a prisoner who raved or collapsed was pitied or scorned on quite different grounds. The scaffold crowd, in other words, practised a judgment that separated the crime from the dying, honouring the manner while abhorring the offence, and it practised that separation weekly, in large numbers, as a civic recreation. A playhouse audience judging staged deaths brought the same trained separation with it. The lovers die, by the scaffold's standards, superbly: composed, articulate, constant to the last syllable, in speeches whose beauty is the period's equivalent of a good end on the ladder. Whatever the audience's doctrine said of the act, its connoisseurship of dying said honour, and connoisseurship is a habit of feeling long before it is a proposition.

The repertory had trained the same response in practice. The Spanish Tragedy, the most popular tragedy of the age that preceded the play, ends with Hieronimo, having revenged his murdered son, killing himself on stage before the court; the play's closing machinery of ghosts and assigned afterlives receives him into its cosmic settlement without pronouncing the sentence that English doctrine had ready for a self-killer. Generations of playgoers had watched that death as the culmination of a wronged father's pathos. The play's earliest title page sold it in the same register, billing The Spanish Tragedy as containing "the lamentable end of Don Horatio, and Bel-imperia: with the pitifull death of old Hieronimo". The genre's marketing language for the aftermath of a staged self-killing, years before Verona's tomb, was the vocabulary of pity, and a title page is a commercial document: it promises buyers the feeling the commodity reliably delivers. Seneca stood behind the repertory as its schoolroom warrant, and the Roman deaths of the history plays and the classical curriculum, Cato, Brutus, Cassius, Lucretia, gave the self-killing of a person of honour a recognised dramatic shape with its own dignity. Against these stood the counter-example that proves the rule: Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, played in the same years, shows the stage perfectly capable of staging the doctrine when it wished. Faustus despairs, refuses repentance, and is carried off by devils in full view, with a chorus that draws the moral in terms Brooke would have recognised. The condemnatory machinery existed, was popular, and was used by the play's greatest contemporary. Its absence from the tomb scene in Verona is therefore a choice, and an audience that knew Faustus could register the choice as one.

### What does Faustus prove about the stage's power to condemn?

Faustus proves that the stage possessed a complete, popular and spectacular vocabulary for condemning despair: devils on stage, a moralising chorus, a protagonist naming his own damnation. A company wanting its audience to condemn a despairing death had every device ready. The tomb scene's refusal to use any of them is measurable, not accidental.

One further distancing device deserves notice, handled briefly because a sibling study owns it. Verona is not London. The play's world is a Catholic, Italian elsewhere, with a friar who hears confessions, a saint's day calendar, and a prince whose justice follows none of the forms of an English coroner's court. Whatever an English audience believed about English self-murderers, the lovers were foreign, Catholic, aristocratic and long ago, and distance of that kind historically loosens the application of home doctrine; Protestant playgoers could pity Italian Catholics on stage for acts they would have condemned in a neighbour, and the period's drama relied on that licence constantly. The confessional colouring of the play and its Friar is treated in full in [The Catholic Question and Friar Laurence](/2027/03/26/catholic-question-friar-laurence/). For the present argument the distance is one more layer of insulation between the audience's doctrine and the audience's tears.

The question of how the church itself framed self-killing, and how the play's several frames, sin, waste and transcendence, compete as readings of the deaths, is the subject of the debate mapped in [Suicide in Romeo and Juliet: Sin or Transcendence?](/2024/05/31/suicide-in-romeo-and-juliet-debate/). The concern here has been the narrower historical one: not what the deaths mean, but what the first audience was positioned to feel about them. On that question the training evidence converges. Schoolroom Rome dignified some self-killings, the repertory pitiable ones drew tears without sentences, the age's poetics named commiseration as tragedy's aim, and the one contemporary play that stages damnation for despair does so by making damnation its whole subject. Verona's tomb asks for none of that apparatus. It asks for grief.

The playhouse training also ran deeper than the repertory. Every grammar-school boy in the audience, and the audience was full of men whose schooling had ended at the grammar stage, had met the honourable suicides of antiquity as set texts before he met them as arguments: Lucretia's story was among the first pieces of connected Latin narrative a schoolboy construed, and Seneca's tragedies, with their stoic deaths declaimed at full length, stood on the curriculum's shelf. The classical counter-current of the church section was therefore not a bookish minority taste. It was the common furniture of male literacy, acquired at the age when furniture settles permanently. A doctrine learned at church from the age of seven competed, in the same heads, with a counter-doctrine learned at school from the same age, and the playhouse was the one room where the schoolroom's version had the stage. Nor should the audience's familiarity with death itself be left out of the training. The play was staged in a city that had buried its thousands in the plague closures of 1592 to 1594 and that buried its ordinary dead, in ordinary years, at a rate that made sudden death and hasty, curtailed funerals familiar experiences in most parishes. The stigma of an unceremonied grave, the period's loudest ritual sentence on the self-killer, fell on ears that had watched ceremony curtailed for hundreds of the blameless. Familiarity does not abolish a stigma, but it files its edge, and the audience that watched two more bodies lowered into a tomb in 1595 brought a newly educated sense of how thin the line could be between the honoured grave and the hurried one.

## What the First Readers and Audiences Left Behind

The surviving reception record, thin as it was shown to be in the questions that close this article, leans the same way as the inquest rolls. The play's first quarto advertised commendation; its second, three years later, billed the story a lamentable tragedy and the lovers most excellent, its pity baked into the marketing language of a commercial enterprise that could not afford to misdescribe its goods to returning customers. When the third quarto appeared in 1609, the billing still sold the tragedy of the lovers, not a warning against them. The First Folio of 1623 placed the play among the Tragedies, shelving it by kind with the stories whose office, on Sidney's account, was admiration and commiseration, and not among the histories, where an exemplum of punished sin would have worn a different face. Just as telling is a silence. The decades around the play were noisy with attacks on the stage, on particular plays and on the morals of the playhouses, and pamphleteers who objected to a fiction's sympathies said so in print. No surviving polemic singles out this tragedy for the offence its design most plainly commits, the refusal to condemn two self-killers, and no preacher is recorded citing it from a pulpit as a warning against pitying the lovers. An argument from silence is the weakest form of evidence, and it is labelled as such here. But the silence sits in a noisy archive, and it is consistent with everything the noisier records say: the culture's official sentence on the suicides was thunderous, available, and, in the playhouse and the parish alike, very largely not applied.

The applause claim itself deserves a calibrated reading rather than either credulity or dismissal. Title-page boasting was a convention, and other quartos of the period advertise performances "sundry times publicly acted" with as little documentary support. A formula, however, is not a fiction: it had to remain plausible to the browsers at the bookstalls, many of whom had stood in the yard themselves, and a publisher who billed a hissed failure as applauded would be advertising against the memory of his own customers. The 1597 boast also sits inside a pattern no single convention explains, a bad quarto rushed into print because demand existed, a corrected edition three years later, a third in 1609, and the play's eventual place in the collected Folio. Properties that audiences condemned did not follow that trajectory. The record cannot certify that every first audience wept, and nothing in this article claims it can. What it certifies is a commercial property whose selling points were lamentation and applause, in a market that punished misdescription with empty benches.

The two theatrical documents, Brooke's aside of 1562 and the quarto's boast of 1597, bookend the story's stage life across thirty-five years, and they speak the same language. Brooke's lost play was received "with more commendation" than a poet could hope for; Shakespeare's play was printed as having been played "with great applause". Commendation and applause are the period's commercial words for an audience's warmth, and between them stands Brooke's preface, the era's most elaborate attempt to chill that warmth with doctrine. The attempt's failure is part of the record: the poem was reprinted, preface and all, in 1587, and the stage went on warming audiences regardless. Print's sentence and the playhouse's response coexisted for decades without the sentence ever managing to become the response, which is, in miniature, the finding of every other archive this article has opened. The doctrine was real, printed, reprinted and unanswered in kind. It was also, in the rooms where the period actually gathered to watch the story, ineffective, and the ineffectiveness is documented at both ends of the period by the testimony of the moralist who most wished it otherwise.

## Where the Scholars Disagree, and What Settles It

The scholarly disagreement is genuine and worth stating at full strength on each side. The doctrine-first case, whose ablest representative is Roland M. Frye in Shakespeare and Christian Doctrine (1963), holds that Shakespeare's audience was formed by the teaching of the English church, heard the plays within that formation, and would have applied its categories almost automatically. On Frye's account, a self-killing presented to such an audience arrived pre-judged: the categories of sin, despair and felony were not opinions a playgoer might hold but the furniture of the playgoer's mind, and the beauty of the verse would no more have suspended them than beautiful verse about theft would have suspended the eighth command in the commandments then read aloud in every parish church. The doctrine-first case has the prefaces on its side, since Brooke and Painter show literate contemporaries applying exactly those categories to exactly this story.

The practice case, built by MacDonald and Murphy from the inquest archive, answers that the furniture of the mind included the jury box as well as the pew. The men who staffed inquests were the same men who filled the playhouse yard, and their documented behaviour under oath was to bend the doctrine wherever bending protected a neighbour's family: concealing deaths, returning accidents, discovering madness. When such men watched a tragedy, the practice case argues, they brought the bending habit with them, and a play that asked for their pity was asking for something their weekly experience had already trained. The practice case also owns the awkward fact that the period's own moralists complained constantly about the softness of juries and the sympathy of crowds; complaints of that kind are evidence that the softness existed and was winning.

The adjudication turns on a distinction the doctrine-first case does not observe: the distinction between the categories an audience possessed and the verdict a work instructed it to reach with them. Frye's framework establishes, beyond serious dispute, that the categories were available and authoritative. It does not establish that the categories governed every response, and the inquest record proves that in the one setting where Elizabethans were sworn to apply the categories to an actual self-killing, they routinely declined to apply them in full. A playhouse is a weaker court than a coroner's inquest, not a stronger one. If neighbours under oath, facing a real corpse and a real widow, found their way to mercy by re-describing the facts, then playgoers facing feigned corpses, in a fiction set in Catholic Italy, under a Chorus that had already assigned the blame to the stars and the feud, faced no pressure toward condemnation except the pressure of habit, and every pressure of craft toward pity. The playwright's craft is the final piece of evidence, and it is decisive because it is measurable: the sentence of condemnation required a single speech from the Prince, the Friar or a father, the dramatist wrote speeches for all three, and the sentence is in none of them. H. C. Goddard, in The Meaning of Shakespeare (1951), saw the mechanism from the audience's side when he wrote of Juliet left in the tomb that she stands abandoned even by religion, thrown back on love alone for courage; the abandonment Goddard names is the play withdrawing every official voice at the moment of death, so that the audience's sympathy has no competitor.

Levenson's qualification survives the adjudication and refines the verdict rather than overturning it. The ending is not warm. The peace is "glooming", the statues are commodities as much as memorials, and no lasting change is promised. But gloom is a species of grief, not of judgment, and a qualified pity is still pity. The first audience's response is best described as pity under the shadow of a known condemnation: the doctrine present as the reason the tomb scene's silence about sin feels so loud, the pity present as the feeling the play actually produces. Condemnation was what the lovers' world, and the audience's world, would have said in court and in church. Pity is what the play made its audience say in the theatre, and the applause on the first title page is the sound of the theatre winning.

The doctrine-first position has one stronger reply that deserves its own paragraph, because it cannot be answered with the inquest rolls alone. Craft, the reply runs, can move feeling without moving judgment: an audience may weep at a fiction and still judge its persons harshly in the cool of the street outside, as congregations were moved by sermons' pathos while assenting to every sentence the pathos decorated. The reply is right about the distinction and wrong about its application here, for a reason internal to the two plays this article has set side by side. Faustus demonstrates what a fiction looks like when feeling and judgment are meant to part company at the exit: the pathos is real, and the frame forecloses it, the Chorus and the devils and the hero's own confession telling the audience exactly which judgment the feeling was not allowed to disturb. Verona's frame does the opposite work. Every closing utterance the play supplies, Prince, fathers, Chorus and couplet, ratifies the feeling and declines the foreclosure, so that a spectator leaving the theatre with a settled condemnation of the lovers would have had to construct that judgment unassisted, against the grain of every sentence the fiction had just handed him, using doctrinal materials the evening had never once activated. Possible, for the exceptionally catechised; but a governing response it cannot have been. The verdict is also stated here in falsifiable form, because a historical claim that no evidence could disturb is worth nothing. A contemporary sermon citing the play as a warning against pitying self-murder, a marginal condemnation of the lovers in an early printed copy, a church court presentment connecting the playhouse with the softening of a verdict: any one of these would materially weaken the case made in this article, and none is known to survive. The case stands on the records that do survive, and it invites correction from the archive rather than assent on trust.

The editorial tradition, the scholars who live longest inside the play's early texts, registers the same pull and is worth a final word. G. Blakemore Evans, introducing the play in the New Cambridge edition, gives his introduction's weight to the sources and to the tragic pattern, and prints Brooke's Romeus in an appendix so that readers can watch the transformation with their own eyes. The appendix is an editorial argument in its own right: it exists because the deletion of the moralising frame is the single most instructive comparison the play affords, and an editor does not build an appendix around an accident. The consequence for any reading of the play follows. The ending's silence about sin is not an oversight to be corrected by importing Brooke's sentence or the catechism's, and a reading that reimposes the condemnation the text withheld mistakes the period's loudest voice for its only one. The period had two voices on self-killing, and the play chose between them with full knowledge of both. Recovering the choice, rather than either voice alone, is what an account of the first audience owes the evidence.

## A Necessary Note on the Subject

This article discusses self-killing as a matter of history, law and literature, and it does so without describing methods or dwelling on the mechanics of any death, in the play or out of it. The distance of four centuries can make the subject feel safely abstract, and for some readers it will not be abstract at all. Anyone who recognises present distress in this history, in themselves or in someone near them, is urged to treat that recognition as the important part of the page and to seek support: from a doctor, from a counsellor, or from one of the listening services and crisis lines that operate in every region, whose whole purpose is to be contacted at exactly such a moment. The Elizabethan parish had a confessor and a community for despair. The equivalents stand ready, and using them is the period's own best advice, recovered from Bright's treatise: despair is a condition to be treated, not a verdict to be believed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch response debate, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch response debate is the table in this article that sets the period's case for condemning the lovers' suicides against the case for pitying them, row by row. On one side stand the church's teaching on self-murder, the felony of felo de se, forfeiture, shame burial and Brooke's moralising preface. On the other stand the merciful practice of coroners' juries, the Roman exempla of the schools, Sidney's poetics of commiseration and the play's own refusal to sentence its dead. Setting the cases side by side exposes the pattern the article argues: every instrument of condemnation existed, and the play declined to use any of them, while every instrument of pity was available and the play used them all.

### Q: What evidence survives for how the first audiences actually responded to the lovers' deaths?

Direct testimony does not survive, so the evidence is circumstantial and of several kinds. The first quarto's title page of 1597 advertises the play as often played "with great applause", and the second quarto's of 1599 calls it a "lamentable tragedy", marketing grief rather than warning. The play's continued place in the repertory and its repeated reprinting show sustained demand. Brooke's preface proves that a moralising response was available in print a generation earlier. Against those documents stand the institutional records of pulpit and inquest. The reconstruction defended in this article weighs all of it together: applause and lamentation from the theatre's own records, condemnation from the church's, and mercy from the jury archive.

### Q: What words did the period use for self-killing, and when did the word suicide enter English?

The period's words were self-murder, self-homicide and the law Latin felo de se, each of which carries a verdict inside the name: a murder, a felony. The neutral noun suicide enters English only in the 1640s, coined by Sir Thomas Browne and printed in his Religio Medici of 1643, decades after the play's first performances. The delay matters to the debate because a culture whose only terms for an act are accusations processes the act differently from a culture with a clinical term. Shakespeare's audience heard of the lovers' end in a language that had no way to name it without judging it, which makes the play's own refusal to apply those names in the final scene all the more conspicuous.

### Q: Why was despair treated as the gravest element of self-murder in the period's theology?

Despair was treated as the gravest element because period theology held that the self-killer died in the act of an unrepented mortal sin, at the one moment when repentance had become impossible. Preachers distinguished sorrow for sin, which could be healed, from despair, the settled conviction that God's mercy did not extend to the sinner, which closed the door repentance needed. The act itself destroyed a life that belonged to God; the despair behind it rejected the mercy that might have answered for the act. On that reasoning the self-murderer's damnation was inferred not from the wound but from the state of the soul presumed to have inflicted it, which is why despair, not the death, carried the heaviest weight in the pulpit's account.

### Q: Why did preachers hold up Judas whenever they spoke of self-murder?

Preachers held up Judas because his story isolated despair as the fatal element. Judas, in the standard pulpit treatment, felt remorse, confessed his betrayal to the priests and returned the silver, yet hanged himself rather than seeking the mercy that Peter, guilty of his own denial, found by weeping and living. The contrast of the two apostles let preachers argue that sorrow alone does not save and that the self-killer's true sin is the refusal of hope. Judas therefore functioned as the type of every self-murderer: not the worst sinner in the story, but the one who despaired of pardon. The typology was so standard that a playgoer meeting a despairing character on stage had the comparison ready without any preacher present.

### Q: Which official texts taught ordinary churchgoers that self-killing was a damnable sin?

The teaching reached churchgoers through the commandments themselves, expounded in catechising so that "Thou shalt not kill" covered the parishioner's own life, and through the sermons and doctrine inherited from Augustine and Aquinas that the clergy retailed from the pulpit. It was reinforced by the visible discipline of the church, which withheld the burial office from those who had laid violent hands on themselves, a refusal later prayer books print as a rubric and Shakespeare's contemporaries knew as standing canon-law practice. No single statute or homily carried the whole doctrine. Its authority came from saturation: the same sentence arrived from the catechism, the sermon, the inquest and the churchyard boundary, each confirming the others to anyone who attended church and buried neighbours.

### Q: How did the period's medicine of melancholy complicate the church's verdict?

The medicine of melancholy complicated the verdict by relocating despair from the soul to the body. Timothy Bright's A Treatise of Melancholie (1586) argued that the melancholic's terrors, including the conviction of personal damnation, were effects of a disordered humour and therefore symptoms, not truths. A sufferer persuaded of his own reprobation was, on Bright's account, a patient misreading a disease. That reframing gave relatives, jurors and playgoers a second framework for self-killing: sickness rather than felony, treatment rather than sentence. The framework was young and contested in the 1590s, and the full medicalisation of the subject belongs to the following century, but its presence in print within the play's own decade means the pitying reading of despair was an Elizabethan possibility, not a later invention.

### Q: Which stage suicides had London playgoers already watched before this play?

The clearest precedent is Hieronimo in The Spanish Tragedy, the era's most popular tragedy, who kills himself on stage after completing his revenge for his murdered son. The play's closing machinery, with Andrea's ghost distributing the afterlife's rewards and punishments, receives Hieronimo without pronouncing the sentence of damnation that English doctrine held ready. Playgoers had also met self-killing in the classical curriculum that the stage constantly recycled, the deaths of Cato, Brutus and Cassius carrying the dignity of Roman honour. Against those stood Marlowe's Faustus, damned in full view for despair. The repertory therefore offered its audience both templates, and the tomb scene in Verona follows the Hieronimo template, pathos without sentence, rather than the Faustus template of spectacular condemnation.

### Q: What did Sidney's Defence of Poesy claim tragedy should make an audience feel?

Sidney's Defence of Poesy, published in 1595, claimed that high tragedy works by "stirring the affects of admiration and commiseration", teaching the uncertainty of the world through the falls of the great. Commiseration in Sidney's vocabulary is fellow-feeling with the sufferer, pity suffered alongside rather than judgment passed from above. The claim matters to the debate because it is the period's own theory of the playhouse: a spectator formed by Sidney's poetics, the most prestigious English account of what tragedy is for, arrived at the tomb scene expecting pity to be the proper product of the spectacle. Condemnation had its institutions, the pulpit and the inquest. The theatre, on the age's own theory, was built for the other response.

### Q: How does the Prologue coach the audience's judgment of the lovers before the action begins?

The Prologue coaches judgment by assigning the catastrophe to fortune and to the feud before any question of the lovers' deserving can arise. The sonnet calls them "star-cross'd", calls their love "death-mark'd", and states that their end is what buries their parents' rage, "which, but their children's end, nought could remove" (Prologue, 10-11). Agency is given to the stars and to the strife, not to the couple's choices, and a death assigned to fortune solicits pity almost by definition, since pity answers suffering that exceeds desert. The Chorus then asks for patience and attention rather than verdict. By the time the audience meets the lovers, the frame for judging them has been set by the play, and the frame is the one Brooke's preface worked to prevent.

### Q: Why is there no inquest, priest or verdict on the manner of the deaths in the final scene?

The absence is a construction, because the scene assembles the forms of an inquiry and then spends them on the feud. The Watch secures the ground, the Prince announces that ambiguities will be cleared, the Friar narrates, a letter corroborates him, and judgment is distributed against the hate, the discords and heaven's scourge. The one question a real inquest existed to answer, whether the dead killed themselves in sound mind, is never asked, and no consequence for the bodies, the goods or the families is mentioned. A dramatist working from Brooke had every model for supplying that verdict. The scene's silence on the manner of the deaths is therefore best read as deliberate: the play stages the machinery of judgment in order to be seen not using it on the lovers.

### Q: Why is Romeo never accused of killing Paris once the tomb is opened?

Romeo's killing of Paris is the final scene's clearest proof that its justice is selective in the lovers' favour. Paris's body is found, his page testifies, the evidence of a homicide by Romeo's hand is complete, and the Prince's inquiry passes over the killing without a charge, folding Paris into the general mourning as another of the feud's costs. In the audience's England, a confessed killing at a tomb would have dominated any inquest. In Verona it is absorbed into the story the Prince chooses to tell, in which the feud and its hate are the criminals and everyone else is a sacrifice or a mourner. The omission shows the scene's verdict being written by sympathy: the facts that would complicate pity are present on stage and deliberately left unprosecuted.

### Q: What do Michael MacDonald and Terence Murphy argue about the gap between doctrine and practice?

In Sleepless Souls: Suicide in Early Modern England (1990), MacDonald and Murphy argue from the coroners' records that the official condemnation of self-killing and the community's response to it diverged persistently. Doctrine called the sane self-killer a felon; practice, operated by juries of the dead person's neighbours, softened the sentence by concealment, by verdicts of accident, and increasingly by findings of insanity, a few in every hundred in Shakespeare's lifetime and the large majority a century later. Their larger claim is that the meaning of the act was transformed less by theologians changing their minds than by jurors changing their verdicts. For the play's debate, their work supplies the missing middle term: an audience trained by the jury box to bend doctrine toward mercy.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence that the first audiences pitied the lovers?

The strongest evidence is the play itself, which is a contemporary document of the response its maker intended and its company sold. The Chorus assigns the deaths to fortune and the feud; the moralising of the source is deleted entire; the final scene stages an inquiry that never asks the inquest's question; the fathers answer the deaths with golden statues and the word "sacrifices"; the closing couplet files the event under woe. Around the text stand the trade documents: a first title page boasting "great applause", a second marketing a "lamentable tragedy". Add the jury archive's proof that Elizabethans habitually bent the doctrine toward mercy, and the pity case rests on the period's records rather than on modern sentiment projected backward.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence that the first audiences condemned them?

The strongest evidence is the print framing the story carried before the play existed. Brooke's address "To the Reader" sentenced the lovers in advance as thralls of "unhonest desire" who counselled with "drunken gossips and superstitious friars" and hasted to "most unhappy death", and Painter's version moralised in the same direction, proving that literate contemporaries applied the church's categories to this exact story as a matter of course. Behind the prefaces stand the institutions: a catechism that read the sixth commandment against the self, a felony that confiscated the goods, and a burial discipline every parishioner had seen at the churchyard boundary. Roland M. Frye's argument generalises the point: an audience formed by that teaching carried its categories into the playhouse as mental furniture, and furniture of that weight does not vanish for two hours.

### Q: How did communities protect the families of suicides from the law's penalties?

Communities protected families at the points where the law depended on local cooperation. A death had first to be reported as a self-killing before any inquest could sit, and deaths were returned as accidents or misadventures, or buried quietly without the record that triggered forfeiture. Where an inquest could not be avoided, juries of neighbours could find the dead person non compos mentis, out of his wits, a finding that carried neither forfeiture nor shame burial. Sara M. Butler's study of medieval verdicts names the motive directly: sympathy for the damage a felony verdict inflicted on the reputations and fortunes of the survivors. The protection was not lawlessness but a standing negotiation between the parish and the crown, conducted verdict by verdict.

### Q: How did popular ballads and cheap print judge real suicides in the period?

Cheap print generally judged real suicides as the doctrine directed, and its testimony is the condemnation case's popular wing. Ballads and pamphlets about notorious self-killings habitually framed the act as the end of a progress through sin, with the devil as tempter and the death as warning, in the same exemplary shape Brooke gave the lovers' tale. That literature is important precisely because it shows the moralising frame alive at street level and not only in pulpits. Its limits matter as much: cheap print dealt in exemplary horror because horror sold copies, and the same neighbourhoods that bought the ballads staffed the juries that quietly returned merciful verdicts on their own dead. Print condemned in general what practice pardoned in particular.

### Q: Did the period judge a daughter's self-killing by the same standard as a man's?

The standard was formally the same and practically inflected by the scripts available for each sex. A daughter's death was read against the honour tradition that the schools taught through Lucretia, whose self-killing the culture half-admired as a defence of chastity even while its theologians condemned it, and against the obedience expected of children, which Brooke's preface makes the lovers' first offence. Juliet dies, moreover, as a wife defending a marriage her church had blessed in secret, a posture the audience could file under fidelity rather than despair. Romeo's death, by contrast, follows a killing and a purchased poison, the act's most felonious colouring. The play exploits the asymmetry quietly: her death is given the language of the bride, his the language of the desperate man, and the period's frameworks of judgment were readier to pity hers.

### Q: Why do scholars disagree about whether doctrine or practice governed the playhouse response?

Scholars disagree because the two bodies of evidence are both genuine and point in different directions, and because each side's method privileges its own archive. Historians of doctrine, following Roland M. Frye, read sermons, catechisms and prefaces and find a culture that condemned; historians of practice, following MacDonald and Murphy, count inquest verdicts and find a culture that bent its condemnation toward mercy at the point of application. The playhouse sits between the archives, a place where doctrine was known and feeling was manufactured. The disagreement is therefore not resolvable by more evidence of the same kinds. It turns on a judgment about which archive describes an audience at the moment of response, and the verdict defended in this article awards that moment to practice and to the play's own engineering.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch response debate to argue that pity governed the first response?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch response debate by conceding the condemnation case at full strength before answering it, since the table's left column is the doctrine the audience demonstrably possessed. The argument then moves to application: the same culture's juries bent that doctrine toward mercy under oath, the play deletes every voice that could pronounce the doctrine's sentence, and the final scene stages an inquiry that blames the feud and commemorates the dead in gold. Closing on the title pages, applause for a tragedy marketed as lamentable, lets the essay rest its verdict on the period's own records. The strongest form of the claim is comparative: condemnation explains what the audience knew, and pity explains what the play made that knowledge do.
