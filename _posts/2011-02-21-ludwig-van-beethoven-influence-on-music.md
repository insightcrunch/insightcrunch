## A giant walking behind him

In the early 1870s a composer in his forties, the most celebrated German musician of his generation, was keeping symphony sketches he had begun before he turned twenty-five and could not bring himself to finish. Friends had seen the material. Parts of it had already escaped into other works, a piano concerto and an orchestral serenade, as though the symphony kept dissolving into safer shapes each time he approached it directly.

<!--more-->

![Beethoven influence on music and the burden his reputation laid on the composers who followed him - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-68.webp)

The composer was Johannes Brahms, and the reason for the paralysis is preserved in the biography written by his friend Max Kalbeck. Brahms told the conductor Hermann Levi that he would never understand what it meant to keep hearing a giant marching behind him. The giant had been dead for more than four decades. His name was Ludwig van Beethoven, and no single episode shows Beethoven influence on music more plainly, because nothing in it concerns notes. Brahms commanded every technique the orchestra of his day possessed. What he lacked, for roughly twenty years, was permission from himself to set a symphony beside nine particular scores and let the comparison happen.

The first symphony was finally performed at Karlsruhe on 4 November 1876, conducted by Otto Dessoff, with the composer forty-three years old and famous across Europe for everything except the one genre that mattered most to him. So the opening question of this article is a genuine one, and it will not be answered in full until the eleventh section, where Brahms gets his own hearing. Who was the giant, and why did a mature composer, armed with complete mastery and an established reputation, freeze in front of him?

The delay cannot be explained by obscurity or by want of success. By the early 1870s Brahms had given the world A German Requiem, the Alto Rhapsody, two orchestral serenades, a piano concerto, the variations on a theme of Handel and the variations on a theme of Haydn. He had publishers competing for his work and a city, Vienna, treating him as its resident master. He also had a prophecy hanging over him. In October 1853, in the journal he had founded, Robert Schumann had announced the twenty-year-old Brahms to the musical public as a genius of the first order, a herald of the art to come. Schumann's endorsement was the making of the young man's name and the fixing of his burden, because the public that believed the prophecy expected its fulfilment in exactly one genre. A symphony was the document everyone was waiting for, and everyone said so.

The claim defended across the thirty-four sections that follow is the one the Brahms episode suggests. Beethoven's deepest influence was not on how music was written but on what music was taken to be. After him, the concert audience expected a symphony to mean something, and every composer who followed had to deal with that expectation whether or not they wanted it. Techniques could be borrowed or refused, and later sections will name both, precisely. The expectation could not be refused, because it lived in the listeners, in the critics, in the institutions that printed the programmes and built the halls. That is the sense in which a dead composer went on governing the behaviour of people who had never heard a note of his played in his lifetime.

## What kind of influence a reputation can be

Influence is a word that covers at least four different transactions, and the failure to separate them is responsible for most of the loose talk on this subject. A composer can take over a technique, as Schubert took over the expansion of the symphonic finale. A composer can adopt a formal model, as Mendelssohn answered the choral finale of the Ninth with a choral finale of his own. A composer can be measured against a standard he did not choose, as Brahms was measured from the day Robert Schumann announced him to the public in 1853. And a composer can inherit an institution, a concert culture that has already decided what a serious work sounds like before a note is played. Beethoven operates in all four registers, but not equally, and the argument of this article is that the last two matter more than the first two.

Here the first finding of the article should be stated plainly, because everything else in it is evidence for it. The influence most often described in the letters, diaries and memoirs of the composers who followed Beethoven was a burden rather than a resource. Brahms is the famous case, but he is not an eccentric one. Composer after composer describes the same configuration: gratitude for the example, resentment of the comparison, and a long strategic effort to find ground the older man had not already occupied. The inheritance arrived as a debt before it arrived as a gift, and the debt was charged not by Beethoven, who was beyond charging anything, but by the audience and the press, who had learned from his career what a composer was supposed to be.

Scott Burnham, in his 1995 book Beethoven Hero, gave this configuration its most precise scholarly description. Burnham argued that the heroic style of Beethoven's middle period became, for the listeners and critics who canonised it, the very definition of what music at its most serious does: a protagonist-like musical process struggles, suffers and arrives. Once that process had been installed as the norm, every later symphony was heard as either reproducing it or failing to. Mark Evan Bonds reached a complementary conclusion in After Beethoven, his 1996 study of originality in the symphony. Bonds showed that Beethoven's example created an imperative his successors could not escape: a new symphony had to acknowledge the tradition Beethoven represented and simultaneously prove that it was not merely repeating him. Debt and novelty were demanded in the same breath, by the same listeners, in the same review columns.

This is why the present article spends so many of its sections in concert halls, editorial offices and committee rooms rather than at the keyboard. The downstream of Beethoven runs through the programme note and the subscription series, through the complete edition and the conductor's podium, through a superstition about the number nine and through the loudspeakers of governments. Those are not decorations on the influence. Measured by their effect on what got written, performed and heard, they are a large part of the influence itself. The scores matter, and sections on Schubert, Berlioz, Liszt, Brahms, Bruckner, Mahler and Tchaikovsky will specify, work by work, what was taken and what was refused. But the scores were received by a culture Beethoven had already retrained, and the retraining is the deeper story.

One discipline will be kept throughout. Every claim of influence made here names the technique or the expectation involved, the works on both sides of the transaction, and the date on which the later musician could first have encountered the earlier one. Claims that cannot meet that standard are examined separately, in the section on overstatement, where they belong. Influence asserted without evidence is not influence but weather: it is in the air, it explains everything, and it can be tested against nothing.


## The immediate Vienna successors

Beethoven died in Vienna on 26 March 1827, and the question of succession was asked in the city at once, in those terms, by people who had known him. The documented career, set out in the companion account of [Beethoven's life and career](/2011/01/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-life-and-career/), had ended with a decade in which the composer had withdrawn from performance, communicated through written conversation books, and produced the late quartets that even sympathetic musicians found bewildering. Whoever inherited his position inherited that bewilderment as well as the fame, and the first finding of the succession is that no single figure in Vienna was able or willing to take the whole of it up.

The practical transmission ran through the pupils. Ferdinand Ries had studied with Beethoven in the first years of the century and carried his manner to London and the Rhineland, writing symphonies and piano concertos that adopt the older man's scale and rhetoric at one remove. Carl Czerny, who had been presented to Beethoven as a boy and studied with him in earnest, became the great relay station of the tradition: Czerny performed the piano concertos, edited the keyboard music with a performer's knowledge, and taught the generation after him, most consequentially Franz Liszt, who received Beethoven's pianism at second hand in Czerny's Vienna studio in the early 1820s. Through Czerny the chain of physical transmission, fingerings, tempi, habits of touch remembered from the source, runs unbroken into the virtuoso age.

Outside the pupil line, Vienna offered talent without succession. Johann Nepomuk Hummel, once a rival of Beethoven's in the city's esteem and the pallbearer generation's most polished pianist-composer, was working in Weimar and Stuttgart and died in 1837 with his reputation already cooling. The city's orchestral life had no permanent concert institution of the kind that would later define it; the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde mounted performances, the Philharmonic as a self-governing orchestra did not exist until 1842, and the symphony as a genre had no Viennese champion of the first rank in the decade after 1827. The immediate successors, in other words, divided the estate among them. The pupils took the technique. The publishers took the catalogue. The public took the memory. Nobody took the position, and the position, a living composer whose new symphony is the central event of the musical year, stood vacant in the city where it had been created.

That vacancy is itself a datum about how this influence worked. Beethoven's example had raised the definition of the position faster than any working musician could fill it. His late works had also made the terms of succession unusually hard to read: the composer who died in 1827 was simultaneously the author of the Ninth Symphony, performed to acclaim in 1824, and of quartets that the critic Adolf Bernhard Marx and others received as the products of a mind sealed off from the world. Successors could attach themselves to the public Beethoven or to the private one, and the choice between those two inheritances organises much of what the following sections describe.

The performance tradition in Vienna was kept warm by one ensemble in particular. Ignaz Schuppanzigh, the violinist who had led the premieres of Beethoven's quartets since the 1790s and whose quartet the composer treated as an extension of his desk, returned from Russia in 1823 and established subscription quartet concerts in the city, the first regular public series of their kind. His players gave the first performances of the late quartets in 1825 and 1826, works written for their hands, and Schuppanzigh's Sunday morning concerts made chamber music a public institution in Vienna in the years when the symphony had no champion. The downstream ran through rooms like his as much as through manifestos: a quartet of professionals, a paying audience seated to listen, a repertoire treated as permanent. Vienna invented that format around Beethoven's most difficult music before it had a hall for his symphonies.

## Schubert, working in the same city

Franz Schubert spent the whole of Beethoven's final decade in Vienna, a young composer of songs and chamber music living a few streets from the older man, and the relationship between them is the first case in this article where the evidence has to be handled with open suspicion. The beloved story, that Schubert visited the dying Beethoven in March 1827 and that Beethoven, shown the songs, recognised a divine spark in their author, comes down through Anton Schindler and the Hüttenbrenner brothers. Schindler's testimony cannot be taken at face value on any point where his own importance is concerned: his falsification of entries in Beethoven's conversation books after the composer's death is documented, and the Schubert visit story flatters everyone involved in the telling. It is treated here as a subject of the reception history, not as a source for an event. What is documented, without intermediaries, is paper. In 1822 Schubert dedicated his Variations on a French Song for piano duet, published as his Opus 10, to Beethoven, a public act of homage from a twenty-five-year-old to the reigning master of the city. Whether the two men ever had a conversation of consequence is not established by any record that survives scrutiny.

The influence that can be tested is in the scores, and it passes the test this article applies. Schubert's Symphony in C major, the work known as the Great, was composed in 1825, a year after the premiere of Beethoven's Ninth in Vienna in May 1824, a performance Schubert, resident in the city and moving in its concert life, had every opportunity to hear and none to ignore. The Great C major attempts what no Schubert work had attempted before: a symphony of Beethovenian duration and public rhetoric, its finale built on driving rhythms and a cumulative momentum that answers the finale of Beethoven's Seventh Symphony and the choral ambition of the Ninth in instrumental terms. What Schubert took was scale and ambition. What he refused was the older man's developmental violence. Where Beethoven's themes are broken down and rebuilt, Schubert's themes are stated, circled, harmonically re-lit and allowed to expand by repetition, a lyric method stretched to symphonic length rather than replaced by a dramatic one.

The same transaction appears in the late chamber music. The String Quintet in C major of 1828, the last autumn of Schubert's life, adopts the gravity and the dimensions of the late Beethoven quartets while filling them with song. Schubert died in November 1828, aged thirty-one, nineteen months after Beethoven, and was buried, at his own wish as his brother Ferdinand reported it, near Beethoven in the Währing cemetery. The Vienna that buried them side by side had already begun the work of pairing them, and the pairing itself became a mechanism of influence: Schubert's music entered the later nineteenth century framed as the lyric alternative inside a story Beethoven dominated, a framing Schumann would make permanent when he rescued the Great C major from a drawer in 1839.

Schubert's case matters to the argument because it shows the burden operating at the closest possible range, in the same city and the same decade, on the one contemporary whose gifts were of the same order. Even he did not experience Beethoven as a set of techniques to be used. He experienced him as the definition of what a symphony was supposed to attempt, and he bent his own, opposite gift toward that definition at the cost, in the Great C major, of a work his contemporaries found unplayable for its length. The expectation reached him before any influence on his harmony or his forms did, and it reached him while the older man was still alive to embody it.


## Berlioz and the programmatic inheritance

France received Beethoven late and then all at once. The symphonies were not a Parisian habit in the 1820s; the Société des Concerts du Conservatoire, founded in 1828 under the conductor François-Antoine Habeneck, made them one, performing the works season after season to an audience learning a repertoire nobody in France had grown up with. In the front of that audience, night after night, sat Hector Berlioz, a medical student turned composer who described the encounters as overwhelming, and who converted the experience into the founding gesture of French romantic music. The Symphonie fantastique of 1830, written when Berlioz was twenty-six and premiered in December of that year, is unthinkable without the Conservatoire Beethoven performances of the two preceding seasons, and Berlioz never pretended otherwise.

What Berlioz took can be named exactly. From Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony he took the licence for a symphony to carry an explicit programme, a sequence of scenes the listener is told about in advance, and from the Ninth he took something larger: the conviction that a symphony could be the vehicle for an idea too big for any other genre. What he added was the idée fixe, a melody assigned to an obsession and dragged through every movement in changing orchestral dress, and the willingness to let a literary subject govern the form outright. What he refused is just as specific. Berlioz had no patience for the Germanic religion of abstract development, and his harmony, orchestration and forms follow theatrical rather than Viennese logic. In Harold in Italy of 1834 and above all in Roméo et Juliette of 1839, his self-described dramatic symphony, he answered the Ninth's boldest stroke by building an entire work around voices used as atmosphere and argument, while withholding intelligible words from most of it. It is the Ninth read by a man of the theatre: the choral finale treated not as a destination but as a permission.

Berlioz also became, in print, one of Beethoven's principal French advocates, and his criticism matters to the institutional side of this article. His essays on the symphonies, written across the 1830s and after and collected in his volume À travers chants in 1862, taught two generations of French readers how to listen to the nine works movement by movement. In those essays the Beethoven who emerges is a poet of the orchestra, a dramatist in tones, which was exactly the Beethoven France needed in order to claim him.

His advocacy extended to the works Paris was least ready for. The late quartets, dismissed in Vienna itself as the products of a deaf man's isolation, found in Berlioz a defender who treated their difficulty as evidence of their seriousness and returned to them in print across his career. He conducted the symphonies himself in the great Paris concerts he organised in the 1840s, in halls seating thousands, treating Beethoven as the box-office foundation on which new French music could be floated. And his devotion had a characteristic edge to it: the Beethoven Berlioz worshipped was a rebel against routine, against the fugue, against the conservatory rulebook, which is to say a Beethoven conscripted into the French romantic wars. The conscription was open and it was effective. France got the Beethoven it needed, and the Beethoven it got then influenced French music in France's own image, a recursion the Wagner sections will show operating at a far larger scale. The transaction is the pattern of this whole middle part of the article: each nation and each strong successor rebuilt Beethoven in the shape of their own project, and the rebuilt figure then exerted the influence, often more powerfully than the scores alone could have done.

## Schumann as critic and advocate

Robert Schumann inherited Beethoven twice over, once as a composer and once as the most gifted music journalist of his generation, and the second inheritance did more historical work than the first. In 1834, aged twenty-three, he founded the Neue Zeitschrift für Musik in Leipzig, a journal created in explicit opposition to the shallow reviewing of the day, and through its pages, under his own name and under the masks of his invented Davidsbund personae, he conducted a campaign for a serious musical culture with Beethoven as its presiding standard. To be reviewed by Schumann was to be measured against Beethoven, and the measurement was not casual: it was the explicit test of whether new music deserved to exist.

His greatest act of advocacy was performed on a manuscript. Visiting Vienna in the winter of 1838 and 1839, Schumann called on Ferdinand Schubert and was shown the score of his dead brother's Great C major Symphony, unpublished and unperformed for more than a decade. Schumann recognised what it was, carried the news back to Leipzig, arranged for Mendelssohn to conduct the work at the Gewandhaus in March 1839, and then wrote the review that fixed Schubert's symphonic reputation. In that review he praised the work's heavenly length, a phrase that converted its greatest liability, its sheer duration, into its meaning: length as abundance, the Beethovenian dimension achieved by lyric rather than dramatic means. In a single episode Schumann the critic had enlarged the Beethoven succession, supplied it with a lyric wing, and demonstrated the power a critic now wielded to make a dead composer canonical and a living reputation after the fact.

As a composer, Schumann's own negotiation with the inheritance was hurried where Brahms's would be slow. His four symphonies were written between 1841 and 1851, the First arriving in the flood of composition that followed his marriage, and they accept the Beethovenian frame, four movements, heroic rhetoric, cyclic recall of themes, while filling it with the intimate, fragmentary lyricism of his piano music. What he took was the frame and the licence. What he could not take was the older man's command of large-scale momentum, and his orchestral writing was criticised for it in his lifetime and revised, by himself and after his death by Mahler, ever since.

The deepest of his interventions, though, was the act of prophecy already described in the first section. The 1853 article Neue Bahnen, New Paths, in which Schumann announced Brahms to the world, was an act of Beethoven succession performed in print: the critic who had crowned Schubert after death now crowned a twenty-year-old in life, and specified the terms, that the newcomer's destiny was to give ideal expression to his time as Beethoven had to his. Schumann died in 1856, three years after publishing it. The prophecy governed the expectations of everyone who reviewed Brahms for the next forty years, none more than Brahms himself. A critic had used Beethoven's name as an instrument, and the instrument worked.


## Mendelssohn, revival and the performing tradition

Felix Mendelssohn's historical importance to this subject is easy to understate because his own music is so poised, but he was, more than any other single figure, the man who converted Beethoven from a recent memory into a permanent institution. As conductor of the Leipzig Gewandhaus concerts from 1835 until his death in 1847, Mendelssohn built the modern concert repertoire in practice: programmes organised around the symphonies of the dead masters, performed as complete works, in a hall where listening to them had become the central civic ritual of a musical city. His famous revival of Bach's St Matthew Passion in Berlin in 1829, when he was twenty, belongs to the Bach story, but its method is the same one he applied to Beethoven: the past treated not as a museum but as a living repertoire that the present had a duty to perform well.

His own answer to the Ninth is the clearest single document of the transaction in his generation. The Lobgesang, Hymn of Praise, performed at Leipzig in June 1840 for the festival marking four centuries of printing, is a symphony in three orchestral movements followed by a long choral cantata, and it was published as his Symphony No. 2. The design copies the Ninth's trajectory, instruments first and voices at the climax, while the content reverses its meaning: where Beethoven's voices celebrate a brotherhood of man arrived at through struggle, Mendelssohn's set biblical texts in a spirit of Protestant ceremony. What he took was the choral symphony as public utterance. What he refused was the heroic individual's rhetoric, the music of conflict and self-assertion that Burnham identified as the heroic style's core. Mendelssohn's Beethoven is the disciplined classic, the master of form and clarity, and through the Gewandhaus, the Leipzig Conservatory he founded in 1843, and his enormous English reputation, that edited Beethoven became the Beethoven of the Victorian world.

The editing had consequences that lasted. A Beethoven framed as the supreme classic, the guarantor of order and taste, could be used by institutions as a standard of respectability, and was. The performing tradition Mendelssohn helped to found also fixed practical habits: the complete symphony performed without cuts or interpolations, the conductor as the work's custodian rather than its presenter, the audience expected to follow an argument across forty minutes. His programming method deserves a closer look, because it became the century's default. Where an earlier concert had mixed novelties and favourites in no particular order, Mendelssohn arranged historical programmes that surveyed the past as a connected narrative, with Beethoven at its summit: a concert in such a series was an argument that music had a history, that the history had a shape, and that the shape pointed at the nine symphonies. The Leipzig Conservatory exported the method in its graduates, young musicians from across Europe and America who carried the canon, and its Beethoven, home with them. Mendelssohn died in 1847, aged thirty-eight, worn out by work. By then the machinery he had built, subscription concerts, a conservatory, a canon, a reverent public, was running on its own, and Beethoven was its presiding exhibit.

## Liszt, transcription and the piano inheritance

Franz Liszt encountered Beethoven through people before he encountered him on the page. His teacher in Vienna in the early 1820s was Carl Czerny, Beethoven's own pupil, and the boy's education was saturated with the sonatas and concertos from the start. The encounter became a life's programme. As the most famous pianist in Europe, Liszt performed the concertos, above all the Emperor, in an age when Beethoven's piano music was still considered severe; as a conductor at Weimar in the 1850s he programmed the symphonies; and as a private act of devotion that became a public utility, he transcribed all nine symphonies for solo piano, work spread across decades and published complete by Breitkopf and Härtel in 1865. The transcriptions matter beyond their virtuosity. In an age before recording, they put the nine symphonies into the hands of anyone in Europe who owned a piano and could play at Liszt's level of difficulty or aspire to it, and they established the principle that these works were a complete body, a testament, to be known entire.

His most public act of Beethoven service was the Bonn monument. When the project to honour Beethoven in his birthplace faltered for lack of money in the early 1840s, Liszt supplied the greater part of the funds from his own concert earnings, organised the festival of August 1845 at which the statue was unveiled, and conducted and composed for the occasion. The boy who had been kissed, in the story his admirers told, by the aged Beethoven had become the impresario of Beethoven's marble afterlife. The anecdote of the kiss is not documented and belongs to the mythology; the bank drafts are documented, and they are the more interesting fact, because they show the virtuoso economy of the new musical world being spent, deliberately, on the cult of the old one.

What Liszt took for his own music was the piano itself as Beethoven had reimagined it. The sonatas, and above all the Hammerklavier, Opus 106, which Liszt was among the first to perform in public in the 1830s when it was widely held to be unplayable, taught him that the instrument could sustain symphonic argument. His Sonata in B minor of 1853, a single span of music in which a handful of themes generate an entire drama, is the Hammerklavier's lesson carried into his own language, and his symphonic poems took from the Ninth and from the overtures the principle that an orchestral work could be organised by an idea. What he refused was the four-movement contract itself. Where Brahms would serve the old forms under protest, Liszt dissolved them, and the dissolution, like the service, was done in Beethoven's name.

The service continued in the classroom. At Weimar after 1848, where Liszt presided over the court theatre and the most famous piano studio in Europe, the Beethoven sonatas stood at the centre of the curriculum his pupils carried away, and his own edition work and teaching notes treated the thirty-two as the foundation on which a pianist's musicianship, not merely his technique, was to be built. The generation of virtuosi formed at Weimar, Hans von Bülow first among them, grew up inside that settlement: Beethoven as the daily bread of the advanced pianist, the measure of the instrument's seriousness, the composer a young player had to come to terms with before being licensed to have opinions about anyone else. The pedagogical machine described in the later section on the sonatas had many builders, but its most glamorous recruiting officer was Liszt.


## Wagner's claim on the Ninth

Richard Wagner made the most consequential claim on Beethoven ever lodged by a successor, and he made it early, in public, with a concert. In April 1846, conducting the Ninth Symphony at Dresden in his post as a Saxon court Kapellmeister, the thirty-two-year-old Wagner published a programme for the performance that supplied the finale with quotations from Goethe's Faust, guiding the audience to hear the work as a drama of despair overcome by joy. Three years later, in exile after the Dresden uprising, he began the sequence of theoretical writings, The Artwork of the Future in 1849, Opera and Drama in 1851, in which the Ninth was assigned its famous role: the point at which instrumental music reached its limit, confessed its insufficiency, and reached for the word. On Wagner's reading, Beethoven himself had pronounced the verdict on the symphony, and the future belonged to the art form that would take the hint, the music drama, whose only serious practitioner was Richard Wagner.

The companion account of [Wagner's life and music](/2018/03/07/richard-wagner-life-and-music/) tells the career in full; what matters here is the structure of the claim. Wagner did not present himself as Beethoven's continuator in the symphony, a competition he would have had to enter on the older man's ground against Mendelssohn and Schumann. He presented himself as Beethoven's executor, the heir who understood what the estate was really for. The Ninth's choral finale, on this account, was not a solution to a compositional problem but a prophecy, and the twenty-two years between the symphony and Opera and Drama were simply the time the world needed to produce the prophet's interpreter. It was a magnificent piece of intellectual capture, and its success can be measured by how thoroughly it set the terms in which the Ninth was discussed for the next hundred years.

Wagner renewed the claim at each stage of his career. In 1870, the centenary of Beethoven's birth, he published his essay Beethoven, which deepened the argument with the help of Schopenhauer's philosophy: Beethoven's music, and the Ninth above all, revealed music as the direct voice of the world's inner nature, and the deaf composer as a clairvoyant saint of art. In May 1872, when the foundation stone of his festival theatre at Bayreuth was laid, the ceremony was crowned by a performance of the Ninth conducted by Wagner himself, the older master's most public work conscripted to bless the building in which the heir's works would be housed. The following year his essay on the performance of the Ninth proposed alterations to its orchestration, doublings and rebalancings intended to clarify what he held to be the work's intentions, intentions the score's own notation was too primitive, in his account, to convey. Interpretation had become revision, offered as fidelity.

The claim was never uncontested. Eduard Hanslick, Vienna's leading critic and Wagner's most formidable opponent, read the same Ninth and heard a great symphony endangered by its finale rather than a symphony redeemed by it, and the quarrel between the Wagnerian and Hanslickian Beethovens structured musical politics in German-speaking Europe for two generations. But the Wagnerian reading had the better story, and stories govern reception. To the end of the nineteenth century and well into the twentieth, the educated listener who knew one thing about the Ninth knew Wagner's thing: that Beethoven had broken the symphony open so that drama could be born.

## What Wagner needed Beethoven to be

It is worth pausing over the mechanics of the capture, because the next finding of this article depends on it. Wagner's reading of the Ninth was built to justify his own project, and it became the standard interpretation for a century. That is not an accusation of bad faith; Wagner believed his account, and parts of it are profound. It is a statement about how influence actually travels. The Beethoven who influenced the later nineteenth century was in large measure the Beethoven Wagner described, a Beethoven filtered through the needs of the most ambitious musician alive, and the filter was not removed when the description was quoted by people who had never read a note of the score.

Consider what the reading required Wagner to emphasise and what it required him to suppress. He needed the first three movements of the Ninth to represent a striving that only the voice could resolve, so those movements are treated in his writings as stages of a single spiritual argument, their purely musical procedures, the scherzo's rhythmic games, the slow movement's double variations, passed over as preliminaries. He needed Beethoven's deafness to be a metaphysical credential rather than a biographical catastrophe, so it becomes one. He needed the succession from Haydn through Mozart to Beethoven to terminate in a crisis, so the flourishing concert culture of Mendelssohn, Schumann and the Gewandhaus, which was busy proving the symphony alive and well, had to be written down as epigonal, a decline from the peak. And he needed Beethoven's own operatic achievement, Fidelio, one opera wrestled into shape across a decade, to stand as evidence that the dramatic instinct in him was straining toward a form he could not yet build, rather than as evidence of how completely his genius belonged to the concert hall.

Esteban Buch, whose political history of the Ninth appeared in French in 1999 and in English in 2003, documented the longer consequence: once the Ninth had been established as a work whose meaning was its message, the message became detachable and reusable by causes Beethoven never heard of, the subject of a later section of this article. Nicholas Cook, in his 1993 handbook on the symphony, traced the same process from the other side, showing how the finale's reception was dominated by the question Wagner had planted, whether the voices represent a triumph or a failure of nerve, so that even listeners who rejected Wagner's answer went on arguing about Wagner's question. That is what it means for an interpretation to become standard: not that everyone agrees with it, but that everyone is obliged to have a position on it.

The general lesson extends well beyond Wagner. Influence between composers is rarely a transaction between two scores. It is a transaction between a score and a story told about a score, and the stories are told by interested parties: by successors who need ancestors, by critics who need narratives, by institutions that need patron saints. Beethoven's case is extreme only in scale. His music was strong enough, and his biography stark enough, to sustain almost any story told about it, and the strongest storyteller of the nineteenth century reached him first and told the story that pointed at himself.


## Brahms and the twenty-year delay

The loop opened in the first section can now be closed, because the giant has a name, a bibliography and a mechanism. The giant was Beethoven, and the mechanism was not imitation but audit. Johannes Brahms had absorbed Beethoven's music earlier and more thoroughly than any musician of his generation. He had been raised on the sonatas, he played the concertos, he collected the autographs, he was consulted about the texts, and he had modelled his earliest published works, the piano sonatas of his teens and twenties, openly on the Hammerklavier's example. He knew precisely what the nine symphonies had done and what a tenth claimant would be judged against. That knowledge, and not any deficiency of power, is what froze him, and the delay it produced is the third finding of this article: Brahms held back his first symphony for roughly two decades, and said in plain terms, to Hermann Levi and through the record Kalbeck preserved, exactly why.

The chronology is documented in his letters and in the recollections of the Schumann circle. A symphony movement in C minor existed by 1854, in the aftermath of Robert Schumann's collapse, when the twenty-one-year-old Brahms was holding the Schumann household together in Düsseldorf. The movement would not settle. Its material was salvaged twice, first into a sonata for two pianos and then, transposed and rebuilt, into the opening of the Piano Concerto in D minor, performed in Hanover and Leipzig in 1859 to a reception cold enough to teach its composer caution. In 1862 he sent Clara Schumann a symphony first movement that she noted in her correspondence, and then the record goes quiet for a decade. The quiet was not idleness. The German Requiem of 1868 made his European reputation without entering the forbidden genre; the orchestral serenades, the Handel variations, the Haydn variations were, among their other purposes, a long technical apprenticeship served in public, each one testing an orchestral skill the symphony would need.

### How did Beethoven influence Brahms?

Beethoven influenced Brahms as both model and obstacle. Brahms studied the symphonies and quartets all his life, took from them his methods of developing variation and his four-movement symphonic frame, and delayed his own first symphony for roughly twenty years because the comparison terrified him.

The fact deserves to be stated flatly, because it is the shareable core of this whole subject. Brahms waited until he was in his forties to release his first symphony, having worked toward it for roughly twenty years, and described the pressure of Beethoven's reputation in his own correspondence and conversation, in terms his friends recorded. Composers had feared comparison before; vanity and rivalry are old. What was new was the structure of the fear. Brahms was not afraid of Beethoven's admirers, or of the critics, though both were lying in wait. He was afraid of the standard as he had internalised it, of the audit he would conduct on himself before anyone else could. The expectation described in the thesis of this article had moved inside the composer's own study, and it worked there for twenty years.

When the First Symphony finally arrived, at Karlsruhe in November 1876 under Otto Dessoff and in Vienna the following month under Hans Richter, the reception performed the succession in public exactly as the prophecy of 1853 had scripted it. Hans von Bülow, the most feared conductor-critic in Germany, hailed the work as the Tenth Symphony, meaning the first legitimate successor to the nine. The compliment was also a cage, and Brahms knew it; the companion account of [Brahms's life and music](/2018/05/07/johannes-brahms-life-and-music/) traces what the cage cost him across the remaining twenty years of his career. He went on to write three more symphonies, each one a fresh negotiation with the same ghost, and none of them delayed as the First had been. The giant, once faced in the open, became a working condition rather than a prohibition. That transition, from prohibition to condition, is the shape the Beethoven burden takes in its most gifted bearer, and it is why his case, rather than any lesser composer's paralysis, is the measure of the phenomenon.

## What Brahms took and what he avoided

The audit metaphor can be made literal in Brahms's case, because the borrowings are itemised in the scores. What he took first was a method: developing variation, the practice of generating an entire movement from the continuous transformation of a small cell of notes, which Arnold Schoenberg, in his 1947 essay Brahms the Progressive, identified as the centre of Brahms's craft and traced directly to Beethoven's example. The opening of the First Symphony, its pounding timpani and its rising chromatic line, is a demonstration: a germinal figure that the whole movement will spend itself exploring. He took, second, the four-movement frame with a finale built to bear the work's full argumentative weight, and in that finale he placed a broad, hymn-like theme in C major whose resemblance to the Ninth's Ode to Joy theme every listener in 1876 noticed. When a friend taxed him with the likeness, Kalbeck reports, Brahms shrugged that it was plain enough for any fool to see. The remark is the opposite of theft discovered. It is citation acknowledged: the heir signing the register in full view, and daring the world to call the signature a forgery.

He took, third, the older man's route back into the Baroque. The finale of the Fourth Symphony of 1885 is a passacaglia, thirty variations over a ground bass adapted from Bach, and it reaches that archaic severity by way of Beethoven's own variation practices, the Eroica finale and the Diabelli Variations having already proved that variation form could carry a symphony's climax. Brahms's archaicism was a strategy as much as a taste. By grounding his most ambitious structures in Bach and Handel, composers Beethoven himself had revered, he found a line of descent that passed behind Beethoven's shoulder. He could be traditional in a way that was older than the tradition that was crushing him.

What he avoided is as consistent. He wrote no opera, refusing the genre in which Wagner had claimed the succession. He wrote no programme symphony and no symphonic poem, and in the War of the Romantics, the pamphlet conflict that divided German music in the 1860s, his name headed the manifesto of 1860 that protested against the claims of the New German School, a document he later regretted signing but never replaced with its opposite. He admitted no voices into his symphonies, closing the door the Ninth had opened. He kept biography out of the scores: no movement of Brahms narrates his loves, his losses or his reading, whatever the temptations of interpreters then and since. And he cultivated, in dress, in manners and in his famous gruffness, a public self that was the anti-Beethoven of the romantic imagination, bourgeois, ironic and unheroic. The avoidance and the borrowing are the same gesture read from two sides. Brahms intended to inherit the craft and decline the mythology, and the four symphonies are the record of how nearly, and at what cost, a great composer can do exactly that.

The negotiation was conducted in public as well as on paper. Between 1872 and 1875 Brahms directed the concerts of Vienna's Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde, the closest thing the city had to an orchestral throne, and from that podium he programmed the classics, Beethoven's symphonies prominently among them, with the authority of a custodian. The heir apparent spent three seasons performing the inheritance for the Viennese public while his own first symphony stayed in the drawer. No anecdote in this article captures the structure of his position better: the man most qualified to succeed Beethoven was employed, in the interim, as one of the high priests of Beethoven's cult, and the priesthood and the paralysis were not separate facts about him. They were the same fact, seen from the podium and from the desk.


## The nine-symphony superstition

### What is the curse of the ninth symphony?

The curse of the ninth is the belief that a composer who completes nine symphonies will die before finishing a tenth, as Beethoven did. It is a pattern assembled by critics and journalists after the fact, sustained by selective counting, and it has no explanatory force, though Gustav Mahler took it seriously enough to leave his ninth symphony unnumbered.

The roll call on which the superstition rests is worth stating, because it dissolves under inspection. Beethoven completed nine symphonies and died in 1827 with sketches for a tenth on his desk. Schubert left the same magic number only under one of several numbering schemes: the Great C major is his ninth, seventh or eighth depending on which edition and which fragments a scholar admits to the count. Anton Bruckner died in 1896 with his Ninth unfinished, which supports the pattern only by changing the charge from completing nine to attempting one. Antonín Dvořák's New World Symphony is his ninth only because four early symphonies, long thought lost, were restored to the catalogue in the twentieth century; in his own lifetime it was published and performed as his fifth. The pattern, in other words, is not found in the record. It is manufactured by choosing the counting scheme that produces it, which is why this article treats the curse throughout as a critical and journalistic construct, a piece of reception machinery, rather than as a fact about composers.

One composer did take it seriously, and his seriousness is the superstition's real substance. Gustav Mahler, who had completed eight numbered symphonies by 1907, called his next symphonic work Das Lied von der Erde, The Song of the Earth, and published it as a song-symphony without a number, a dodge his circle understood perfectly. He then wrote a Ninth Symphony, remarking to his wife Alma, in her later account, that the danger was now past because the unnumbered work had absorbed the fatal count. He died in May 1911 with a Tenth Symphony in draft. Arnold Schoenberg, writing on Mahler in 1912, gave the superstition its canonical statement, suggesting that the ninth symphony stood as a limit no composer since Beethoven had been permitted to pass. The essay is moving, and it is also a specimen of the machinery at work: a great composer-critic consecrating a pattern his own grief had selected.

Against the pattern stand the uncounted majorities. Joseph Haydn wrote more than a hundred symphonies and died at seventy-seven. Mozart wrote more than forty. Dmitri Shostakovich, working in the full glare of the Beethoven tradition and under pressures Mahler never faced, wrote fifteen symphonies and died after completing them all. Ralph Vaughan Williams finished nine symphonies and then lived on, dying in 1958 at the age of eighty-five with the ink on the Ninth barely dry and no tenth attempted for reasons of age rather than dread. The base rates explain everything the curse pretends to explain: composers who wrote nine symphonies were usually old, orchestral careers are long, and death is not a critic. What the superstition actually measures is the strength of Beethoven's position in the culture. No other composer generated a fatal number. The nine symphonies had become a unit of fate because they had first become the unit of the art.

## Bruckner, Mahler and the inherited scale

Anton Bruckner began his symphonic career even later than Brahms, in his forties, and under a double allegiance that defines his case: he worshipped Wagner and he worshipped Beethoven, and his symphonies are the attempt to house the first worship inside the second's architecture. The debt to the Ninth is structural and avowed. Bruckner's symphonies adopt the Beethovenian span, the long first movement built on three themes, the adagio as the work's spiritual centre, the finale as a summation in which earlier material returns transfigured, and they stretch that span across durations the older man never attempted, with an organist's sense of registration and silence. When the remains of Beethoven and Schubert were transferred to Vienna's Central Cemetery in 1888, Bruckner attended, and accounts of the ceremony describe the old man handling the exhumed skull of Beethoven with a devotee's reverence. The anecdote is reported rather than filmed, and it is set down here as the reception historians report it, but nothing in Bruckner's documented life makes it unlikely. His Beethoven was a saint, and his symphonies are offerings laid, movement by movement, on the altar.

What Bruckner refused is equally instructive. He refused the heroic protagonist. Where Beethoven's symphonic drama, on Burnham's reading, enacts a self struggling toward victory, Bruckner's enacts a worshipper approaching a mystery, and the difference is audible in every finale that dissolves into chorale rather than triumph. The inheritance of scale without the inheritance of plot is one of the clearest demonstrations in this article that what passed from Beethoven to his successors was separable: dimensions could be taken, meanings declined.

Mahler, who conducted Bruckner and Beethoven across Europe and America, took the dimensions and restored the plot at an even larger scale. His symphonies quote, answer and argue with Beethoven's throughout: the Second ends with a choral resurrection that is the Ninth's finale rewritten for a more anxious age, the Eighth marshals voices on a scale meant to outnumber the older work's forces, and his performances of the Ninth itself, in Hamburg, Vienna and New York, continued Wagner's practice of retouching the orchestration to suit modern instruments and halls, a liberty he defended as loyalty to the work's effect over its notation. Mahler's famous reported dictum, delivered to Sibelius in Helsinki in 1907 and preserved in Sibelius's account of their conversation, that a symphony must embrace the world, is the expectation of this article's thesis stated as a job description. No composer before Beethoven had been expected to embrace the world in a symphony. After him, the expectation was the price of the genre, and Mahler paid it in full, and then, as the previous section recorded, tried to cheat the numbering of the bill.

The two men's devotions also met on a single stage. In 1873 Bruckner travelled to Bayreuth carrying the scores of his Second and Third symphonies and asked Wagner to choose which of them should bear the dedication; Wagner chose the Third, and Bruckner, overwhelmed, recorded the day in terms of discipleship. The symphony he brought home, saturated with quotations of Wagner's operas inside a Beethovenian frame, was mocked in Vienna as the collision of the two cults, and Hanslick's criticism made the mockery permanent in the press. Yet the collision is precisely the historical point. By 1873 the two inheritances, Beethoven's architecture and Wagner's drama, were the twin poles of symphonic ambition, and a composer of Bruckner's gifts experienced them not as alternatives to be weighed but as authorities to be reconciled, at whatever cost to his own peace. The burden had become structural: it no longer depended on any one man's opinion of his chances.


## Tchaikovsky and the Russian reception

Russia took Beethoven into its institutions before it took him into its heart, and Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's divided response is the most fully documented case in this article of reverence without love. The conservatories founded by Anton Rubinstein in St Petersburg in 1862 and by his brother Nikolai in Moscow in 1866 built their curricula on the German canon, with Beethoven's symphonies and sonatas at its centre, and Tchaikovsky, the Moscow Conservatory's most famous graduate and then its professor, taught inside that canon for a decade. His private writings tell a second story. In his correspondence with his patron Nadezhda von Meck, and in his diary, Tchaikovsky returned repeatedly to the same confession, paraphrased in his letters in several forms: that he bowed before Beethoven's grandeur, prostrated himself before the achievement, and yet could not love the music as he loved Mozart's, which he described in terms of sunlight and tenderness. The distinction he draws is exact, and it matters here. Beethoven was the law. Mozart was the love. A composer can organise a career around either, and Tchaikovsky's symphonies are the record of a man trying to satisfy the law while following the love.

The scores show the negotiation in technical detail. The Fourth Symphony of 1878 opens with a brass fanfare that Tchaikovsky himself, in a letter to von Meck, identified as fate, the implacable force that prevents happiness, and the device is Beethovenian in its function: a motto that interrupts the symphonic argument and demands to be answered, much as the opening of the Fifth Symphony had been heard to do since the earliest critics. The Fifth Symphony of 1888 goes further, running a single motto theme through all four movements in changing characters, a cyclic procedure Tchaikovsky had learned from the Beethoven of the Ninth and the late quartets by way of Berlioz and Liszt. What he refused was the German discipline underneath. His letters complain of the labour in Beethoven's developments even while he submits to their example, and his symphonies solve the problem his own way, by sequence, repetition and orchestral colour where Beethoven would have argued by fragmentation and return.

The Russian reception also supplies one of the clearest cases of Beethoven as an instrument of state culture long before the political section of this article. The Imperial Russian Musical Society's concerts made the nine symphonies the core of orchestral life in both capitals; Beethoven's music was the measure by which Russian orchestras proved themselves European. When Russian composers of the nationalist circle, Balakirev's pupils, resisted the conservatory canon, it was Beethoven they had to resist, and even their resistance was shaped by him: Mussorgsky's and Borodin's symphonic ambitions were formed in the shadow of the same nine works their teachers administered. Influence by refusal is still influence, and Russia, which received Beethoven as an imported law, felt the compulsion in its purest form.

Tchaikovsky left the perfect emblem of his divided allegiance in 1887, when he assembled the suite he called Mozartiana, orchestrating four of Mozart's piano pieces with a tenderness and wit that amount to a public declaration of love. No equivalent act of homage to Beethoven exists in his catalogue, though Beethoven's pressure is present in symphony after symphony. The law received the labour; the love received the gift. Documented in the scores and in the correspondence alike, that division is the most honest map this article can offer of how the influence actually felt from inside a major career: not as inspiration, the word the programme notes prefer, but as jurisdiction, a court in which a composer was perpetually on trial before a judge whose music he respected, feared and could not bring himself to adore.

## The symphony as a vehicle for meaning

Underneath every case examined so far sits a change in what a symphony was understood to be, and the change can be dated with unusual precision. In 1810 the Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung published E. T. A. Hoffmann's review of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, the most consequential piece of music criticism ever written. Hoffmann, himself a composer, argued that instrumental music was the most romantic of the arts precisely because it had no subject but the infinite, and that Beethoven's music opened a realm of the monumental and the immeasurable in which the listener's intimations of a higher world were awakened. The review's sentences were quoted, translated and absorbed across Europe for a century. Mark Evan Bonds, in Music as Thought, his 2006 study of listening in the age of Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, showed how this way of hearing was constructed in the decades around 1800: the symphony, a public genre of entertainment, was redefined by critics and philosophers as a vehicle for ideas, a text to be interpreted, and Beethoven was installed as its supreme author.

The consequences compound through everything this article has described. If a symphony is a text, it can be misread, and critics are needed to police the readings; the critical apparatus of a later section follows. If a symphony means something, then a new symphony is a claim about the world, and its composer a thinker, which is the burden Schumann laid on Brahms. If a symphony means something, its meaning can be contested, summarised, taught, and eventually conscripted. And here a third loop of this article must be opened, because its closing lies far ahead, in the section on the Ninth as a political instrument. How does a symphony end up used by governments that disagree with each other about everything else? The question sounds like a paradox of the twentieth century. Its conditions were created in 1810, in a Leipzig journal, by a critic explaining why wordless music was the deepest kind of speech.

The crucial feature of the new understanding was its openness. Hoffmann's Beethoven meant enormously and specified little. The Fifth's famous four-note opening could be heard as fate knocking, because Hoffmann's framework licensed hearing it as something, while neither the score nor the composer supplied an authorised programme to settle what that something was. The Ninth complicated the openness in the most productive possible way. It supplied words, Schiller's ode, brotherhood, joy, the embrace of millions, at the end of three movements of wordless argument, so that the work seemed to reveal its own meaning while leaving the meaning general enough to survive translation into any creed that prized unity and striving. A symphony had become a vessel that nations, movements and institutions could fill. Beethoven did not design it for that purpose, and the later sections will insist on the distance between the work and its uses. But the vessel's shape, public, sublime, underdetermined, hospitable to interpretation, is the form his influence took at the level of culture, beneath every technique his successors borrowed or refused.


## The concert hall as a secular institution

The influence described so far operated on identifiable people. It also operated on rooms, budgets and timetables, and through them on everyone who has attended a concert since. At the start of the nineteenth century the public concert was a sociable miscellany: a programme might offer a symphony movement, an aria, a concerto movement and a popular song in alternation, refreshments were served, audiences arrived and departed during the music, and the social purposes of the evening ranked alongside the musical ones. By the end of the century the same institution had become a rite: complete canonical works, performed in silence, in a hall designed like a temple, by an orchestra of specialists, before an audience whose membership was a mark of cultivation. Beethoven's music was the central exhibit of the transformed institution and one of the principal causes of the transformation, and the mechanism deserves to be traced rather than assumed.

The economic historian of music William Weber documented the repertoire side of the change in his 2008 study of musical taste: across the nineteenth century, programmes in the great musical cities shifted from a majority of works by living composers to a majority of works by dead ones, with Beethoven at the head of the new canon. A repertoire of classics has institutional needs that a repertoire of novelties does not. It needs permanent orchestras kept in training, halls built for permanence, publishers with back catalogues, and an audience educated to want the same works repeatedly. The century supplied all of them: the Vienna Philharmonic constituted itself in 1842, the New York Philharmonic in the same year, the Berlin Philharmonic in 1882; the Musikverein opened in Vienna in 1870, the new Gewandhaus in Leipzig in 1884, the Concertgebouw in Amsterdam in 1888, Symphony Hall in Boston in 1900, each designed, whatever the architect's intention, with the acoustics and the sightlines of a shrine.

Inside those rooms a new etiquette was being enforced, and here the second loop of this article opens in earnest. Why did audiences stop talking during concerts? A Viennese or Parisian audience of 1820 chatted through performances as a matter of course, applauded between movements and during them, demanded encores of single movements, and treated the concert as an evening out among acquaintances. A concert audience of 1900 sat in disciplined silence and regarded a cough as a small social crime. The transformation was not a natural improvement in manners. It was taught, policed and fought over, hall by hall and decade by decade, and the music of Beethoven stands at the centre of the usual explanations, because his works were the ones most often cited by the reformers as demanding, and repaying, a quality of attention the old sociable concert had never asked for. The temple model also had a popular rival, which keeps the story honest: the promenade concerts that began at London's Queen's Hall in 1895 under Robert Newman and the young Henry Wood offered the same canonical repertoire, Beethoven included, at prices working listeners could pay, in a hall where the audience stood, strolled and refreshed itself in the old manner. The Proms prospered, and their survival shows that reverence was one settlement among several rather than a destiny. But the prestige economy belonged to the temple, the critics wrote from the temple, and it was the temple's version of Beethoven, silent, complete and canonical, that the institutions exported. The next section takes the question up directly and gives it the article's fourth finding.

## Silence in the audience and the invention of reverent listening

### Why do audiences sit in silence at concerts?

Audiences sit in silence because nineteenth-century institutions taught them to. Reforming conductors, critics and hall managers suppressed conversation, applause between movements and late seating over several decades, arguing that difficult canonical works, Beethoven's above all, required unbroken attention. Silence was not the discovery of a natural law. It was an etiquette invented to serve a repertoire.

The evidence that the old behaviour was normal is embarrassingly rich. At the premiere of Beethoven's Seventh Symphony in December 1813, in a Vienna benefit concert for wounded soldiers, the audience demanded and received an immediate repetition of the slow movement in the middle of the performance, applause interrupting the work to encore its most beautiful part, and the composer, who conducted, took it as a triumph. Nobody in the hall thought the work had been violated. The idea that a symphony is an inviolable span, to be received entire and in silence, would have struck that audience as a strange asceticism. James H. Johnson, in his 1995 study of listening in Paris, traced the French side of the change across the century: audiences at the Opéra and the Conservatoire concerts moved, between the 1820s and the 1880s, from animated sociability toward the absorbed silence that later generations mistook for the eternal posture of serious listening. Johnson's Parisians did not become better people. They were retrained, by repertoire, by criticism, by theatre management and by each other.

The retraining had instruments. Applause was herded, first to the ends of movements, then, in the most rigorous houses, to the end of the work. Gaslight and then electric light allowed auditoriums to be darkened during performance, and Wagner's Bayreuth festival theatre of 1876, with its darkened house, hidden orchestra and fan-shaped seating that abolished the hierarchy of boxes, supplied the model of attention as devotion. Programme notes, the subject of the next section, instructed listeners in what they were about to hear and, by implication, in how to behave while hearing it. Critics scolded talkers by name in print. And underneath the discipline ran the argument from the music itself: a development section that takes ten minutes to work out its premises punishes the inattentive listener with incomprehension, and Beethoven's music, the longest, densest and most argumentative in the repertoire, was therefore the strongest recruiting sergeant the silence campaign ever had.

So the loop can be closed with the article's fourth finding. The convention of silent, attentive concert listening is substantially a nineteenth-century invention, installed by institutions in the decades after Beethoven's death, and his reputation was its chief warrant. Every modern concertgoer who has been hushed, or has hushed another, is living inside a piece of Beethoven's downstream influence as surely as any composer who borrowed his forms. The expectation had become behaviour; the behaviour had become morality; and the morality now guards the music that created it, in halls where the talkative sociability of Beethoven's own audiences would be treated as a failure to understand Beethoven.


## The programme note and the critical apparatus

The silent audience of the previous section needed something to do with its attention, and the nineteenth century supplied it with homework. The analytical programme note, a printed essay distributed at concerts explaining the work's structure, themes and meaning in advance, was developed into a standard feature of concert life above all in Britain, where George Grove, later the editor of the great dictionary of music that bears his name, wrote notes for the Crystal Palace concerts in London from the 1860s onward that set the model: the themes identified, the movements mapped, the listener equipped to follow the argument like a reader with a commentary. The Viennese and German halls developed their own equivalents, and by the century's end no serious orchestra issued a bare list of works. The programme book had become a small critical journal delivered at the door.

The programme note is easy to mock and important to understand, because it changed who controlled a work's meaning. A symphony encountered cold is an experience; a symphony encountered after two pages of Grove is an experience with a thesis, and Beethoven's symphonies, the most discussed music in the repertoire, accumulated the thickest commentary. Generations learned from programme notes that the Fifth was about fate, that the Eroica had shed its Bonaparte dedication, that the Ninth crowned a life of struggle with universal joy, and those compressed narratives, repeated at every performance, became the default settings of listening. The note also disciplined the audience it informed: a listener who has been told what to listen for has been quietly told to listen. Interpretation had become a branch of publishing, and publishing needs copy forever. Their modern descendant is the pre-concert talk, slides and all; a lecturer preparing one can open the deck in a browser-based presentation viewer such as [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and face the same task Grove faced, compressing a symphony's reputation into the minutes before it sounds.

Around the note grew a full critical apparatus with Beethoven at its centre: the collected edition, the thematic catalogue, the biography industry, the specialist journal, the university seminar. Its organising device, and its most durable legacy, was the three-period scheme, the division of Beethoven's output into an early period of apprenticeship, a middle period of heroic mastery and a late period of inward transcendence. The scheme was popularised by Wilhelm von Lenz in his 1852 book Beethoven et ses trois styles, drawing on suggestions from the Belgian critic François-Joseph Fétis, and it proved indestructible because it converted a catalogue into a story with the shape of a life: struggle, triumph, transfiguration. Composers after Beethoven were then fitted with periods of their own, and music history as taught ever since is written in a grammar the Beethoven apparatus invented to describe its subject.

## Beethoven and the rise of the professional critic

The professional music critic, the salaried journalist whose judgement could make or retard a career, is a nineteenth-century figure, and Beethoven was his founding brief. The Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung of Leipzig, founded in 1798, had shown what a serious musical press could be, and Hoffmann's reviews for it, including the 1810 Fifth Symphony review examined earlier, set the standard of criticism as literature. Through the century the multiplying concert life of Europe generated hundreds of posts for reviewers, and the reviewers, needing copy, standards and copy again, built their authority on the canon they administered. The companion survey of [how critics shaped reputations](/2026/07/21/how-critics-shaped-reputations/) follows the profession across the whole series; here the point is narrower. The critic's power and the Beethoven cult grew up together, each supplying the other's need.

Eduard Hanslick in Vienna is the type specimen. Appointed to the Neue Freie Presse and eventually to a university chair in aesthetics, Hanslick wrote his treatise On the Musically Beautiful in 1854 partly as a defence of Beethoven's absolute music against the Wagnerian claim that the Ninth had superseded it, and he spent a long career adjudicating new music against the classical standard, Brahms upheld, Wagner resisted, Bruckner caught painfully between. In London, George Bernard Shaw, writing music criticism in the 1880s and 1890s under the name Corno di Bassetto, performed the same office with opposite manners, using wit to insist that Beethoven was a living test of musical intelligence rather than a museum ornament. Between Hanslick's professorial authority and Shaw's journalism stands the range of the new profession, but the subject at the centre is the same. A critic writing about almost anything in 1880 was, in the last analysis, writing about the music's distance from Beethoven.

Paris built the same profession on the same subject by a different route. François-Joseph Fétis founded the Revue musicale in 1827 and from 1832 mounted historical concerts in the capital at which the music of earlier masters, Beethoven at their head, was performed as a surveyed inheritance rather than a novelty. Fétis wrote voluminously on Beethoven, corresponded about him, edited and judged him, and modelled for France the figure of the critic as scholar-administrator of a canon. Between Fétis's Parisian enterprise and the Leipzig journals, the infrastructure of modern musical opinion, the review, the historical concert, the biographical dictionary, was substantially complete before 1850, and in both cities it had been erected, in large part, to house one composer's reputation.

The apparatus also professionalised judgement about the past. The great collected biographies, the documentary life of Beethoven assembled by the American Alexander Wheelock Thayer from 1849 onward and published in German between 1866 and 1879, established the modern standard of evidence for a composer's life, archives before anecdotes, and the standard was then applied retroactively to every earlier figure and demanded of every later one. Criticism, scholarship and the concert hall thus formed a single system, and the system's effect on living composers was the burden this article began with. To write a symphony after 1850 was to submit it to institutions that Beethoven's example had called into being and that Beethoven's example still governed. The giant Brahms heard behind him walked, in part, on feet the critics had made for him.


## The complete edition and the authoritative text

A reputation that governs institutions eventually requires a fixed text, and Beethoven's was fixed early and monumentally. Between 1862 and 1865 the Leipzig firm of Breitkopf and Härtel published Ludwig van Beethovens Werke, a complete edition of the works in twenty-four volumes, with a supplementary volume following in 1888. Complete editions were in the air, the Bach Gesellschaft had begun its work in 1851 and Friedrich Chrysander's Handel edition in 1858, but the Beethoven edition had a distinctive cultural function: it defined the canon as a bounded object. The works, all the works, and nothing but the works, between uniform covers, on the shelves of every conservatory and serious household. To influence music after that date was, in part, to be measured against a printed totality that any critic could consult and any student could be assigned.

The edition created its own scholarship. Because a complete text invites questions about what the text should be, Beethoven studies professionalised around it: Gustav Nottebohm, working in Vienna from the 1860s, founded the study of the sketchbooks, publishing his examinations of the surviving sketches in the 1860s, 1870s and 1880s and demonstrating, page by page, that the music which sounded inevitable had been arrived at by relentless trial. The Bonn Beethoven-Haus, founded in 1889, acquired the composer's birthplace as museum and archive, the model for the composer-house institutions that now dot Europe. The thematic catalogue of Georg Kinsky, completed by Hans Halm and published in 1955, gave every work its number and its documentary dossier. In the twentieth century the Munich publisher Henle began a new critical edition, Beethoven Werke, in 1961, and its volumes have been replacing the nineteenth-century text source by source ever since, a reminder that the authoritative Beethoven is a moving target administered by institutions.

None of this machinery is innocent in the story of influence. A composer whose every sketch is catalogued becomes a model of what a composer's Nachlass should look like, and later estates, Brahms's papers preserved in Vienna, Mahler's manuscripts curated by his widow and his publishers, were assembled in his image. The sketch studies in particular fed the central myth and corrected it at once: the documents show labour, false starts and revision on a scale that demolished the picture of effortless inspiration, while the sheer volume of the labour, preserved and venerated, confirmed the picture of the composer as a heroic worker whose struggle was itself the meaning.

The letters built the other half of the archive. Beethoven's correspondence began to appear in print in the 1860s, in editions prepared by Ludwig Nohl, who also assembled one of the first full biographies from the documents, and the reading public met the man in his own irascible, funny, businesslike prose. The effect on the influence was considerable. A composer whose letters can be read becomes a personality rather than a monument, and the personality, deaf, embattled, negotiating with publishers and princes, fitted the heroic narrative so neatly that the narrative absorbed the documents instead of being corrected by them. The modern critical edition of the letters, prepared at the Beethoven-Haus and published in the 1990s, runs to seven volumes. That a composer's shopping lists and quarrels should merit such apparatus is itself the datum: the archive had become an institution, and institutions, as this article has argued throughout, are where influence does its longest work. Lewis Lockwood, whose 2003 biography Beethoven: The Music and the Life set the modern balance between the documents and the legend, drew on this archive to insist that the working process, not the deafness and not the mythology, is where the authority of the music begins.

## Performance practice and the changing orchestra

Beethoven's symphonies were written for the orchestras of Vienna in the first three decades of the nineteenth century, forces of perhaps forty to sixty players, strings in single figures per part beyond the first desks, winds in pairs, natural brass, timpani. The nineteenth century played them on ever larger instruments, and the sound of Beethoven changed under his successors' hands as surely as his meaning did. Orchestras doubled and tripled their string bodies to fill the new halls; the valved brass and the modern woodwind smoothed out the rough edges the original instruments had given the lines; conductors balanced the whole toward blend and weight. The Beethoven of the Gewandhaus in 1890, sonorous, massive, slow-breathed, was in acoustic terms a different composer from the Beethoven of the Theater an der Wien in 1808, and both were presented as the authentic article.

The argument about tempo shows the process at its most documented. In 1817 Beethoven published metronome marks for his first eight symphonies, among the earliest such indications for major works, and the marks are fast, in several movements startlingly fast against the practice that later hardened. For a century and a half conductors mostly ignored them as unplayable or unwise, products of a defective metronome or a deaf man's abstraction. Then, in the 1980s, the historically informed performance movement took them at their word. Roger Norrington's recordings of the nine symphonies with the London Classical Players, made between 1986 and 1989, played the works at or near the printed tempi on period instruments with a small orchestra, and the effect on listeners raised on the monumental tradition was electric and divisive in equal measure. John Eliot Gardiner's cycle with the Orchestre Révolutionnaire et Romantique, released in 1994, carried the experiment to a wider public. Whatever the verdict on individual movements, the episode proved something structural: Beethoven performance had become an argument about history itself, conducted in public, with the scores as evidence. No other symphonist of his century generates that argument. His music is where the modern idea that performance is interpretation, answerable to documents, was fought out.

The changing orchestra also registers his influence in reverse, as cause rather than effect. The modern symphony orchestra is, in its constitution, an instrument built to play the Beethoven repertoire: its string strength, its doubled winds, its percussion section, its very seating plan were standardised across the nineteenth century on the works at the centre of the canon, and orchestras from Tokyo to Chicago are still audited, by players and audiences alike, on how they play the nine symphonies. A young orchestra proves itself on the Eroica. A new hall is opened with the Ninth. The institution described in an earlier section as a secular temple has a liturgy, and the liturgy is a cycle of nine works by a man who died before the temple was built.


## The conductor's rise and the interpretive tradition

The nineteenth century invented the conductor as the public now knows him, a silent figure who plays nothing and is held responsible for everything, and Beethoven's scores were the instrument of the invention. A Haydn symphony can be led from the keyboard or the first violin desk, and was. A Beethoven symphony, with its exposed transitions, its sudden reversals, its demand that forty minutes of music land as one argument, rewards the single coordinating will, and the century produced a line of specialists to supply it. Mendelssohn conducted with a baton and a score at the Gewandhaus. Hans von Bülow, who coined judgements the way other men coin money, made the interpretation of Beethoven a public philosophy, taking the Meiningen orchestra on tour in the 1880s as a demonstration instrument and insisting that fidelity to the text and freedom of insight were the same virtue seen from two sides. Arthur Nikisch carried the art to a hypnotic economy of gesture. By 1900 the conductor was the priest of the secular institution described earlier, and his scripture, the test of his ordination, was the nine symphonies.

The twentieth century split the priesthood into two churches whose quarrel is itself a chapter of Beethoven's influence. Arturo Toscanini, whose cycle of the symphonies with the NBC Symphony in the winter of 1939 became a landmark of broadcasting, stood for the text: brisk tempi, exact observance, the score as law and the conductor as its incorruptible clerk. Wilhelm Furtwängler stood for the event: elastic tempi, long crescendos of meaning, the score as a script for a drama that exists only in performance, and his wartime and postwar Beethoven, recorded in Berlin and Vienna, remains the document of that church. Otto Klemperer, who had known Mahler, built monuments of granite slowness in his London recordings of the 1950s and 1960s. Herbert von Karajan recorded the complete cycle four times across three decades, in the 1950s, in 1963, in 1977 and in the early 1980s, a feat no other conductor attempted, because no other repertoire justified it commercially: the nine symphonies had become the one product every generation of listeners must buy again.

Underneath the famous names, the interpretive tradition operates in the profession's plumbing. Orchestral musicians win their jobs by playing Beethoven. The standard audition lists of every major orchestra are anchored in excerpts from the symphonies, the third, fifth, seventh and ninth above all, and a string player in Seoul or Chicago spends formative years perfecting the same four pages a Berlin player perfected a century ago. Conducting students are set the same scores as their first serious study. The tradition is therefore not a metaphor. It is a training system, a labour market and a rite of passage, all organised around the works of one composer, and it reproduces his centrality automatically, one audition at a time.

## The piano sonatas as a pedagogical cycle

If the symphonies govern the orchestra, the thirty-two piano sonatas govern the teaching studio, and they have governed it for so long that their rule feels like a fact of nature. It is a construction, and its architects can be named. Carl Czerny, Beethoven's pupil, edited the sonatas and taught them to the next generation, Liszt among them. The publishing industry of the nineteenth century issued the sonatas in graded selections for students. Hans von Bülow, whose edition of the sonatas became a standard text, supplied the slogan that fixed their place: he called them the pianist's New Testament, with Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier as the Old. The metaphor did its work. A testament is not a repertoire; it is a scripture, complete, ordered and sufficient, and the thirty-two sonatas have been taught as one ever since, a single book in thirty-two chapters through which every serious pianist is expected to pass.

The pedagogy built a biography into the student's fingers. Because the sonatas span the whole career, from the F minor sonata of 1795, dedicated to Haydn, to the C minor sonata of 1822, teachers arrange them as a life in music: the early sonatas for lessons in form, the middle sonatas, the Waldstein and the Appassionata, for lessons in power, the last five for lessons in whatever the teacher takes transcendence to mean. A student who has climbed the ladder has absorbed the three-period scheme of the critics without ever reading it, and carries it into every other repertoire as the natural shape of a composer's life. Examination boards institutionalised the ladder: the graded syllabuses of bodies such as the Associated Board of the Royal Schools of Music, founded in 1889, place individual sonata movements at the upper grades, so that admission to the profession is certified, in part, by Beethoven.

The recording era crowned the cycle. Artur Schnabel's complete recording of the sonatas for His Master's Voice, made between 1932 and 1935 under the auspices of the Beethoven Sonata Society, was the first complete sonata cycle on record, a subscription monument sold in advance to members who received the discs in instalments. Schnabel had performed the cycle entire in Berlin in 1927, the centenary year of Beethoven's death, and the gramophone project carried that feat into the world's drawing rooms. Since then the complete cycle, in the hall and on record, has stood as the pianist's summit attempt; dozens of pianists have recorded the thirty-two, and the announcement of a new cycle is still an event in musical journalism. No other body of piano music functions this way. Beethoven's sonatas are not merely taught. They are the curriculum by which teaching itself is organised, and every pianist who submits to them re-enacts, at the keyboard, the argument of this article: the expectation precedes the technique, and the institution delivers both.

## The quartets and the chamber repertoire

Chamber music makes the same point in a quieter register. The sixteen string quartets, and above all the five late quartets of 1824 to 1826, became across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the connoisseur's inner sanctum of the entire repertoire, the works against which performers and listeners alike measure their own seriousness. Their early reception was baffled respect: the Grosse Fuge, the original finale of the Quartet in B flat, Opus 130, was received at its first performance in March 1826 with such incomprehension that Beethoven's publisher, supported by the composer's friends, persuaded him to replace it with a lighter finale and publish the fugue separately, as Opus 133. The substitute movement was the last music he completed. The episode is usually told as philistinism rebuked, but it is better read as the institution forming: a quartet finale had become important enough to argue about, to publish separately, to write essays on.

The late quartets' authority grew as the professional string quartet grew up, and the two histories are one. The Busch Quartet's Beethoven in the 1930s, the Budapest Quartet's residencies at the Library of Congress in the 1940s, the complete cycles that became every serious quartet's credential across the century: in each case the ensemble proved itself on the late works as orchestras prove themselves on the symphonies. Igor Stravinsky, no man's disciple, called the Grosse Fuge a work that would be contemporary forever, and the remark, from the composer who built a career on refusing the Beethovenian inheritance, marks the depth of the capture. Even refusal had to begin by genuflecting to the quartets.

What the quartets did for chamber music as a whole was to relocate its centre of gravity. Before them, chamber genres were largely convivial, music for skilled amateurs in domestic rooms. After them, and after the prestige their difficulty accumulated through a century of advocacy, the string quartet became a public, professional, almost priestly medium, the form in which composers from Brahms and Dvořák to Bartók and Shostakovich deposited their most private and ambitious thought. Bartók's six quartets and Shostakovich's fifteen are unthinkable without the precedent that a quartet can bear that weight. The expectation travelled from the audience to the genre itself: a quartet is where a composer is now expected to be most serious. Beethoven did not invent that expectation in a day. The late quartets, the replaced finale, the essays, the cycles and the credentials did, across a hundred years of downstream work, which is how the influence of a reputation is actually transmitted: slowly, institutionally, and then all at once in the assumptions nobody remembers making.


## The Ninth as a political instrument

### Why has the Ninth Symphony been used politically?

The Ninth has been used politically because its finale sets a universal text, Schiller's call for human brotherhood, to a melody simple enough for anyone to sing, while the symphony's prestige lends authority to whoever performs it. States and movements with incompatible programmes have therefore been able to claim the same work as their own anthem.

The loop opened in the section on the symphony as a vehicle for meaning can now be closed. A symphony ends up used by governments that disagree with each other when three conditions meet: the work carries enormous prestige, so that performing it borrows authority; its meaning is exalted but unspecified, so that the borrower can supply the specification; and at its climax it sets words of universal fellowship to a tune a crowd can sing, so that the borrowing can be enacted by thousands of voices at once. The Ninth meets all three conditions more completely than any other work in the repertoire. Esteban Buch's political history of the symphony, published in French in 1999 and in English in 2003, remains the fullest documentation of what followed, and this section draws its catalogue of uses from the record Buch and other historians have assembled. The uses are reported here as documented facts. What the music itself endorses, if the question even makes sense, is a separate matter on which this article takes no position and attributes every interpretation to its interpreter.

The German state tradition came first. Through the nineteenth century the Ninth was performed at national commemorations as the supreme expression of German culture, and the Nazi regime continued the practice with its own inflection: on 19 April 1942, the eve of Hitler's birthday, Wilhelm Furtwängler conducted the symphony with the Berlin Philharmonic in a ceremony opened by a speech from Joseph Goebbels, whose handshake with the conductor at the close became one of the famous photographs of music under that regime. The performance claimed Beethoven as the patrimony of the Reich. At the same period, and after the war in the Soviet sphere, the same work was being claimed for the opposite cause: official doctrine in the Soviet Union and its allied states presented Beethoven as a revolutionary humanist and a forerunner of socialist humanism, and the Ninth stood at the centre of state musical ceremony in East Berlin as in Moscow, its finale read as the brotherhood of peoples under socialism. One work, two capitals, two incompatible doctrines, each certain the music was on its side.

The melody's detachability completed the process. The Council of Europe adopted the Ode to Joy tune, in an arrangement prepared by Herbert von Karajan, as the European anthem in 1972, and the European Community later took it up in the same wordless form: the absence of text, which might look like a lack, was the point, since words would have chosen one language and one version of the fellowship. The united German team at the Olympic Games of 1956 to 1968, drawn from two states that recognised no shared government, competed under the same melody as its anthem. Rhodesia under Ian Smith chose the tune outright as a national anthem, with new words, Rise, O Voices of Rhodesia, from 1974 until 1979. In Beijing in 1989, students occupying Tiananmen Square broadcast the Ninth among the music played over the square's loudspeakers. And at Christmas 1989, weeks after the Berlin Wall opened, Leonard Bernstein conducted the symphony in both halves of the divided city with an international orchestra and choir, substituting in the finale the word Freiheit, freedom, for Schiller's Freude, joy, announcing his belief, which Schiller scholars have contested, that the poet's original word had been Freiheit before caution amended it.

The fifth finding of this article can now be set down in its brief's own terms. The Ninth has been used as an official anthem by states and movements with incompatible politics, which tells us more about the music's openness than about his views. Beethoven's documented political opinions, formed in the shadow of the French Revolution and its aftermath, belong to the biography article and to the Vienna of 1824. The regimes and movements in this catalogue were not expressing those opinions; they were renting the symphony's authority to bless their own, and the symphony could be rented by all of them because its culminating text blesses fellowship in the abstract, naming no nation, no party and no creed. The openness is not an accident of the music's weakness. It is the direct product of the process traced in the section on meaning: a work installed by two centuries of criticism as the supreme statement of human striving arrives in the political arena as a blank cheque of sublimity, and history is largely the record of who filled it in.

## Uses the composer could not have authorised

Between the state ceremonies and the concert hall lies a penumbra of uses that no institution authorised and no estate could prevent, because Beethoven died in 1827 leaving no mechanism by which his intentions might police his afterlife. The uses in this section are smaller than governments, and in some ways they reveal the process more clearly, since nobody's doctrine required them. They happened because the music was there, famous, unowned and available.

The melody of the Ode to Joy became a hymn tune. In 1907 the American writer Henry van Dyke wrote the hymn text beginning Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee, set to Beethoven's melody, and the pairing entered hymnals across the English-speaking world, where congregations have sung Schiller's tune to a Presbyterian's words ever since, in a devotional setting its composer never contemplated. The opening of the Fifth Symphony became a signal. In 1941 the BBC's V for Victory campaign, built on the Morse code letter V, three dots and a dash, recognised the rhythm in the symphony's first four notes and used recordings of them as the call sign of broadcasts to occupied Europe: a German composer's motif conscripted as the sonic emblem of resistance to Germany, an irony noted at the time and powerless to stop the usage. In Japan, the Ninth acquired a folk institution of its own: German prisoners of war at the Bandō camp had performed the symphony in 1918, the first performance in Japan, and from that seed grew the year-end tradition of massed performances, the Daiku, in which choirs of thousands, many of them amateurs who rehearse all year for the purpose, sing the finale every December in halls across the country.

The strangest use of all left the planet. When NASA launched the Voyager probes in 1977, each carried a golden record of sounds and music from Earth, assembled by a committee chaired by the astronomer Carl Sagan as a message to any finder. Among the music chosen to represent the species stands the Cavatina from Beethoven's String Quartet in B flat, Opus 130, the movement whose replacement finale story this article has already told: the private, valedictory slow movement of a deaf composer's last years, selected as one of the artefacts by which humanity wished to be known. No committee consulted the composer, and none could. The canonisation had long since made his consent unnecessary, which is precisely the condition this section exists to describe.

Each of these uses shares the structure of the political ones: prestige borrowed, meaning supplied locally, author unavailable for comment. What distinguishes them is the innocence of the transaction, and the innocence clarifies the rule. Beethoven's music functions in the wider culture as a common currency of significance, legal tender everywhere precisely because no central bank defends its value. A hymn writer, a wartime broadcaster and ten thousand Japanese choristers are doing, without theory, what Wagner did with theory and what governments do with ceremony. They are spending the expectation his name creates. The composer who could not hear his own late works has become the most overheard composer in the world, in versions, settings and causes he could not have imagined, and the traffic is not a corruption of his influence. On the argument of this article, it is his influence, in its most widely distributed form.


## Beethoven in film and popular culture

Popular culture received the reputation at full strength and simplified it into a handful of durable emblems. In Charles Schulz's comic strip Peanuts, from 1951 onward, the boy Schroeder plays Beethoven on a toy piano beneath a bust of the composer, evangelising for the music to an unconverted neighbourhood of children; two generations of readers who never entered a concert hall learned from a comic strip that Beethoven was the name for musical seriousness itself. In Stanley Kubrick's film A Clockwork Orange of 1971, the Ninth Symphony is the ecstasy and the undoing of its violent young protagonist, Beethoven's music presented as a force too strong for the society that tries to condition it out of him. The film fixed, for a mass audience, an association between the Ninth and dangerous transcendence that concert performances have had to reckon with since. Its soundtrack carried the association further: Wendy Carlos's electronic realisations of the Ninth's scherzo and finale, issued on the film's soundtrack album in 1972, sold the symphony back to the record-buying public in synthesised form, a Beethoven for the age of the Moog, and listeners who would never have bought a symphony record bought that one.

The biographical film Immortal Beloved of 1994 built its plot on the letter Beethoven wrote in July 1812 to an unnamed woman he called his immortal beloved, and asserted an identification of the addressee that the film presented as settled. Scholars have argued the identity for two centuries; the biographer Maynard Solomon made the case for Antonie Brentano in 1977, other candidates have had their advocates, and no identification commands the field. The film's version is recorded here as a film's version. What matters for this article is the appetite it fed: the mass market wanted Beethoven's life to resolve into a single passionate secret, the biographical counterpart of the single meaning the political users wanted from the Ninth.

The popular musicians delivered their own verdicts in titles. Chuck Berry's 1956 record Roll Over Beethoven addressed the classical canon in the person of its presiding figure and announced, in jest that was also a market report, that the new music intended to displace it; the Beatles recorded the song in 1963 and the Electric Light Orchestra stretched it into an orchestral rock performance in 1973, quoting the Fifth Symphony's opening against Berry's tune. Walter Murphy's A Fifth of Beethoven, a disco arrangement of the Fifth Symphony's opening movement, reached the top of the American charts in 1976. The jokes only work because the audience knows whose chair is being tipped. In each case the popular work is not using Beethoven's music so much as his position, the standing of the man whose name means the old order of musical value. Popular culture, like the concert hall before it, conducts its business in the currency of his expectation.


## The name as a measure applied to other composers

By the later nineteenth century the name had completed its transformation from a man into a unit. Critics reached for Beethoven the way engineers reach for a standard metre, to certify the size of something new. Hans von Bülow's verdict on Brahms's First Symphony, the Tenth, is the purest case: the compliment placed the work not beside other symphonies of 1876 but inside a sequence that had closed in 1827, and declared the sequence reopened by permission of its newest entry. Schumann's prophecy over the young Brahms worked the same way in advance, promising the public a successor before the successor had written a note the public could judge. The measure was applied to performers as readily as to composers, and to whole nations as readily as to men. Jean Sibelius studied the Beethoven symphonies and quartets throughout his life, and Finnish and German critics measured his symphonies against the nine as a matter of routine criticism, as though a Finnish symphony became legible only when its distance from the Viennese standard had been reported. New musical nations announcing themselves in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries were routinely certified in the press by the discovery of a local Beethoven, a formula whose compliment consisted of erasing whatever was local about the man it praised.

The pattern the table makes visible is the article's argument in miniature. The musicians closest to Beethoven in craft, Schubert, Brahms, Bruckner, Tchaikovsky, record the inheritance as a burden, because they worked in his genres and felt the audit at close range. The musicians who converted him into a warrant for something else, Berlioz with the programme, Liszt with the new piano, Wagner with the music drama, record him as a resource, because they spent his authority outside the genres he had closed. The institutions, which are not artists and feel no audit, took him as pure resource: a canon is an endowment. Influence, tabulated, turns out to be less a flow of techniques than a distribution of permissions and prohibitions, and the distribution follows the shape of the expectation this article has been tracing from its first section.

## Composers who worked deliberately against him

### Which composers rejected Beethoven's model?

Claude Debussy rejected the Beethovenian symphony as a model for French music, Igor Stravinsky built his neoclassicism against Beethovenian expression while revering the late quartets, Erik Satie made a career of refusing Germanic solemnity, and John Cage argued in print that Beethoven's influence had misdirected Western music toward structure and away from sound itself.

The refusals deserve a section of their own, because they are the strongest evidence that the expectation, and not any technique, is the core of the phenomenon. A technique can be ignored by a composer who never learned it. The expectation had to be fought, in public, by composers who knew exactly what they were refusing. Debussy, writing musical criticism in Paris in the first years of the twentieth century under the mask of Monsieur Croche, treated the symphony after Beethoven as a road the French had no business travelling, and mocked the cult of thematic development as a professorial routine, an arithmetic exercise mistaken for expression. His own orchestral music, La mer above all, written between 1903 and 1905, is the refusal made audible: spans of colour and rhythm that accumulate like weather rather than argue like a case, in three movements that decline every obligation the Beethovenian symphony imposes. Debussy admired the late quartets and the Pastoral Symphony. What he rejected was the model, the belief that his music had to pass the German examination.

Stravinsky's case is the subtler one, since his admiration for the late quartets has already been quoted in these pages. What his neoclassicism of the 1920s refused was not Beethoven's music but Beethoven's contract with the listener: the heroic self, the confessional programme, the symphony as spiritual autobiography. In the lectures published as his Poetics of Music in 1942, Stravinsky distanced his art from expression as a programme and praised order, constraint and the objectivity of the musical object, a doctrine aimed at the romantic Beethoven cult rather than at the scores. Erik Satie refused at the level of scale and tone, answering Germanic solemnity with miniatures, deadpan titles and, in his furniture music of 1920, music designed on principle not to be listened to, the exact antithesis of the reverent silence the Beethoven institutions had spent a century installing. John Cage carried the refusal to its theoretical root. In the essays collected in Silence in 1961 and in his later writings, Cage argued that Beethoven's influence, transmitted through the conservatories, had fixed the Western ear on harmony, structure and the composer's intention, and deafened it to sound as such; his entire aesthetic, chance operations, silence as material, the composer as a man who lets sounds be themselves, is a systematic unbuilding of the Beethovenian edifice.

The conclusion writes itself, and it confirms the thesis from the negative side. To organise an aesthetic against Beethoven, as Cage did, is still to be organised by Beethoven. The refusers needed him as much as the heirs did, as the fixed point their compasses swung away from. A composer's influence is usually measured by his imitators. By that measure Beethoven is very large. Measured by the energy his opponents had to spend escaping him, he is larger still.

## Where the influence claim is overstated

An article arguing that Beethoven's influence ran through expectation rather than technique owes its readers a section of scepticism about its own subject, because the expectation machine manufactures influence claims as a by-product, and many of them fail inspection. The failures have a pattern. They attribute to Beethoven, singly, developments that were collective; they mistake the textbook's need for a turning point for history's possession of one; and they survive because they are flattering to repeat.

He did not single-handedly free the composer from patronage. The biography shows a man who worked the aristocratic system with great skill: dedications sold and resold, a famous annuity contract of 1809 in which Archduke Rudolph and the Princes Lobkowitz and Kinsky undertook to pay him a salary precisely so that Vienna would not lose him to a court appointment elsewhere. The freelance market Brahms later faced was built by publishers, concert societies and copyright law across the whole century, not decreed by one man's example. He did not replace the minuet with the scherzo in a stroke of originality; Haydn's late minuets are already fast, witty and metrically mischievous, and Beethoven's own practice hardened gradually across the 1790s. He did not invent dramatic symphonic writing from nothing. He revered Cherubini above his living contemporaries and said so, and the French revolutionary school of Cherubini and Méhul, with its public rhetoric and its rescue operas, is in his music's bloodstream alongside Haydn and Mozart.

The deafness requires the same discipline, because the standard influence narrative treats it as the engine of the late style: cut off from the world, the story runs, he heard inwardly and wrote the future. Lewis Lockwood's biography is the modern corrective most worth naming here. Lockwood insists on the documentary record of the working method, the sketchbooks showing the late music arrived at by the same laborious trial as the early music, and on the study the late works embody, of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier, of Handel, of counterpoint as a discipline deliberately reacquired. Deafness shaped the life catastrophically and the career's logistics completely. As an explanation of style it is a piece of romantic criticism, Hoffmann's heirs preferring a metaphysical cause to a technical one. The influence machine prefers its causes metaphysical, because metaphysical causes cannot be checked.

The comparison with the article's companion case is instructive, and it is why the two studies were commissioned as a pair. The account of [Mozart's influence on music](/2010/12/07/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-influence-on-music/) found a transmission narrower than the reputation: specific techniques, named takers, an enormous cultural footprint wrapped around a modest technical legacy. Beethoven's claims run the other way. The footprint is enormous and so, often, are the claims, and the claims grow less testable as they grow larger, because a reputation that governs institutions generates its own evidence: the programme notes cite the critics, the critics cite the histories, the histories cite the programme notes. Breadth of claim, in this subject, is not a sign of strength. It is the signature of the machine. The next section therefore applies the test the brief for this article prescribes, and applies it to the article's own favourite example.


## Testing one influence claim properly

The test used throughout this article has three parts, and it is time to run it in full view on a single claim, the strongest available, so that its workings and its limits are both visible. The test asks an influence claim to name the technique or device involved, to exhibit the two scores, one on each side of the transaction, and to produce a date of access, evidence that the later composer could have encountered the earlier work before writing his own. A claim that passes all three parts has earned belief. A claim that passes one or two has earned a footnote. A claim that passes none is weather.

Take the claim that Beethoven influenced the finale of Brahms's First Symphony. The device: a broad, diatonic, hymn-like theme in the major key, introduced after a long introduction in which fragments of it have been prepared, functioning as the finale's principal subject and as the symphony's destination. The two scores: the finale of the Ninth Symphony, composed by 1824, and the finale of Brahms's First, completed in 1876, whose main theme shares the Ode to Joy melody's contour, its stepwise rise and its hymn character closely enough that the kinship has been heard by every audience since the premiere. The date of access: not merely available but saturated. Brahms had studied the Ninth for decades, owned and annotated Beethoven material, moved in the circle of the Schumanns who treated the work as scripture, and acknowledged the resemblance in conversation when it was put to him, in the remark recorded by Kalbeck and discussed in the Brahms sections of this article. The claim passes all three parts, and passing them shows what a passed claim actually establishes. It establishes resemblance, access and acknowledgement. It does not establish that Brahms needed the Ninth to write his finale, that the finale is unoriginal, or that the resemblance was the point rather than the price. The most famous influence claim in the symphonic repertoire, tested properly, comes out true and narrow.

Now run the test on the kind of claim the influence machine issues daily: that every symphony of struggle and triumph written after 1827 descends from Beethoven. The device cannot be named, since struggle and triumph describe plots, not techniques. The scores cannot be paired, since the claim covers hundreds. Access is vacuous, since everyone had access. The claim fails all three parts, and its failure is the sixth and final finding of this article, the rung the whole ladder has been climbing toward. Most popular claims of Beethoven's influence fail the test of naming a technique, two scores and a date of access, and the claims that survive are narrower than the reputation suggests. Keeping a dossier of the kind the test requires, scores side by side, dates of access documented, resemblances itemised, is clerical work before it is insight; a listener assembling one can keep the notes, lists and listening logs together in the offline encrypted notes app obtained from [the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/), which keeps the material on the user's own machine rather than in a borrowed cloud. The clerical discipline is the point. Influence is a claim about documents, and documents reward those who file them.

## What his reputation cost his contemporaries

Influence has a ledger with two columns, and the debit column is populated by the men who had the misfortune to be good in Vienna and its orbit while the standard was being set. This section names them, as the discipline of this article requires, and states what happened to their standing, without speculation about what they might have written in a world without Beethoven.

Ferdinand Ries, Beethoven's pupil and assistant in the great years, wrote eight symphonies and a shelf of piano concertos that were played across Europe in his lifetime; they vanished from the programmes within a generation, filed as the master's manner without the master. Johann Nepomuk Hummel, whose piano concertos and septet were the height of fashion in the 1820s and who had played in the same rooms as Beethoven for thirty years, died in 1837 already sounding, to the new criticism, like the past. Louis Spohr, among the most famous musicians alive at mid-century, Kapellmeister at Kassel, inventor of the chin rest and the conductor's baton in Germany, wrote ten symphonies and a library of concertos; his autobiography records his honest bewilderment before the late Beethoven quartets, which he could not follow, and posterity took his bewilderment as his verdict on himself. His music left the repertoire almost entire. Muzio Clementi, who had faced Mozart in contest in 1781 and lived to publish Beethoven in London, saw his own sonatas eclipsed by the very catalogue his firm helped to spread. Anton Diabelli survives in the histories chiefly as the publisher whose little waltz Beethoven transformed into the Diabelli Variations, his own considerable output unplayed. Carl Czerny, the relay station of the whole tradition, composed a thousand works and is remembered for the exercises by which other men's music is learned. Friedrich Kalkbrenner completes the pattern from Paris: in the 1820s and 1830s he was among the most celebrated pianist-composers in Europe, a partner in the Pleyel firm, the teacher every ambitious family wanted, and a man who once proposed to give the young Chopin a three-year course of correction. His concertos and sonatas were the standard currency of the Parisian salon. They are unplayed now, while the salon's other claimant, Beethoven, dead before Kalkbrenner's fame peaked, fills the halls Kalkbrenner built his career in. The eclipse was not a judgement delivered once. It was delivered nightly, by programme committees choosing between reputations, for a hundred years.

The mechanism was not malice, and it was not a conspiracy of critics; it was the arithmetic of the canon described in the section on the concert hall. A repertoire organised around a fixed pantheon has finite room, and the works chosen to fill it were chosen, decade after decade, by institutions that Beethoven's example had trained. Each generation's shortlist orphaned another shelf of the generation before. The cost fell heaviest on the near-great, the men whose music was similar enough to Beethoven's to be compared with it directly and different enough to lose. Distance was protection: Weber's operas and Rossini's survived in their own genres. It was the symphonists and the piano composers of Beethoven's own city who paid, in the coin of their music's disappearance, for the standard his reputation set. Their eclipse is not an argument against his greatness. It is a measurement of what greatness, institutionalised, does to the neighbourhood it occupies.

## Beethoven influence on music: expectation rather than technique

The evidence is now assembled, and the thesis of the first section can be restated with its proofs attached. Beethoven's influence on music operated through four channels that this article has traced in turn: through successors who took techniques and refused others, always in his shadow and sometimes by his leave; through interpreters, Wagner supreme among them, who rebuilt him into the ancestor their projects required; through institutions, the silent hall, the programme note, the complete edition, the conducting profession and the conservatory curriculum, which converted his example into the operating system of musical life; and through the open meaning of the Ninth, which let states, movements, hymn writers and film makers spend his authority on causes he never heard of. The channels differ in everything except their common source. None of them is a technique. All of them are forms of expectation: the expectation that a symphony must mean, that a composer must matter, that an audience must attend, that a masterpiece must be argued about forever.

The composers who followed him could refuse the techniques, and the best of them did, Debussy refusing the symphony, Liszt refusing the four-movement frame, Cage refusing the premise of the whole edifice. What none of them could refuse was the audience Beethoven had trained: listeners who arrived at the hall expecting significance, critics who measured new work against the standard, institutions that filed every newcomer in the pantheon's shadow or its light. Brahms, the most gifted of them all, spent twenty years learning that the giant behind him was not a man and not a set of scores but a way the world had learned to listen. When he finally walked forward, the giant walked with him, and has walked with every symphonist since.

There is a discipline the subject itself teaches, and this article has tried to practise it: the influence that can be proved is always smaller than the influence that can be felt, and the historian's duty is to keep reporting both without confusing them. The provable claims are in the scores and the archives, the borrowed theme, the documented access, the letter that names the pressure. The felt influence is in the hush before the first chord, in the student's ladder of sonatas, in the conductor's audition, in the anthem without words and the anthem with the wrong ones. Beethoven's case is the supreme instance of both at once, which is why his name became the measure this article found applied to everyone else. That is the influence. It is narrower than the legend in its techniques, and wider than the legend in its reach, and it is still operating every time a hall falls silent before a note has sounded.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why is Beethoven considered a turning point in music history?

The turning point is less a change in musical language than a change in musical status. Before Beethoven, a symphony was the grandest form of public entertainment, written for occasions and judged by the pleasure of the evening. After him, the symphony had become the genre in which a composer was expected to deposit his largest thoughts, a work to be studied, re-performed and argued about for generations. The composer changed with the genre: from a supplier of music to an author whose complete output formed a canon, whose sketches were worth publishing and whose intentions bound performers after his death. Audiences changed too, learning to listen for meaning across forty minutes of wordless argument. Critics, editors, conservatory teachers and hall managers rebuilt their professions around that canon, with Beethoven at its centre. Other composers wrote some of the era's most original music, but the redefinition of what a composer is, and of what a serious listener does, crystallised around his career and his posthumous reputation.

### Q: What is the nine-symphony superstition?

The nine-symphony superstition is the belief that a composer who completes nine symphonies will die before finishing a tenth, taking Beethoven's nine and his early death as the pattern. It is a journalistic and critical construct rather than a fact of musical life, and it survives by selective counting. Schubert reaches nine only under one of several numbering schemes; Dvořák's New World Symphony was counted his fifth during his lifetime and became his ninth only after lost early symphonies were restored to the catalogue; Bruckner died with his Ninth unfinished, which changes the charge from completing nine to attempting one. Gustav Mahler is the one major figure documented as taking the pattern seriously: he left Das Lied von der Erde unnumbered after his Eighth Symphony, wrote a Ninth believing the danger past, and died in 1911 with a Tenth in draft. Arnold Schoenberg gave the superstition its canonical statement in his 1912 essay on Mahler. Haydn, Mozart and Shostakovich, with 104, more than forty and fifteen symphonies respectively, stand as the arithmetic refutation.

### Q: How did Beethoven's reputation affect later composers?

The reputation worked as both endowment and audit. It endowed later composers with a public trained to treat new symphonies as major statements, with institutions eager to perform them and critics obliged to take them seriously. It audited them by supplying the standard against which every new work was measured, usually before it had been heard twice. Robert Schumann used the reputation as an instrument in 1853, announcing the young Brahms as the heir the age had been waiting for, and Hans von Bülow used it again in 1876, hailing Brahms's First Symphony as the Tenth. Composers responded along a spectrum this article traces in detail: Schubert bent his lyric gift toward Beethovenian scale, Berlioz and Liszt spent the authority on new genres, Wagner converted it into a warrant for music drama, and Brahms, Bruckner and Mahler carried it as a burden inside the symphony itself. Even refusal was shaped by it. Debussy and Cage organised their aesthetics against the Beethoven model, which is another way of being organised by it.

### Q: Why did Brahms wait so long to write a symphony?

Brahms waited because he had internalised the standard his symphony would be judged against, and he described the pressure himself. His friend Max Kalbeck recorded his remark to the conductor Hermann Levi that he would never understand what it meant to keep hearing a giant marching behind him, meaning Beethoven. The documentary record supports the scale of the delay. Symphonic material existed by 1854, when Brahms was twenty-one; part of it was salvaged into the Piano Concerto in D minor after the Leipzig performance of 1859 failed. A symphony movement reached Clara Schumann in 1862. Thereafter Brahms built his European reputation in other genres, the German Requiem, the serenades, the variation sets, while serving what amounted to a public apprenticeship in orchestral writing. The First Symphony was finally premiered at Karlsruhe in November 1876, when he was forty-three, and was instantly received as a succession document. The delay was not a failure of invention. It was the working of an expectation inside the one composer best equipped to feel it.

### Q: What did Wagner claim about the Ninth Symphony?

Wagner claimed that the Ninth Symphony proved instrumental music had reached its limit and was obliged to reach beyond itself for the human voice. In his theoretical writings of 1849 to 1851, The Artwork of the Future and Opera and Drama, he read the choral finale not as a solution to a compositional problem but as Beethoven's own verdict on the symphony: absolute music confessing its insufficiency and pointing toward the union of music and drama. The conclusion Wagner drew was that the future belonged to the music drama, his own art form, with himself as Beethoven's executor rather than his competitor. He had prepared it years earlier, conducting the Ninth at Dresden in 1846 with a programme that glossed the finale through quotations from Goethe's Faust, and he renewed it in his centenary essay Beethoven of 1870 and in his ceremonial performance of the work at the laying of the Bayreuth foundation stone in 1872. The reading was an argument with a purpose, and it became the standard interpretation for a century.

### Q: How did Schumann promote Beethoven's music?

Schumann promoted Beethoven chiefly through print, as the editor of and principal writer for the Neue Zeitschrift für Musik, the journal he founded in Leipzig in 1834 to campaign for a serious musical culture. In its pages Beethoven was the presiding standard: new music was reviewed by its distance from his example and found promising or wanting accordingly. Schumann's advocacy was practical as well as rhetorical. In Vienna in the winter of 1838 and 1839 he discovered the unpublished score of Schubert's Great C major Symphony in the keeping of the composer's brother Ferdinand, arranged its premiere under Mendelssohn at the Leipzig Gewandhaus in March 1839, and wrote the review praising its heavenly length, which fixed Schubert's place in the succession. His most consequential act came in October 1853, when his article Neue Bahnen announced the twenty-year-old Brahms to the public as the heir for whom the age had been waiting. A critic had used Beethoven's name to crown a successor in advance, and the crown governed expectations of Brahms for forty years.

### Q: Did Schubert know Beethoven personally?

The homage is documented and the acquaintance is not. In 1822 Schubert dedicated his Variations on a French Song for piano duet, published as his Opus 10, to Beethoven, an act of reverence from a young composer to the master of his city. The famous story, that Schubert visited the dying Beethoven in March 1827 and that Beethoven, examining the songs, recognised a divine spark in their author, rests on the testimony of Anton Schindler and the Hüttenbrenner brothers. Schindler's evidence cannot be taken at face value where his own importance is concerned: his falsification of entries in Beethoven's conversation books after the composer's death is documented. No record that survives scrutiny establishes a substantive meeting between the two men, and historians treat the visit as a product of the reception history rather than an event in it. What passed between them passed on paper and in sound: Schubert heard the Ninth in Vienna in 1824 or had every opportunity to, and his Great C major Symphony of 1825 answers its scale in instrumental terms.

### Q: How did concert hall behaviour change in the nineteenth century?

It changed from sociability to ritual. At the century's opening a concert was a miscellany shared with conversation: audiences talked through performances, applauded between movements and during them, arrived late, took refreshments and demanded encores of single movements, as the first audience of Beethoven's Seventh Symphony did in 1813 when it had the slow movement repeated in the middle of the work. Across the following decades the institutions of the new concert culture retrained them. Programmes centred on complete canonical works; applause was herded to the ends of movements and then to the end of the concert; halls were darkened during performance, a practice Wagner's Bayreuth theatre of 1876 modelled in its most rigorous form; programme notes instructed listeners in what to attend to; critics and managers scolded talking in print and in person. By 1900 the default posture in a serious hall was absorbed silence, and it has remained the governing etiquette since. The discipline was invented, largely after Beethoven's death, and his difficult, argumentative music was the reformers' strongest argument for inventing it.

### Q: Why did programme notes become standard?

Programme notes became standard because the nineteenth-century concert asked something new of its audience: repeated attention to long, difficult works by dead composers, performed complete. A listener facing forty minutes of a Beethoven symphony needed equipment, and the analytical note supplied it, identifying the themes, mapping the movements and suggesting what the work meant before a note sounded. The model was set in Britain by George Grove, whose notes for the Crystal Palace concerts in London from the 1860s onward combined structural guidance with biographical narrative in a form orchestras across Europe and America adapted. The notes served the institutions as much as the listeners. Subscription audiences returning week after week to a fixed canon could be educated into loyalty, and the note was the curriculum. It also served the work's reputation: generations learned from programme notes that the Fifth Symphony concerned fate and the Ninth universal brotherhood, and those compressed accounts, reprinted at every performance, became the default settings of listening for the whole repertoire.

### Q: How did the role of the conductor develop?

The conductor developed from a timekeeper embedded in the orchestra into the single interpretive authority the audience watches. Haydn's generation led from the keyboard or the first violin desk, and much early Beethoven was directed the same way. The scores themselves drove the change: long spans, exposed transitions and sudden reversals reward one coordinating will, and the nineteenth century produced specialists to supply it. Mendelssohn conducted at the Leipzig Gewandhaus with baton and score, modelling the custodian of complete canonical works. Hans von Bülow then made interpretation a philosophy, insisting that fidelity to the text and freedom of insight were the same virtue. The twentieth century split his inheritance. Arturo Toscanini, whose Beethoven cycle with the NBC Symphony in 1939 became a broadcasting landmark, stood for the score as law; Wilhelm Furtwängler stood for the performance as event, with elastic tempi and long-breathed drama. Herbert von Karajan recorded the nine symphonies complete four times, proof that the conductor's Beethoven had become the industry's central product.


### Q: How has the Ninth Symphony been used by governments?

Governments and movements across the political spectrum have used the Ninth as an instrument of legitimacy, and the catalogue is documented in Esteban Buch's political history of the work. The German state tradition claimed Beethoven as national patrimony; on 19 April 1942 the symphony was performed in Berlin for the eve of Hitler's birthday, conducted by Wilhelm Furtwängler and introduced by a speech from Joseph Goebbels. Official culture in the Soviet Union and its allied states presented the same work as revolutionary humanism's heritage, central to state ceremony in Moscow and East Berlin. The Council of Europe adopted the Ode to Joy melody, arranged by Herbert von Karajan, as the European anthem in 1972, and the combined German Olympic team of 1956 to 1968 competed under it while the two German states shared no government. Rhodesia under Ian Smith used the tune as a national anthem between 1974 and 1979. Students in Tiananmen Square broadcast it in 1989, and Leonard Bernstein conducted it in Berlin that Christmas, substituting freedom for joy in the finale.

### Q: Are the piano sonatas a teaching cycle by design?

No. Beethoven composed the thirty-two piano sonatas between 1795 and 1822 for the ordinary occasions of a working life: publication, dedication, sale and his own performance in the early years. He issued no cycle, numbered no progression and left no pedagogical plan. The cycle was constructed after his death by the teaching profession. Carl Czerny, his pupil, edited the sonatas and taught them to the next generation, Liszt included. Nineteenth-century publishers issued them in graded selections, and Hans von Bülow's description of the set as the pianist's New Testament, against Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier as the Old, fixed the metaphor of a complete scripture to be traversed in order. Examination boards built the sonatas into their upper grades, and Artur Schnabel's complete recording of 1932 to 1935 supplied the modern monument. The pedagogical cycle is therefore a fact about Beethoven's reception, not about his intentions: the institutions of teaching organised his output into a ladder, and generations of pianists have climbed it believing, not unreasonably, that the ladder was the work.

### Q: Is Beethoven's influence greater than Bach's?

The two influences have different shapes, and the comparison is more useful than the verdict. Bach's influence ran chiefly through craft: the Well-Tempered Clavier as a teaching text copied by Mozart and Beethoven themselves, counterpoint as a discipline transmitted from master to pupil, a body of technique that later composers absorbed at the desk. For decades after his death in 1750 his public repertoire presence was small; the revival that began with Mendelssohn's 1829 performance of the St Matthew Passion restored the works to the concert hall. Beethoven's influence ran chiefly through expectation and institution: it never slept, because the concert culture that carried it was built during his lifetime and canonised him immediately. If the measure is documented transmission of technique, Bach's claim is the deeper one. If the measure is the reshaping of what music was taken to be, what audiences demanded and what institutions enforced, Beethoven's is the wider. A serious answer names the channel before it names the winner.

### Q: Should a composition student study Beethoven's scores?

Yes, and the useful way to study them is as process rather than monument. The scores alone can mislead, because the finished surfaces conceal the labour; the sketch literature, founded by Gustav Nottebohm's publications on the sketchbooks in the nineteenth century and continued by scholars since, shows the same material tried, rejected and rebuilt across years, which is the part a student can actually use. Work on specific problems in specific scores: the transitions of the Eroica, the finale construction of the Ninth, the compression of the late quartets, where a movement's whole argument may be generated from a few notes. Lewis Lockwood's biography Beethoven: The Music and the Life is the modern guide most worth reading alongside, because it keeps the documents and the legend in view at once. The warning is Brahms's. Studying the scores as scripture, a standard of worth before they are understood as solutions to problems, reproduces the paralysis this article describes. Study them as a workshop, tools included, and they teach.

### Q: Is it possible to write a symphony free of Beethoven's shadow?

In technique, yes; in reception, only partly. Composers have repeatedly written symphonies that decline the Beethovenian contract of argument, struggle and apotheosis. Debussy declined the genre's German form altogether. The American minimalists of the later twentieth century, Steve Reich and Philip Glass among them, built large orchestral works on repetition and gradual change rather than development, and Glass's symphonies from the 1990s onward hold to that discipline inside the genre's name. What the composer cannot control is the hearing. Audiences and critics trained by two centuries of the canon receive every new symphony against the expectation the canon installed, so that a work which refuses argument is reviewed as a refusal, defined by the thing it avoids. That is why the shadow is best understood as a property of listeners and institutions rather than of scores. A composer can leave it. The hall, the programme note and the review bring it along, and part of a modern symphonist's real compositional problem is deciding what to do about the escort.

### Q: How do I test whether a Beethoven influence claim holds?

Apply three questions, in order. First, what is the technique or device alleged to have travelled: a formal procedure, a kind of theme, a way of building a finale? If the claim names only a mood or a plot such as struggle and triumph, it fails at the first question. Second, exhibit the two scores, the passage in Beethoven and the passage in the later composer, and confirm that the resemblance is specific rather than generic. Third, produce a date of access: evidence that the later composer could have encountered the earlier work, in score or performance, before writing his own. The claim that Brahms's First Symphony finale answers the Ninth passes all three: the hymn-like finale theme is specific, the scores can be laid side by side, Brahms's decades of study are documented and he acknowledged the resemblance when it was put to him. Most large claims about Beethoven's influence fail one or more of the questions, and the ones that survive come out narrower and more valuable than the reputation that issued them.

### Q: How do I compare a Brahms symphony with a Beethoven one?

Compare them as arguments, movement by movement, with the same set of questions. Ask first how each opening movement generates its material: Beethoven typically states a compact motif and subjects it to fragmentation and rebuilding, while Brahms works by developing variation, in which the theme is never merely repeated but continuously re-derived, a distinction Arnold Schoenberg drew in his 1947 essay Brahms the Progressive. Ask where the weight falls. In both composers the finale is built to carry the work's conclusion, so set the finale of Brahms's First beside the finale of the Ninth and listen for what the younger man cites, the hymn-like breadth, and what he refuses, the voices and the text. Ask what each slow movement is doing in the design, release or centre. Score reading settles arguments that listening alone leaves vague, and the exercise repays doing in that order: hear both works entire, then open the scores at the transitions, which is where each composer's real decisions are filed.

### Q: Which recordings show the Beethoven interpretive tradition clearly?

Choose recordings that embody the tradition's main positions, all of them made well within the recorded era's maturity. For the text as law, Arturo Toscanini's cycle of the symphonies with the NBC Symphony, broadcast in the winter of 1939, remains the document: brisk, exact, unsentimental. For the performance as event, Wilhelm Furtwängler's wartime and postwar Beethoven, above all the Ninth recorded in Berlin in 1942 and the Bayreuth Festival performance of 1951, shows the opposite church at full strength. Herbert von Karajan's 1963 Berlin cycle represents the postwar mainstream at its most polished. For the sonatas, Artur Schnabel's complete recording of 1932 to 1935 is the founding monument of the cycle on disc. For the quartets, the Busch Quartet's recordings of the 1930s set the standard their successors still answer. The historically informed argument is clearest in Roger Norrington's cycle with the London Classical Players, recorded between 1986 and 1989 at Beethoven's metronome marks, and in John Eliot Gardiner's 1994 cycle. Set any two side by side and the tradition stops being an abstraction.

### Q: What should I listen for when comparing the Ninth with the Eroica?

Listen first for how each work begins its argument. The Eroica, composed in 1803 and 1804, opens with two hammered chords and a theme in the cellos that keeps tripping over a note foreign to its key, so that the whole first movement becomes the story of that instability. The Ninth, composed two decades later, opens with a murmur of open fifths from which the theme assembles itself gradually, as if the music were condensing out of silence. Compare the slow movements next: a funeral march of public grief in the Eroica, against the Ninth's adagio of private contemplation built as double variations. Then compare the finales, which is where the younger work's solution becomes the older problem's successor. The Eroica's finale builds its triumph from a bass line, through variations, entirely in instruments. The Ninth spends three movements in instruments and then, after its finale has audibly rejected the earlier movements' themes, admits voices. Hearing the two finales in sequence is the shortest route to understanding what Wagner later claimed the Ninth had proved.

### Q: Which of Beethoven's contemporaries lost standing?

The heaviest losses fell on the respected professionals of his own city and genres, the men close enough to be filed as lesser versions of it. Ferdinand Ries, his pupil, wrote eight symphonies that were performed across Europe and vanished within a generation. Johann Nepomuk Hummel, a star pianist and composer of the 1820s, died in 1837 already sounding to critics like the past. Louis Spohr, among the most famous musicians alive at mid-century, left ten symphonies and a shelf of concertos that have almost entirely left the repertoire; his autobiography records his honest inability to follow the late quartets, and posterity treated the confession as a verdict. Muzio Clementi's sonatas were eclipsed by the Beethoven sonatas his own London firm helped to publish. Anton Diabelli is remembered chiefly as the publisher of the waltz Beethoven transformed. Carl Czerny composed a thousand works and survives as the author of exercises for other men's music. The mechanism was the canon's arithmetic: a fixed repertoire has finite room, and institutions chose the master over the neighbourhood.
