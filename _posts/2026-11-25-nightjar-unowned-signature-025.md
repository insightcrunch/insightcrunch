## Cold Open

![Nightjar: The Signature That Nobody At All Will Own - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-07.webp)

Perrin Loft keeps the specimen books on the lower shelf where the damp cannot reach them first. The records office in Carrowmere sits one floor above the canal cut, and on a wet afternoon the air carries canal water, coal smoke from the bonded warehouses across the basin, and the thin sour smell of paste from the binding room. Rain finds the tall windows in a steady hush. It beads on the iron frames, runs down in crooked lines, and gathers on the sill above the ledgers. Below, barges knock softly against stone. Ropes creak. A warehouse bell sounds once for a load cleared and once again for a load refused. Inside, the lamps burn early. Their light pools on varnished wood and on the pale covers of the books Loft has laid out in a row.

He works with clean hands and a blotter that he changes twice a day. The form lies to his left, weighted at one corner by a brass rule. It is the Accord requisition form that Juno Sarte brought in, the one that made the Dravin alloy purchase lawful on its face, and Loft has been asked a plain question about it. Does the authorising signature at its foot belong to anyone the Accord can name. He is not asked who wrote it. He is not asked why it was written. He is asked only whether it matches.

By the middle of the afternoon he has four answers, and each answer is no.

The refusals arrive politely, in order, and each one is correct on the paper in front of him. Requisitions and Stores sends its specimen book by runner with a note pinned inside the cover, listing its authorised signatories for the relevant period in a clerkly hand. Loft turns the pages with care. The book holds signatures entered in ink, each one repeated three times on its line, each one witnessed below. He compares the form against every name that could lawfully sign for Requisitions and Stores. The loops do not match. The pressure does not match. The way the pen leaves a stroke does not match. He writes the result on his worksheet and returns the book with thanks.

Works and Ways answers in person. Its senior clerk, a woman named Anselm who wears her sleeves protected by dark cuffs, carries the book herself and stays while Loft works, as Works and Ways always stays. She watches without impatience while he moves from page to page. Her department guards its signatories closely and with reason, and its book is the neatest of the four, ruled and indexed and mended at the spine. The signature on the form resembles one of the Works and Ways hands at a glance, enough to make Loft slow down, enough to make Anselm lean a fraction closer. At the second look the resemblance fails. The initial letter is formed from the opposite direction. The final stroke rises where every specimen falls. Anselm sees it when he sees it. She nods once, not in triumph, only in confirmation, and takes her book away under her arm.

Movement and Passage refuses by letter before its book even reaches him, and then sends the book anyway, because Movement and Passage understands that a letter without evidence is only an opinion. Its signatories sign in a practical style, most of them with abbreviated names and a hard downward cut that Loft knows well from movement warrants. The signature on the form has no such cut. It is unhurried and complete, each letter given its space. He checks the full list twice, once forward and once back, the way he was taught when he first came to the office as a boy who was trusted mainly to carry boxes and to keep his mouth shut. No match waits in either direction.

The Office of the Accord Seal is last and most formal. Its book arrives in a locked case, and its refusal arrives with it, drafted before the comparison is made, on the reasonable ground that the Office of the Accord Seal keeps a separate lodgement register for specimens of this class and the signature appears nowhere in that register. Loft is permitted to verify the claim himself, and he does. The Seal office signatures are formal to the point of severity, written by people who expect their names to be read after they are gone. The signature on the form is not severe. It has a kind of ease in it, a practiced ease, which makes it harder rather than easier to place. Ease can belong to anyone who has signed the same thing many times. He closes the case. He returns it. The refusal stands.

Four departments. Four specimen books. Four polite refusals in one afternoon, each one honest, each one procedurally sound, each one leaving the signature exactly where it began, present on the form and owned by nobody.

Loft sits for a while with the form in front of him after the last runner has gone. Rain continues at the windows. The canal darkens early at this season, and the warehouse lamps across the water come on in a broken line, doubled in the wet stone of the quay. Someone in the outer office moves a trolley of files and the small wheels complain at the threshold. Someone else laughs, briefly, at nothing that reaches Loft. His tea has gone cold in its thick cup. He drinks it anyway, because waste is noticed in a records office even when larger failures are not.

His worksheet holds four short entries in his own hand, each with a time beside it. He reads them again to be sure that he has recorded what happened and not what he expected to happen. That distinction is the whole of his training. A records assistant who records expectation corrupts the file for everyone who comes after him, and Loft, who has no other distinction in the Ninth Office and seeks none, holds to the distinction with private pride. Requisitions and Stores, no match. Works and Ways, no match. Movement and Passage, no match. Office of the Accord Seal, no match, register checked. The words are plain. They do not accuse anyone. They do not excuse anyone. They say only that the books were opened and the name was not there.

He considers, briefly, whether he has made an error of scope. Perhaps the form belongs to a class signed by a fifth authority. Perhaps an older book, retired and stored in the lower vault, holds the hand he needs. He turns the form over. The class mark is printed at the head, clear and current. The requisition number falls inside the sequence that the four departments share. The paper stock is current. The printed lines are current. Nothing on the sheet suggests an older authority or an exceptional route. He has seen exceptional routes before. They announce themselves with seals and riders and cross references that take half a morning to chase. This form announces nothing. It proceeds from office to office in the ordinary way, it authorises payment in the ordinary way, it releases bonded stock in the ordinary way, and at its foot it carries a signature that the ordinary way cannot account for.

Loft copies his worksheet onto the return slip in duplicate. His hand is steady. He has copied worse news than this, though not often news of this shape. Usually a mismatch means a forgery, and a forgery has a kind of comfort in it, because a forgery points at a person who made a false thing. This is not that comfort. The form itself shows no sign of tampering. The paper is right, the printing is right, the numbering is right, the chain of stamps is right and in the right order. Only the authority is missing. A lawful purchase stands on the sheet, complete in every particular except the person who lawfully authorised it.

Before he files anything, he does the work once more in miniature, because a result that cannot survive a second pass is not a result at all. He lays the form beside his worksheet and checks each entry against the book that produced it, page number by page number, in the order the books came to him. Requisitions and Stores, the third signatory, the stroke that fell short. Works and Ways, the initial formed from the wrong direction. Movement and Passage, the missing cut. The Office of the Accord Seal, the register with its ruled columns and its empty space where this name ought to stand if it stood anywhere. Each check lands in the same place as the first. The exercise costs him twenty minutes and the last of the light at the windows, and he counts the cost as well spent. A refusal that has been tested twice can be carried upstairs without apology. A refusal tested once is only an opinion wearing a uniform.

He places the original in a folder and the copies in two separate trays, one for the requesting section and one for the file. He notes the folder number on his day list. He notes the times again from the worksheet, because times matter when four departments answer on the same afternoon and someone later asks whether they could have spoken to one another. They could not, in any way that helps. Requisitions and Stores answered before Works and Ways arrived. Works and Ways answered before Movement and Passage sent its book. The Office of the Accord Seal had drafted its refusal before any of the others were known to him. Four separate offices looked at the same signature and said, in their own phrasing, that it was not theirs. He writes that down too, in neutral words.

When his work is done he locks the specimen shelf, though the books themselves have all gone home to their departments. He hangs his coat over the back of his chair to dry where the stove can reach it. He washes his hands at the basin in the corner, drying them on the rough towel that the office provides, and he checks the form folder one final time before he leaves it in the night safe. The folder sits squarely on the shelf inside. The label faces outward. Tomorrow another assistant may take it up, or the same question may come back to him with a new instruction attached. Either way the record will say what he found. Four books opened. Four refusals given. A signature that is clear, practiced, and unowned, waiting on a form that made a purchase lawful.

Outside, the rain eases. The canal settles. A last barge moves under the bridge with its lamp lit, slow on the dark water, and the warehouse doors close one by one along the basin for the night.

## Tasking

Petra Dunmore named the cost before she named the job, which was how Mira Kovac knew the job was already short of what it needed.

"You cannot run a protection detail and a document trace at once," Dunmore said. "Not properly. You will do both badly, and I am asking you to do both anyway, because there is nobody else I can send to Carrowmere this week who can read a corridor and a file in the same hour."

They sat in the small room Dunmore used when a conversation was not to be overheard by the corridor outside her own office. It had no window and one lamp with a green shade that made the papers on the table look older than they were. Mira rested her hands flat on either side of the folder Dunmore had placed in front of her and did not open it at once. The delay was small. Dunmore noticed it and said nothing about it, which told Mira that the delay had been expected.

Inside the folder lay a copy of the requisition form Juno Sarte had brought in, the one that had made the Dravin alloy purchase lawful, and beneath it a single sheet from the records office in Carrowmere recording four comparisons and four refusals. Mira read the sheet first. Requisitions and Stores, Works and Ways, Movement and Passage, the Office of the Accord Seal. Each department had opened its specimen book. Each had answered in its own form of words. None of them owned the signature. She read the sheet a second time more slowly, because the first reading had skimmed a line in the middle and she had felt it skim, a flattening of attention that came now without warning and left her unsure which words she had actually taken in. She went back to the top and made herself take the lines in order. The second reading held.

Dunmore watched her without filling the silence. She was an analyst by training and a patient woman by policy, and she understood that information given too quickly to a depleted officer was information wasted.

"Sarte is in Carrowmere," she said at last. "She has been there since she came in. We put her in a quiet lodging near the bonded warehouses, with a woman from the port office checking on her twice a day and a lock on the door that she controls from inside. It was meant to be for two nights. It has been longer, because the form raised questions nobody wanted to answer in a hurry."

Mira turned the requisition copy over, though she knew its back would be blank. The signature at the foot of the front page held her eye. It was clear and practiced, neither hurried nor disguised, and it matched nothing in four books. Whether it matched anything anywhere was the first thing she would have to settle in Carrowmere, in vaults and retired registers and any place a specimen might be kept outside the four departments that had already answered, because a fifth book, if one existed, changed the shape of the whole matter, and if none existed then the absence itself was the fact she would have to work from.

"Who wants her," Mira said. It was not quite a question.

"Three live possibilities," Dunmore said. "I will give them to you as possibilities and no more, because we have proof of none of them. Valtessa wants her. She was theirs, she left, and services do not enjoy being left. The seller wants her. She can speak to the alloy and to the route it took, and the seller has already shown a preference for quiet endings. And the Accord wants her quiet. Not dead, necessarily. Quiet. A witness who says nothing is almost as useful as a witness who cannot be found, and considerably cheaper."

Mira considered the three in turn and felt her attention narrow as she did so, as if the room had grown smaller around the folder. Narrowing was the shape her tiredness took now. It did not announce itself as fatigue. It arrived as a reduction in what she could hold at once, three possibilities becoming two, two becoming one bright point and a blur at the edges, until she set the folder down and deliberately widened her view to the lamp, the table, Dunmore's patient face, in order to begin again. She had been reduced before, after heavy reads, and she knew the discipline for it. Smaller steps. Nothing carried in the head that could be carried on paper. No decision made at the moment it first presented itself, because the first presentation was always the most confident and the least examined.

"Staffing," she said.

"Reni, and that is nearly all," Dunmore said. "I can give you a local liaison for the lodging and a driver who knows the warehouse quarter. I cannot give you a second team. If you set Reni to watch Sarte, nobody watches the approaches. If you set her to the approaches, nobody sits with Sarte. If you take the trace yourself, you are away from the lodging for hours at a time with files in your hands and your attention in a vault. That is the doing both badly that I mentioned."

Mira nodded. She was turning over, without yet forming it as a plan, whether the two jobs could be made to share a spine, the trace telling her where the threat would come from and the detail positioned accordingly, or whether that was the kind of elegant solution that tired officers invented because the plain solution, which was to ask for more people, had already been refused. She let the thought sit unformed. In Carrowmere she would need to know whether Sarte was sleeping and whether she was fit to be moved at short notice, because a principal who had not slept in days moved slowly, startled badly, and made decisions that a rested principal would not make, and none of that appeared in a lodging report written by a kind woman from the port office who recorded meals taken and visitors received and nothing about the hours between midnight and dawn.

"The court position," she said.

"Unclear, and it may not stay unclear for long," Dunmore said. "Valtessa has made noises through intermediaries about a lawful return. Nothing filed that we have seen. Whether they move through the courts first or reach covertly first is the difference between a week of paperwork we can watch and a night we cannot. If a request is filed, we will hear of it, though perhaps not quickly. If nothing is filed, that silence will also be information, of a less comfortable kind."

Mira opened the second folder Dunmore slid across, which held what the Office had on the lodging. A plan of two floors, drawn by hand. A street sketch. A note on the neighbouring buildings, most of them bonded warehouses with night watchmen and high windows. The accommodation was adequate and no more. She studied the plan and found, as she had expected, that nobody had mapped the service corridor that ran behind the lodging rooms on the upper floor, the narrow passage by which coal and linen and refuse moved without crossing the guest stairs. It appeared on the plan as a thick line, a wall where no wall was. Whether that corridor could be walked end to end, where it entered, where it left, who used it and at what hours, were questions the plan did not answer, and until they were answered the lodging had an approach that existed on paper as solid and in fact as unknown.

She made a note of the corridor on the back of the lodging sheet, in pencil, in her own shorthand, and then a second note beneath it listing what she would need in Carrowmere that nobody had thought to send ahead. A key to the lodging that did not pass through the port office. The name of the night watchman on the nearest warehouse and the hours he walked his round. A room where she could read files without being observed from the street. None of it was exotic. All of it would take time to arrange, and time was the currency she was shortest of, shorter even than people. She could feel the task dividing in her mind into the part that watched and the part that searched, and she could feel the seam between them, thin as paper, where the failure would come if it came. Her work would be to keep that seam from tearing for as long as Sarte needed her to, and to know, without flattering herself, the hour at which it began to tear.

She closed the folder. The small delay came again when Dunmore asked whether she understood the tasking, a pause of a breath or two while the words arranged themselves, and she was aware of resenting the pause and of setting the resentment aside as one more thing she could not afford to carry.

"I understand," she said. "Sarte stays alive and stays talking. The signature gets traced as far as it can be traced. I do the first with too few people and the second with too little time, and I tell you at once if one of them starts to fail, rather than letting it fail quietly to protect the other."

"That is the tasking," Dunmore said. "One more thing, and it is not a courtesy. Your state. You are entering this reduced and we both know it. I am not asking you to confirm it for the record. I am telling you that the record already says it, and that if the pressure comes and you find the narrowing closing in, you are to trade ground for time and call it in, not spend what you do not have to hold a position we can retake." She gathered the folders into a single stack and squared them with care. "Carrowmere, then. Keep her alive, Mira. And find out whose hand the Accord has been obeying all this time, if you can. If you cannot, bring me the proof that it belongs to nobody. That will have to be enough for the people above me, because it is the truth, and the truth is the only version of this that does not fall apart later."

Mira took the folders and stood, and the room seemed for a moment to tilt very slightly and then to settle, and she waited until it had settled before she walked to the door. In the corridor she paused, set the folders down on the narrow ledge by the stairs, and read the Carrowmere sheet one final time, committing the order of the refusals to memory in the slow way that held.

## Ground Truth

Mira began with the building, because the building was the part she could learn before anyone tried to use it against them.

The Office accommodation rooms occupied the upper floor of the annexe at Carrowmere, a plain block behind the main offices. There were six rooms in a row along a single corridor running from the stair head to a blank end wall, each with a door facing the corridor, a small window facing the yard, a bed, a chair, a table fixed to the wall, and a washstand. Each door carried a lock that could be turned from inside and a second lock, higher up, that could only be turned from the corridor side with a key held by the duty officer. Anyone placed in those rooms was both sheltered and held, and the difference depended on who held the second key and how honestly the roster for it was kept.

Behind that corridor ran the service corridor, reached by a narrow door beside the stair head that most visitors never noticed because it was painted the same colour as the wall and had no handle on the corridor side. Mira walked it twice before noon and once again after, learning it by distance and touch. It was lower and narrower, its ceiling crossed by pipes in grey cloth lagging, and it ran the full length of the block behind the rooms, so each room had in its back wall a small service hatch at waist height for linen, hot water cans, and meal trays. The hatches were still there. Most had been screwed shut from the service side and then unscrewed again by someone who had not recorded the work. Two were stiff. One, serving the third room, swung at a touch and latched only if lifted as it closed. Mira noted which, and in what order.

The service corridor had three other doors. At the far end a door gave onto the back stair, descending to the yard and a gate locked at night and checked on the rounds. At the midpoint a locked door opened into a store for bedding and sealed water. Near the stair head a third locked door held the fuse boards and speaking tubes no longer trusted. The back stair door took the large iron key in the duty room. The store and fuse room took smaller keys from the same ring. The painted door took no key from the service side: it could be pushed open from within the service corridor, and from the room corridor only by pressing its lower panel while lifting, a trick written down nowhere Mira could find.

The rounds pattern at night was regular enough to be learned, and regular enough to be used by anyone else who cared to learn it. The night porter walked the yard at the top of each hour and tried the gate. The duty officer walked the room corridor at twenty minutes past the hour, pausing at each door, and entered the walk in the night book. The service corridor was walked once, at the second hour after midnight, by night staff with cans for the early risers, and otherwise not at all unless a tap knocked or a hatch stuck. Between walks there were stretches of forty minutes when, on paper, nobody whose name was in any book was anywhere near the accommodation rooms. Mira copied the pattern into her notebook, and the times again in reverse order, because a pattern read backwards showed its gaps more plainly. The gaps were not small.

A protected person could be moved into those rooms. Mira settled that before considering whether it should be done. The annexe standing orders allowed the Office to house a person under informal protection for a limited period when no safer lodging could be kept unrecorded elsewhere. But a destination for a protected person had to be filed inside Accord systems. There was no lawful way, and no honest informal way she would sign, of placing Juno Sarte in a room that did not exist on any record. The filing was an entry in the accommodation register, a copy to the duty roster, and a notice in a sealed envelope in the duty room safe, naming the person, the room, the reason in general terms, and the officer responsible. Sealed, but filed. General, but named. Those systems were read by Accord clerks, summarised in Accord returns, and reachable by anyone with a plausible reason and a stamp no desk had time to question. To move Sarte there was to write down, in the Office's own hand, the one fact the rest of the work was trying to keep scattered: where she slept.

Mira set that fact aside without deciding it. Dunmore, in tasking her, had named the cost plainly: she could not run a protection detail and a document trace at once and do either well. She would have to do both thinly. So she split the work the only way the people available allowed, and wrote the split down where both of them could see it, because a split that lived only in her head would fail the first time she was in the wrong corridor at the wrong hour.

Reni Vosk would stay with Sarte. Reni was twenty one, junior in rank and not junior in steadiness, with the gift of being present in a room without making it feel watched. She would stay at the modest house on the edge of Carrowmere where Sarte was lodged, whose landlady believed her guest was a clerk recovering from a fever of the chest. Reni would eat there, sleep across the landing, keep the stair and street door in her awareness without appearing to, and move only if Mira sent word in the agreed form or came herself. Her whole task, in one line in the notebook, was the woman, not the paper.

Perrin Loft would work the specimen books. Perrin was a records assistant with the patience for comparison work, which is rarer than intelligence and, for this task, more useful. He would take the photograph of the requisition form Sarte had brought out of Valtessa, its authorising signature in a hand that flowed and then stopped, as if the writer had been certain of the line and uninterested in being read afterwards. He would carry copies, never the photograph itself beyond the records floor, to each department whose specimen books might contain that hand, ask in the prescribed form whether the signature matched any specimen held, record each answer in the words given, and bring the four answers back together, not one at a time, because Mira did not want her judgement bent by the order in which honest men and women happened to reply.

Mira herself would work the corridor and the room: the service corridor and room corridor, the stairs and yard gate, and how a person who wished Sarte harm, or wished her elsewhere, or wished her silent, might approach the places where Sarte was or might soon be; and the room Sarte presently occupied, and in prospect whichever accommodation room might be assigned. That work could not be delegated to Reni, who did not have the gift, or to Perrin, whose hands were full of paper. Mira would also hold the whole shape in her head at once, the woman and the paper and the building, which was the part Dunmore had warned her she would do badly. By the second day her endurance, already reduced by the weeks before it, was a ration she could feel herself spending, with no reserve she could honestly promise anyone.

The paper came back first, in the sense that paper worked in hours and people worked in days. Perrin returned on the afternoon of the second day with a folder held against his chest with both hands, and laid his work out in the order in which the specimen books were kept in the central records, which was not the order in which he had visited them. He explained that distinction twice, and Mira let him, because care of that kind was the whole value of the man.

The signature matched no specimen book. That was the finding, and it was complete.

He had run the photographed signature against the specimen holdings of every department that could plausibly have authorised a requisition of that class, by the book in the plain sense: book open, specimen sheet beside the copy, comparison made line by line and stroke by stroke by the keeper of each book or by Perrin under the keeper's eye. Four departments held specimen books for authorising officers of the relevant grade. Four had been asked. Four had answered, and each answer, Perrin was at pains to establish, was honest. He had watched whether any keeper hesitated in the wrong place, asked each to show the whole range of hands for the grade, and returned to one department a second time, on a pretext about a date, to see whether a different clerk on a different day gave the same answer in different words. It was.

He had set the four answers out in a table, because a table was the form in which records kept such things, and four denials side by side said something four letters in a folder did not. Mira read it between them, and when she later copied it into her report she copied it exactly, wording and all, because the wording was the evidence.

| Department | Denial wording as recorded | Date of answer, in story terms |
|---|---|---|
| Department of Requisitions and Stores | Not ours. No officer of this department signs in this hand, and no specimen in this book approaches it. | The afternoon of the comparisons, first hour |
| Department of Works and Ways | This hand is unknown to this book. The form is ours in pattern and the signature is not ours in fact. | The afternoon of the comparisons, second hour |
| Office of the Accord Seal | We hold no specimen that answers to this signature, and we certify none was ever lodged with us. | The afternoon of the comparisons, third hour |
| Department of Movement and Passage | Denied. The signature authorises movement we did not order, in a hand none of our authorising officers owns. | The afternoon of the comparisons, before the evening rounds |

Each denial was different in its language, which was one reason she believed them. A denial composed in common tended to arrive in matching phrases, because agreement smooths wording the way handling smooths a coin. These had not been smoothed. Requisitions and Stores was blunt and a little offended. Works and Ways had separated form from signature, conceding the pattern and refusing the hand. The Office of the Accord Seal had gone further than asked, denying not only a present specimen but any ever lodged, a claim Perrin had checked against its lodgement registers and found consistent. Movement and Passage, the most natural fit for a requisition that moved material across a border, had denied both the order and the hand, then closed its book with a firmness that suggested the keeper wished the question had never entered his room.

Mira asked the questions she was required to ask and two she was not. Had any keeper asked to keep the copy. No. Had any pressed beyond the prescribed form about who had brought the question. One, at Works and Ways, and had accepted the prescribed answer. Had any specimen come close. Perrin shook his head and then qualified it. There had been hands with a similar slope, and hands with a similar way of finishing a stroke and lifting. There had been no hand with the same combination, and the combination was the signature: a set of decisions made together, at speed, over years, until they became a hand. This hand was practised and at ease, not a clumsy copy swelling in one place and starving in another. It simply was not a hand any of the four books held.

Four departments. Four honest denials. A signature at the foot of a lawful looking form, authorising a purchase and a movement that had in fact happened, been paid for, carried, and delivered, and that belonged, on the evidence of every specimen book in which it should have lived, to nobody at all. That closed one question and opened the larger one on which the ground truth of this case rested: not whose hand it was, but who, in the present week, wanted the woman who had photographed it.

Mira laid out the possibilities that evening, the table on one side of her notebook and a map she had drawn of the annexe corridors on the other, and made herself state each in its strongest form before weighing any of them, because a possibility stated weakly was a possibility dismissed without work.

The first was that Valtessa wanted Sarte back. Juno Sarte was a Valtessan port inspector. Manifests and requisitions and sealed consignments had passed under her eye as routine, until she understood that a particular chain was not what its papers said: the absence at the heart of the technique being sold along that chain was not an accident of manufacture but the product itself, and the alloy that carried it moved under requisitions whose authorisations she could not trace. She had photographed a form she should have processed and forgotten, then spent six weeks deciding what to do with it, sleeping less and telling no one, until the deciding became heavier than any choice it was deciding between. She had come to Carrowmere: a defection in progress, unfinished and dangerous to everyone, most of all to the service she had left, which could not know how much she had understood or whom she had told. A service in that position wants its inspector back, to question her, contain her, discredit her in advance if she speaks, and learn the shape of the hole she has left in its knowledge of itself. All of that was true, and live.

The second was that the seller wanted her. The seller, whoever stood behind that word in the chain Sarte had uncovered, was not a service and did not behave as services behaved. A service had territory, letterheads, specimen books, and a reputation to keep plausible. A seller had a product, a route, and arrangements that worked only so long as they were not described aloud by someone who had watched them from inside a port office with a clear eye and a long memory. Sarte could describe them: the pattern of consignments, the intervals, the way manifest weights related to weights at delivery, as a witness whose whole working life had been exactly such records. To the seller she was not a defection. She was a description waiting to be given, in a room the seller did not control. A seller who learned she was lodged in a town where the chain had buyers and carriers would want her. Not back. Just her. The distinction mattered, because the methods that followed from each decided where a protection detail should rest its attention at night.

The third was that the Accord wanted her quiet. Mira wrote this one most carefully, because it involved the house she herself served and because care, in such a case, was not loyalty but method. The Accord was not one thing. It was departments, as the specimen table had just demonstrated in four different wordings, and offices and seals and keepers of books, and among its parts there were, beyond honest question, hands that had processed the chain Sarte had uncovered, whether in knowledge or in the kind of incuriosity that is paid for precisely because it does not ask to be called knowledge. A signature that belonged to nobody was a fact about the Accord before it was a fact about anything else: somewhere inside its own systems a form could be authorised in a practised hand no specimen book held, and would move, and be paid, and be honoured at borders, and leave no officer who could be asked to answer for it. There were people inside such a system who would regard Sarte not as a witness or a defector but as a noise to be stopped before it reached a room where it would have to be answered. Quiet, in that usage, was a wide word. It covered a woman persuaded to go home and say she had been ill, a woman whose account was taken in full and filed where accounts did not return, and worse things at the far end of the word. The Accord as an idea wanted none of those things. The Accord as people with interests inside buildings might want any of them, depending on who learned of her first. And Mira could not protect her from the Accord by standing outside it. She was the Accord, in the person of the Office, and every tool she had wrote Sarte's name into Accord paper.

Three live possibilities. She did not rank them, because ranking required evidence she did not yet have, and a detail built for the first and surprised by the second failed in a different place from one built for the third and surprised by the first. The detail had to be built for all three at once, with one junior officer, one records assistant, and herself, reduced as she was. That was the ground truth under the ground truth. The rest was workmanship.

She went to Sarte's room that night because some things about a protected person could not be learned from a landlady's account or a junior officer's report, however good both were, and Reni's reports were good. Reni met her on the landing and gave her account in the order Mira had taught her: doors, visitors, meals, sleep. The doors had been as they should be. No visitors beyond the landlady and a boy with coal. Sarte had eaten at midday and again, a little, in the evening. As to sleep, Reni hesitated, and the hesitation was itself a report. She did not think Sarte slept. She lay down, the house went quiet, but in the morning the bed looked slept beside rather than slept in, and twice Reni, waking across the landing, had heard no sound and understood, from the quality of the silence, that the woman was awake and keeping herself quiet so as not to wake the house. Mira sent Reni down to the kitchen on an errand that would take ten minutes and was not an errand at all, because what she was about to do she did by preference alone: her gift, watched, made witnesses of people who had not asked to carry what it found.

The room was small and clean and smelled of the landlady's soap and of paper, because Sarte kept her few papers in a cloth folder on the table and opened it often. Mira did not touch the folder. She stood just inside the door, hands empty, attention lowered to the level at which the gift worked: not objects, but what rooms had absorbed from people who had lived among them at length and in feeling. Weight did not yield names, dates, or intentions that could be carried into a hearing. It yielded what a life had pressed into a place by repetition and force of feeling, without labels, so the whole discipline of the gift lay in refusing to label it faster than the evidence allowed.

She let the room come to her, and it came not as a picture but as a condition of her own body, a sympathetic weather. Wakefulness first, of a particular kind. Not the bright wakefulness of listening for a step on the stair, though some of that lay thin over the top. Beneath it, older and deeper, a sustained wakefulness that had become the room's normal state, of a woman who had stopped expecting sleep to solve anything and had reorganised her nights around its absence, filling them with review: a form remembered, a signature remembered, a dock at a particular hour, a colleague's face at a remark that had meant nothing then and had been reweighed since until it meant several things, none certain. And under the review, held down by it the way a stone holds papers in a draught, dread. Not panic, which was brief and loud and spent itself. This dread was quiet and budgeted, lived with long enough to have its own economy, the dread of a person who had counted the cost of what she had done and decided, in full knowledge of the figure, to go on paying it in instalments.

Beneath even that was a decision. The gift did not give its content. It never gave content of that kind, and that refusal was not a failure of the gift but its boundary, the line that kept it honest. What it gave was the decision's shape in feeling: a thing held. Held for weeks. Taken up each night and examined and not set down, not because the holder could not decide but because she had decided and was doing the harder work of continuing to have decided, day after day, in lodgings and fear and the company of strangers kind to her in a foreign office's accents. Six weeks of deciding, Sarte had said in the earlier work, in plain words, and this room confirmed it in the only currency the gift dealt in. She had not drifted to Carrowmere on a panic. She had walked there slowly, carrying a photograph and a decision she was still carrying, undiminished, in a room that had learned her wakefulness by heart.

Mira withdrew gradually, as she had been taught to withdraw from cold water, because a reading ended too sharply left its weather in the reader. Even so the cost arrived, in the body first. A heaviness settled behind her eyes that was not sleepiness but its opposite, a wired, grainy alertness she recognised as borrowed wakefulness, which would keep her own sleep shallow that night and the next. Her hands had the fine unsteadiness that came when the gift had been held open longer than her present strength allowed, and she put them flat on the door frame, grounding herself in painted wood that had absorbed nothing from anyone. Reduced endurance was a phrase in a brief. This was what it meant: every reading spent from a smaller purse, and the purse did not refill on the schedule the work required.

She found Reni in the kitchen and gave the conclusion in the language of the detail, not of the gift. Sarte is not sleeping, Mira said. She has not slept properly for weeks, and she will not start because we ask her to or the house is quiet. But she is fit. She is resolved. She is eating, she is steady, she is not a person coming apart, and she is not to be treated as one. If she asks for something to help her sleep, that is her business and the doctor's if she wants one. A tired woman who is resolved is still resolved, and we build for the woman she is. Reni nodded and wrote nothing down, which was correct, and Mira saw how much of this detail already rested on a twenty one year old in a kitchen with her sleeves pushed up, and how well that judgement bore the weight.

The question of the move was put formally the next morning, because Mira would not have it decided in a corridor, and opposed formally, on the record, by the officer who would have to carry it.

Corin Vell had held the accommodation duty at the annexe for four years, longer than the posting usually kept a person, by saying no in writing when other officers said yes aloud and forgot afterwards they had said it. He was neither brave nor timid by his own reckoning. He was a man who had learned what a building could and could not promise, and distrusted any plan that asked it to promise more than its locks and roster could deliver in the small hours, when the staff was thinnest and the promises were tested.

He made his objection in the duty room, the register closed before him, his objection entered, at his own insistence, in the duty book before he spoke it aloud, so the speaking could not later be remembered as stronger or weaker than the writing. He made it plainly, without raising his voice.

Concentrating a wanted person in the Office's own building, he said, made her findable in exactly one place. At present the woman was in a private house, under a plain story, in a street of similar houses, and finding her required work: a name traced, a lodging identified, a landlady approached or watched, each step leaving a trace and taking time. Move her into the annexe and every step was done for whoever came looking. The destination would be filed, as it had to be filed. The room would be numbered. The roster would name the officers responsible, the register the period of stay, the sealed envelope in his own safe the woman herself, and all that paper sat inside a building clerks, porters, visiting officers, and tradesmen entered daily on business giving any of them, or anyone who could borrow their errand, a lawful reason to be within a corridor's length of her door. He did not accuse his own staff. A system did not need a traitor to leak, he said. It needed only to be a system, summarising and copying and carrying forward, until a fact entered once in a sealed envelope existed in outline in a dozen places no one thought of as places where secrets were kept.

And the building itself would not help them as much as they hoped, because its service corridors served too many masters. He knew those corridors. He had walked the second hour round in his first year and met, each time with a lawful reason in hand, a kitchen porter taking a short way to the yard, a clerk collecting bedding for a visiting party, a woman cleaning pipes who answered to the works department and not to him, and once a lost visitor with a candle, directed wrongly by a doorkeeper, wandering the service way and opening hatches in innocent error. Too many keys. Too many errands. Too many departments with a right to the back of the building and no duty to report their use of it to the officer who held the front. If they brought the woman here, they should know they were not bringing her into a fortress. They were bringing her into a lodging house with better paperwork, and the paperwork was the danger.

He closed the duty book and slid it to its place at the edge of the desk. He would carry out the move if ordered. He wished the book to show he had opposed it, and why: to oppose a thing completely, on the record, and still do it properly if done over his objection was, as far as he was concerned, the whole of his profession.

***

Mira did not answer him beyond thanking him and asking that a copy of the entry be held with the filing if the filing was ever made. She carried the objection away intact, because he was right about the one thing at its centre, and she had known it since she first wrote the word filed in her notebook. Concentrating Sarte made her findable in exactly one place. That was not a flaw a better argument could dissolve. It was a property of the move itself, and the service corridors serving too many masters was a description of the building she had spent two days learning, stated by a man who had learned it over four years.

She went that night to the service corridor, because the room corridor, walked and listened at and entered in books, was the building's presented face, and presented faces absorbed very little. People composed themselves in presented places. In the passages behind them, where no duty required a pause at each door, a building kept the sediment of its real use.

The corridor at night was cold in the way of unheated service spaces, pipes ticking as they cooled, lagging giving off a faint smell of dust and old cloth. Mira entered from the back stair with the iron key Perrin had signed out for her, let the door close, and stood in the dark a full minute before she began. She mapped as she went, because mapping was the first duty and the gift the second. Doors, in order from the back stair: the yard door, locked, its bolt stiff in the lower socket; the store, locked; the six hatches, in room order, two stiff, one lifting as it closed, three sound; the fuse room, locked; the painted door, yielding at its lower edge as the porter had shown her. Locks, noted by key and feel. Rounds times, checked against the night book: yard at the hour, rooms at twenty past, this corridor at the second hour after midnight and not otherwise, which meant she could stand there a long stretch of the night in a place the building's routine did not visit. The corridor could be mapped. It had been mapped, in a notebook that now held its doors, locks, hatch faults, and rounds in a hand she could read in poor light. That question, at least, she could close.

Then she lowered her attention and let the corridor come to her as Sarte's room had come, and what came was entirely different in texture and entirely the same in its refusal to give her what she most wanted.

Someone had walked this corridor nightly for months without reason.

The sediment was unmistakable once she stopped looking for anything else. It was not the rounds, which had duty in it and haste at the end of a long night. It was not the kitchen staff, which carried purpose and the next task. Beneath those honest layers, older by accumulation and smoother by repetition, lay a patience that had worn the corridor the way water wears a step: someone who came not to go anywhere in particular but to be here, walking its length at an unhurried pace, pausing in no single place often enough to mark it, turning at the ends without impatience, night after night, for months, in a rhythm that had long stopped needing a reason the walker could have stated aloud. Regular. Patient. Unhurried. Mira felt the regularity in her own breathing and corrected it deliberately, because the gift at this depth lent its findings rather than describing them, and borrowed patience was dangerous in a corridor where patience might be the whole method of an enemy. The walking itself could be the point: a presence kept in training, a route kept warm, a building kept known by feet that would, on the one night the route was needed in earnest, not have to think about a single door.

What she did not feel, and this was the limit that bit hard enough to mark the whole case, was anything about who. No name gathered in the corridor. No face assembled out of the patience. No rank, department, errand, or allegiance presented itself, because weight gave affect and never identity, and months of a patient walker had pressed nothing into this place except patience itself, perfectly preserved and perfectly anonymous. The walker could have been the lost visitor grown methodical, or a servant with a private sorrow that walking soothed, or the advance education of a hand that intended, one night, to come along this corridor to a hatch that lifted as it closed, beside a room a filing somewhere named. All those readings fitted. None was entitled to her belief. She made herself record the finding in its exact, unsatisfying form, because a finding recorded more strongly than it was felt would become, downstream, a fact in someone else's mouth, and people died of facts of that manufacture.

Affect without identity. A corridor walked nightly for months by someone patient, for no reason the corridor could tell her. A room where a woman did not sleep and went on deciding. Four honest denials of a hand that had authorised real movement in the real world. Three live possibilities, none discharged: a service wanting its inspector back, a seller wanting its witness, a house of many departments wanting its embarrassment quiet. And whether the answer would come through courts and filings and ordinary pressures, or covertly, along a service corridor at an hour the rounds did not cover, still open, because nothing she had learned had earned her the right to close it.

Mira locked the back stair door behind her, returned the iron key to its hook, and stood a while in the yard in cold air that belonged to nobody and had absorbed nothing, until her hands were steady, the borrowed patience had left her breathing, and her own reduced endurance was the only tiredness she carried. Then she went to write it all down, in that order, before any of it could improve itself in memory into something cleaner than the truth.

## The Collapse

The court request arrived in a plain folder, and it undid the neatest part of the case before Mira had finished reading the second page.

She read it standing at the long table in the records room, because sitting would have made it feel like correspondence and it was not correspondence. It was a formal request, filed openly through the courts, asking for Juno Sarte to be returned to Valtessan custody. It named Sarte. It named the grounds. It set out a hearing timetable, with dates for submissions and dates for replies and a date on which a judge would hear argument from both sides in a room where anyone entitled to be present could hear it too. It had been lodged through the proper clerk, stamped through the proper office, and copied to every party the rules required it to be copied to, including the Office.

Mira turned the pages again, more slowly.

A service that meant to take a woman off a street did not do this. A service that meant to lift her from a lodging house in the night, or to intercept her between one safe address and another, did not first announce itself to a court, bind itself to a timetable, and put its reasons in writing where they could be argued against. It might file papers as cover. She considered that. She considered it for a full minute, with the folder open and the stamp marks in front of her, and she set it aside, not because cover was impossible but because this filing was the wrong shape for cover. Cover was thin. Cover was late and vague and built to be withdrawn. This was thick with particulars. It committed Valtessa to a public account of why Sarte belonged back in its custody, and it exposed that account to challenge, and it gave the Office weeks in which to prepare its answer. If the filing was a blind, it was a blind that cost its makers more than the thing it was meant to hide was worth.

What it looked like, read plainly, was a service going to law because it wanted its inspector back and did not intend to steal her.

The reasonable reading died there, on the table, without drama. For two days the case had rested on a shape that anyone could follow. Valtessa had the motive, and the motive was public, and nobody had to invent it. Sarte had carried an Accord requisition form out of a chain that was not hers to break, and she had carried it to Carrowmere, and her own service wanted her for it. The timing fitted. The interest fitted. A Valtessan attempt on Sarte explained itself in one sentence, and an explanation that short was a comfort in a week when nothing else in the case would shorten. Mira had not believed it whole. She had kept it at the front of the board because it was reasonable, and because a protection detail had to be built against something, and the reasonable threat was the one you built against first.

Now the front of the board was empty.

Dunmore came in with the specimen table under one arm and set it down beside the folder without asking what the folder was. He had the look of a man who had checked a thing three times and disliked it more on the third checking than on the first.

"You have seen it," Mira said.

"I have seen a copy," he said. "Filed openly. Copied to us as of right. I had the clerk confirm the stamp, because I wanted it to be a forgery. It is not a forgery."

"No," Mira said. "It is what it says it is."

"Which removes our best suspect."

"It removes our best story," she said. "Those are not the same thing, and we have been treating them as if they were."

She pulled the specimen table toward her. It was the artifact the whole trace had produced, and it was a strange kind of evidence, because what it documented, line by line, was an absence. Four departments. Four specimen books. Four requests, each properly made, each asking the same question in the same terms: is this authorising signature yours. Four answers, each set down with its wording and its date, and not one of them an admission.

The first department had answered in the language of the registry: the signature did not appear in its book for the relevant years, and no officer of that department was authorised in that form. The second had answered in the language of refusal with regret, the courtesy of a busy office that had nevertheless done the search and named the clerk who did it. The third had answered briefly, almost curtly, and had added the name of the department it believed the signature must belong to, which was the fourth. The fourth had answered at greatest length, with a history of its own authorising forms attached, and had named the second department in return. Round the table it went. Each denial pointed, where it pointed at all, at another chair at the same table, and every chair was empty.

Mira had read the table a dozen times as a problem in handwriting. Now she read it as a problem in conduct.

"We have been assuming one of them is lying," she said.

"We have been assuming it because the alternative is worse," Dunmore said.

"Say the alternative."

"That the signature belongs to nobody. That it authorises because the form says it authorises, and it has gone on doing so because nobody, in any of the four, ever had cause to stand in its way."

She let that sit. It was worse. A liar was a person, and a person could be found, and a case with a person in it had a shape a protection detail could use. A signature that belonged to nobody had no habits, no address, no hour of the day when it was careless. It could not be watched. It could only be understood, and understanding it would not, by itself, keep Sarte alive through the week.

But the denials had to be settled first, on evidence and not on preference, and Mira went through them again the way she would have gone through a row of doors, testing each one for the give that meant it was not locked at all.

The registry department had opened its book. That mattered. A department shielding one of its own did not need to open anything; it could answer from policy, in general terms, and let the question die of vagueness. This one had named its clerk, its book, and its years, and had invited a second inspection if the Office wished to make one. Mira had made one. Perrin Loft had stood in that registry with the specimen under his hand and gone page by page through hands that were authorised, and the signature on the requisition was not among them, and the clerk who kept the book had been annoyed at the suggestion that the book might be incomplete, which was the annoyance of a man defending his own work and not another man's secret.

The second department had answered with regret and with detail. Its specimen book was smaller, its authorising officers few enough to be listed on a single sheet, and each of those officers accounted for his own hand in writing when asked, without being told why he was asked. One of them had offered, unasked, the information that he had refused to sign a requisition in that form some years before because the form asked for more than his warrant covered. A department building a lie did not volunteer a refusal from its own history. It volunteered nothing. It kept its surface smooth.

The third department, the curt one, had been curt with everyone for years; its brevity was a habit of the office and not a defence of this signature in particular, and when pressed it had produced its outgoing register for the month the requisition bore, and the register had no gap in it where such a document could have been entered and removed. Mira had counted the entries herself, twice, at a desk that was too small, while the department's keeper watched her count and said nothing at all, which she had come to read not as concealment but as the patience of a man who knew what his register would show.

The fourth had sent its history, forms upon forms, the whole genealogy of how its authorising mark had changed across the years, and the requisition signature matched none of its stages, early or late. It had also sent the history without being asked for all of it. Concealment rationed; it gave the minimum and defended the minimum. These four had given more than the minimum, each in its own manner, and a shared lie would have smoothed those manners alike. These did not sound alike.

"They are honest," Mira said at last. "All four of them."

"You are certain."

"I am as certain as the evidence allows, which in this case is a good deal. Nobody is lying about the signature. Nobody in those four departments signed that form, and nobody there is sheltering the man or woman who did. The suspicion toward the departments collapses with the rest. We should stop spending hours on it."

Dunmore looked down at the table, at the four wordings in their four hands.

"Then the denials are the fact," he said. "Not the obstacle to the fact. The fact itself."

"Yes," Mira said. "Write it that way."

That was the second collapse, quieter than the first, and in its way larger. The trace had been built as an accusation looking for a target. It ended as a description. Four departments, four honest denials, and a signature in continuous use that no living office would own. Whatever had authorised the purchase that Sarte's form recorded, it had not come out of the ordinary machinery of the Accord as that machinery now stood. It had come out of a place in the machinery that the machinery itself could no longer see.

The corridor was the next thing to fall, and it fell in the opposite direction. It did not clear. It clouded.

Mira had walked the service corridor of the building where Sarte was lodged on each of the last three nights, at hours she had varied on purpose, and she had read what the building held in the way she had been given to read such things. There had been, on each night, the weight of someone who had walked that corridor nightly for months without reason. Not a resident's weight, which carried the small settled purpose of a person going to a door that was theirs. Not a servant's weight at an hour of duty, which carried the errand in it. This was a presence that had worn the route the way water wears a channel, by repetition, and had no errand in it that she could find. She had reported it in those terms and no stronger, because those were the terms the evidence allowed. Weight gave her affect and habit. It gave her no face, no name, no errand, and she had refused, in her own notes as much as to Dunmore, to let it give her more.

Now Reni Vosk came back from the building's lower office with the stores and laundry rounds in a copied schedule, and laid it beside the corridor map, and the two documents agreed with each other in a way that spoiled the night work entirely.

The rounds were real. They were nightly. They had been nightly for longer than Sarte had been in Carrowmere. A stores porter went the length of the service corridor with a hand cart at the hour when the kitchens closed their books. A laundry orderly followed later with returns for the upper floors, and a second stores round, smaller, went through before the night staff changed. The timings were not exact, because rounds never were; they spread across a band of the night, and the band covered, almost exactly, the hours in which Mira had read the walker in the corridor.

"Almost exactly," Reni said. She had her finger on the schedule and she did not move it. "Your hours sit inside their band. All three nights."

"I know," Mira said.

"So the walker could be the porter."

"The walker could be the porter, or the orderly, or the second stores round, or a man who has learned the rounds and walks inside them because a man walking inside them is invisible. Corridor presence alone proves nothing. It never proved much. Now it proves nothing at all."

What remained true was narrower and she held to the narrowness. Someone had walked that corridor nightly for months without reason she could read. The rounds gave that walking a dozen innocent owners and one guilty one, and weight could not separate them, because weight had never been able to separate anyone from anyone. She thought, because the case kept returning her to it, of the certificate book in an earlier week of this same affair, and of what its weight had held and what it had refused to hold: two hands across the occasions she had read, one unhurried and habitual, a hand at home in its work, and one in a hurry on a few occasions only, pressed where the habitual hand was not pressed. The weight had given no face to either hand. It had given no name to either hand. It had told her that hurry existed in the chain and where the hurry sat, and it had told her nothing else, and everything she had built since had been built by other means, on paper and in registers and in the spoken word of clerks. That was the discipline of the gift and she kept it now. The corridor told her a route was worn. It did not tell her who had worn it, and she would not pretend otherwise to make the night easier to plan.

Sarte herself, when Mira went up to her, supplied the last of the collapses, and supplied it without knowing she had done so.

The inspector sat by the window of the lodging room with the curtains drawn against it, which was her own precaution and a good one, and she had the requisition form's copy face down on the table beside her, which was also her own precaution and told Mira something about the week Sarte had spent with the thing. She was a composed woman whose composure cost her visibly, and she answered questions the way she had plainly answered them all her working life, in order, without ornament, stopping when the answer stopped.

"I have been asked three times now whether I saw the man who signed it," she said. "By your Dunmore. By the clerk who took my first statement. By you, on the first day, though you asked it better. I did not see him. I never saw any signer. I saw the form. The form came into the chain at the point where my inspection began, already authorised, already complete. My work was the goods and the prices and whether the purchase answered its description. The signature was above my work. It was not part of my work. I noticed it only when I understood what the purchase was."

"And when you understood," Mira said, "what did you do."

"I copied the form. I reported the purchase through the channel that exists for reporting it. And the channel did nothing, for eleven days, and on the twelfth day a man I had never met knew my name and my lodging and the hour I took my meals, and told me, very politely, that my report had caused difficulty for people who had not expected difficulty from that quarter. That is when I came to Carrowmere."

"What did he want from you."

"Then? To frighten me. Now?" Sarte turned the copy over at last, and looked at the signature as Mira had looked at it, as at a thing that would not resolve. "Now I think they want my testimony. Not the form. The form is a copy; copies can be called copies, and disputed, and lost in an office fire of the mind. What cannot be disputed so easily is the chain as I watched it happen. Who handed the goods over. Who received them. What was said at the warehouse door and what the tally clerk wrote and then unwrote. I am the only person who stood in the chain and is willing to say where she stood. That is what the seller's side wants from me, Mira. Not the paper. The witness. If I am gone, the form is an orphan document and they can let it die of neglect. If I live and speak, the form has a voice."

"That changes the value of the courts to them," she said.

"It changes the value of everything. A hearing gives me a room to speak in. Valtessa, whatever else it wants, has just asked for that room to be built, with dates. The seller's side wants no room, ever, anywhere. Draw your own map from that."

She did. The map had been redrawing itself all afternoon and Sarte had just set its final line. The threat to this woman was not her own service, proceeding by law, in the open, toward a hearing at which Sarte would be more dangerous to her enemies than she was in hiding. The threat was the side that needed her silent, and that side had already shown it knew her name and her lodging and the hour she took her meals, in a city that was not Carrowmere. Hiding her in lodgings chosen for their obscurity had been the first answer to that knowledge. It was no longer a good answer. Obscurity was a defence against being found. Sarte had already been found once, before she ever reached this city, and what had found her then could find a lodging again.

Which left the question of where a protected person could be put, and here the Accord's own rules closed around the case like a hand.

Mira went over it with Dunmore in the lower office, with the building's plan between them, because he had raised the obvious alternative and she wanted it killed properly, on the record of their own reasoning, before the night came.

"A house outside the system," he said. "A private address. No filing, no register, no Office paper. We know two or three that would hold her."

"We know two or three that held people before the rules changed," Mira said. "A protected person's destination must be filed inside Accord systems. That is not custom. That is the rule as it stands, and it stands because of what happened the last time a protected person was held off the record and something went wrong and no one could say where she had been. If I move Sarte tonight to a private address and do not file it, I am in breach, and the breach will be found, and when it is found the protection itself is voidable by anyone who wants it voided. And if I do file it, as I must, then the destination exists inside the Accord. It can be read by anyone with the access to read such filings. Moving her does not take her out of the Accord's sight. Moving her writes her new address in the Accord's own hand and leaves it in the Accord's own drawer."

"Then a private house buys her nothing but our breach."

"It buys her a worse building with no watch, no rounds, no controlled doors, and a filing that still betrays the address. It buys her less."

"And Office accommodation."

"Office accommodation is filed too. The record exists inside the Accord either way. That is the ground truth of this case and we do not get to choose whether it is true. We only get to choose what stands around the record. Around a private address there is nothing. Around Office accommodation there are doors that answer to us, a corridor we have mapped, rounds we have timed, and officers of our own on the floor. If the threat is outside the Accord, that building is the hardest place in Carrowmere for it to reach her. If the threat is inside the Accord..." She stopped.

"Say it," Dunmore said.

"If the threat is the Accord itself, or a piece of it, then the filing betrays her wherever we put her, and the building makes no difference to the betrayal. It makes a difference to the attempt. A man still has to come down a corridor we are standing in."

He was quiet for a moment. "There is no safer site than Office accommodation that the Office can actually hold," he said. "That is the sentence, is it not. Not that it is safe. That nothing safer exists which we are permitted, and able, to hold."

"That is the sentence," Mira said. "The ground truth carried a question all week: where can she be put that the signature cannot follow. The answer is nowhere, because the signature is not a place or a person we have found; it is a standing authority inside the system that protects her. So the question resolves to a different one. Not where is she beyond reach. Where is the reach slowest, and the corridor longest, and the watch our own. That is a decision, not a discovery. I will make it tonight."

She said it as a decision because that was what the collapse had left her. Every comfortable reading was gone. Valtessa was going to law. The departments were honest. The corridor proved nothing. The form was not the prize; the witness was. And the system that would record wherever Sarte slept was the same system whose unowned signature had started the whole affair. There was no clean ground to stand the woman on. There was only the best of the unclean ground, chosen with open eyes, and a corridor, and the hours of the rounds, and whatever came down them.

Mira folded the court request back into its plain folder and set the specimen table on top of it, four honest denials holding down the end of the reasonable reading, and went up the stairs to tell Sarte that she would move that night.

## Pressure

Mira moved Sarte that night with the filing already made and the record already sitting inside the Accord systems where anyone with the access could read it, and she did the work herself, and Sarte carried her own small bag down the back stair without complaint and asked only once, at the turn of the stair, whether the new room had curtains, and Mira said that it did and that they were already drawn, and Sarte nodded as if curtains were a currency she had learned to count her safety in, and perhaps they were. The Office accommodation stood four streets from the lodging house, in a building the Office kept for officers in transit and for persons in Sarte's position and he gave up the room on the second floor at the end of the service corridor without asking the name of the woman who would sleep in it, and Mira walked the room before Sarte entered it and checked the window and its fastening and the door and its lock and the heavy wardrobe that stood against the wall beside the door and the washstand and the chair, and she moved the chair six inches from where it stood and then she brought Sarte in and set her bag on the bed and told her the rules of the night in short plain sentences. The door stayed locked. The curtains stayed drawn. She did not answer a knock in any voice. She did not open for Mira's own knock unless Mira gave the word they had agreed on the stair, and if anything happened in the corridor she was to get behind the bed, away from the door, and stay there until Mira herself came in and spoke her name. Sarte repeated the rules back without error and sat down on the bed with her hands folded, and Mira read the room's weight as she left it and found a woman not sleeping and not likely to sleep, braced and wakeful in the dark behind the drawn curtains, and she closed the door and heard the lock go over and stood a moment with her hand flat on the wood, knowing what she had done in the plainest terms there were. If the threat was the seller's side, she had put Sarte behind the strongest doors the Office held in Carrowmere. If the threat was the Accord itself, she had walked Sarte into it herself, filed the address in its own systems, locked its own lock, and taken up her position inside its own walls to wait for whatever it chose to send. There was no third reading. She had chosen with the choice in full view and she did not decorate it now. She went down the service corridor to where Reni Vosk waited by the stores door, and the night settled into its rounds around them both.

The corridor was the map she had drawn made solid, and she set her watch by it as she had planned to do, with Reni placed where the service corridor bent at the laundry stair, out of the straight sight of Sarte's door but close enough to reach it at a run, and herself placed in the shallow recess where the corridor widened for the porters' carts, from which she could see the door and the whole straight length that led to it and the head of the main stair besides. The rounds gave the night its shape and she used the shape without trusting it, because she had learned that afternoon what corridor presence proved and what it did not. The stores porter came through with his cart at the hour the kitchens closed their books, and the cart had a wheel that complained at every third turn, and Mira let him pass through her attention and out of it because his weight was the weight of a man doing the same work he had done the night before and would do the night after, and the laundry orderly followed in the later band with the returns folded over one arm, and after the orderly had gone the corridor emptied into the gap that the rounds themselves left, the long quiet interval between the last of the night's legitimate traffic and the changing of the night staff, and it was the gap she had marked on the map in Dunmore's office and the gap she had planned around, because a man who needed time at a door would come in the gap, when the corridor belonged to no one's duty. Her endurance was not what it had been at the start of the week and she did not pretend otherwise to herself in the dark of the recess. Reduced was the word the brief of her own condition carried and reduced was the fact. Her attention, which on a rested night would have held the whole corridor and the stair and the bend and the door in one unbroken field, now held them in turns, and she had to move it deliberately from point to point like a lamp carried from window to window, and twice in the first hour of the gap she found that her reading of the corridor's far end had thinned to nothing while she watched the near end, and she corrected it not by trying harder, which was the error of tired people, but by narrowing what she asked of herself to the door and the straight length and the bend, in that order, and letting the stair go, because Reni's position covered the stair's approach by the bend and a reduced watch honestly narrowed was worth more than a wide one falsely claimed. When her reactions felt a fraction behind the corridor's small sounds she noted the fraction and allowed for it in where she stood, a half step closer to the door than pride would have put her, so that the distance would pay what her speed could not. None of it was error. All of it was depletion, accounted for, priced into the ground she had chosen, and the corridor stayed quiet, and the gap wore on, and Sarte's door stayed shut and locked at the end of the straight length with its wakeful weight behind it, and then, well inside the gap, in the part of the night where no round was due and no cart wheel should have complained, a man came up the main stair and turned into the service corridor and walked its length toward Sarte's room as if he had every right to the floor under his feet.

He walked like the rounds. That was the first thing. He walked at a porter's pace with a porter's unconcern, neither hurrying nor loitering, and she spent her attention instead on the things that were wrong. He carried nothing. No cart, no returns, no bundle, no keys that sounded. The rounds carried the building's goods; that was what the rounds were. A man in the gap with empty hands was not a round. And he did not look at the doors he passed. The porter looked, the orderly looked, because the corridor was their work and the doors were its landmarks, but this man kept his eyes at the height of a man counting without looking, and Mira understood with a clarity that needed no gift and no weight to support it that he was counting the doors from the stair, and that he knew the number he was counting to, and that the number was Sarte's. He knew the gap. He knew the room. Both facts arrived in her together and she was already moving when they finished arriving, out of the recess and along the wall, and she spoke Reni's name once, not loudly, in the tone they had agreed would mean the thing itself and not a rehearsal of it, and Reni came off the bend at once and the man heard them both in the same moment and stopped being a porter.

He went for the door. Not for Mira, not for the stair, for the door, which told her the woman behind the door was the whole of his errand. He reached it two strides before Mira did and his hand was at the lock with something small and bright in it when Mira came into him from the side and put her shoulder and her whole reduced weight into the space between his body and the door, and the corridor, which had been a map and a timetable and a theory all evening, became at once a narrow place of wood and plaster and breath. He was strong in the way of men who have been chosen for a door and not for a discussion. He turned on her and tried to make room for his arm by making room of her, against the wall, and the wall was hard and the room behind her was the wrong room to be pressed toward, so she gave him the wall instead, stepping inside his reach where his strength had no lever and putting her forearm across the front of his shoulder and driving him the half pace sideways that put the door frame's edge between his balance and his intention. He struck at her once, short and professional, and she took it on the raised arm and the blow landed and was absorbed and did not move her off the door, and she got her hand to his wrist, the wrist of the hand that held the bright small thing, and she kept it. That was her method and it had always been her method, in this corridor as in every place before it. She did not trade blows. She took ground and she took hold and she took away, one at a time, the things a man needed in order to do harm: his distance first, then his free hand, then his balance, then his errand. She turned his held wrist down and in, not far, only to the point where holding the bright thing became a choice between the thing and the wrist, and he chose the wrist and the thing fell and Reni, coming in on his other side as the plan had placed her to do, kicked it away down the corridor. Then he had both of them to deal with and the corridor was too narrow for what he was trying to do, and he knew it, and he tried the one thing left to a man who has lost the door and the tool and the initiative, which was to break backward out of their holds and make the stair and take his failure home to whoever had sent him, and Mira could not allow the stair, because a man who knew the gap and the room number loose in the night was a question that would be asked again at another door on another night, and she went with him as he broke, which was the dangerous moment and she knew it as she committed to it, her tired body a fraction behind her intention so that his elbow came closer to her face than it should have come, depletion and not misjudgement, and she paid for the fraction not with her face but with her position, giving up the wall and taking him instead into the open doorway recess of the room next to Sarte's, the empty room, whose door stood ajar as the keeper had left it, and inside that recess the furniture she had noted on her first walk of the floor did the work she had arranged the evening to let it do. She put him against the heavy table that stood just inside the empty room's door. She put him there with her hip and her shoulder and the whole line of her body, and the table took his weight and hers and did not move and with the table at his back his balance had nowhere further to go, and Reni was there at his free side and Reni's hand closed on his other arm, and for one moment the three of them were locked in that doorway in a silence broken only by breath, and Mira said, in a voice she kept deliberately ordinary, that he could stop now, that the door behind her was not going to open, that the woman was not going to be reached, that he was held and would be held and that the only choice remaining to him was how much of himself he spent against a table. He tested the holds once more, thoroughly and then he stopped, and the stopping was real, and Mira felt the fight go out of the corridor all at once and leave the three of them standing in its place.

Reni's arm had been opened across the forearm in the moment Mira had not seen, the moment at the door when the bright small thing was still in play and Reni had come in on the man's free side and something in that close work had found her. Mira did not look at it closely and did not ask her to show it, because there was work first, and she set the work in its order and watched Reni do it with the arm in question and take the measure of the thing by what it cost. Reni went to close the empty room's door and her hand arrived at the latch a moment after her intention did, so that she had to look at the latch to finish what her hand had begun, which she never had to do. She took the man's second arm from Mira to bind his hands and her grip on that side closed late and closed short, and she shifted her stance to make up the difference with her body instead of her hand, without comment, and the sentences she spoke while they worked came out shortened to their bones. Door. Yes. Hold him. Now. Her world had narrowed to the width of the holds she could still make on time, and she worked inside the narrowing without complaint and without waste, and when Mira, having secured the man's hands with the cord from the porter's cart that stood forgotten at the corridor's end, told her to sit on the empty room's step and keep her arm still and raised, Reni obeyed a fraction later than obedience usually came from her, because the instruction had to travel through the same narrowed country as everything else that arm was being asked to do. And yet. When the man was down and bound and breathing hard against the table's leg, and Sarte's door down the corridor was still shut and its lock still whole, Reni looked at her own forearm where it rested raised on her knee, and a steadiness came into her that Mira had not seen in her before and did not entirely like. She was not shaken. She was not performing calm for an audience of senior officers. She sat in the corridor of a building that had just tried to kill the woman in their charge and she attended to her arm the way a person attends to a new instrument they have been handed, with interest, with a quiet and private satisfaction at finding it in her possession, and she said, in her ordinary voice, that it was nothing, that she had had worse from a kitchen knife as a girl. It was offered as a door closing on the subject, from the inside, by a young officer who had discovered something about herself in the last four minutes and was in no hurry to share the discovery. Mira let the door stay closed. There would be time, later, in a lit room, with the arm properly seen to, to worry about what Reni Vosk was pleased about, and worrying about it then would be a senior officer's duty and she would do it. Now there was a corridor to finish.

Sarte had not opened her door. Through everything, the shouting that had not quite become shouting, the table, the breath, the bound man's one outburst when the cord first tightened Sarte had kept the rules of the night exactly as she had repeated them back on the bed's edge hours before. Behind the bed, away from the door, until Mira herself came in and spoke her name. When Mira at last knocked and gave the agreed word and said her name through the wood, there was a pause in which furniture moved, the bed being climbed out from behind, and then the lock went over and Sarte stood in the doorway in her night clothes with a coat over them, composed and pale and entirely unhurt, and she looked once at the bound man and once at Reni on the step and once at Mira, and she said that she had heard a table, which under the circumstances struck Mira, in the exhausted country her own mind had narrowed to, as the most accurate report of a fight she had ever been given. The corridor was secured in the plain senses that mattered. The man was held, alive and whole enough to answer every question that would now be put to him. The stair was Reni's again, watched from the step she could not yet leave. The main door below was sent for and barred by the keeper, who had come up in his night coat with a face that said he had understood the building's new use without a word of explanation. And Mira stood at last with her back to Sarte's door, in the corridor she had mapped and mistrusted and chosen anyway, and felt the reduced thing in her settle toward something lower still, and she did not mistake the settling for failure, because Sarte was standing unhurt in a doorway behind her and the man who had known the gap and the number was sitting bound against a table that had not moved, and that was the whole of what the night had been for.

## The Silence

Mira stood in the registry doorway and said nothing.

The room had settled into the particular quiet that comes after paper has been moved from one pile to another and nobody wants to be the first to move it again. Perrin Loft had gone. The specimen table lay where he had left it, squared to the edge of the desk, the form Sarte had brought lying beside it at a slight angle, as if the two documents were not quite willing to touch. Outside the window the light was failing in the slow Carrowmere way, and the corridor beyond the door carried only the ordinary sounds of an Office building at the end of its working day.

She let the facts arrange themselves without her help.

Four departments had been asked. Four departments had answered. Each answer had been checked against a specimen book, in the presence of the officer who kept that book, by a man whose whole employment was the careful handling of other people's handwriting. None of the four had hesitated in the manner of a clerk protecting a superior. None had asked for time, or for the form to be left with them overnight. Each had looked, compared, and denied, and the denial in each case had the plain shape of a thing that was simply true. The signature was not theirs. Their books, which recorded every authorised hand in order of enrolment, contained nothing that resembled it closely enough to argue about.

That was the first fact, and she turned it over slowly.

The second fact was quieter and, in its way, stranger. Valtessa had asked for Sarte back through the courts. Not through a side door, not through a quiet word between liaison officers, not through any of the informal pressures that services apply when they wish a matter settled without a record. Through the courts, with a timetable, with filings, with dates that other people could read. A service that intends to take a woman does not first announce, in writing, the lawful route by which it would prefer to have her. A service that files a request has accepted, at least for the present, that the woman will remain where she is while judges consider paper. The request did not make Valtessa innocent. It made Valtessa legible.

The third fact was older, and it had no face.

In a certificate book, weeks earlier, her weight had held two hands. One had been unhurried and habitual, the hand of a person performing a task so familiar that the body no longer consulted the mind about it. The other had been in a hurry a few times, only a few, pressing where the habitual hand rested, hurrying where the habitual hand lingered. She had never seen either hand. She had never seen a face to put above either wrist. Weight gave her affect and habit and haste, and it gave her nothing else, and she had learned, at cost, not to ask it for more than it could honestly carry. But the hurried hand had stayed with her, because haste in a place built for routine is a kind of signature in itself, and because a person who hurries only sometimes is a person who usually belongs where he is standing.

She stood in the doorway and held the three facts together without forcing them to agree.

Four honest refusals. A court request proceeding in daylight. A hand that had hurried, a few times, in a book of certificates signed as a matter of course. None of them named a person. None of them, alone, proved anything she could act on in front of a superior. Together they described a shape, and the shape was not the shape of a forgery in the ordinary sense, nor the shape of a rival service reaching in from outside, nor the shape of any single department quietly disowning its own work.

The building ticked and settled around her. Sarte was alive, upstairs, in a room with a number that very few people had any reason to know. A man was held who had known that number anyway.

Mira breathed once, evenly, and prepared to say the thing that all three facts had been waiting for her to say.

## Close

When she spoke, she spoke to Dunmore first, because Dunmore was the analyst and the record would be built from his notes, and then to the room, because Reni was standing by the inner door and Perrin Loft had been brought back with his table under his arm and both of them needed to hear the conclusion stated whole, once, without qualification creeping in at the edges to soften it.

"The signature belongs to nobody," she said.

She let that stand for a moment before she built on it, because she had watched conclusions fail before now when they were hurried past the people who would have to act on them.

"Four departments were asked. Four departments denied it. Each denial was given against that department's specimen book, checked page by page, and each denial was honest. I am not saying that as a courtesy to the officers who gave them. I am saying it because Loft watched the comparisons made, and because a dishonest denial has a shape, and none of these had it. Nobody asked for the form to be left behind. Nobody asked who else had been approached. Nobody performed surprise. They looked at a signature they did not recognise, in a book that records every hand they have ever authorised, and they said so."

Reni shifted her weight against the door frame and said nothing, which Mira noted and approved of.

"The signature is not forged from any of them," Mira said. "That is the next point, and it is a separate point. A forgery copies a real hand. It has an original somewhere, and the work of a forgery inquiry is to find the original and measure the distance between it and the copy. There is no original here. Loft compared the strokes against every enrolled specimen in four books, including hands retired, hands suspended, and hands belonging to officers now dead whose specimens remain on file for exactly this purpose. Nothing matched closely enough to be a source. Nothing matched closely enough to be a near relation. The hand that wrote on Sarte's form was not imitating any authorised hand in those books. It was writing as itself, confidently, in a form it had plainly used before, and it belongs to no one any of those departments will enrol, admit, or own."

She looked down at the form itself, lying beside the table, its authorising flourish dark and self assured in the lower box.

"It has been used as if it were authority," she said. "That is the offence, and it is also the whole of what we know about it. A requisition moved because that mark sat at the foot of it. Goods and permissions and movements followed from it, in the ordinary way, because clerks do not interrogate a signature that arrives in the correct box on the correct paper. The mark performed authority. It did not possess it. Those are different things, and the difference is the case."

Dunmore, who had been writing, stopped. "And the man," he said.

"The man is the second matter," Mira said, "and he is not the larger one."

She said it carefully, because she could feel the room wanting the man to be the answer. A man taken alive, in a corridor, in the course of an attempt on a protected person, was a satisfying shape for a case to end in. He could be questioned. He could be charged. He could be held up as evidence that the detail had worked. All of that was true, and none of it was the fact that mattered most.

"The attempt came inside an Accord building," she said. "Not at a gate. Not on a road between buildings. Not in lodgings taken in haste in a town where we had no footing. Inside this building, in a service corridor, outside a room in Office accommodation, at an hour chosen because the rounds left a gap at that place. He knew the gap. He knew the room number. Neither piece of knowledge was available outside these walls, and neither could have been guessed closely enough to risk an attempt on the strength of the guess. He walked a route that is not marked for visitors, at a pace that did not attract the porter, because he understood the routine of this house well enough to move inside it without breaking its surface. That is the fact that matters more than the man. Whoever sent him, and I am not naming a sender, because we have not established one, had the use of this building's own routines. The building was used against the person the building was sheltering."

No one spoke. Loft held his table against his chest like a shield he had not noticed raising.

"What he knew is recorded," Mira said. "The rounds gap, as he described it and as the duty log confirms it. The room number, and the floor, and the corridor by which he approached it. The hour he chose and the hour he was stopped. All of it is in the log of the attempt, signed by the officers present, and a copy goes with the specimen table into the case file. What he did not give us is also recorded, as an absence, in the same words. Who sent him is not established. Why that room was chosen on that night rather than another is not established. Whether he expected help inside the building is not established. I will not have those gaps filled by inference in any report that carries my name. An absence recorded honestly is worth more to the next officer than a conclusion recorded hopefully."

She thought, without saying it in those terms, of other cases in which an honest absence had been the only useful inheritance. She thought of [the death that was prepared before it was done](/2026/11/22/nightjar-taken-first-022/), and of how much of that work had consisted of refusing to let a tidy sequence stand in for a true one. The discipline was the same here. Record what the corridor gave. Leave the rest open.

"Now the disposition," she said. "Sarte survives the week."

She said it as a finding, not as a hope, and she watched Reni hear it as one.

"She remains in Office accommodation, under protection, and she has agreed to stay and to cooperate. That agreement was given this morning, in her own words, without conditions attached by us and without promises made by us that we are not authorised to make. She understands that cooperation is not a bargain for a particular outcome. She understands that the court request stands, and that it will proceed on its own timetable, on paper, slowly, in daylight, which is precisely where we want it. A claim that moves by filings and hearings is a claim we can read as it moves. Valtessa's request will be answered through the same channel in which it was made, by the officers whose work that is, and Sarte will be told of each stage as it reaches her. Nothing about her position will be settled in a corridor again if this Office can prevent it."

"And the form," Dunmore said.

"The specimen table is filed as the case artifact," Mira said. "Loft's table, with the four departments, the denial wording of each exactly as it was given, and the date of each comparison. It is filed with the form it concerns, and a copy is filed separately, so that the comparison can be repeated by any officer who doubts it without handling the original form. That table is the artifact of this case. Not the man. Not the corridor. The table. It proves a negative, which is the hardest kind of proof to keep, and it will be kept properly."

Before she turned to the cost, she dealt with the week itself, because Dunmore's notes would be read by officers who had not lived through it hour by hour and who might otherwise mistake survival for luck.

"The detail held," she said. "That sentence covers a great deal of unremarkable work, and I want the unremarkable work on the record. The move into this building was made at the hour chosen, by the route chosen, with the register entry made before the move and not after it. The room was watched in the manner agreed. The rounds were walked as the duty log shows them walked, and where the pattern left a gap, that gap is now known to us because a man used it, and it will be closed in the standing orders for this building from tonight, by the duty officer, with the change entered in the same log. Sarte was not left alone at any point at which the plan said she would not be left alone. When the attempt came, it came against a detail that was in position, and it failed against that detail, in that corridor, for that reason. I do not want the report to read as if she survived because the attempt was half hearted. It was not half hearted. It was prepared, informed, and timed. It failed because the people placed against it were where they had undertaken to be."

She paused there, because there was a second half to the sentence and she wanted it given its proper weight.

"It also failed inside our own walls, and both halves of that will stay in the record together. A report that keeps only the first half teaches the next detail commander that the building can be trusted. A report that keeps only the second teaches her that nothing can. The truth, as usual, is less comfortable than either. The building sheltered Sarte and admitted her attacker by the same routines, on the same night, and any officer who reads this file before taking charge of a protected person in Carrowmere should have to hold those two facts at once, as we have had to hold them."

Dunmore wrote that down without looking up, and Mira was grateful, in passing, for analysts who did not editorialize with their faces.

She turned then, deliberately, to the cost, because a close that did not name its cost was a close that had not finished.

"Cost," she said. "Reni Vosk took a cut across the forearm in the corridor, stopping a man who had come further into this building than any unauthorised person should have come. The arm has been stitched. She will keep the use of it. She was on duty, in position, and she did the work the detail asked of her, and the record will say so."

Reni smiled then, briefly and unmistakably, with the bright, private satisfaction of a young officer who has been tested in the one way that cannot be simulated in training and has not been found wanting. Mira saw it and felt the worry rise in her more sharply than it had risen at the sight of the stitched arm itself.

"She is pleased about it," Mira said, and she said it to Dunmore, for the record, in the same level tone as everything else, because she wanted it written down by someone other than herself. "That is my concern, and I want it in the notes as a concern, not as a criticism. A cut that heals is a small price and we have all paid larger. Pleasure in it is a different matter. An officer who is pleased to have been hurt in the course of the work will not guard against being hurt again in quite the same way as an officer who regards it as a failure of position. I am not disciplining her. I am naming it, here, while it is still small enough to name."

Reni's smile faded, not into shame, but into attention, which was the better outcome and the one Mira had intended.

Later, when the notes were being copied and Loft had gone back down to his registry with his table duplicated and his afternoon's work suddenly the centre of a case file, Mira found herself still thinking about how ordinary the machinery of the deception had been. Nothing in Sarte's form had glowed or stank or announced itself. It had arrived in the correct series, on the correct paper, and it had been processed by people doing their jobs in good faith, rather as [the purchase that was lawful on the Accord's own paper](/2026/11/24/nightjar-lawful-purchase-024/) had been processed, each clerk trusting the box above his own because the system asked him to. And behind that, older still, she could feel the shape of [the afternoon the certificates were signed without being read](/2026/11/23/nightjar-paper-origin-023/), a whole afternoon of habitual hands moving down a page while one hand, a few times, hurried. Systems did not fail at their dramatic points. They failed at their routine ones, where nobody looked, because looking had never before been necessary.

She closed the case on that thought, and signed the log.

## The Notch

One thread moved that evening, and it moved only a short distance, and it did not resolve.

Mira filed the specimen table and the form together herself, although filing was Loft's work and he would have done it more neatly. She wanted the physical act of placing them in the same folder, in the same hand, on the same evening, because she had learned that the order in which paper enters an archive decides, years later, whether a connection can still be seen by whoever comes looking for it. The table went on top, because the table was the finding. The form went beneath it, because the form was the question the finding answered and, at the same time, did not answer at all.

A signature with no owner, used for years.

She wrote the carried question on the folder in her own hand, in the plain words she had used in the room, because euphemism in a registry becomes, in time, a second filing system that nobody can search. The requisition chain was where it lived. That much the week had shown her. Forms moved along that chain from hand to hand and office to office, gathering authority as they travelled, and somewhere along it a mark had been set down, repeatedly, by a hand that no department enrolled and no specimen book contained. How many forms carried it, she did not know. How far back its use extended, she did not know, beyond the plain evidence that clerks had stopped seeing it as anything other than routine, and routine of that depth is not built in a month. What it had authorised in total, across all the movements and permissions that had followed from it, nobody in Carrowmere could yet say, and she suspected the total, when it was finally counted, would be the part of this case that mattered longest.

There was a practical side to the filing as well, and she attended to it with the same care, because a question that cannot be found again is no better than a question never asked. She entered the folder in the registry index under the form's series and under the name of the protected person, and she added a cross reference under the specimen table's own heading, so that an officer approaching the matter from the document side and an officer approaching it from the protection side would arrive at the same folder by different roads. She told Loft what she had done, and where, and made him repeat it back, not from any doubt of his competence but because a filing known to only one person is a filing already half lost. He noted it in his own hand, in his own book, and the small duplication pleased her more than she could have explained to anyone outside a registry. Paper survived by being in two places, remembered by two people, for two slightly different reasons.

She also considered, and rejected, sending a copy of the table upward that night. There would be a report, in due course, through the proper channel, and it would carry the finding at the size the finding deserved. A table sent alone, ahead of its narrative, invited a reader to decide what it meant before he knew what it had cost to establish, and Mira had no intention of letting the most important negative this office had produced in her service be summarised by a stranger in a hurry. The folder would wait in its drawer. Waiting, she had come to believe, was not the opposite of action. It was frequently the form action took when it was being done properly.

She did not write any of that on the folder. Speculation on a folder becomes, in time, a finding that nobody remembers making. She wrote the question, the date of the filing in the registry's own convention, and her initials, and she placed the folder in the cabinet drawer reserved for matters that remained open inside the Accord's own house.

Somewhere in that house, a hand had been signing that nobody would own. It had signed calmly, in the correct box, in a building full of people whose profession was noticing, and it had been believed. The house itself would have to answer for that eventually, in whatever slow and internal way the house answered for anything. Tonight it was enough that one folder existed in which the question was stated exactly, neither enlarged nor softened, and that Sarte was alive in a room upstairs, and that a man was held who could not explain how he had known what he knew.

Mira closed the drawer. It ran smoothly and latched without a sound, which in that old cabinet was as close to an acknowledgement as furniture offered.

On her way out she passed the small side room where Reni had been told to rest and had instead, predictably, not rested. The door stood open. Reni was sitting by the lamp with her sleeve folded back, holding her own stitched forearm up to the light, turning it slowly, curious rather than shaken.

## Case File

### Q: How are specimens enrolled in an Accord specimen signature book?

An officer's specimen is enrolled on the day authority is first granted, in the presence of the book keeper and one witness from outside the officer's own section. The officer signs three times in the book, in the ordinary working hand, without ornament or deliberate variation. Name, rank, department, and the instrument granting authority are recorded beside the specimens in the keeper's hand. Later changes of hand through age or injury require a fresh enrolment, cross referenced to the earlier entry, which is never removed from the book.

### Q: Who authorises a requisition in each department, and how is that authority delegated?

Authority to authorise a requisition rests with the officer named in each department's standing instrument for the class of requisition concerned. Delegation is permitted only downward, only in writing, and only for a stated period or a stated absence. A delegated authorisation is enrolled in the specimen book before it may be used, with the delegation instrument copied beside the specimen. Authority exercised by an officer whose delegation has lapsed is treated in audit as unauthorised, however regular the signature itself may appear.

### Q: What accommodation does the Office provide, and how is it secured?

Office accommodation consists of rooms within Office buildings set aside for protected persons, witnesses, and officers travelling on duty. Rooms are allocated by number through the duty officer, and the allocation is recorded in a register kept apart from general building records. Floors used for accommodation are served by the ordinary corridors and by the service corridors, and both routes are covered by the night rounds. Visitors are not admitted to accommodation floors, and no list of occupants is displayed or circulated beyond the detail assigned.

### Q: What is a court return request between member services, and how does its timetable run?

A court return request is a formal application by one member service asking that a person held or sheltered by another be returned through judicial process rather than by informal arrangement. The requesting service files its grounds, the receiving service files its answer, and a hearing is listed after both filings are complete. Intervals between stages are fixed by the court's own practice and are measured in weeks rather than days. Until the court directs otherwise, the person's position remains unchanged, and each stage is notified in writing to both services.

### Q: How does the service corridor system in the Carrowmere Office building work?

Service corridors run behind the principal rooms on each floor, connecting stores, laundries, and duty posts without crossing the public passages. They exist so that building work, deliveries, and cleaning can proceed without disturbing hearings or interviews. Access is by service doors that are kept latched and are intended for porters, stores staff, and officers on building duties. The corridors are included in the rounds pattern, but at longer intervals than the principal passages, since they are assumed to be empty for most of the night.

### Q: How is a signature compared against a specimen book in practice?

The comparison is made by the book keeper or a records assistant trained in the registry method, with the questioned signature and the book open together under the same light. Letter formation, joining habits, pressure patterns, and the treatment of capitals are noted in order, and the comparison proceeds through every enrolled specimen that could plausibly be the source. A conclusion of no match is recorded only after the whole relevant section of the book has been examined. Hasty comparisons are not accepted in audit, and a comparison may always be repeated.

### Q: What does an unowned signature mean in Accord audit practice?

An unowned signature is one that no department will accept as its own after comparison against its specimen book, and for which no enrolment, delegation, or retirement record can be produced. Audit practice treats such a signature as a defect in the chain of authority rather than as a defect in any single document. Every transaction that relied on it is flagged for review, in order of value and sensitivity. The finding is recorded centrally, since an unowned signature discovered in one registry may also appear in the papers of another.

### Q: How is a protected person's destination filed?

When a protected person is moved into Office accommodation, the destination filing records the building, the floor, and the room number, together with the detail assigned and the authority for the move. The filing is made by the officer commanding the detail and is held in the protected register, which is separate from accommodation registers and from general correspondence. Access to the filing is limited to the detail, the duty officer, and the commanding officer's nominated deputy. Changes of room require a fresh filing, made before the move takes place.

### Q: How do night rounds and stores rounds run in Office buildings?

Night rounds are walked by the duty porters and a junior officer at intervals set by the building's standing orders, covering principal passages, accommodation floors, and entrances in a fixed order that varies by night. Stores rounds are separate and concern locks, seals, and the condition of stored material rather than the presence of persons. Both rounds are logged with times at each checking point, and the log is signed at handover. A gap in a rounds pattern, once noticed, is reported to the duty officer the same night.

### Q: How does the Office hold a prisoner taken inside its own building?

A person taken inside an Office building is held in the building's detention rooms under the authority of the duty officer, pending interview and a decision on charge or transfer. The circumstances of the taking, including the place within the building and the knowledge the person displayed, are recorded before the first interview takes place. Medical needs are attended to first, and the holding is logged at each handover of the watch. Holding inside the building of capture is reviewed daily, since familiarity with the building is itself a security consideration.

### Q: How does Valtessan court process approach the reclaiming of an inspector?

Valtessan practice treats an inspector abroad as an officer on detached duty whose return is, in the first instance, a matter for formal request. The reclaiming service files its grounds with the court, identifying the officer, the duty from which recall is sought, and the provisions relied upon. Informal recovery is regarded within that system as a serious irregularity, and officers are instructed that a filed request suspends any other means of securing return. The process is slow by design, reflecting the weight Valtessan institutions place on recorded procedure.

### Q: What does a records assistant in the Carrowmere registry actually do?

A records assistant receives, registers, compares, and files the paper on which the registry's work depends. The duties include maintaining specimen books, conducting signature comparisons under the registry method, preparing tables of findings for officers, and keeping the archive in an order that later officers can search. Assistants do not decide cases, but their tables and comparisons are the evidence on which decisions rest, and their work is therefore checked and countersigned. A careful assistant is regarded in the registry as a safeguard in human form.

### Q: How is denial wording recorded in an audit table?

When a department denies a signature, the exact words of the denial are recorded in the audit table beside the department's name, rather than a summary prepared by the officer requesting the comparison. The date of the comparison and the identity of the book keeper present are entered in the same row. This practice exists because summaries drift, while a recorded denial in its original words can be tested later against the recollection of the officer who gave it. Tables prepared in this form are accepted as case artifacts.

### Q: What happens to a requisition whose authorisation cannot be owned?

The requisition is suspended as an authority, although actions already taken under it are not automatically undone. Each action that followed from it is reviewed separately, to establish whether it can be supported on another ground or must be reversed through the proper channel. The form itself is retained and filed with the findings, never destroyed, since it is the principal evidence of the defect. Departments are notified in general terms that the authorisation is not to be relied upon in future transactions of the same class.

### Q: What is Sarte's position as a witness under Office protection?

A witness under Office protection remains a free person whose movements are arranged, for safety, in consultation with the detail assigned to her. Protection does not create custody, and it does not oblige the witness to answer questions beyond those she has agreed to address. Her cooperation is recorded as given, and the limits of it are recorded with equal care. She is entitled to be told of any legal process concerning her position, including a court return request, at each stage at which that process moves.

### Q: What is the difference between a forged signature and an unowned one?

A forged signature imitates an existing authorised hand, and its inquiry seeks the original from which the imitation was made. An unowned signature imitates nobody. It presents itself in the correct place, in a confident hand, and performs the function of authority without corresponding to any enrolled specimen or delegation. The practical difference is considerable, since a forgery points toward a forger working from a model, while an unowned signature points toward a gap in the system of enrolment and checking itself.

### Q: How does the Office log an attempt inside its own building?

An attempt inside an Office building is logged by the duty officer in a dedicated section of the building log, recording the place, the hour, the persons present, and the outcome, before the end of the watch in which it occurred. Statements from the officers involved are attached, and any knowledge displayed by the person attempting, such as familiarity with rounds or room allocations, is recorded as a separate observation. The log entry is copied to the commanding officer and to the registry, since such attempts usually concern paper as well as persons.

### Q: What are Reni Vosk's rank and duties as a junior officer?

A junior officer of Reni Vosk's grade serves on protection details, rounds, escort duties, and registry support as assigned, under the direction of the officer commanding the detail. Junior officers are expected to hold a position, observe accurately, and report promptly, rather than to direct an operation. Service on a live detail is regarded as the principal training the Office offers at that grade, and an officer's conduct under pressure is recorded in her file in factual terms, including injuries received and the manner in which they were borne.

### Q: How are protection details staffed when an officer must do two jobs?

Standing practice prefers that protection and investigation be staffed separately, since each suffers when the same officer carries both. Where numbers require one officer to do both, the commanding officer records the arrangement as a known defect in the detail, sets priorities between the two duties in writing, and reviews the arrangement daily. Tasks are sequenced rather than combined wherever possible, so that the protected person is never left uncovered while documentary work proceeds. The defect is closed formally when separate staffing becomes available again.

### Q: What is the history of the Carrowmere registry's specimen archive?

The Carrowmere specimen archive began as a single book kept by the registry's first keeper, in which the authorised hands of the founding departments were enrolled together. As departments multiplied, the single book gave way to a book for each department, kept under common rules so that comparisons could be made across the whole archive. Retired and deceased officers' specimens were retained from the beginning, a decision later credited with resolving several disputed authorisations. The archive is now regarded as the registry's memory, and its continuity is protected accordingly.
