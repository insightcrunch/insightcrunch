Nothing in Indian law requires a Bill to be examined by a committee before Parliament votes on it. Parliamentary committees are the machinery through which detailed Bill scrutiny is supposed to happen, and the machinery is real: committees take evidence, examine clauses, summon officials and produce reports that governments must answer. But every stage of that machinery is switched on by a discretionary decision, and the switch belongs to the presiding officer of a House, to the House itself, or to the government of the day. The Constitution does not mention committees at all. No statute does either. The committee system lives in the rules of procedure that each House has made for itself, and what those rules create they leave optional.

That single fact explains most of the argument about scrutiny in Indian lawmaking. When a Bill is referred to a committee, the process slows down, evidence from affected parties enters the record, and drafting defects are often caught before they become litigation. When a Bill is not referred, nothing unlawful has happened. The reader who wants the full route a Bill travels from introduction to assent, through readings, voting and the President's signature, will find it in the account of [how a Bill becomes law in India](/2022/02/01/how-a-bill-becomes-law-india/); the subject here is narrower and, in the series' judgment, more consequential. It is what happens, or fails to happen, between introduction and the vote: the scrutiny stage, who controls it, and what the published record shows about how it has been used.

The argument of the article is the one its findable artifact makes visible. Set the stages of scrutiny side by side, eight in all, from consultation on a draft before introduction to examination of the rules made under an Act after enactment, and ask of each stage whether it is required, who decides whether it happens, and what the record shows. The column of requirements is almost empty. Pre-legislative consultation is an executive policy that a ministry may depart from by recording reasons. Reference to a committee is at the discretion of the Speaker or the Chairman, or of the House on a motion that the majority can defeat. A committee's report is a recommendation that the government may accept, dilute or ignore, answering only through a statement of the action taken. The time the House itself spends on a Bill is allotted by a business advisory committee dominated by the government's programme. And none of these choices can be questioned in a court, because the Constitution bars judicial inquiry into the validity of parliamentary proceedings on the ground of irregularity of procedure. Scrutiny in India is therefore not a duty that institutions discharge but a choice that political actors make, Bill by Bill. The series has documented, statute by statute, what follows when that choice is made in one direction. This article describes the architecture within which the choice is made.

## Committees as creatures of the rules

The first thing to fix is the source of the committee system, because its source explains its fragility. Article 118 of the Constitution empowers each House of Parliament to make rules for regulating its procedure and the conduct of its business. The committees of Parliament exist because the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in the Lok Sabha and the corresponding Rajya Sabha rules create them, define their composition, and state their functions. A committee is accordingly not a constitutional body like the Election Commission, and not a statutory body like the authorities this series has profiled elsewhere. It is an internal organ of a House, constituted under rules that the House can amend by its own decision, serviced by the secretariat of that House, and answerable to the House that created it.

This has two consequences a reader should carry through the article. The first is flexibility. Because committees are creatures of rules, Parliament has been able to redesign the system without legislation: the departmentally related standing committees were created in 1993 by changes to the rules, on the reports of the Rules Committees of the two Houses, and enlarged in 2004 the same way. The second consequence is the absence of any external enforcement. A rule of procedure binds within the House through the authority of the presiding officer; it does not create rights that a citizen can enforce outside. When a rule says a Bill may be referred, and no referral happens, there is no breach of anything a court can recognise, a point developed in the section on Article 122 below.

### Where do parliamentary committees get their authority?

Parliamentary committees derive their authority from the rules of procedure each House makes under Article 118 of the Constitution. They are not created by the Constitution or by any statute. The Speaker and the Chairman constitute, reconstitute and refer business to them under those rules, and their reports go to the House that owns them.

The rules build committees of two broad kinds. Standing committees are permanent in the sense that the institution continues from year to year, although their membership is renewed annually; they include the financial committees, the departmentally related standing committees, and a set of committees concerned with the internal working of the Houses, among them the committee on subordinate legislation and the committee on government assurances. Ad hoc committees are created for a defined task, typically the examination of one Bill or one subject, and cease to exist when they submit their report. Select committees of one House and joint committees of both Houses are the ad hoc forms that matter for legislation.

Membership follows two different methods. Members of the financial committees are elected by the House from among its members in accordance with the principle of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, which is the mechanism that guarantees the opposition a share of seats roughly matching its strength on the floor. Members of the departmentally related standing committees are nominated by the Speaker or the Chairman from among the members of their respective Houses, again with party strength in view. Ministers are barred from membership of the financial committees and of the departmentally related standing committees alike, because the function of these bodies is to examine the executive, and a minister sitting on one would be examining his own department. A member who is appointed a minister after nomination ceases to be a member of the committee.

Committees sit when the House is not sitting as readily as when it is, deliberate behind closed doors, and take evidence on oath or affirmation from officials, experts and members of the public who submit memoranda. Their reports are presented to the House, or to the presiding officer when the House is not in session, and are printed and made public. The minister concerned ordinarily makes a statement on the action the government proposes to take on the recommendations. None of this machinery is described in the Constitution. All of it is described, in detail, in the rules and in the directions issued by presiding officers, which is why an aspirant who learns the committee system from the constitutional text alone learns almost nothing about it.

## The financial committees: scrutiny of money after the vote

The oldest layer of the committee system is financial, and it predates the Constitution. The Public Accounts Committee was first set up in 1921 under the Government of India Act, 1919, and has continued without interruption. It consists of twenty-two members, fifteen from the Lok Sabha and seven from the Rajya Sabha, elected annually. Its chairman is appointed by the Speaker from among its members, and by a convention observed since 1967 the chairman is drawn from the opposition. Its function is to examine the appropriation accounts of the government and the audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General, and to satisfy itself that money voted by Parliament was spent within the scope of the demand, that it was spent by the authority competent to spend it, and that the expenditure conforms to the authority that governs it. It is, in the language of the textbooks, a post-mortem examination: the money has already left the treasury by the time the committee looks at it.

The Estimates Committee, constituted in 1950, is the largest committee of Parliament, with thirty members, all from the Lok Sabha. It examines the estimates presented to the House and suggests economies, improvements in organisation and efficiency, and alternative policies consistent with the policy underlying the estimates. Where the Public Accounts Committee asks whether money was spent as authorised, the Estimates Committee asks the prior question whether the same purpose could have been served for less. The Committee on Public Undertakings, set up in 1964 with twenty-two members drawn fifteen from the Lok Sabha and seven from the Rajya Sabha, applies the same two questions to the public sector undertakings, examining their reports and accounts and the audit reports on them.

One further feature of the financial committees is the material they work on, because it gives their scrutiny a documentary spine that Bill committees have to create for themselves. The Public Accounts Committee examines the accounts and the audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General, which are caused to be laid before Parliament under Article 151 of the Constitution; its examinations therefore proceed paragraph by paragraph through an independent constitutional audit, with the ministries called to explain the transactions the audit has flagged. The Estimates Committee, first constituted in 1950 on the recommendation of the then Finance Minister, John Mathai, works from the estimates themselves and from the ministries' replies to its questionnaires, and its reports on a department's organisation range well beyond the year's figures into the structure of the programmes the figures fund. The Committee on Public Undertakings works from the annual reports and audit of the undertakings. In each case an external, recurring document compels the examination into existence annually, which is the design property the Bill side of the system lacks.

These three committees matter to an article on Bill scrutiny for a reason that is easy to miss. They are the part of the committee system that a government cannot switch off. Their examination of expenditure, estimates and undertakings proceeds every year as a matter of course, because the business they examine is generated every year whether anyone refers it or not: audit reports are laid, estimates are presented, undertakings file accounts. The contrast with Bill scrutiny is exact. A Bill reaches a committee only because someone with the power to send it decides to send it. The financial committees show that Parliament knows how to build scrutiny that runs on its own momentum when it wants to. The legislative committees show what happens when scrutiny is left to run on invitation instead.

The financial committees also carry the template of the action taken loop that recurs throughout the committee system. A committee reports; the government replies, recommendation by recommendation, stating what it has accepted, what it has implemented and what it declines to do; the committee may then present an action taken report commenting on the replies, and in a further round may record its displeasure where a reply is evasive. Nothing in the loop compels. The government's obligation is to answer, not to comply, and the committee's sanction is publicity and the record. That distinction, between the duty to answer and the absence of any duty to comply, is the single most important fact about committee power in India, and it applies to reports on Bills exactly as it applies to reports on accounts.

## The departmentally related standing committees

The committee system in its modern form dates from 1993. Reports of the Rules Committees of the two Houses, adopted on 29 March 1993, led to the constitution of seventeen departmentally related standing committees with effect from April of that year, covering between them every ministry and department of the Union government and replacing three subject committees that had been set up in 1989. In July 2004 the system was restructured: the number of committees rose from seventeen to twenty-four, and the membership of each fell from forty-five to thirty-one. Sixteen of the twenty-four committees are serviced by the Lok Sabha secretariat and eight by the Rajya Sabha secretariat, and the chairperson of each committee is appointed by the presiding officer of the House whose secretariat services it.

Each committee has thirty-one members, twenty-one from the Lok Sabha nominated by the Speaker and ten from the Rajya Sabha nominated by the Chairman. The term of a committee does not exceed one year, after which it is reconstituted, so membership rotates and a member who serves with distinction has no tenure to rely on. A minister is not eligible for nomination, and a member appointed to the Council of Ministers ceases to sit. The short term and the exclusion of ministers are deliberate design choices: the committee is meant to be a body of backbenchers and opposition members looking at a department from outside, renewed often enough that it does not become a second establishment.

### How many departmentally related standing committees are there?

There are twenty-four departmentally related standing committees, sixteen serviced by the Lok Sabha secretariat and eight by the Rajya Sabha secretariat. The system began with seventeen committees in 1993 and was enlarged on restructuring in July 2004. Each committee has thirty-one members and a term of one year.

Rule 331C of the Lok Sabha Rules states the functions of these committees, and the Rajya Sabha rules state the corresponding functions for the committees serviced by that House. A committee considers the demands for grants of the ministries allotted to it and reports on them, with the express rider that its report shall not suggest anything of the nature of a cut motion. It examines Bills pertaining to its ministries that are referred to it by the Speaker or the Chairman and reports on them. It considers the annual reports of its ministries and reports on them. And it considers national basic long term policy documents presented to the Houses, if those documents are referred to it. The rule then states the boundary of the whole enterprise: the committees shall not consider matters of day to day administration of the ministries allotted to them. The design is scrutiny of policy, legislation and expenditure at the level of principle, with the running of the department left to the minister and the House's other devices of accountability.

The demands for grants function deserves attention because it is the one legislative function these committees perform every year without any discretionary trigger. After the Budget is presented, the House stands adjourned for a period while the committees examine the demands of their ministries in detail, hear officials, and report. The reports return to the House before the demands are discussed and voted, and they frame the discussion that follows. This is the committee system working as designed: examination first, vote afterwards, the sequence built into the calendar rather than left to a decision. It stands in visible contrast to the treatment of Bills, where no such sequence exists and examination happens only when someone chooses it. The examination of annual reports and of policy documents likewise proceeds on the committee's own initiative within its allotted subjects. Only the Bill function is referral-dependent, and it is the Bill function that has contracted.

What a committee does with a referred Bill is described in a later section. What belongs here is the structural point about coverage. Because every ministry is allotted to some committee, there is no Bill for which no committee exists. A Bill on any subject has a natural committee home whose members have spent the year examining that department's grants, reports and policies. The institutional capacity for universal Bill scrutiny was built in 1993 and enlarged in 2004. What was not built was any rule requiring the capacity to be used.

## Select committees and joint committees: the ad hoc route

Alongside the standing system runs an older instrument. A House considering a Bill may, on a motion adopted for the purpose, refer the Bill to a select committee of that House, composed of members of that House named in the motion, with the minister in charge ordinarily among them. Where both Houses are to be involved, the Bill may be referred to a joint committee, whose members are drawn from both Houses in the proportion the motions of the two Houses specify. A select or joint committee is constituted for the one task, takes evidence, considers the Bill clause by clause, and dissolves when its report is presented. It has no continuing existence, no other subjects, and no successor.

The ad hoc route has features the standing route lacks. Its membership is chosen for the Bill rather than inherited from an annual nomination, so a House can staff it with members who have a particular interest or expertise in the subject. Its mandate can be framed by the motion that creates it. And because it is created by a vote of the House, its constitution carries a political weight that a presiding officer's reference does not: the House has decided, in public and on the record, that this Bill needs examination. Joint committees have been used for the Bills a Parliament most wants to be seen examining, and the joint committee on the personal data protection legislation, which PRS Legislative Research records as having met seventy-eight times over more than two years before reporting, shows the ceiling of what the instrument can do when time and membership are given to it.

The price of the ad hoc route is that it requires a majority to vote for delay. A motion to refer a Bill to a select committee is a motion the treasury benches can defeat, and when the debate on a Bill has become a contest of numbers, the motion for scrutiny is usually the first casualty. PRS Legislative Research has recorded the pattern in the debate on the constitutional amendment for economically weaker sections, where a member's motion to send the Bill to a select committee failed for want of support from other parties. The standing committee route does not require such a vote, because the presiding officer may refer a Bill on his own authority under the rules. In practice the two routes converge on the same determinant: the willingness of those who control the parliamentary timetable to spend time on examination.

A distinction worth fixing for examination purposes, because it is routinely confused, is that a joint parliamentary committee constituted to inquire into a subject or an alleged irregularity is a different animal from a joint committee on a Bill, even though the names overlap. The one examines a text and reports amendments; the other investigates facts and reports findings. Both are ad hoc, both draw members from both Houses, and both dissolve on reporting, but their outputs are different kinds of document and their politics are different too. The select and joint committees discussed in this article are the Bill committees, the ones that stand in the path between introduction and enactment.


## The committee on subordinate legislation: scrutiny after enactment

Every Act in this series ends, as a working instrument, in rules. The parent statute states the policy and the skeleton; the operative detail, the forms, the fees, the timelines, the thresholds and often the substance, is supplied afterwards by rules, regulations, notifications and schemes that the executive makes under delegated power and lays before Parliament. The series has traced this pattern in field after field, and its clearest single account is in the technology law cluster, where the operative content of the digital statutes sits in instruments made under them, a point the [technology law guide](/2019/03/01/technology-law-india-complete-guide/) develops at length. The question the brief for this article insists on is simple: who, inside Parliament, looks at those instruments?

The answer is a committee almost no citizen has heard of. Each House has a committee on subordinate legislation, constituted first in 1953, consisting of fifteen members nominated by the presiding officer. Its function is to scrutinise and report to the House whether the powers to make regulations, rules, sub-rules and bye-laws conferred by the Constitution or delegated by Parliament are being properly exercised within the scope of the delegation. The committee examines the instruments laid before the House against a settled set of questions. Does the instrument accord with the general objects of the parent Act, or does it travel beyond them. Does it impose a tax, which delegated power cannot do. Does it operate retrospectively where the parent Act gave no authority for retrospective effect. Does it impose a fee or charge without clear authority, or bar the jurisdiction of the courts by a route the parent Act did not sanction. Was it laid before the House as the parent Act required, and was there undue delay in making or in laying it. Does it achieve the purpose it professes in language a person subject to it can understand.

That checklist is the only systematic examination the Indian legislative system applies to delegated legislation, and it is applied by thirty members across the two Houses and their secretariats to a volume of instruments that runs to thousands each year. The committee's reports are careful and often pointed: they have recorded instruments that exceeded the delegation, rules laid months or years late, and provisions whose effect was to amend the parent Act by executive action, which is the one thing delegated power can never legitimately do. Where a rule is framed so much more weakly than the parent Act that the statute's promise drains away in the instrument, the pattern the [article on reservation and accessibility rights under the disability legislation](/2017/08/15/rpwd-reservation-accessibility-rights/) records in its field, the committee on subordinate legislation is the body whose remit squarely covers the defect, and whose report is the natural place for the defect to be named.

Its weakness is not remit but attention. The committee cannot annul an instrument. It reports; the ministry replies; the instrument meanwhile operates, because laying procedures in Indian statutes rarely give the House a vote on the instrument at all, and a motion to modify or annul a rule, where the parent Act permits one, is a rarity in the business of either House. Reports of the committee are seldom debated. The result is an exact institutional parallel to the Bill problem: the scrutiny exists, it is professionally done, and its findings enter a record that few read and no one is obliged to act on. When the series describes a statute whose rules arrived late, or whose rules contradict its sections, or whose operative scheme was rewritten by notification, the failure is rarely that no institution was charged with looking. It is that the institution charged with looking could only look.

## The committee on government assurances

A smaller committee completes the map of the scrutiny bodies, because it polices the currency in which scrutiny trades: words spoken by ministers on the floor. When a minister, answering a question or intervening in a debate, gives an assurance, a promise or an undertaking that something will be done, examined or supplied, the statement is not left to the memory of the House. In each House a committee on government assurances, first constituted in 1953 and consisting of fifteen members in the Lok Sabha committee, extracts the assurances from the proceedings, records them, and pursues the ministries for their implementation within the period the committee allows, ordinarily three months. Ministries report the action taken or seek extensions; the committee reports to the House on assurances implemented, on assurances dropped with the committee's leave, and on assurances that remain pending beyond their time.

The committee's relevance to Bill scrutiny is indirect but real. Passage debates are full of ministerial assurances offered in place of amendments: that a concern will be met in the rules, that a class of cases will be protected in administration, that a review will follow. Where a Bill is passed quickly, on the strength of such assurances, the assurances committee is the only body that tracks whether the words were made good. Its record is therefore part of the honest account of what fast passage costs and what it does not. An assurance implemented is a scrutiny outcome achieved by a side door; an assurance pending for years is a promise that discharged a debate and nothing else. The committee cannot compel implementation either. It can name the default, and it does.

## Reference: the decision on which everything turns

The route by which a Bill reaches a committee is short to state. After a Bill is introduced, it may be referred to the departmentally related standing committee covering its ministry by the Speaker or the Chairman, as the case may be, under the rules governing the committees' functions. Alternatively, a member may move, at the stage when the Bill is taken up for consideration, that it be referred to a select committee of the House or to a joint committee of both Houses, and the House decides the motion by vote. The minister in charge may support such a motion, oppose it, or himself propose reference to a standing committee. Bills replacing ordinances, Bills certified as money Bills, and appropriation Bills travel on timetables or by routes that in practice take them outside this mechanism, and constitutional amendment Bills have gone both ways, some to standing or select committees and some straight to a vote.

No step in that route is obligatory. There is no provision of the Constitution, no statute, and no rule of either House that requires a Bill, or any class of Bill, to be referred to a committee. The presiding officer who declines to refer breaches nothing. The House that defeats a reference motion breaches nothing. The government that whips against reference and passes its Bill the same week breaches nothing. This is the proposition for which this article exists, and it is the proposition most often misstated in commentary on Parliament, where reference is described as a stage of the legislative process in the same sense as the readings and the vote. It is not a stage. It is an option.

Why then does reference happen at all. Partly from convention built up after 1993, when the standing committee system was new and presiding officers used the reference power freely enough that examination became the expectation for major Bills. Partly from calculation: a government may want a committee's imprimatur on a contentious measure, or may want the delay a committee provides while opinion settles, or may judge that amendments are coming anyway and prefer them drafted in a committee room rather than improvised on the floor. Partly from pressure: opposition parties, affected industries, state governments and the press can make non-reference itself the story, and reference is sometimes the price of a quieter passage. And partly from the character of individual presiding officers, whose differing use of the same discretionary power across terms is one of the explanations the referral record offers for its own variation. The committee stage described in the [farm legislation cluster's account of the protest and the repeal](/2014/05/01/farmers-protest-legislative-lessons/) shows both the demand for reference at its most intense and the consequences that followed from its refusal.

The timing of reference matters to its value. Reference follows introduction, which means the government has already fixed the policy, drafted the text, obtained cabinet approval and presented the Bill as its settled position. A committee examining a Bill is therefore examining a text its author has publicly committed to, and the political cost of large change is higher than it would have been at the draft stage. That is the structural reason the pre-legislative stage, examined below, matters so much in principle, and why its weakness compounds the weakness of the committee stage: by the time scrutiny is possible at all, the text has hardened.

## Inside the committee room: what examination actually consists of

When a Bill is referred, the committee secretariat prepares a background note, the ministry files a detailed briefing, and the committee settles a programme of sittings. The committee may invite memoranda from the public, and for Bills of wide effect it issues a press release inviting written submissions from individuals, associations and experts. It hears the secretary of the ministry and the legislative counsel who drafted the Bill, and it hears outside witnesses: regulators, state governments where the subject touches them, industry bodies, professional associations, academics and citizens who will live under the provision. Oral evidence is recorded. Members then deliberate on the evidence and consider the Bill clause by clause, proposing amendments among themselves and testing whether the ministry will accept them, because a recommendation the ministry has signalled it accepts is worth more than one it will resist on the floor.

### Can a court strike down an Act because the Bill was never sent to a committee?

No. Article 122 of the Constitution bars a court from calling in question the validity of proceedings in Parliament on the ground of alleged irregularity of procedure, and a decision not to refer a Bill is a matter of procedure within the House. The resulting Act stands or falls on its substance, not on the scrutiny it received.

The report that emerges states the committee's recommendations, often with the recommended amendments set out, and members who disagree may append minutes of dissent setting out their position and their reasons. Dissent notes are not a failure of the committee; they are one of its products, because they preserve for the House and for later readers the objections that the majority did not accept, in a form more considered than a floor speech. The report is presented to the House and the Bill then returns to the floor for consideration, where the government moves the amendments it accepts, members may move others, and the House votes. A committee report changes a Bill only to the extent that the House, voting, adopts its recommendations. The committee proposes; the House disposes; and because the majority on the floor is ordinarily the same majority that controls the reference decision, the committee's real influence lies in the evidence it has placed on record and the drafting defects it has exposed in terms the government finds embarrassing to defend.

That influence is larger than the formal position suggests, and it has one feature floor debate lacks. Committee deliberation is not governed by the whip in practice, and it is conducted away from the cameras. Members of the treasury benches can concede a drafting defect in a committee room without conceding anything in public, and officials can explain what a clause will actually do with a frankness the floor does not elicit. Committees examining Bills have secured changes to provisions their governments had defended in public, precisely because the room allowed the change to be made without a defeat. PRS Legislative Research records that committees examining Bills held on average nine meetings to finalise their reports during the seventeenth Lok Sabha, and that half of the reports on Bills in that term were presented within one hundred and fifteen days of reference. The personal data protection reference, at seventy-eight meetings over more than two years, is the extreme of the distribution, not its centre. A committee stage measured in weeks or a few months is the norm when it happens at all.

The one-year term of the standing committees presses on this work in a way worth noting. A committee reconstituted annually may lose, midway through a long reference, members who heard the early evidence. Successive committees inherit references and files. Select and joint committees escape this because they live exactly as long as their task. The standing committees compensate with the continuity of their secretariats and the accumulation of subject knowledge across references, which is why the same committees' reports on demands, annual reports and Bills in one ministry's field read as chapters of a continuing audit rather than as disconnected documents.

### Why are committee deliberations held behind closed doors?

Committee sittings are not public, and the evidence and deliberations are confidential until the report is presented. The design is deliberate: members are expected to examine a Bill on its merits across party lines, and confidentiality is what allows a member of the ruling party to press an official, or concede a defect, without performing for an audience.

## The record: referral, sittings and time

The centrepiece of the article is the record, and it must be stated with its sources and its periods attached, because committee referral is a subject on which single unsourced figures circulate endlessly. The figures that follow are those published by PRS Legislative Research, the research organisation that tracks the business of Parliament Bill by Bill and term by term, and each is stated for the term to which PRS assigns it.

The proportion of Bills referred to committees, measured across the whole term of each Lok Sabha, was sixty per cent in the fourteenth Lok Sabha, seventy-one per cent in the fifteenth, twenty-eight per cent in the sixteenth, and sixteen per cent in the seventeenth. The sequence is the article's central exhibit. The standing committee system was built in 1993 and enlarged in 2004; the fifteenth Lok Sabha's seventy-one per cent shows the system operating near the level its designers intended, with examination the default for major legislation and non-reference the exception requiring explanation. The fall to twenty-eight per cent and then to sixteen per cent is not a marginal adjustment. It is the conversion of examination from a default into an exception, effected without any change in the rules, by the use of a discretion the rules had always contained. Nothing in the rules changed between the fifteenth term and the seventeenth. What changed was the decision.

The same direction appears in the raw material of scrutiny, which is time. PRS compilations of the working of the Lok Sabha put the first Lok Sabha at about one hundred and thirty-five sitting days a year, and the average for the 1950s and 1960s at about one hundred and twenty days a year. The seventeenth Lok Sabha sat on two hundred and seventy-four days in all, an average of fifty-five sittings a year, the lowest figure for any Lok Sabha that completed its term. Fewer sitting days compress everything: the time for debate, the willingness to spend weeks on a committee stage, and the room for the unglamorous business of questions and calling attention through which departments are kept alert. A House that sits for fifty-five days a year and carries a full legislative programme will pass Bills quickly or not at all, and quick passage is what the record shows.

Within the seventeenth Lok Sabha, PRS records that one hundred and seventy-nine Bills, excluding finance and appropriation Bills, were passed; that fifty-eight per cent of Bills were passed within two weeks of their introduction; and that thirty-five per cent were passed with less than one hour of discussion in the House. The budget figures belong in the same picture, because demands for grants are the standing committees' guaranteed annual business: PRS records that about eighty per cent of budgeted expenditure between 2019 and 2023 was voted without discussion of the relevant demands, and that in 2023 the entire budget was passed without discussion, the demands being put to vote together at the end of the allotted days by the procedure known as the guillotine. The committee reports on those demands existed; the committees had examined and reported as the calendar requires. The floor stage that the reports exist to inform did not happen. Scrutiny's product was manufactured and then not consumed.

Two cautions belong with these figures, and the article states them because the record deserves to be argued from accurately. The first is that referral percentages are computed on the Bills of a term as PRS classifies them, and percentages published by other trackers on other bases, for a single session or a single year, will differ; the figures above are consistent with each other because they are one organisation's series across four terms. The second caution runs the other way. A low referral rate is not by itself proof that every unreferred Bill was defective, any more than a high referral rate is proof that referred Bills emerged well drafted. Committees have reported Bills with their worst provisions intact, and floors have caught defects committees missed. What the referral figures measure is the use of an institution, not the quality of its output. The quality evidence comes from the statutes themselves, and that is the series' territory: the defect identified in litigation years after enactment, the rule that rewrote the section, the threshold nobody tested against a worked example. Article after article in this series has found the operative failure of a statute at a point where evidence taken in a committee room, from the people the provision would govern, was the examination most likely to have caught it.

### Do opposition members sit on the committees that examine Bills?

Yes. Members of the financial committees are elected by proportional representation, which guarantees the opposition a share matching its strength, and members of the standing committees are nominated with party strength in view. Chairpersons are drawn from both the ruling and opposition parties, and the Public Accounts Committee is chaired by an opposition member by convention.

## Consultation before introduction: the 2014 policy

The first row of the artifact table is the stage that precedes Parliament altogether. By a decision of the Committee of Secretaries taken on 10 January 2014, communicated by the Legislative Department of the Ministry of Law and Justice on 5 February 2014, the Government of India adopted a Pre-legislative Consultation Policy. The policy directs every ministry and department to place draft legislation in the public domain for a period of at least thirty days before the proposal goes to the Cabinet, together with a justification for the proposal, its broad financial implications, and an assessment of its impact on the environment, on fundamental rights, and on the lives and livelihoods of the people it will affect. Where the legislation affects a particular group, the publicity is to be given in a manner that reaches that group. The comments received are to be summarised, the summary is itself to be placed in the public domain, and the feedback is to accompany the proposal when it goes forward.

The policy is well designed on paper, and its status is its weakness. It is an executive instruction, not a statute, not a rule of either House, and not a constitutional requirement. It binds ministries as a matter of internal discipline. It carries its own exception: a ministry may depart from consultation where it considers consultation not feasible or undesirable, recording its reasons. It creates no right in any citizen to be consulted, and its breach has no consequence that any forum is charged with imposing. The ministry that skips consultation answers, at most, a parliamentary question about it.

The compliance record has been assessed, and the assessment is not favourable. PRS Legislative Research, tracking the Bills introduced in Parliament against the policy, recorded that of the three hundred and one Bills introduced since the policy was adopted, two hundred and twenty-seven were presented without any prior consultation. For the sixteenth Lok Sabha taken separately, of one hundred and eighty-six Bills introduced, forty-four were placed in the public domain for comment, and of those forty-four, twenty-four were published for a period shorter than the thirty days the policy prescribes. Read together, the figures describe a policy observed in a minority of cases and observed in full in a smaller minority still. Nor is the direction of reform toward compulsion: the government has informed Parliament that there is no proposal to make pre-legislative consultation mandatory, a position that keeps the first stage of scrutiny exactly where the rest of the architecture keeps every stage, inside executive discretion.

Consultation and committee examination are sometimes confused in commentary, and the distinction matters for the citizen reader in particular, because these are the two doors through which the public enters lawmaking. Consultation happens before introduction, is run by the ministry, and takes the form of written comment on a published draft. Committee evidence happens after introduction, is run by Parliament, and takes the form of memoranda and oral hearings before members. A citizen organisation that misses the first door may still use the second, but only if the second door is opened, which is the reference decision. When both doors stay shut, a Bill travels from a ministry's file to the statute book without any structured occasion on which the people it governs were invited to speak to its text. That journey, and not any single statute, is the subject the record in this article documents.

## Why no court can supply the missing requirement

It is natural to ask whether the courts can do what the rules do not, and treat an unexamined Bill as a defective one. The constitutional text answers the question directly. Article 122 provides that the validity of any proceedings in Parliament shall not be called in question on the ground of any alleged irregularity of procedure, and that no officer or member of Parliament exercising powers for regulating procedure or maintaining order shall be subject to the jurisdiction of any court in respect of the exercise of those powers. The corresponding provision for state legislatures is Article 212. Deciding whether a Bill goes to a committee, allotting the time for its debate, and putting its clauses to the vote are proceedings in Parliament and regulation of procedure in their purest form. A failure to refer, a consultation never held, a debate of forty minutes on a Bill of two hundred clauses: none of these is a ground on which a court may touch the resulting Act.

The boundary of the protection was tested in Raja Ram Pal v Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha, decided in 2007, where the Supreme Court held that the bar in Article 122 protects proceedings tainted by irregularity, meaning departure from the rules the House has made for itself, but does not protect action that is grossly illegal or unconstitutional in substance. The distinction is real but it does not reach the scrutiny problem. Skipping a committee is not even an irregularity, because no rule requires the step that was skipped; it is the regular exercise of a discretion the rules confer. The Act that results is judged, if it is challenged, on the ordinary grounds: legislative competence, fundamental rights, and the other limits the Constitution places on what Parliament may enact. Its content is examinable. Its path is not.

This is why the series insists that the scrutiny deficit is a political fact and not a legal wrong. Remedies for it lie where the discretion lies: in the rules the Houses make for themselves, which could require reference of defined classes of Bill, as the rules of some other legislatures do; in the practice of presiding officers; and in the calculations of governments about the cost of legislating unexamined. The one reform that requires no one's permission is already available and chronically underused, and it is the committee on subordinate legislation, which can examine the rules under any Act whether or not the Bill was ever examined. The section on that committee above records why its findings, too, wait on attention. The architecture is consistent at every level: examination exists, compulsion does not.


## The case for speed and the case for scrutiny

The decline in reference is defended, and the defence deserves to be stated at the strength its makers would claim for it. The first limb is democratic. A government is elected on a programme and is answerable for delivering it; the committee stage adds weeks or months to a Bill, and the time is not neutral, because legislation deferred is often legislation defeated by the calendar. The second limb is practical. Many Bills are technical, consolidating or urgently corrective: a Bill replacing an ordinance must be passed within six weeks of the House reassembling or the ordinance lapses, and a reference measured in months is a reference that decides the question by default. Finance and appropriation business runs on constitutional deadlines that no committee timetable can be allowed to break. The third limb is that the House is not bypassed when a committee is. Every Bill is still introduced, debated to the extent the House chooses, and voted clause by clause in form; members who believe a Bill needs examination can say so, move the reference motion, and take the vote. On this view, the referral figures record not the suppression of scrutiny but the preference of elected majorities for the floor over the committee room, and the preference of a legislature is not a scandal. Ministers resisting reference motions on the floor have put the point in terms of the mandate: the House, they argue, is the committee of the whole nation, and it is sitting.

The criticism, stated at its strongest, begins by denying that the floor and the committee room are substitutes. Debate tests rhetoric; a committee tests text. The floor cannot summon the secretary of the ministry and ask what a clause will do to a pending class of cases, cannot hear the small industry that a threshold will extinguish and the large one it will spare, and cannot sit with the legislative counsel and redraft a definition, all of which a committee does as routine. The criticism then points to the correlation the series has assembled without intending to: statutes whose central defect was found by a court years after enactment, statutes whose rules rewrote their policy, statutes passed in days and amended within the year. Each is the biography of a Bill that received no evidence stage. The juvenile justice legislation carries the sharpest version of the record, because there the committee stage happened and its advice was set aside: the standing committee that examined the Bill advised against the provision permitting children in the sixteen to eighteen group to be tried as adults for heinous offences, the government proceeded with the provision, and the [account of the juvenile age debate](/2016/06/01/juvenile-age-debate-india/) traces what the advisory record contained and what became of it. A recommendatory report can be ignored; the episode shows the price is paid later, in the working of the provision, by people who were not in the room.

PRS Legislative Research, in its published analysis of the law-making process, makes the institutional version of the criticism: that pre-legislative scrutiny, committee examination and post-enactment assessment are the three checks a sound process applies to every law, that the first is exclusively in the government's hands, the second is the legislature's but is controlled in practice by the executive that commands the majority, and the third is not institutionalised at all. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution made the connected recommendation on time, proposing that minimum working days be fixed for the two Houses, one hundred and twenty for the Lok Sabha and one hundred for the Rajya Sabha, a proposal that has been repeated by presiding officers and private members' Bills and adopted by neither House.

Where the evidence supports a verdict, the stance of this series is to give one, and here it does. The defence succeeds on its narrowest point and fails on its largest. It is true that no rule was broken in any of the fast passages the record describes, and true that urgency is sometimes real. But the referral series, sixty, seventy-one, twenty-eight, sixteen per cent across four terms, is not a series about urgent Bills. It is a series about a default being reversed. The exceptional case for speed explains a session; it cannot explain a term, still less two. And the costs the criticism identifies are not speculative: they are the defect litigation, the corrective amendments and the rewritten rules that fill the rest of this series. A system that builds twenty-four committees, staffs them, allots every ministry to one of them, and then examines one Bill in six has not abolished scrutiny. It has made scrutiny a favour. Favours are withdrawn exactly when they are most needed, which is on the contested Bill, the rushed Bill and the Bill whose text will not bear the light the committee room provides.

## The budget exception: the scrutiny that happens every year

There is one part of the legislative calendar in which committee examination is not a favour but a fixture, and it is worth isolating because it proves the rule about everything else. When the Budget is presented, the general discussion ends and the House adjourns; the demands for grants of the several ministries then stand referred to the departmentally related standing committees as a matter of course. Each committee examines the demands of its ministries over the recess, hears the secretary and the financial advisers, and reports to the House before the demands are taken up for discussion and voting. No presiding officer decides whether this examination happens. No motion is needed. The sequence, examination first and vote afterwards, is built into the financial procedure of the House, and it has operated every year since 1993 through governments of every complexion.

The contrast with Bills is not a contrast between two subjects but between two designs. For demands, the framers of the committee system treated examination as a stage, and wrote it into the calendar. For Bills, they treated examination as an option, and wrote in a power instead. The budget exception also shows what the option would look like if it were exercised universally: a fixed recess in the life of every Bill, a defined period in the committee room, evidence on the record, and a report in members' hands before the first vote on the text. Nothing about the machinery would need to change. Only the trigger would.

The budget process also supplies the vocabulary of floor scrutiny that the committee reports feed into. When demands return to the House, members move cut motions, of three recognised kinds: the policy cut, which proposes reducing a demand to one rupee and expresses disapproval of the policy underlying it; the economy cut, which proposes a specified reduction and complains of waste in the expenditure proposed; and the token cut, which proposes a nominal reduction of one hundred rupees to ventilate a specific grievance. The motions are seldom carried, since the majority that sustains the government sustains its demands, but they structure the debate, and the committee report on the ministry's demands is the document from which the best cut motion speeches are built. On the final day allotted for the demands, the presiding officer puts all outstanding demands to the vote together, whether they have been discussed or not.

### What is the guillotine in the budget process?

The guillotine is the procedure by which, on the last of the days allotted for voting on demands for grants, the Speaker puts every outstanding demand to the vote at the appointed hour, discussed or not. It guarantees that the financial business finishes on schedule, at the price of demands passing without any debate.

The events PRS recorded for the budget of 2023, in which the entire set of demands was voted without discussion, are the guillotine operating at the limit: the schedule held, the appropriation followed, and the committee reports on the demands, duly prepared and presented, informed a debate that never took place. The episode matters to this article because it separates two failures that commentary often merges. The committees had done their work. It was the consuming stage, the floor, that failed, because the floor's time is allocated politically and can be lost to disruption or compression. A complete account of scrutiny has to follow a Bill or a demand through both stages, the examination and the use made of it, and the record shows each stage failing in its own way in different years.

## What a committee stage adds: the drafting dividend

The value of examination is usually argued in democratic terms, but its most concrete yield is technical, and it can be specified. A Bill in a committee room is exposed to four kinds of test that the floor cannot administer. The first is the definitional test. Committees proceed clause by clause with the draftsman present, and a definition that will govern thousands of future disputes is asked, perhaps for the first time, what it includes and what it leaves out. The second is the threshold test. Figures in Bills, the income limit, the penalty ceiling, the size of enterprise covered, are put against worked examples supplied by witnesses who administer or suffer the regime, and a threshold that produces an absurd result at the margin is visible in a memorandum in a way it never is in a speech. The third is the machinery test: who issues the licence, which tribunal hears the appeal, what happens while the rules are unwritten, whether the commencement clause stages the Act or fires it whole. Officials answering a committee must answer these questions on the record, and the answers frequently disclose that the machinery clauses were the least considered part of the draft. The fourth is the consistency test, in which a committee compares the Bill with the statute book it amends and finds the cross-reference that fails, the repeal that overshoots, and the provision that duplicates a power already located elsewhere.

Each of these tests has a characteristic failure mode that the series has documented in enacted law. The definition left wide becomes the litigation of the following decade. The threshold untested at the margin becomes the exemption that swallows the rule or the inclusion that catches the unintended. The machinery clause unexamined becomes the authority never constituted, the tribunal never staffed, the appellate route never notified. The commencement clause unexamined becomes the Act in force in name whose operative provisions await rules for years. None of these failures requires bad faith anywhere in the system. They require only the absence of the four tests, and the four tests require a room, a witness list and time.

There is also a dividend paid to the government itself, which the case for speed systematically understates. A Bill examined is a Bill whose weaknesses the government learns about while it can still repair them by its own amendments, moved with dignity as acceptances of a committee's recommendations. A Bill unexamined carries its weaknesses into the courts and the newspapers, where repair requires either a defending judgment or an amending Act, both slower and more expensive than the committee stage would have been. Governments that treat scrutiny as a tax on their programme are, on the record this series has assembled, mispricing it. The committee stage is the cheapest point in the entire life of a statute at which a defect can be corrected, and the price rises at every subsequent stage: cheap in committee, dearer on the floor, dearer still in an amendment, and dearest of all in a decade of litigation conducted by citizens who did not draft the clause that injured them.

## The third check that barely exists: scrutiny after enactment

A complete scrutiny architecture has a stage after enactment, and the Indian system has fragments of one that have never been assembled. The committee on subordinate legislation examines the rules, which is post-enactment scrutiny of the instrument level. The departmentally related standing committees examine the annual reports of their ministries, which is post-enactment scrutiny of the administrative level, and a committee that follows a ministry year by year does accumulate the knowledge of whether a statute it once examined is working. Some statutes carry their own review devices: provisions for laying reports before Parliament, committees or authorities required to report periodically, and occasionally a clause requiring the Act itself to be reviewed. The essays of PRS Legislative Research on the law-making process identify the same gap from the other side, describing periodic assessment of a law's functioning as the third principle of a sound legislative process and recording that it is not institutionalised, being divided without ownership among the executive, the legislature and other constitutional bodies.

The consequence is that the Indian system, which under-examines Bills before enactment, also under-examines Acts after it, and the two failures are connected by more than symmetry. A legislature that knew it would revisit each major statute after a defined period, with the committee's evidence on its working before it, would face a known future reckoning for every defect it waved through, and the knowledge would discipline the earlier stages. Post-enactment review also changes the politics of the committee room: a recommendation deferred is not necessarily a recommendation lost, if the review stage exists at which the deferred question returns with evidence attached. In the absence of the third check, every scrutiny battle must be won at the Bill stage or not at all, which raises the stakes of the reference decision and helps explain why governments resist it so determinedly on the Bills that matter most to them.

## Why the default reversed: reading the referral series

The four figures, sixty, seventy-one, twenty-eight and sixteen per cent, invite an explanation, and the honest method is to test the explanations the record can bear. One explanation is arithmetic: the volume and pace of legislation grew, sessions shrank, and reference is a consumer of the scarcest resource, which is time. The sittings figures support the premise. A House meeting for fifty-five days a year, against one hundred and twenty in the early decades, has less slack, and a committee stage measured against that calendar is a larger proportional claim than it was when the House sat twice as long. But arithmetic cannot be the whole account, because the fifteenth Lok Sabha, which referred seventy-one per cent of its Bills, did not sit dramatically longer than its successors. The resource contracted gradually while the referral rate fell off a cliff between the fifteenth and sixteenth terms.

A second explanation is the structure of the majority. The high-referral terms were terms of coalition government, in which the treasury benches contained parties with their own positions to negotiate, and the committee room served as a place where the government's own allies could be satisfied without public confrontation. A single-party majority has less internal use for the committee room and more capacity to carry the floor, and the referral rate fell as single-party majorities returned. The explanation is structural rather than partisan, and it predicts, correctly, that the identity of the party in office matters less to the referral rate than the shape of its majority. A third explanation is the ordinance route and the session calendar working together: a government that legislates between sessions and presents the House with replacement Bills under a six-week clock has built itself a standing argument against reference, since the clock is real and the lapse of an ordinance is a genuine event. The argument is available even where the urgency that produced the ordinance was itself a choice.

A fourth explanation concerns presiding officers, whose practice has varied and whose reference power is personal to the office. Two presiding officers applying the same rules to similar Bills can produce different referral rates across a term, and the record of particular terms bears the marks of particular occupants of the Chair. This explanation is the most uncomfortable for an institutional account, because it locates the fate of a designed system in individual disposition, but it follows necessarily from the design: where a power is discretionary and unreviewable, its exercise will vary with its holder, and the variation is not an aberration from the system but the system operating as built. The four explanations are not rivals so much as layers, and they converge on the article's thesis. The referral rate is set by the interaction of time, majority structure, legislative method and personal practice, which is to say by politics in the broadest sense, and by nothing else. There is no floor beneath it in law. The sixteen per cent of the seventeenth Lok Sabha was lawful in every particular, and a lower figure would have been lawful too.

## The working conditions of scrutiny

Committees do their work with resources that deserve a paragraph of their own, because the quality of examination is not independent of them. A committee is served by officers of the secretariat of its House, who prepare background notes, maintain the file of earlier reports on the ministry, arrange the evidence and draft the report for the committee's adoption. The secretariat's continuity is the system's memory: members rotate annually, chairpersons change, and the officers remain, which is why a committee's reports across years can read as a continuing audit of a department. Members themselves are parliamentarians with constituencies, floor duties and other committees, and committee work is fitted around all three; sittings are held both during sessions and between them, and the examination of a ministry's demands or a contested Bill is superimposed on the ordinary political week.

Committees may take the assistance of experts and may call for papers and records from ministries, which must be supplied subject to the recognised exceptions for documents whose disclosure the government claims would be injurious to the safety or interest of the state. Evidence is ordinarily taken on the record, and the verbatim proceedings of a committee are confidential to it until the report is presented, at which point the report and the minutes of dissent become public documents. The evidence itself is published with the report in the ordinary practice of Bill committees, so that the memoranda of affected parties and the answers of officials enter the permanent record on which later courts, administrators and historians draw. A great deal of what is known about how a statute was expected to work, when a court later asks the question, is known because a committee once asked it first.

The constraint that binds hardest is not staff or access but the calendar intersection: committees can only examine what is sent to them, within the time the parliamentary timetable allows, with members whose attendance the whips of the floor also claim. The nine-meeting average for Bill reports in the seventeenth Lok Sabha, recorded by PRS, is the footprint of that constraint: nine sittings is enough for a ministry briefing, a round of outside evidence and clause by clause consideration of a Bill of ordinary size, and it is not enough for a Bill that rebuilds a field. The data protection reference, with seventy-eight meetings, shows what the same instrument does when the timetable yields. Between those two figures lies the practical range of Indian Bill scrutiny, and where in the range a given Bill falls is decided, once again, by the reference and the time granted with it.

## Reading a committee report

For the journalist, the researcher and the aspirant, the report is the usable product, and it repays a knowledge of its anatomy. A committee report on a Bill ordinarily opens with the record of the reference and the committee's proceedings, states the salient features of the Bill as the ministry presented them, summarises the evidence, and then takes the Bill's provisions in order, recording the committee's observations and recommendations clause by clause or theme by theme. The recommended amendments are set out in a form the government can lift into its official amendments. Minutes of dissent follow, signed by the members who append them. The ministry's replies, when they come, form the action taken document. A reader who wants the strongest criticism of a Bill that exists anywhere in the official record should read the dissent minutes first; a reader who wants to know what will actually change should read the recommendations the ministry's evidence sessions signalled it would accept.

Reports are published by the two secretariats and laid on the table of the House, and the committee lists and bulletins of each House record which Bills stand referred and to which committee. A reader who downloads a report to work through its clause by clause recommendations can [open the committee document in the ReportMedic office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) in the browser, which suits the way these reports are in practice read, in sections and against the text of the Bill they examine. That public trail is what makes the referral record in this article possible: reference is one of the few legislative decisions that is both discretionary and fully documented, announced when taken and conspicuous when withheld on a Bill of public moment. The aspirant should hold the distinctions the reports embody, because they are the distinctions the examinations test: standing against ad hoc, election against nomination, report against action taken, recommendation against amendment, and throughout, the line between the House's power to examine everything and its practice of examining what it is sent.

## Reference before 1993: the select committee habit

The standing committee system is younger than the Republic, and understanding what it replaced explains what it was for. In the first decades after independence, the examination of major Bills was episodic and ran through select committees constituted Bill by Bill. Major codifying measures and major reforms were sent to select committees as a matter of legislative habit, and the reports of those committees, the evidence they took and the changes they made are part of the drafting history of the statute book of that era. The habit had costs the designers of 1993 set out to remove. A select committee had to be invented afresh for each Bill, its membership negotiated, its expertise assembled from nothing, and its work died with its report, leaving no institution that followed a ministry from year to year. A Bill that nobody chose to refer received nothing at all, and there was no coverage of the departments between Bills: no annual examination of demands, no continuing audit of a ministry's working, no body that knew a field before the Bill arrived.

The 1993 design answered each defect in turn. Coverage became universal, because every ministry was allotted to a committee. Expertise became cumulative, because the same committee examined a ministry's demands, its annual report, its policy documents and its Bills in succession. The trigger for the non-Bill functions became the calendar rather than a decision. What the designers did not change was the trigger for Bills. Reference of a Bill remained a choice, as it had been when the choice was exercised through select committee motions, and the new system's great improvement, that a committee existed for every subject, coexisted with the old system's great weakness, that the committee could be left idle. The referral series of the fourteenth to seventeenth Lok Sabhas is the record of the new machinery being used first at something close to its intended rate and then at a fraction of it. The machinery did not deteriorate. Its use was discontinued in large part, which is a different diagnosis and points to a different remedy.

## Scrutiny of constitutional amendment Bills

Constitution (amendment) Bills occupy a particular place in the scrutiny record, because their subject is the Constitution itself and because they are not subject to the joint sitting procedure, each House having to pass them by special majority. The record of their examination is mixed in an instructive way. PRS Legislative Research has noted that the major constitutional amendments of the period it surveyed, those concerning the National Commission for Backward Classes, the Goods and Services Tax, the exchange of enclaves with Bangladesh and the National Judicial Appointments Commission, were all examined by parliamentary committees. The Goods and Services Tax amendment was examined by a select committee of the Rajya Sabha whose report shaped the Bill's passage through that House. Against those instances stands the amendment for reservation for economically weaker sections, introduced, debated and passed by both Houses within days, with the motion for reference to a select committee moved and lost, and no committee stage of any kind. The two patterns coexisted within the same decade and under the same rules.

The constitutional point is that amendment Bills stand in greater, not lesser, need of the committee stage, for two reasons the series has had repeated occasion to state. The first is entrenchment: an ordinary statute defectively drafted can be amended by ordinary majority, while a defective amendment may require the full special majority and, for some subjects, ratification by half the states to repair. The second is the basic structure doctrine, which makes the validity of an amendment depend on judgments about the Constitution's identity that litigation resolves slowly and at the highest level; a committee stage at which constitutional counsel and the state governments are heard is the only examination an amendment receives before it enters that territory. None of this has hardened into practice. Amendment Bills are referred when the politics of the moment rewards reference, and the reward calculation is the same one that governs ordinary Bills.

## The committee record in court: Kalpana Mehta

One judicial anchor belongs in this article beyond Article 122, because it fixes what a committee report is worth once it exists. In Kalpana Mehta v Union of India, decided in 2018, a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court considered whether a report of a parliamentary standing committee could be relied upon in court proceedings. The Court held that it could: a committee report is a public document of which judicial notice may be taken, it may be used as evidence of the facts it records and as an external aid in understanding the context of legislation, and reliance on it does not amount to questioning the validity of proceedings in Parliament. The Court equally held the limits. The report cannot be challenged in court, the members and officials who made it cannot be summoned or questioned about it, and the privilege that protects parliamentary proceedings protects the making of the report itself; it is the use of the finished document, not an inquiry into its production, that the judgment permits.

The holding gives the committee stage a legal afterlife that its recommendatory status on the floor might obscure. A report that the government ignores is not waste paper. It enters the material that courts may consult when the statute is later construed or challenged, and the evidence recorded in it, the ministry's own explanations of what a provision was meant to do, the defects noticed and the assurances given, can be placed before a court by any litigant. Litigants have used committee material in precisely this way, and the standing committee record on a Bill has been read in constitutional litigation as part of the statute's context. The asymmetry is worth stating plainly. Parliament may decline to examine a Bill at all, and no court can object. But if Parliament does examine it, the examination acquires a permanence and a legal usability that outlast the majority that commissioned it. The committee room is the one place in the legislative process whose work product is guaranteed readers, eventually, in the forum where the statute's meaning is finally settled.

## The states: the same architecture with less machinery

The committee question has a state dimension that the figures in this article, drawn from Parliament, should not be allowed to hide. State legislatures work under the corresponding provisions of the Constitution, with rules of their own, and the research record assembled by PRS Legislative Research on state assemblies shows a thinner version of every pattern described above. PRS has recorded that members of state legislative assemblies sat, on average, for about twenty-eight days a year across the period its study covered, against the Lok Sabha's fifty-five in its sparsest term. Committee systems in the states vary widely: some assemblies maintain subject committees of long standing, in others the committee structure is skeletal, and reference of Bills to committees is in many states the exception rather than the practice. The bar on judicial inquiry applies in the states through Article 212 exactly as Article 122 applies at the Centre, so the discipline in a state assembly is political in precisely the same way.

The consequence for the statute book is cumulative. A very large share of the legislation that governs daily life, on subjects in the State and Concurrent Lists, is state legislation, and the scrutiny it receives is the scrutiny of assemblies that sit less, staff less and refer less than Parliament does. The series' state variation articles record statutes whose operative content was settled in rules and litigation because no examination stage intervened. The architecture at the state level is the architecture of this article with every weakness deepened, and the citizen who learns from the parliamentary record how to ask whether a Bill was examined should ask the same question of the assembly that governs his municipality, his land record and his local taxes, in the expectation of a thinner answer.

## Prorogation, dissolution and the life of a reference

Two procedural events test the committee system each term, and their treatment completes the institutional picture. Prorogation, the end of a session, does not disturb committee work: committees are not creatures of the session, their sittings continue between sessions, and a reference made in one session is worked on through the interval and reported in the next. This continuity across prorogation is one of the quiet strengths of the design, since it gives the committee stage a calendar of its own when the floor has none. Dissolution of the Lok Sabha is the harder event. Bills pending in the Lok Sabha lapse on dissolution, and a reference of such a Bill ends with it; the committee's unfinished work on the Bill has no object left to attach to. Bills that originated in the Rajya Sabha and stand referred to its select committees are in the position of that House's business generally: the Rajya Sabha is a continuing body, is not dissolved, and its committees are reconstituted on their own cycle. The annual reconstitution of the standing committees absorbs both events without drama. A committee reconstituted after a dissolution takes up its ministry's demands and reports afresh, and if the lapsed Bill is introduced again in the new House, the reference decision is taken again, by the new presiding officer, on the new politics. The record of scrutiny thus resets with each Lok Sabha in a way the record of the executive does not, which is one more reason the referral rate is a property of a term and must always be quoted with its term attached.

## Three passages that mark the range

Abstract argument about scrutiny gains its force from instances, and three passages mark the practical range of the system better than any average. At one pole stands the Goods and Services Tax amendment. A constitutional amendment redesigning the fiscal federation was referred to a select committee of the Rajya Sabha, which took evidence, reported, and saw a number of its concerns accommodated in the Bill's final shape and in the assurances that accompanied its passage. The episode is remembered in the series' taxation cluster as a case where the committee stage did the work the textbooks assign it: it gave the states, the traders and the tax administrations a structured hearing inside the process, and it gave the government a negotiated text whose passage it could sustain. Nothing about the episode was compelled. The reference was a political decision, taken because the arithmetic of the Rajya Sabha and the federal character of the subject made unexamined passage the more dangerous course, which is the article's thesis operating in the reader's favour for once.

At the other pole stand the farm laws of 2020. Three Bills restructuring agricultural marketing were passed without reference to a standing or select committee, in a session compressed by a pandemic, over demands for a division and for reference that the majority resisted. The laws met sustained protest outside Parliament, the Supreme Court stayed their implementation, and within little more than a year Parliament repealed them. The episode is treated in its own cluster of this series, and it is cited here for one feature only. Every argument the committee stage exists to test, the effect of the new marketing regime on the smallest sellers, the operation of the dispute provisions, the boundary between the statute and the states' field, was an argument that had to be conducted somewhere. It was conducted on the road and in the court instead of in the committee room, at a cost in time, in order and in the statute book's credibility that dwarfed the weeks a committee would have taken. The repeal is the extreme entry in the ledger of the drafting dividend computed the other way: the price of the examination that was saved.

Between the poles stands the personal data protection reference, the longest committee examination of a Bill in the period the record covers. A joint committee of both Houses heard evidence across seventy-eight meetings over more than two years, produced a report with a substantially reworked draft, and appended dissent notes that mapped the disagreements the majority had not resolved. The government then withdrew the Bill rather than proceed on the committee's text, and the subject returned later in a new draft. The episode resists both morals. It is not a story of scrutiny triumphant, since the examined text was never enacted. It is not a story of scrutiny futile, since the committee's hearings built the public and expert record on which the successor legislation was drafted and debated. It is a story of what the committee stage reliably produces even when it does not produce amendments: an evidence base, a clause by clause map of the disagreements, and a set of questions that no subsequent draft can pretend were never asked. A legislature deciding whether to refer a Bill is buying that record whether or not it buys the recommendations, and the record, as the Kalpana Mehta section above explains, has a legal life of its own.

## The attention problem

A scrutiny system can fail at the last inch even when every earlier stage has worked, if nobody reads the report. Committee reports are laid on the table, printed and placed on the websites of the two Houses, and there the publicity ordinarily ends. Reports are seldom debated as reports. The press covers committee work episodically, most reliably when a dissent note gives it a conflict to report, and the considered middle of a report, the clause on which the committee persuaded the ministry to think again, is not news in the form in which news is made. Ministries know this. The action taken statement is drafted for a reader who is assumed to be the committee itself and no one else, and replies that would not survive a public reading survive comfortably in the obscurity of the printed report.

The point is structural rather than cynical. Scrutiny is an information system, and information that is produced but not consumed disciplines no one. The chain has four links: the committee must examine, the report must state, some audience must notice, and the noticer must be able to impose a cost, in the House, in the press or at the election. The Indian system's first two links are professionally built and its last two are vestigial. That is why the same report can be, in the same week, an excellent document and a dead letter. It is also why the reforms most often proposed in the literature of the subject are not only about reference: they include televised evidence, published action taken tracking, and a fixed parliamentary day for committee reports, each aimed at the consumption end of the chain. The committee on subordinate legislation is the limiting case of the attention problem, a body whose entire output is technical, whose reports are the driest documents Parliament produces, and whose subject, the rules in which statutes actually live, is the one this series has found most consequential and least examined by anyone else.

## The whip and the room

One working condition deserves separate statement because it explains committee behaviour that puzzles outside observers. The party whip, which binds members' votes on the floor on pain of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, does not in terms govern deliberation inside a committee, and committee recommendations are not whipped votes. Members are therefore freer in the room than on the floor, and the freedom is used: the cross-party character of most committee reports, adopted without a division, is a standing feature of the system that the floor's arithmetic would not predict. Chairpersons, drawn from both sides of the House across the twenty-four standing committees, have their own institutional interest in a report that the House respects, and a chairperson's reputation is built on the report and not on the debate.

The freedom has limits that honesty requires stating. A committee's majority is drawn from the same parties as the House's majority, and on a question the government treats as central, the committee's recommendation will predictably align with the government's position, by persuasion, by selection of the reference, or by the simple composition of the room. Dissent notes exist precisely for the cases where alignment fails on the merits as the minority sees them. The system should therefore be valued for what it is: not an independent check that can override the majority, which no internal organ of a House can be, but a procedure that forces the majority to encounter the evidence, answer the drafting objections on the record, and own the defects it decides to keep. Encountering evidence is not nothing. A government that has heard, in a closed room, exactly what a clause will do, and proceeds, has made a different and more answerable decision than a government that never had to hear it, and the difference is preserved in a document that courts and citizens may later read.

## The laying requirement: Parliament's paper grip on rules

The committee on subordinate legislation works downstream of a mechanism that deserves its own explanation, because it is the only point at which the whole House, and not merely a committee, is formally connected to delegated legislation. Modern Indian Acts routinely carry a laying clause, in a standard form whose substance is this: every rule made under the Act shall be laid before each House of Parliament for a total period of thirty days comprised in one or more sessions, and if both Houses agree in making any modification in the rule, or agree that the rule should not be made, the rule shall thereafter have effect only in the modified form or be of no effect, without prejudice to the validity of anything previously done under it. The clause is a standing invitation to Parliament to intervene in the instrument after it is made, within a defined window, by agreement of both Houses.

The invitation is almost never accepted. Motions to modify or annul a rule are rarities in the business of either House, governments do not schedule time for them as a matter of course, and the thirty days run out in the press of other business. The laying clause therefore operates in practice as a notification device: it guarantees that the instrument is placed on the table and printed, that the committee on subordinate legislation can take it up, and that a member who reads the bulletin can discover that the rule exists. Notification is not nothing. Much of the series' evidence about delegated legislation turns on instruments that were obscure, delayed or unknown to the people they governed, and the laying discipline, with the committee's examination of delays in laying, is the system's answer to obscurity even where it is not an answer to excess. But the distance between the clause's design and its use is the scrutiny story in miniature. A power of annulment held by both Houses, exercisable by motion, with a thirty-day window, is a real power. It sleeps because using it costs the scarcest currency in the building, which is floor time controlled by the government the rule belongs to.

There is a further refinement the aspirant should hold, because examinations test it and the series' delegated legislation articles assume it. Not all laying clauses are equal. Some parent Acts require the instrument to be laid in draft and to wait for approval before it operates, the affirmative pattern; the standard clause described above is the negative pattern, under which the instrument operates from its making and the Houses may intervene within the window; and some older or special provisions require laying without any modification power at all, pure formality. Which pattern a statute carries is a drafting choice made in the parent Act, which returns the question to the committee stage of that Act: a committee examining a Bill that delegates wide power can press for the affirmative pattern for the widest delegations, and the absence of any such pressure, in Bill after Bill, is one of the quiet ways the scrutiny deficit writes itself into future law.

## Assembling the series' evidence

This article is the mechanism chapter of a series whose other chapters are the evidence, and it is worth assembling, in closing the analysis, the recurring shapes that evidence takes, because each shape is a prediction about what unexamined Bills produce. The first shape is the statute whose commencement waited on rules, so that Parliament's text existed and the operative regime did not, sometimes for years: the gap was not debated because the Bill's machinery clauses were never tested against the question a committee asks first, which is what happens the day after assent. The second shape is the statute whose rules travelled beyond its sections, enlarging an exemption, narrowing a right, or supplying a threshold the parent Act had deliberately or accidentally left in the air: the defect was born in an instrument no committee stage of the parent Bill had fenced, because fencing delegated power is committee work. The third shape is the statute whose central term was litigated to the Supreme Court, where the record of what the legislature had been told about the term, by witnesses who foresaw the dispute, either existed in a committee report or existed nowhere. The fourth shape is the statute amended within a short period to repair what enactment had broken, the repair Bill travelling, often as not, by the same unexamined route as the original.

Set against those shapes, the scrutiny record of this article is not an institutional curiosity. It is the upstream variable of the series' entire downstream evidence. When the referral rate stood at seventy-one per cent, the system was testing most Bills against the four shapes before they could form. At sixteen per cent, it tests one Bill in six and leaves the rest to be tested by administrators, litigants and courts, in that order and at rising cost. The committee system itself, its coverage, composition, functions and working methods, remains what it was in the high-referral years, available in full. That is the finding the article leaves with the reader, because it converts an argument about procedure into a question any citizen can ask about any Bill, and answer from the public record in a minute. Was it sent to a committee. If it was not, everything else about the law that follows, its defects and their discovery, was decided in advance by the people who chose not to send it.

## The Rajya Sabha's share of scrutiny

The committee system belongs to both Houses, and the Rajya Sabha's part in it has features the Lok Sabha's does not. Eight of the twenty-four departmentally related standing committees are serviced by the Rajya Sabha secretariat, with chairpersons appointed by the Chairman, and a Bill in that House may be referred to the committee covering its ministry just as in the other. The Rajya Sabha maintains its own committee on subordinate legislation and its own committee on government assurances, so the post-enactment and floor-assurance scrutiny described above runs in parallel in the two Houses. A Bill passed by the Lok Sabha and transmitted to the Rajya Sabha may there be referred to a select committee of that House, and the device has been used for Bills on which the revising chamber wanted a record of its own: PRS Legislative Research records, for example, that in the seventeenth Lok Sabha's term one Bill, the Surrogacy (Regulation) Bill, was referred to a Rajya Sabha select committee, while four Bills went to joint committees.

The Rajya Sabha's committee role interacts with its constitutional position in a way worth one paragraph. The Council of States cannot amend a money Bill and has only fourteen days to return it with recommendations; its legislative leverage over ordinary Bills is real but ultimately subject to the joint sitting arithmetic. Committee examination is the arena in which the Rajya Sabha's influence is least arithmetical, because evidence taken and defects documented in its committees enter the record that the Lok Sabha and the government must answer, and a select committee report of the Rajya Sabha on a Bill transmitted from the other House is a statement by the revising chamber in its most considered form. Governments with large Lok Sabha majorities and weaker Rajya Sabha positions have therefore had a particular reason to be economical with reference in the upper House as well, and the committee statistics of PRS, which are compiled for Parliament as a whole, reflect the practice of both Houses.

## What reform would take

The remedies proposed for the scrutiny deficit are unusually cheap as constitutional engineering goes, because the whole system lives in rules that each House controls. A House could amend its rules to require that every Bill, or every Bill of defined classes, stand referred to the appropriate standing committee unless the House resolves otherwise on a stated ground of urgency. It could fix the minimum period a reference carries, protect the evidence stage for Bills affecting fundamental rights or creating offences, and schedule the action taken statements for notice. None of this requires a constitutional amendment, a statute, or the other House's concurrence for its own proceedings. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution addressed the adjacent question of sitting days with its recommendation of fixed minima, and conferences of presiding officers have repeatedly discussed the committee system's strengthening. The instruments of reform are all internal, which is the final proof of the article's thesis: the obstacle to mandatory scrutiny is not legal capacity but political preference, and preferences change when the cost of the present arrangement becomes visible enough. The purpose of articles like this one, and of the referral statistics compiled term by term by the research organisations, is to keep that cost visible in the only currency the system respects, which is an attributed public record.

It should be recorded, in fairness to the design, that mandatory reference is not costless even as an idea, and its advocates within the system acknowledge the trade. A universal reference rule converts the committee stage into a toll every Bill must pay, including the consolidating, the corrective and the genuinely urgent; drafting the urgency exception so that it does not swallow the rule is the same problem, one level up, as the exception in the consultation policy. The committee system's own capacity is also finite: twenty-four committees examining every Bill, in addition to demands, annual reports and policy documents, would need more sittings, more staff and longer terms than the one-year cycle provides. A serious reform therefore pairs the trigger with the capacity, longer committee terms, research support attached to committees rather than to members individually, and evidence taken on the record as the default. The debate about scrutiny, conducted at that level of specificity, is the debate the subject deserves and the one the referral statistics are meant to force.

## The citizen's door in working detail

Because the committee evidence stage is one of the two points at which the public can address a Bill's text directly, its mechanics deserve a practical account, and the mechanics explain both its value and its skew. When a Bill of wide public effect is referred, the committee ordinarily authorises a press communique inviting memoranda from interested persons and bodies within a stated period. A memorandum is a written submission addressed to the committee through the House secretariat: it identifies the clauses it addresses, states the objection or the support, and proposes the amendment sought, and the committees' practice is to treat a precise, clause-anchored memorandum as the currency of the realm. Persons and bodies may be invited, on the strength of their memoranda or their known expertise, to tender oral evidence, at which members question them and the answers are recorded. Stakeholder ministries other than the sponsoring ministry, regulators within the field and state governments may likewise be heard or may file their views.

The skew is inherent in the instrument. Organised interests, industry associations, professional bodies and established civil society organisations monitor references, maintain the capacity to draft a memorandum in a fortnight, and appear when called. The unorganised, the small trader inside a threshold, the beneficiary of a scheme being restructured, the district official who will administer the clause, rarely know that a reference has occurred until the report is printed. Committees mitigate the skew when they actively seek out the unorganised, through study visits, through inviting administration-level witnesses and through taking the evidence of bodies that represent diffuse interests, and the best committee records in the series show that mitigation in operation. The point for the reader is directional: the evidence stage is open in design and uneven in use, and a citizen or an association that learns to watch the committee lists of the two Houses, and to write to a reference when one is announced, is using the one door in the legislative process that is unlocked from the citizen's side. The other door, the consultation on the draft, stands in the ministry's porch. This one stands in Parliament's.

Committees also choose work of their own. Each departmentally related standing committee selects subjects for examination in its field during the year, takes evidence on them and reports, building the subject knowledge that its Bill work draws on; the financial committees likewise select the audit paragraphs and undertakings they will pursue. This suo motu layer of the system's work is the least reported and among the most durable, because it is how a committee becomes, over successive annual reconstitutions served by the same secretariat, the closest thing the legislature has to a standing inspectorate of a department. It is also the layer most dependent on the committee's own energy, since no one refers these subjects to it and no calendar compels them. Even the self-starting part of the scrutiny machine, in other words, runs on choice, and the quality of a term's committee work is set as much by twenty-four chairpersons' ambitions as by any rule.

## The verdict on the architecture

The committee system is the most complete scrutiny machinery the Indian Parliament has ever possessed, and its weakness is located at exactly one joint: nothing obliges anyone to use it. The constitutional basis is adequate, because rules made under Article 118 can create and have created every institution described in this article. The composition rules are sound, mixing election and nomination, excluding ministers, renewing membership annually and guaranteeing the opposition its share. The functions are correctly drawn, reaching grants, Bills, annual reports and policy documents, and stopping short of day to day administration. What the design omits is a trigger that fires without permission.

Until a trigger exists, the quality of scrutiny a Bill receives will remain what the brief for this article calls it: a function of political choice at every stage. The reader who wants to know what scrutiny a particular Bill is likely to get should therefore ask political questions, not legal ones. Does the government want the delay or the endorsement. Does the presiding officer use the reference power freely. Is the Bill one on which evidence would help the government or hurt it. Is there an ordinance clock running. The answers predict the scrutiny better than any rule book, because the rule book, on examination, contains no rule that answers the question. This article is general information about the legislative process and is no substitute for the record of the House itself: a reader following a live Bill should verify from the bulletins and committee lists of the two Houses which stage that Bill has reached and which committee, if any, holds it, since reference is a decision that can be taken or withheld at any point after introduction.

The series closes its account of the legislative process where it began. A statute is not its text alone but the whole record around it, and the committee stage is where the most useful part of that record is made: the evidence, the dissent, the defect found in time. The record exists to be made. Whether it is made is decided, Bill by Bill, by the people the record would examine.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which parliamentary committees examine legislation?

Bills are examined chiefly by the twenty-four departmentally related standing committees, which cover every ministry between them and examine the Bills pertaining to their ministries when the Speaker or the Chairman refers those Bills. A Bill may instead be sent by the House to a select committee of that House, or to a joint committee of both Houses, constituted for that Bill alone. The financial committees, the Public Accounts Committee, the Estimates Committee and the Committee on Public Undertakings, examine expenditure, estimates and undertakings rather than Bills, and the committee on subordinate legislation of each House examines the rules made under Acts after enactment.

### Q: Is referring a Bill to a committee compulsory?

No. No provision of the Constitution, no statute and no rule of either House requires a Bill to be referred to a committee. The Speaker or the Chairman may refer a Bill to a departmentally related standing committee after its introduction, and the House may vote to send it to a select or joint committee, but every route is discretionary. A presiding officer who declines to refer, a House that defeats a reference motion and a government that passes its Bill without a committee stage all act within the rules. Referral is a political decision, taken Bill by Bill, and not a stage that the legislative process guarantees.

### Q: How many Bills are actually referred to committees?

The proportion is measured by PRS Legislative Research across the whole term of each Lok Sabha, and it has fallen sharply. Sixty per cent of Bills were referred to committees in the fourteenth Lok Sabha, seventy-one per cent in the fifteenth, twenty-eight per cent in the sixteenth and sixteen per cent in the seventeenth. The figures are one organisation's consistent series and should always be quoted with the term to which they belong, because percentages published for a single session or on a different base will differ. The direction across four terms is not in dispute: examination changed from the default treatment of major legislation into the exception.

### Q: What happens to a committee's recommendations?

A committee's report is recommendatory. When a Bill returns to the House, the government moves the amendments it has accepted, members may move others, and the House decides by vote; recommendations the government rejects have no further force. For reports on demands, annual reports and subjects, the ministry files a statement of the action taken, replying recommendation by recommendation, and the committee may present an action taken report commenting on the replies. The government's obligation throughout is to answer, not to comply. A recommendation therefore succeeds by persuasion and publicity: by the evidence behind it, the embarrassment of rejecting it, and the use members make of it in debate.

### Q: What is the difference between a select committee and a joint committee?

Both are ad hoc committees created to examine one Bill and dissolved when they report. A select committee is a committee of one House, composed of members of the House in which the Bill is pending, constituted by a motion of that House. A joint committee is constituted by both Houses acting together, on motions adopted in each, and its members are drawn from both Houses in the proportion the motions specify. A joint committee is ordinarily chosen for Bills of exceptional width or controversy, where the examination is to carry the authority of Parliament as a whole. Either kind takes evidence, considers the Bill clause by clause and reports recommended amendments to the House or Houses that created it.

### Q: Who scrutinises rules made under a statute?

The committee on subordinate legislation of each House. The committee, first constituted in 1953 with fifteen members in each House, scrutinises the rules, regulations and other instruments laid before Parliament and reports whether the delegated power has been properly exercised within the scope of the parent Act. It examines whether an instrument travels beyond the Act, imposes a tax or a charge without authority, operates retrospectively without sanction, bars the courts improperly, or was laid late. It is the only body that examines delegated legislation systematically. Its reports are recommendatory, and it cannot annul an instrument; annulment or modification requires action by the Houses themselves under the parent Act's laying clause.

### Q: What does pre-legislative consultation require?

The Pre-legislative Consultation Policy adopted by the Government of India in 2014 directs every ministry to place draft legislation in the public domain for at least thirty days before the proposal goes to the Cabinet. The draft is to be accompanied by a justification for the proposal, its broad financial implications and an assessment of its impact on the environment, on fundamental rights and on the lives and livelihoods of those affected, and where a particular group is affected the publicity must reach that group. Comments received are to be summarised, and the summary itself placed in the public domain. The policy is an executive instruction, not a law: a ministry may depart from it for recorded reasons, and no citizen can enforce it in court.

### Q: Why does committee scrutiny matter?

Because it is the only stage of lawmaking at which a Bill's text is tested against evidence before it hardens into law. A committee summons the officials who drafted the Bill, hears the people and industries the clauses will govern, examines definitions and thresholds against worked examples, and checks the machinery clauses that decide whether the Act will function. Defects found in the committee room are repaired by amendment at negligible cost; the same defects found later are repaired by litigation, corrective legislation or administrative failure, at costs borne by citizens who had no part in drafting. Scrutiny also creates the public record of what a provision was meant to do, which courts may later consult when the statute is construed.

### Q: Who decides whether a Bill goes to a standing committee?

The presiding officer of the House in which the Bill stands: the Speaker in the Lok Sabha and the Chairman in the Rajya Sabha, acting under the rules that empower them to refer a Bill to the departmentally related standing committee covering its ministry. The minister in charge may indicate the government's preference, and members may press for reference, but the decision is the presiding officer's. The alternative route belongs to the House itself, which may adopt a motion to send the Bill to a select or joint committee. In practice the two routes are governed by the same calculation, the willingness of those who control the timetable to spend time on examination.

### Q: What is the difference between a standing committee and an ad hoc committee?

A standing committee is a permanent institution of the House, constituted under the rules for a continuing field of work and reconstituted with fresh membership every year. The financial committees and the twenty-four departmentally related standing committees are standing committees: they exist whether or not any particular business is referred to them, and they carry continuing functions such as examining demands for grants and annual reports. An ad hoc committee is created for one defined task, such as the examination of a single Bill or an inquiry into a single subject, and it ceases to exist when it submits its report. Select committees and joint committees on Bills are ad hoc committees.

### Q: Can a committee change the text of a Bill itself?

No. A committee examining a Bill considers it clause by clause and recommends amendments, but the recommendation changes nothing by itself. When the Bill returns to the House, the government ordinarily moves the amendments it has accepted as official amendments, and members may move others; the House adopts or rejects each by vote. The committee's influence is therefore persuasive, resting on the evidence it has recorded and the quality of its drafting. In practice, recommendations signalled as acceptable during the evidence stage, when officials indicate what the ministry can live with, are frequently adopted, while recommendations the government resists succeed only if the House insists against the treasury benches.

### Q: How does the Business Advisory Committee affect the time a Bill gets on the floor?

The Business Advisory Committee of each House recommends the allocation of time for the business the House is to transact, including the hours allotted to the discussion of individual Bills, and the House ordinarily adopts its recommendations. The committee is chaired by the presiding officer and includes members drawn from across parties, but government business dominates the agenda it arranges. Its decisions are the other half of the scrutiny story: a Bill may receive a committee stage measured in months and a floor stage measured in minutes, or the reverse. PRS Legislative Research recorded that thirty-five per cent of Bills in the seventeenth Lok Sabha were passed with less than one hour of discussion, which is what an allotted hour looks like at scale.

### Q: What does the Committee on Government Assurances do with a minister's promise?

It records it and pursues it. The committee, first constituted in 1953 and consisting of fifteen members in the Lok Sabha, extracts from the debates and question answers every assurance, promise or undertaking given by a minister on the floor, and requires the ministry to report its implementation, ordinarily within three months. Ministries may seek extensions or ask that an assurance be dropped, and the committee decides whether the request is justified. Its reports to the House list assurances implemented, assurances dropped with its leave and assurances long pending. The committee matters most where a Bill was passed on the strength of promises made in debate in place of amendments, because it is the only body that checks whether the words were made good.

### Q: Why can a minister not be a member of a departmentally related standing committee?

Because the committee's function is to examine the executive, and a minister is the executive. The rules make a minister ineligible for nomination to a departmentally related standing committee, and a member who is appointed to the Council of Ministers after nomination ceases to be a member of the committee. The same principle excludes ministers from the financial committees. The design ensures that the examination of a department's demands, reports and Bills is conducted by members who hold no office in that department and answer for none of its decisions. A minister appears before the committee in the other capacity, as a witness's political head and as the authority that will answer the report, not as one of the examiners.

### Q: How do the financial committees differ from the departmentally related standing committees?

The financial committees, the Public Accounts Committee, the Estimates Committee and the Committee on Public Undertakings, are organised around money rather than ministries: they examine expenditure already incurred against the audit reports, estimates before they are spent, and the accounts of public undertakings. Their members are elected by the Houses by proportional representation, and their business arises every year from documents the system generates automatically. The departmentally related standing committees are organised around ministries, their members are nominated by the presiding officers, and they examine demands for grants, annual reports and policy documents as well as Bills. The decisive difference for legislation is that the financial committees' work is triggered by the calendar, while a standing committee sees a Bill only when it is referred.

### Q: Can members of the public give evidence to a committee examining a Bill?

Yes, and the committee stage is one of the two points in lawmaking at which the public is invited in. When a Bill of wide effect is referred, the committee ordinarily issues a press communique inviting written memoranda from individuals, associations and experts within a stated period. Memoranda that address the clauses precisely and propose specific amendments carry the most weight. The committee may then invite selected persons and bodies to give oral evidence, which is recorded and published with the report. The door is open but unevenly used, because organised interests monitor references and can respond within the period, while citizens affected by a clause may learn of the reference only when the report appears.

### Q: Does a committee examine a Bill clause by clause?

Yes. After the general discussion of the Bill's principles and the taking of evidence, the committee proceeds through the text clause by clause, considering the drafting of each provision, the definitions it relies on, the thresholds it sets and the machinery it creates, with the ministry's officials and legislative counsel present to answer. Amendments are proposed and tested within the committee, and those the committee adopts are recommended in its report. Clause by clause consideration is the feature that distinguishes committee examination from floor debate, where clauses are frequently put to the vote together under the time allotted, and it is where errors of cross-reference, inconsistencies with the parent law and unworkable procedures are most often caught.

### Q: Is a committee's report debated in the House?

Ordinarily, no. A committee report is presented to the House and printed, but it is not itself put to debate or vote as a document. Its life in the House is indirect: the recommendations enter the consideration of the Bill through the amendments the government accepts and moves, and members draw on the report, and on the minutes of dissent appended to it, in their speeches on the Bill. Reports on demands for grants inform the debate on those demands in the same way. The absence of a debate on the report itself is one reason committee work receives little public attention: the House consumes the product inside other business and never discusses the document as such.

### Q: What is a minute of dissent in a committee report?

It is the written statement of a member or members of the committee who disagree with the report's conclusions, appended to the report and signed by them. A minute of dissent sets out the points of disagreement, the evidence relied on and the amendments or findings the dissenting members would have preferred. Dissent minutes are a regular feature of reports on contested Bills and are not a sign of failure: they preserve the minority's objections in a considered form, create the record on which the House and later readers can judge the majority's position, and frequently identify the provisions that later generate difficulty. The report speaks for the committee; the minute of dissent speaks for the members who could not join it.

### Q: What can the Committee on Subordinate Legislation do when rules exceed the parent Act?

It can examine, report and recommend, and it cannot annul. Where the committee finds that a rule travels beyond the delegation in the parent Act, imposes an unauthorised levy, operates retrospectively without sanction or was laid after undue delay, it reports the defect to the House and seeks the ministry's explanation and corrective action, and it may follow the matter through the ministry's reply. The power to strike the instrument down does not lie with the committee: modification or annulment requires the Houses themselves to act under the laying clause of the parent Act, or a court to hold the instrument ultra vires in litigation. The committee's report is the systematic record of such defects, and litigants and members alike draw on it.
