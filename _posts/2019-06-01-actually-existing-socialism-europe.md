Actually existing socialism is the name this article gives to the European states governed by communist parties between 1945 and 1989, and the name matters because it was not invented by the systems' enemies. It began as the rulers' own description of their societies, a phrase that asked to be judged on what existed rather than on what had been promised. Three facts should be fixed before any chronology is attempted. The societies gathered under the label were distinct from one another in language, in level of development, in the route by which their governments arrived, and in the degree of room their citizens managed to carve out. Each of them produced serious Marxist critics of itself, men and women formed inside the doctrine, employed by its universities and institutes, who turned its own categories against the apparatus that claimed to embody them. And each state answered those critics in a consistent way, with prison, with exile, with removal from posts and from print, which is a pattern of behaviour that carries analytical weight of its own.

![A reading table with the critics table, samizdat typescripts and a map of the bloc, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-27.webp)

The standard picture of the region during those decades is drawn from outside: a bloc imposed by an occupying power, held together by fear, punctuated by revolts that were crushed, and dissolved when the guarantee of external force was withdrawn. That picture is not false, and parts of it will be confirmed in what follows. It is, however, radically incomplete, because it treats the inhabitants of these states as objects of a geopolitics rather than as thinkers about their own condition. Inside every one of these societies there existed a literature, some of it published and much of it circulated in typescript, that asked what kind of social formation this actually was, who ruled it, in whose interest, and by what right. The authors of that literature were not liberals manqués and not agents of a foreign power, whatever the indictments said. They were, in the main, communists: party members, veterans of illegal movements under fascism, professors of Marxism-Leninism, editors of theoretical journals. Their analyses were penetrating precisely because they were internal. They knew the doctrine from within, they knew the apparatus from within, and they could measure the distance between the two with an insider's instruments.

This article is organised around that internal literature and around the claim it supports, which the series calls the internal critique: the sharpest accounts of these formations were written by Marxists who lived under them, and the systems' habit of answering those accounts with prison, exile, or dismissal is itself the weightiest confirmation of what the accounts said about the ruling apparatus. Around that centre the article sets the imposition and the variations, the risings of 1953, 1956, 1968, and the long Polish sequence, the Yugoslav experiment in workers self-management and the philosophical school that grew up beside it, the reform economists who tried to combine planning with market coordination, and the ending of 1989. The mechanics of central planning are treated in the companion study of [soviet planning how it worked](/2019/04/15/soviet-planning-how-it-worked/), and the question whether the Soviet system itself deserves the name socialist is argued in [was the ussr socialist](/2019/05/15/was-the-ussr-socialist/); both are linked rather than restated here, so that this article can keep its attention on the European states and on the people inside them who named what they saw.

## Actually existing socialism: a phrase with two lives

Few political phrases carry their own refutation inside them as neatly as this one. To call a society actually existing socialism is to concede, in the same breath, that there exists another socialism against which the actual one might be measured and found wanting: the socialism of the theory, of the founding texts, of the movement's own promises. The rulers who popularised the phrase intended the opposite emphasis. In their usage the adjective did the work of a shrug: whatever the ideal might be, this was the thing itself, built under real conditions by real people, and criticism that proceeded from the ideal was therefore criticism of reality for failing to be a book. The critics who borrowed the phrase accepted the gift and reversed the emphasis. If this was socialism as it actually existed, then the gap between the existence and the doctrine was a fact about the doctrine's custodians, and it could be documented street by street and statute by statute.

### Where does the phrase actually existing socialism come from?

it began as an official self-description in the early 1970s, asserting that whatever socialism might be in theory, this was socialism as it actually existed. Critics adopted the phrase precisely because it conceded a gap between the theory and the system.

The phrase belongs to a family of official formulas that marked the settling of the bloc into permanence. An earlier formula had spoken of developed socialism, a stage on the road in which the foundations were declared complete and the remaining task was perfection of what stood. The newer wording went further in one respect: it lowered the horizon. A society described as actually existing was asking to be excused from comparison with its own founding promises, and the excuse was offered as maturity. Dissident and critical writers across the region heard the concession immediately. In samizdat essays and in the émigré press the phrase was repeated with a stress on the first word, and the stress did the analytical work: actually, as opposed to nominally; existing, as opposed to achieved. By the end of the decade the term had completed its migration and belonged to the critics, which is why reference works now tend to record it as a critical coinage. The record runs the other way, and restoring the direction matters, because the phrase's origin in official speech is the first piece of evidence that the systems' own custodians had quietly abandoned the claim that the ideal and the reality coincided.

### Why did the official phrase prove so useful to the critics who borrowed it?

Because it surrendered the strongest defensive position the regimes possessed, which was the appeal from the flawed present to the radiant intention. Once the rulers themselves insisted on being judged by what existed, every documented feature of existence, the queue, the privilege, the censored page, became admissible evidence about the system rather than a regrettable accident befalling it.

There is a second reason the borrowed phrase served the critics so well, and it concerns the kind of argument each side could sustain. Official apologetics had always relied on a division of labour between theory and circumstance: the theory was sound, the circumstances were hostile, and the distance between promise and performance was charged to encirclement, backwardness, and war. A phrase that directed attention to the actually existing whole made that division harder to maintain, since the whole included the circumstances' products after four decades of rule, at which point the circumstances themselves had been substantially made by the system. Critics did not need to deny that encirclement and backwardness were real. They needed only to ask how long an explanation remains an explanation before it becomes a description of the thing explained, and the borrowed phrase let them pose that question in the rulers' own vocabulary.

## The map was never one colour

The word bloc does a great deal of quiet damage in accounts of the region, because it invites the reader to imagine a single substance poured into several national moulds. The governments that took power between 1945 and 1948 shared a template: a single ruling party fused with the state, a planned economy directed from a centre, a monopoly of organised public expression, and an alliance that subordinated foreign policy to Moscow. Beneath that shared template almost everything else differed. Poland retained a largely private peasantry after a failed collectivisation and a Catholic Church with its own institutional power. Hungary after 1956 developed the most permissive cultural climate in the region and, from 1968, the most market-inflected economy. Czechoslovakia entered the period as an industrial country with a democratic past and a mass communist party that had won a plurality in a free election. East Germany was half a nation, its legitimacy permanently shadowed by the other half across the border. Romania pursued an increasingly autarkic and personalist course while remaining inside the alliance's forms. Bulgaria hewed closest to the Soviet pattern. Yugoslavia, after 1948, stood outside the alliance altogether and built a doctrine of its separateness.

### How did communist governments come to power across the region in the founding years?

By routes that mixed coalition, coercion, and genuine support in proportions that differed by country. Communist parties entered broad governments under Soviet sponsorship, took control of interior ministries and security organs, dismantled rivals piece by piece in what opponents named salami tactics, and by 1948 held undivided power everywhere east of the Elbe except Finland.

The proportions matter for everything that follows, because the internal critics' later standing depended partly on how their societies remembered the founding. In Czechoslovakia the party's electoral strength was real, and the February crisis of 1948 could be narrated by its defenders as a constitutional resolution rather than a foreign imposition, which made the subsequent disillusion of Czech Marxists a story of a promise betrayed from inside a movement many had freely joined. In Poland the founding was remembered far more widely as an imposition sustained by an external army, which gave Polish criticism a national as well as a doctrinal register and helps explain why the Polish opposition, alone in the bloc, eventually built a mass organisation. In Hungary the party had been small and the takeover nakedly dependent on Soviet power, a memory that fed the explosion of 1956. In Yugoslavia the partisans had liberated most of the country with their own forces, which is why Belgrade could defy Moscow in 1948 and survive the defiance: the regime's legitimacy had a domestic source that no other government in the region possessed in the same degree.

### Why were the societies of the bloc less uniform than the map suggested?

Because the template met different materials. A plan imposed on an industrial economy with a skilled working class produced different frictions from the same plan imposed on a peasant society; a party ruling a Catholic nation faced a rival institution no decree could dissolve; a divided country measured itself daily against its other half.

Those differences widened with time rather than narrowing, which is one of the quiet facts the bloc vocabulary conceals. The 1950s looked most alike, because terror and forced industrialisation were common instruments applied in a common sequence. From the mid-1960s the paths diverged visibly: Hungary experimented with prices and enterprise autonomy while Czechoslovakia, after 1968, froze into a deliberate immobility that its rulers advertised as stability; Poland lurched between liberalisation and crackdown in a rhythm set by its streets, while East Germany built the most complete apparatus of surveillance and the most generous system of social provision in the region, the two achievements related. A historian who treats the bloc as a unit will miss the variation that made some of these societies reformable in pieces and others brittle as a whole, and will miss why the ending, when it came, took a different form in each capital.

## The official self-understanding in its own terms

No account of these systems is honest that does not first state how they described themselves, in the vocabulary of their own documents. The official doctrine held that the means of production had passed into social ownership, that exploitation of man by man had therefore been abolished, and that remaining inequalities were residues and imperfections rather than structures. The party's leading role was presented not as a monopoly of power but as the organised consciousness of the working class, the institution through which a society that owned its economy collectively could deliberate about its direction. Elections, soviets and councils, trade unions, and the press were described as channels of a democracy broader than the parliamentary kind, because it rested on economic equality rather than merely formal rights. Shortages and queues were transitional; privilege was denied as a category; censorship was characterised as the protection of a young social order against hostile propaganda financed from abroad.

### What did the rulers of these states say their societies were?

They said their societies were socialism under construction or, in the later formula, socialism developed and actually existing: economies owned socially and directed by plan toward need, polities in which the working people ruled through their party and its mass organisations, and orders whose defects were survivals of the old world rather than products of the new one.

Stated in its own terms, the doctrine had an internal coherence that critics ignore at the cost of understanding their opponents. If ownership is the decisive relation, then nationalisation really had transformed the foundation, and a manager appointed by a ministry really was a different figure from an owner drawing profit, even where the experience of the shop floor felt similar. If the party genuinely embodied the class's historical interest, then opposition to the party could be redescribed as opposition to the class by those too backward or too compromised to know their own interest, and repression could be experienced by its administrators as pedagogy. The internal critics' originality lay in refusing to attack this structure from outside, with liberal premises it could shrug off. They attacked it from inside, accepting for argument's sake that ownership and class were the decisive categories, and then asking who, under those categories, actually disposed of the surplus, who decided, who could be removed and by whom. That is why their work could not be answered by the standard apologetic repertoire, and why it was answered by other means.

The doctrine also contained a theory of its own critics, which deserves recording because it shaped the fate of every figure in this article. Dissent from within the movement was classified as revisionism when it appealed to the founding texts against the leadership, and as leftist deviation when it demanded faster transformation, and both classifications carried an implication of objective service to the external enemy whatever the dissenter's subjective loyalty. A critic could therefore be a decorated veteran, a survivor of the camps of the fascist period, a professor whose textbook taught the doctrine to a generation, and still find his argument processed by the apparatus as a security matter rather than an intellectual one. The critics table later in this article records what that processing looked like in individual lives; here it is enough to register that the official self-understanding had no category for loyal internal criticism of the system's structure. Criticism of details, of implementation, of individual cadres, was solicited in controlled forms. Criticism of the structure was, by definition, something else.

## East Berlin: the first rising

The first explosion came in the state that considered itself the bloc's model pupil. In June 1953 construction workers on the Stalinallee, the showcase boulevard of the East German capital, downed tools over raised work norms that amounted to a cut in pay imposed in the name of building socialism faster. Within a day the stoppage had become a general rising across the republic, and the demands had travelled the distance that such demands always travel in these systems: from norms to prices, from prices to the government, from the government to free elections. Soviet armour ended the rising, and the regime drew the conclusion that its survival rested on a guarantee it did not itself control. The episode established the grammar of every subsequent crisis in the bloc. An economic grievance, administered by a state that was simultaneously employer, landlord, shopkeeper, and policeman, could not remain economic, because there was no institution through which it could be pressed that was not the state itself. Every dispute was therefore potentially a dispute about the whole order, and the rulers knew it, which is why they treated a wage claim and a revolution as points on a single slope.

### What set off the East Berlin rising?

A rise in work norms on the capital's prestige building sites, decreed as part of a drive to raise output without raising wages. The construction workers struck, marched on the government quarter, and found their economic demand answered by silence until the crowd itself began demanding the government's resignation and elections it could trust.

The literary aftershock of 1953 is part of the record, because the most famous response came from a writer the regime counted as its own. Bertolt Brecht, returned from exile to build a theatre in the new state, wrote privately of a government that, having lost the people's confidence, might find it simpler to dissolve the people and elect another. The lines circulated as a reproach long after they were written, and their endurance illustrates a theme this article will meet repeatedly: the sharpest formulations about these systems were produced by people who had wanted them to succeed. The workers on the Stalinallee had not read the founding texts as an indictment. Many had understood themselves as the class in whose name the state ruled, and their rising posed a question the doctrine had no procedure for hearing: what does it mean when the ruling class strikes against its own state?

## Hungary: revolution and councils

The Hungarian revolution of October and November 1956 remains the bloc's most concentrated lesson in what these societies contained. It began with students and writers, many of them formed inside the ruling movement, demonstrating in solidarity with Polish reform and against the local Stalinist leadership. It became, within days, a national revolution: the government of Imre Nagy, a communist reformer carried into office by the street, withdrew the country from the Warsaw Pact and proclaimed neutrality; the Soviet leadership, after hesitation, sent the army back in; and the fighting that followed destroyed the experiment. Two features of those weeks matter most for the argument of this article. The first is the social form the revolution improvised. Workers councils sprang up in the factories and districts, elected, recallable, jealous of their independence from any party including a reformed ruling one, and they outlasted the military defeat by weeks, negotiating and striking after the government itself had fallen. The second feature is the identity of the revolution's intellectual authors. The Petőfi Circle debates of the preceding summer, in which communist intellectuals had dismantled the record of the Rákosi years in public, supplied the revolution's vocabulary; the philosopher György Lukács, the most eminent Marxist thinker in the country, served briefly as a minister in Nagy's last government.

### What did the Hungarian workers councils demand?

Control of the factories by those who worked in them, exercised through elected and recallable councils; the withdrawal of foreign troops; a free press; and political pluralism including parties beyond the ruling one. Their programme joined the demand for national independence to the demand that social ownership mean disposal by the producers rather than administration over them.

The councils are the point at which the Hungarian events cease to be a national rising and become evidence in the argument about the system itself. Here were industrial workers, organised without any party's permission, arriving independently at the institutional demand that the internal critics were elaborating in texts: that the decisive question was not the legal form of ownership but the location of control. The councils did not ask for the restoration of private owners. They asked that the factories be run by the people the factories nominally already belonged to, and the request was refused by the power that claimed to rule in those people's name. A reader who wants a single episode in which the internal critique was enacted rather than written could do worse than the Central Workers Council of Greater Budapest, bargaining with a government installed by foreign tanks for the right of workers to manage their own workplaces, and dissolving only under direct threat in December. Nagy was executed in 1958 after a secret trial, a fact recorded here without elaboration because the record needs none. Lukács was deported and later allowed to return, and his school, the Budapest philosophers who appear later in this article, inherited both his prestige and his predicament.

## The long Polish road

Poland supplied the bloc's longest and most instructive sequence of crises, because Polish society possessed resources no other society in the region could match: a church that had never been broken, a countryside that had defeated collectivisation, and a working class in the great coastal and Silesian plants that learned from each defeat and kept the minutes. In 1956 Polish workers in Poznań rose over wages and norms, and the crisis brought Władysław Gomułka, a communist with a prison record under his own movement, to power on a wave of national expectation that he spent years disappointing. In 1968 the regime answered student protest with an antisemitic purge conducted in the language of anti-Zionism, driving much of the country's intellectual class, including some of its most distinguished Marxists, into exile. In December 1970 shipyard workers on the Baltic coast struck against price rises announced before Christmas and were shot at the gates of their yards; the leadership changed, borrowed heavily abroad, and bought a decade of calm. In 1976 workers at Radom and Ursus struck again against prices, and this time intellectuals organised a committee to defend the arrested, joining the two halves of Polish opposition in a single structure. In August 1980 the Gdańsk shipyard struck over the dismissal of a crane operator and free trade unions, and the regime, unable to shoot and unwilling to yield, signed an agreement legalising an independent union. Sixteen months of dual power followed before martial law interned the movement's leadership in December 1981.

### Why does the Polish sequence run longer than any other in the bloc?

Because each Polish crisis left an institution standing. The church survived every campaign against it; the private peasantry survived collectivisation; the defence committee of 1976 converted episodic revolt into permanent organisation; and the union of 1980 converted organisation into a mass membership that martial law could imprison but could not uninvent, so that each round began where the previous round had ended.

Running through the Polish sequence is a transformation in the opposition's own theory that matters to this article's centre. The early Polish critics, including the two young militants whose open letter appears in the critics table, argued as Marxists that the bureaucracy was a ruling class and that the remedy was a revolution of the producers against it. The later Polish opposition, schooled by the failures of 1968 and 1970, increasingly argued in a different register: Adam Michnik's essay of 1976 proposed a new evolutionism, a strategy of building independent social institutions and leaving the apparatus to rot around them rather than storming it, and the defence committee practised exactly that. The shift from a Marxist critique of the system to a civil critique alongside it marks the moment when the internal critique, in its Polish homeland, began to hand its authority to a successor language of rights and self-organisation. The union that emerged in 1980 spoke both languages at once, which was its strength: its demands joined free unions and a free press to workers control and socialised property, and historians still argue over which half of the programme its millions of members would have chosen had choice been possible.

## Czechoslovakia: reform from above

The Prague Spring inverted the usual direction of crisis in the bloc. Elsewhere pressure rose from the street against a reluctant leadership; in Czechoslovakia in 1968 the initiative came from the leadership itself. Alexander Dubček's party, responding to economic stagnation, to Slovak grievances inside the federation, and to a generation of writers and economists who had been dismantling the old certainties in print for years, launched a programme of socialism with a human face: censorship abolished in practice, rehabilitation of the victims of the trials, federalisation of the state, and an economic reform, designed by Ota Šik, that would have subjected enterprises to market signals while retaining social ownership. The Action Programme of April 1968 is the closest the bloc ever came to an official document of internal reform written in the movement's own language, promising that the party would lead by authority earned rather than by monopoly guaranteed. In August the armies of five allied states ended the experiment in a night, on the stated ground that socialism in one member country was the common property of all and could be defended against its own government.

### What did the invasion of August settle, and for how long?

It settled the question whether a ruling party in the bloc could reform the system from within while remaining in the alliance: the answer given was that it could not, because the reform's logic, free expression, accountable authority, enterprises answering to buyers, dissolved the monopoly on which every neighbouring regime also rested, and the settlement held for twenty years.

The Czechoslovak aftermath, called normalisation, is as instructive as the spring. Dubček was removed in 1969; hundreds of thousands of party members were expelled or driven out in screenings that asked each person to affirm that the invasion had been fraternal assistance; the reform economists scattered into exile or into boiler rooms; the philosopher Karel Kosík, whose book of 1963 had given Czech Marxism an international standing, was removed from his university and forbidden to publish. A society that had attempted reform through its own ruling party was punished with a deliberate, administered hopelessness, and the lesson was read carefully in every other capital. After 1968 no leadership in the bloc attempted structural reform from above until Moscow itself changed course in the mid-1980s, by which time the Czechoslovak precedent was quoted in reverse: reform from above was what happened when the centre lost control of its own instrument, and the men of 1968, patiently working as stokers and window cleaners, became the living footnote to the doctrine that the system could correct itself.

## What the risings had in common

Stand back from the four sequences and a single structure repeats. Each crisis began with a grievance that the system's own ideology had taught its citizens to regard as legitimate: norms that cheated the worker in a workers state, prices raised by decree in an economy that claimed to have abolished exploitation, censorship in a culture that claimed to have liberated the mind. Each crisis escalated because no legitimate channel existed through which the grievance could be pressed to a conclusion; the unions were transmission belts, the press was an organ, the courts were a department. Each was answered, at the decisive moment, by force or the threat of it, applied in 1953 and 1956 and 1968 by foreign armies and in Poland by the state's own, and each answer confirmed the proposition the internal critics were formulating in the same decades: that between the apparatus and society there was no mediating institution the apparatus did not own, so that every conflict was finally a conflict about the apparatus itself. The risings also shared a social signature that embarrassed the official doctrine more than any text could. The crowds were not composed of dispossessed owners or foreign agents. They were composed, again and again, of the industrial working class in whose name the whole structure claimed to rule, joined by the intellectuals the system had educated. A doctrine that explains opposition as the residue of the old order has difficulty with opposition that is the new order's own product, educated in its schools, employed in its factories, and quoting its classics.

There is a further common feature, quieter than the others, which the critics themselves noticed and used. In each crisis the apparatus, at the moment of danger, reached past every institution of the socialist state it had built, past the unions and the councils and the party's own rank and file, to the single instrument it trusted: organised coercion, whether its own police or an ally's army. The internal critics made much of this ordering of trust. A ruling stratum confident that the social order expressed the population's interest would, in a crisis, appeal to that population; a stratum that experiences society as a threat to be managed will appeal to force. The risings therefore function in the critics' literature as experiments the system performed on itself, revealing under pressure which of its institutions were real and which were scenery. That reading may be contested, and the strongest objection to the critics, stated later in this article, contests the conclusions drawn from it; but the reading's raw material is the public record of 1953, 1956, 1968, and 1980, and no account of the bloc that omits the risings can explain why the internal literature took the shape it did.

## The internal critique: the claim at the centre

It is time to state the article's central claim with the precision it needs. Across the bloc, over four decades, the systems generated from within themselves a body of Marxist analysis that described them as a distinct social formation, ruled neither by the associated producers of the doctrine nor by a restored capitalist class, but by a bureaucratic stratum whose power rested on its monopoly of disposal over the socialised means of production and of organisation over society. That body of analysis is what this series names the internal critique. Its authors disagreed with one another about much: about whether the formation was a degenerated socialism, a new class society, or a sui generis order of state dependence; about whether it could be reformed, required revolution, or would evolve; about what should replace it. They agreed on the location of power and on the test that proved it. The proof they offered was not only the content of their analyses. It was the biography of the analyses themselves. A system confident that its critics were mistaken could refute them in the journals it owned outright. These systems, with complete monopolies of print and appointment, instead imprisoned Rudolf Bahro, jailed Jacek Kuroń and Karol Modzelewski, drove the Budapest philosophers from their posts, silenced Karel Kosík, exiled Włodzimierz Brus and Leszek Kołakowski, and suppressed the Praxis journal. The response is evidence because it discriminates: it is the behaviour of an apparatus that experiences structural criticism as a threat to its existence, which is precisely what the critics said the apparatus was.

A word about the table's construction, since a table is an argument in compressed form. The rows are ordered by the year of the analysis, and the ordering discloses a rhythm: the critique begins at the top of the systems, with a vice president of Yugoslavia and a philosopher in the East German party's own academy, and it descends and widens with each decade, from office holders to professors to students to workers' advocates, until by the 1970s it is being written by people the system had educated entirely within itself. The column of consequences is deliberately flat in wording. Imprisoned, exiled, removed: the flatness is the point, because the variety of the analyses, humanist, economic, philosophical, organisational, stands against the monotony of the answers they received, and a reader who grasps that contrast has grasped the internal critique's evidentiary core.

## Milovan Djilas and the new class

The first great statement of the internal critique came from the highest possible altitude inside a ruling party. Milovan Djilas had been a partisan commander, a vice president of Yugoslavia, and the regime's chief ideologist when he began publishing essays arguing that the revolution's promises were being consumed by the stratum administering them. His book The New Class, published abroad in 1957 after its author had already lost his posts and his freedom of movement, argued that the communist bureaucracy constituted a class in the strict sense the doctrine itself supplied: a group defined by its relation to the means of production, which in a nationalised economy meant not legal title but monopoly of administration, the power to dispose of the social product and to distribute privilege through office. Djilas was imprisoned for the book and for interviews given to foreign journalists, and released and imprisoned again across the following decade. His analysis set the terms that later critics refined or resisted. Many of them resisted the word class, preferring stratum, caste, or apparatus, because the bureaucracy could not bequeath its positions as property and reproduced itself through appointment rather than inheritance. The refinement matters and the critics table preserves it, but the founding insight survived every refinement: in a society where the state owns the economy, control of the state is the form that ownership takes, and a monopoly of control held by a self-recruiting body is a structure of rule, not a regrettable style of management.

## Wolfgang Harich and Robert Havemann: the East German beginning

East Germany produced its internal critics early and punished them in proportion to their standing. Wolfgang Harich, a philosophy professor and editor who had built the party's own publishing house for philosophy, drafted a platform in 1956 for a democratic reform of the republic: free discussion, an independent judiciary, a road to German unity through negotiation, all within a socialist order he continued to affirm. He showed the platform to the Soviet ambassador and to journalists, was arrested within days, and was sentenced at a show proceeding to ten years, of which he served eight. Robert Havemann, a physical chemist with an anti-Nazi resistance record and impeccable party credentials, began lecturing at the Humboldt University on the philosophy of science in terms that denied the official doctrine its claim to settle questions outside its competence; his lectures drew thousands, his party expelled him in 1964, his university dismissed him, and in the 1970s the state placed him under house arrest in his own village while his books appeared in the West. The two cases mark the boundaries of the permissible in the bloc's most doctrinally rigid state: a reform platform communicated through channels was treason, and a lecture series on epistemology was an affair of state. Both men insisted to the end that they were communists correcting their own movement, and the insistence was not a protective colouring. It was the substance of the offence, because a heretic inside the doctrine threatened the apparatus in a way an external enemy never could.

## The open letter: Kuron and Modzelewski

In Poland in the mid-1960s two young militants of the ruling party's own youth movement composed the most systematic of all the internal critiques, an open letter addressed not to foreign opinion but to the party and the working class of their own country. Jacek Kuroń and Karol Modzelewski argued from the doctrine's premises with a rigour the apparatus found unanswerable in print. The central political bureaucracy, they wrote, decides the distribution of the national income, fixes the goals of production, and stands over the producers as the effective owner of the means of production; it is therefore a class, and its interest is opposed to the interest of the workers it rules, whose strikes and risings the letter read as class struggle in the classical sense conducted against a state that called itself theirs. The remedy proposed was a revolution of the producers: workers councils with real authority, abolition of the political police, freedom of organisation and publication, a republic of councils in which the bureaucracy's monopoly would be broken rather than persuaded. The letter circulated in typescript in 1965; its authors were arrested before it could be formally delivered, tried, and sentenced to prison terms, Kuroń to three years and Modzelewski to three and a half.

### What did Kuron and Modzelewski claim in their open letter?

That the central political bureaucracy was a ruling class in the doctrine's own sense, since it monopolised decisions over production and distribution while the producers who nominally owned the economy decided nothing; that Polish workers' revolts were therefore class struggles against that class; and that the remedy was a workers revolution establishing council rule.

The letter's afterlife diverged between its two authors in a way that maps the Polish opposition's own evolution. Modzelewski, imprisoned again in 1968 and again under martial law, remained a historian of medieval Europe and a socialist of the council tradition, sceptical of the market turn his country later took. Kuroń became the central figure of the defence committee of 1976 and the chief strategist of Solidarity, revising his revolutionary programme into the self-limiting revolution that built independent institutions and declined to storm the state, and eventually serving as a minister in the first post-communist government, administering social policies amid a transition whose social costs he publicly anguished over. The trajectory from the letter to the ministry is not a simple story of moderation. It records what two decades of the system's answers taught the critics about the costs of the maximal programme, a lesson the strongest objection to the internal critique, considered later, presses hard: that the councils of the letter existed nowhere for long, and that their authors' own later practice conceded more to the objection than their texts ever did.

## Karel Kosik and the dialectics of the concrete

Czechoslovakia's contribution to the internal critique was philosophical in form and total in implication. Karel Kosík's Dialectics of the Concrete, published in 1963, distinguished between the pseudo-concrete world of administered routine, in which things appear as fixed objects to be managed, and the concrete totality that critical thought recovers by showing how that world is made and can be remade by human practice. The vocabulary was scholastic; the target was not. A society administered as a stock of objects, in which people encounter the economy and the state as alien machinery whose motions they service, was for Kosík a society in which the doctrine's promise of human self-creation had been inverted into its opposite, and he developed the charge through readings of Kafka and of the Prague everyday as much as through the classical texts. The book was translated across Europe and gave Czech Marxism an authority out of proportion to the country's size; its author helped prepare the intellectual ground of 1968 and served the reform moment in its cultural councils. After the invasion he was removed from his institute, expelled from the party in 1970, and forbidden to teach or publish, supporting himself outside academic life while his manuscripts circulated among students in typescript. He outlived the system and returned to print after 1989, one of the few figures in the critics table to see the world his silencing had been meant to prevent.

### What happened to Karel Kosik after the invasion?

He was removed from his academic post, expelled from the ruling party in 1970, and prohibited from teaching and publishing for two decades. His later work circulated in typescript among students. The punishment fitted the pattern exactly: no public refutation of his dialectics was attempted by the institutions that had once published him.

Kosík's case isolates a feature of the internal critique that the more political texts can obscure. His offence was not a programme. He proposed no councils, no market mechanism, no party reform; he proposed a way of seeing, in which the administered world is recognised as a human product and therefore as changeable by the humans who produce it. The apparatus treated a metaphysics as a security matter because it understood, correctly on its own premises, that the pseudo-concrete was not a philosophical error but its own mode of existence: a system that must present its arrangements as nature cannot afford a philosophy that presents them as history. The same logic explains why poets, historians of the national past, and philosophers of science all appear in the bloc's punishment records alongside economists and organisers. Where a single institution claims the whole of truth, every discipline becomes a frontier.

## The Budapest School and the dictatorship over needs

Hungary's internal critics gathered around the figure of Lukács, whose students formed what came to be called the Budapest School: Ágnes Heller, Ferenc Fehér, György Márkus, and their circle, philosophers formed in the master's late seminars and employed, until they were not, by the Hungarian Academy and the universities. Their collective work reached its sharpest statement in a volume published abroad in 1983, Dictatorship over Needs, which analysed the Soviet-type societies as a distinct formation in which a paternalist apparatus does not merely command production but undertakes to define the needs production exists to satisfy, allocating recognition and goods according to its own hierarchy of purposes and treating needs it has not authorised as deviant. The analysis cut deeper than the standard charge of inefficiency. A market society may fail to satisfy needs; a liberal order may leave them to private fortune; the formation the Budapest philosophers described claimed jurisdiction over the needs themselves, so that the citizen's inner inventory of wants became an object of administration, and dissatisfaction could be processed as a defect of the dissatisfied. The school's members lost their posts in the 1970s after a party resolution condemned their positions; several emigrated, Heller and Fehér to Australia and later to the United States, Márkus to Australia, where the work continued in exile.

### What is a dictatorship over needs, in the Budapest School's sense?

A social order in which the ruling apparatus claims authority not only over production and distribution but over the definition of legitimate need itself, deciding which wants count as socialist and which as selfish, backward, or hostile, so that the citizen's desires are administered before they can become demands the system must answer.

The Budapest analysis also supplied the internal critique with its most developed account of why these systems could not reform themselves by decree from the top. A dictatorship over needs, the school argued, is not a policy that a wiser leadership might relax while keeping the structure; it is the structure's way of reproducing consent, substituting the apparatus's plan of needs for the population's own articulation of them. Partial reforms that left the jurisdiction untouched would therefore regenerate the whole, as the school's members watched happen after each Hungarian thaw. The argument matters for the assessment of the reform economists later in this article, because it predicts, from inside Marxist theory, the pattern the reforms actually displayed: genuine improvements in supply and in the texture of daily life, purchased at the price of leaving the decisive monopoly intact, and therefore reversible the moment the monopoly felt threatened. The school's own fate illustrated the thesis with an economy no novelist would dare. Philosophers who argued that the apparatus administered needs were administered out of their posts by the apparatus, their books unprinted in their own language, their seminars dissolved; and the episode was processed by the official press, when it was mentioned at all, as a personnel matter.

## Konrad, Szelenyi, and the intellectuals on the road to class power

A second Hungarian work completes the school's picture by turning the class instrument on the critics' own stratum. György Konrád, a novelist and social researcher, and Iván Szelényi, a sociologist, composed in the early 1970s an analysis arguing that under state socialism the intellectuals, the possessors of certified knowledge employed by the redistributive state, were on the road to becoming a ruling class in their own right, since a system that allocates by plan must elevate those who know how to plan, legitimate, and administer. The manuscript was confiscated in 1974, its authors arrested and interrogated; Szelényi was expelled from the country, Konrád remained under surveillance and published his fiction abroad. The book appeared in English in 1979 and discomforted every party to the argument. It discomforted the apparatus, obviously. It discomforted Western admirers of the internal critics, who preferred their dissidents unambiguously democratic. And it discomforted the critics themselves, by suggesting that the stratum from which nearly all of them came was not merely the system's victim or its conscience but one of its candidate successors, a suggestion with obvious bearing on how the internal critique should be read: as testimony from inside a contest for the future of the formation, not as a view from nowhere. The critics table includes the work because the internal critique is strongest exactly where it is most unsparing, and a tradition that could turn its instruments on its own authors' class position had earned the right to be taken seriously as analysis rather than as grievance.

## Rudolf Bahro and The Alternative

The most ambitious single work of the internal critique came from inside the East German apparatus itself. Rudolf Bahro had been a party functionary and an editor at a Berlin publishing house when he completed, in secret and over years, the manuscript of The Alternative in Eastern Europe, published in the West in 1977 while its author gave interviews to Western television from his East Berlin flat explaining that he was a communist and that his book was an internal matter of his own movement. The state arrested him within days and sentenced him in 1978 to eight years for the transmission of information, a charge whose flimsiness measured the book's importance. An international campaign by Western socialists and communists, for whom Bahro had become a test of whether the doctrine could still criticise its own states, pressed for his release; he was amnestied in 1979 and expelled to the West, where he disappointed several of his audiences by moving toward ecological politics.

### What did Rudolf Bahro argue in The Alternative?

That the Soviet-type societies were not socialist but a distinct proto-socialist formation in which a politbureaucratic apparatus ruled subaltern producers, monopolised the surplus, and blocked the development it claimed to lead; that the division of labour inherited from capitalism was the material root of the blockage; and that liberation required a cultural revolution of consciousness.

Bahro's central positive concept was the surplus consciousness, the measure of human capacity, education, imagination, and aspiration, that the system's own schools and factories produced in excess of the subordinate roles the system had available for it to fill. A regime that educates everyone and employs their education narrowly manufactures its own gravedigger in a form the classical theory had not anticipated: not the immiserated worker but the frustrated graduate, the engineer stamping forms, the teacher reciting a catechism she has examined and found empty. The concept deserves its place in the critics table's logic because it explains the social composition of dissent in the late bloc better than any imported theory of totalitarian atomisation. The critics were the surplus consciousness at work, and the apparatus's punishments were an attempt to waste, exile, or imprison a resource its own development kept replenishing. Bahro also refused the two easiest exits from his analysis. He did not conclude that the remedy was a return to the market, which he regarded as a regression behind the socialisation already achieved; and he did not conclude that a nicer leadership would suffice, since the apparatus in his account was a structure with interests, not a group of mistaken men. What he proposed instead, a league of communists educating society toward the abolition of the old division of labour, was the vaguest element of the book, and the hardest challenge to the internal critique seizes on exactly that vagueness and generalises it.

### What did the surplus consciousness mean in Bahro's analysis?

It named the excess of educated capacity over the roles a command society could offer. Mass schooling produced citizens whose abilities and expectations overflowed the subordinate functions assigned to them, and that overflow, blocked from expression in work or politics, accumulated as the system's characteristic discontent and as the social base of its internal critics.

## What happened to the critics: the pattern of response

Gather the fates recorded in this article and in the critics table and a taxonomy emerges, consistent across countries that agreed about little else. The first instrument was removal: from the institute, the university, the journal, the party, in screenings and resolutions whose language was administrative and whose meaning was public. The second was prohibition: the book unprinted in its own language, the name unmentionable in print, the lectures cancelled, so that a thinker could be famous in translation and nonexistent at home. The third was exile, sometimes chosen under pressure and sometimes imposed by expulsion or by the simple device of permitting a journey and annulling the return. The fourth, reserved for those who organised, published at home without permission, or addressed the working class directly, was prison: Harich, Djilas, Kuroń, Modzelewski, Bahro, and behind them the thousands of unnamed defendants of the risings. Two features of the taxonomy carry the analytical weight. The severity of the response tracked not the critic's hostility to socialism but the critic's proximity to the system's own claims: the loyal insider arguing from the founding texts drew heavier punishment than the declared liberal, because the insider contested the apparatus's title to its own doctrine. And the response was never, in the structural cases, refutation. The journals that might have answered Bahro reviewed him not at all; the institutes that might have answered the open letter answered its authors with a court. An apparatus that commands every press in a country and still reaches for the police has filed its own assessment of its arguments' strength, and the internal critics were entitled to cite the filing.

The point deserves one more turn, because it is the hinge between this article's evidence and its claim. Defenders of the systems sometimes replied that every state punishes sedition and that the critics' fates prove nothing about the truth of their analyses. The reply fails on the facts of the taxonomy. The punished acts were, in the central cases, the writing of philosophy, the drafting of an economic programme, the delivery of lectures on dialectics, the circulation of a letter addressed to the author's own party. Where organisation was punished, the organisation proposed was of the kind the doctrine itself celebrated: councils of workers, independent unions, a league of communists. A state may of course punish any of these things if it chooses; the choice is what requires explanation, and the critics' explanation, that an apparatus ruling in the producers' name cannot permit the producers or their theorists to organise independently without dissolving its own title, accounts for the pattern with fewer assumptions than any alternative yet offered. That is the sense in which the response is evidence for the analysis, and it is the sense the namable claim of this article, the internal critique, is intended to fix in the reader's memory.

## Why the sharpest analyses came from inside

The internal origin of the critique was not an accident of biography; it followed from the structure of knowledge under these systems. An external observer of the bloc worked from statistics the regimes fabricated, from interviews the regimes staged, and from a theoretical expectation, whether sympathetic or hostile, formed elsewhere. The internal critic worked from the texture of administered life: the meeting at which the decision announced had been taken elsewhere, the journal issue withdrawn, the colleague vanished into a boiler room, the plan figure everyone in the ministry knew to be a fiction negotiated rather than a quantity measured. Such knowledge could not be imported, and it could not be faked, which is why the internal literature still reads as reportage from a country that no longer exists while much external commentary reads as a projection of the commentator's own politics. The internal critics also possessed the one credential the apparatus could not dismiss as ignorance. They had been formed by the doctrine, many had served it at cost to themselves under fascism or in the building years, and their charge was therefore not that the promise was alien but that it had been confiscated. When Kuroń and Modzelewski computed the bureaucracy's share of the national income from official statistics, or when Bahro anatomised the apparatus from inside its own publishing house, the work carried an evidentiary signature no émigré polemic could forge.

There is a cost to internal knowledge, and honesty requires naming it, because it shapes the strongest objection. The critics wrote as participants in a tradition whose categories they largely retained: class, surplus, the division of labour, the withering of the state. Those categories illuminated the apparatus brilliantly and the alternatives dimly. A critic formed inside the doctrine could describe with unmatched precision what the system was; the same formation made it harder to imagine institutions the tradition had not already theorised, and the programmes in the critics table are correspondingly thin where they are not borrowed from the councils of 1956 or the Yugoslav experiment. The objection that the critics demanded an improved edition of a system incapable of improvement draws its force from exactly this asymmetry between their analysis and their programme. This article will state that objection at full strength in its own section and answer it as far as it can be answered. Here the point is only to register that the internal critique's authority and its limit have the same source, and that the extra questions in the article's closing section return to the separation every student should learn to perform: between a critic's analysis, which may be exact, and a critic's programme, which may be unbuildable, since the two achievements are independent and history is full of diagnosticians whose prescriptions would have killed the patient differently.

## Yugoslavia: the break with Moscow

Yugoslavia stands in this article as the bloc's great exception and its most useful experiment, the one case in which a ruling communist party, expelled from the Soviet alliance, attempted to build a different institutional answer to the questions the internal critics were asking. The break of 1948 was not of Belgrade's choosing in its timing. The Yugoslav partisans had won their own war, governed with a confidence the other parties lacked, and pursued their own line in the Balkans with an independence Moscow read as insubordination; the expulsion from the Cominform in June 1948 charged them with nationalism and Trotskyism and expected their collapse. Collapse did not come. The regime survived on Western aid it had not sought, on its own security apparatus, and on a genuine popular rallying against foreign dictation, and it then faced the intellectual problem the expulsion had created: if the Soviet model was not socialism's only form, what was Yugoslavia's? The answer elaborated over the following decade, social ownership administered by the producers themselves through workers self-management, was simultaneously a legitimation of the break, a bid for a distinct place between the blocs, and a real institutional departure whose workings this article must examine rather than merely celebrate.

### How did Yugoslavia come to break with Soviet direction?

Through a collision between a partisan regime that had liberated its own country and a Soviet leadership that expected obedience in foreign policy, cadre selection, and economic arrangement. The alliance expelled Yugoslavia in 1948; the regime survived the expulsion, accepted Western assistance without entering the Western alliance, and converted its defiance into the founding charter of a separate road.

The break's consequences ran in two directions at once, and both belong in the record. Externally it produced the non-aligned posture that gave a small Balkan state a world role, the summits with Nehru and Nasser, and a foreign policy of genuine independence that no other European communist state enjoyed. Internally it licensed a decade of relative intellectual openness in which Yugoslav Marxists could criticise the Soviet system by name, publish philosophy that engaged the young Marx of the manuscripts, and debate their own arrangements in print, a latitude unimaginable in Prague or Warsaw in the same years. The same regime, however, maintained a political police, a camp on the island of Goli Otok for supporters of the Soviet line, and a party monopoly it never relinquished; Djilas, the break's own ideologist, discovered the monopoly's limits from inside a cell. Yugoslavia was therefore never the free alternative of its own propaganda. It was something more valuable for analysis: a system that changed one decisive variable, the organisation of the enterprise, while holding the party monopoly constant, which makes it the closest thing the bloc affords to a controlled experiment on the internal critics' claims.

## Workers self-management: the experiment begins

The experiment's founding act was the law of June 1950 handing the management of state economic enterprises to their working collectives, introduced in the National Assembly with the slogan factories to the workers and elaborated across the following decades into an elaborate constitutional order of self-managing communities of labour, communes, and delegations. Formally, the workers council of each enterprise, elected by the whole collective, chose the management board and decided the distribution of the enterprise's income between wages, investment, and reserves, within frameworks set by law and by the banking system. Social ownership replaced state ownership in the juridical theory: the means of production belonged to society, and the associated producers administered them directly rather than through a ministry. Over time the system extended beyond the factory into schools, hospitals, housing, and cultural institutions, and spawned a vast juridical literature and a professional stratum of self-management lawyers and theorists.

### What did the self-management law actually transfer?

Formal authority over the enterprise to its elected workers council: the choice of directors, the distribution of net income between personal incomes, accumulation, and common funds, and a voice in plans and prices, all inside constraints set by federal law, by the banks that supplied credit, and by a party that retained its monopoly outside the factory gate.

What the councils changed should not be understated, because the most serious objection to the internal critique depends on Yugoslavia having been a façade, and it was not. Yugoslav workers possessed rights inside their enterprises that no worker in the command economies possessed: to elect and recall managers in contested votes that sometimes removed incumbents, to argue the income distribution in assemblies whose minutes were real, to strike, as Yugoslav workers did in growing numbers across the decades, in stoppages the press reported and the law tolerated. Yugoslav citizens travelled, worked abroad in the millions, read translations, and argued with foreign visitors in a register visitors from the East found startling. The standard of consumption, the supply of the shops, the texture of daily life differed from the bloc's as chalk from cheese, and Yugoslavs knew it and said so. Any account that calls self-management cosmetic fails its first contact with a Yugoslav factory archive or a Yugoslav memoir, and the misreading is corrected in this article's closing inventory for exactly that reason.

### What problems did self-management generate inside the enterprise?

The collective that owned its income had reasons to distribute rather than invest, to resist hiring that would dilute income per head, and to borrow for wages from banks it influenced; enterprises behaved as collective property of their insiders, regions diverged as rich republics outpaced poor ones, and unemployment was exported to foreign labour markets rather than solved at home.

The system's economists, Yugoslav and foreign, catalogued the pathologies with a candour the regime permitted because the pathologies could be blamed on imperfect implementation. An enterprise maximising income per member will underinvest, since investment's returns arrive after members depart; will restrict employment, since each new member divides the income; will favour capital intensity it does not pay for at scarcity prices, since credit was politically allocated and cheap; and will defend its market position with the instincts of an owner, which the Yugoslav firm duly did, duplicating capacity republic by republic and lobbying its commune and its bank as any interest group lobbies. Inflation and foreign debt accumulated through the 1970s as the federation refinanced the consequences, and regional inequality, between Slovenia and Kosovo above all, widened into the federation's gravest political problem. The internal critics inside Yugoslavia, the Praxis philosophers above all, argued that these were not imperfections but expressions: self-management confined to the enterprise, inside a party monopoly and a market the party supervised, had produced a new fragmentation rather than associated production, group ownership in place of social ownership. Whether that verdict or the kinder one, a genuine experiment overwhelmed by debt and nationalism, is nearer the truth, the experiment's existence is the fact the argument of this article needs: a critic's programme, workers councils disposing of their enterprises, was actually built, at national scale, for four decades, and its results were mixed in ways that can be specified rather than merely asserted.

## The Praxis school and the Korcula summer school

Beside the self-management experiment grew the most distinguished philosophical school the bloc produced, and the school's fate is the Yugoslav chapter of the internal critique. The journal Praxis began publication in Zagreb in 1964, with a Croatian and an international edition, under editors including Gajo Petrović and Rudi Supek, and with an editorial board that gathered Mihailo Marković and Svetozar Stojanović in Belgrade, Milan Kangrga in Zagreb, and correspondents across Europe who included some of the century's notable Marxist and critical thinkers. Its programme was Marxist humanism: a return to the young Marx of the 1844 manuscripts, to alienation as the central critical category, and to the claim that socialism meant the abolition of every structure, including the socialist state's own bureaucracy, in which human beings confront their own products as alien powers. The school's philosophical content belongs to the series article on [marxist humanism explained](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/) and is linked rather than restated here; this article owns the school's position as internal criticism within a governing communist state, which was unique. Praxis criticised the Soviet system freely, as the regime's own line invited. It then criticised Yugoslav bureaucracy, Yugoslav privilege, and the gap between self-management's theory and its practice, in the same vocabulary and with the same rigour, and thereby crossed the only line that mattered.

### Why was the Korcula summer school more than a conference?

Because for a decade the Adriatic island gathering joined the Yugoslav philosophers to the wider European left, Bloch and Marcuse and Fromm among its visitors, in open argument conducted on the territory of a communist state; it made the school a European institution, gave its younger members a world to belong to, and showed that world's existence to the regime's own students.

The end came by administration rather than by trial, in the Yugoslav manner. After the student movement of 1968 in Belgrade, which the school had sympathised with and the regime had outmanoeuvred, pressure accumulated through the party organisations of the universities; funding for the journal was withdrawn, the summer school was denied its island, and in 1975 eight Belgrade professors associated with the school were suspended from teaching by a special law of the Serbian assembly, removed not for anything the criminal code could name but by legislative designation. The journal ceased in the mid-1970s; an international edition survived briefly abroad. The philosophers responded as the internal critics elsewhere had: Marković and his colleagues continued to write, to publish abroad, to receive the students who sought them out privately, and to embody the proposition that a regime which must silence its own philosophers by statute has conceded the argument it declined to conduct. The Yugoslav difference was real even here, in the manner of the silencing: no Praxis figure was imprisoned for the journal, the suspended professors kept their salaries for a period and their passports eventually, and the school's books remained in the libraries. A difference in the manner of suppression is not a small thing to those who live it. It is also, for the analyst, a difference of degree along the taxonomy this article has already drawn, and the taxonomy's final column in the critics table records the Yugoslav entries in the same flat words as the others.

## A different system, or the same system with better scenery?

The question whether Yugoslavia was different from the rest of the bloc deserves a direct treatment because both camps in the old argument had reasons to exaggerate. The regime's own claim, that self-management constituted a third road between capitalism and the command economy, was a legitimation device and should be discounted as one. The hostile claim, that the whole construction was scenery over an identical dictatorship, fails against the evidence assembled in the preceding sections: the travel, the contested council elections, the strikes, the journals, the consumption, the open border to labour migration. The sober verdict distinguishes the variables. In the organisation of the enterprise and the texture of daily life Yugoslavia differed genuinely and substantially; in the location of final political authority it did not differ at all. One party monopolised the state, commanded the security apparatus, appointed the judiciary's masters, and retained, as 1975 demonstrated, the power to suspend its critics by law whenever their criticism touched the monopoly itself. Yugoslavia therefore functions in this article's argument as the experiment that separates the internal critics' claims. Where the critics charged the apparatus with monopolising disposal inside the economy, Yugoslavia relaxed the monopoly and life visibly changed, which supports the critics' economics. Where the critics charged that the monopoly of political organisation was the system's foundation, Yugoslavia kept the monopoly and reproduced, in milder doses, the system's characteristic responses, which supports the critics' politics. The strongest objection to the critics reads the same experiment in the opposite direction, and its reading will be given in full in its place.

## The reform economists: Sik and the Czechoslovak design

Parallel to the philosophical critics, and mostly unknown to the same readers, ran a second internal tradition: the reform economists, who accepted the system's premises and attempted to repair its mechanism. Their premise was that the command economy's failures, shortage, waste, the suppression of innovation, the falsification of information as it climbed the hierarchy, were not necessary consequences of social ownership but of a particular method of coordination, the physical plan imposed by instruction, which could be replaced by a regulated market operating among socially owned enterprises. The most complete statement of the design came from Czechoslovakia. Ota Šik, director of the Economics Institute of the Czechoslovak Academy and the architect of the New Economic Model adopted in stages from 1965, proposed that enterprises respond to prices and profit within a framework of macroeconomic planning, that monopolies be broken, that managers answer to results rather than to instructions, and, in his later work, that employees hold a stake in their enterprises' capital as a neutral form of ownership between state and private hands.

### What did Ota Sik propose for the Czechoslovak economy?

A regulated market among socially owned firms: prices freed to signal scarcity, enterprise income tied to sales rather than to plan fulfilment, central planning withdrawn to macroeconomic proportions and investment frameworks, and workers' councils with a real share in enterprise decisions, a design he carried into the reform programme of 1968 and defended in exile thereafter.

Šik's design became the economic content of the Prague Spring, and its fate therefore demonstrates the political law the reformers kept rediscovering. A market among enterprises requires that enterprises be allowed to fail and workers to move; it requires prices that tell truths the ministries may not wish to hear; it requires, above all, that the centre surrender the instrument through which it rewards loyalty and punishes independence, the allocation of resources by instruction. The Czechoslovak reform was thus never only an economic programme, and its suppression in 1968 was overdetermined: the invasion's stated grounds were political, but the reform's economics alone dissolved the command structure on which every allied regime's domestic power rested. Šik emigrated, taught in Switzerland, and spent his exile refining the design and reflecting on why its carriers had been unable to defend it, a reflection that led him, unusually among the reformers, to the political conclusion his generation of economists had resisted: that the economic mechanism could not be changed while the political monopoly that the mechanism served remained untouched.

## Hungary's New Economic Mechanism

Hungary attempted the reformer's road without the spring, by licence from above rather than by pressure from below, and the attempt is the bloc's most sustained exercise in market socialism inside a command system. The New Economic Mechanism, introduced in January 1968 under the party leadership of János Kádár and designed by economists among whom Rezső Nyers was central, abolished compulsory plan instructions for most enterprises, freed many prices to respond to supply and demand within regulated bands, tied enterprise income and managerial reward to profit, and opened space for small private and cooperative activity at the economy's margins. The context was the settlement after 1956: Kádár's bargain offered rising consumption and a loosened cultural grip in exchange for political quiet, and the mechanism was the bargain's economic engine. For several years it worked as designed. Hungarian shops filled in a way visitors from allied capitals remarked on, agriculture flourished through a hybrid of cooperatives and household plots, and the phrase goulash communism entered the languages as shorthand for the bloc's most livable arrangement.

### How did Hungary's New Economic Mechanism differ from the Czechoslovak design?

It was granted by the leadership as an instrument of consolidation rather than won by a reform movement, it proceeded by calibrated steps that left the party's commanding posts and the large enterprises' political supervision intact, and it therefore survived its birth, retreating under pressure in the 1970s and advancing again in the 1980s rather than dying in a single night.

The mechanism's rhythm of advance and retreat is the data from which the reform tradition's limits can be read. When the mechanism threatened the interests it touched, the large industrial ministries, the heavy industrial workforce concentrated in giant firms, the allied regimes alarmed at Hungarian prices telling Hungarian truths, the centre reimposed controls, recentralised investment, and protected the great enterprises from the discipline the design prescribed for everyone else. János Kornai, the Hungarian economist whose anatomy of shortage became the reform generation's own self-critique, drew the lesson in terms of the soft budget constraint: an enterprise that expects rescue cannot be disciplined by prices, and rescue is what a party-state owes its own creations. Kornai's later work supplies the objection that most threatens the internal critique with its central mechanism, and it is a mark of the Hungarian tradition's quality that the objection's best formulator was himself a product of the reform milieu, formed at the economics daily and the Academy in Budapest, arguing from inside the same world as the critics he would eventually answer.

## Polish reform thought: Lange, Kalecki, Brus

Poland's contribution to the reform tradition was the most theoretically distinguished and the least implemented, a combination the country's history made almost structural. Oskar Lange, who had answered the Austrian calculation objection in the 1930s with the proposal that a planning board simulate a market by trial and error, returned from American exile to advise the Polish October of 1956 and spent his last years on the cybernetics of planning, imagining the computer as the market's successor. Michał Kalecki, whose economics of employment and growth had anticipated and then surpassed much of the Keynesian canon, advised the Polish government after 1956, resigned in protest at the misuse of his counsel, and developed in his late essays a theory of the political business cycle under socialism and of the intermediate regimes of the developing world that younger economists across three continents read as a master key. Włodzimierz Brus, a professor at Warsaw and a veteran of the party's own ideological apparatus, published in 1961 the book that became the reform tradition's Polish charter, arguing that a decentralised model with a regulated market was not a retreat from socialism but the only form in which social ownership could function without the command system's waste and falsification.

### What did Wlodzimierz Brus argue about the market in a socialist economy?

That central allocation by instruction necessarily wasted resources and falsified information, that socially owned enterprises therefore needed genuine market relations among themselves with prices formed by exchange, and that the plan should govern through economic instruments rather than commands, a position he later extended, in exile, into the claim that marketisation without political pluralism would stall.

Brus's own biography closes the Polish argument. Caught in the purge of 1968, dismissed from his chair, he emigrated in 1972 to Britain and a chair at Oxford, where his work completed the trajectory the whole reform tradition was travelling: from the mechanism to the polity. In his later writings with Kazimierz Łaski he argued that the market reforms kept failing at the same frontier, the point at which economic decentralisation required an independent judiciary, a free press to publish the prices' bad news, and associations able to defend the new arrangements against the apparatus that sponsored them, at which point the reform ceased to be a mechanism and became a constitution. The reform economists had begun by promising the regimes a way to keep power and gain efficiency. They ended, the honest ones, by demonstrating to their own satisfaction that the efficiency and the monopoly were alternatives. That demonstration, reached by technicians rather than by philosophers, is among the internal critique's strongest corroborations, and it was reached, like the rest of the critique, at the price of the demonstrators' careers at home.

## Why the reforms stalled where they stalled

Draw the three national cases together and the stall has a common anatomy, which the participants themselves diagnosed with increasing clarity. A reform that frees prices must let some prices rise, and a regime whose legitimacy rests on stable prices for necessities experiences each rise as a political event, as the Polish leadership learned in 1970 and 1976 and 1980 when price announcements detonated the streets. A reform that disciplines enterprises must let the undisciplined fail, and a regime that has promised full employment as socialism's proof experiences each closure as a confession. A reform that rewards profit must tolerate visible inequality between enterprises and regions, and a regime that legitimises itself as equality's guardian must either disown the inequality or disown the reform. Above all, a reform that replaces instruction with instruments deprives the apparatus of its daily substance, the thousand decisions through which loyalty is purchased and dependence renewed, so that the apparatus as an order of men has a material interest in the reform's failure quite apart from doctrine. The reforms therefore oscillated: introduced under the pressure of stagnation, extended until they touched the monopoly's interests, retracted under the pressure of the interests, and resumed when stagnation deepened, a cycle visible in Hungary across two decades and in Poland across three. The economists of the bloc had a phrase for the resting point of the cycle, neither plan nor market, and the phrase names the condition in which several of these economies spent their last decades, too reformed to command and too commanded to coordinate.

## Everyday life inside the systems

The internal critique was written by intellectuals, but its evidence was the daily experience of millions, and an article on these societies owes that experience a section of its own. The texture of life in the late bloc is captured in a cluster of institutions no plan ever announced. There was the queue, not an occasional inconvenience but a standing allocation mechanism that converted money into time and distributed goods to those with hours to spend and information about deliveries. There was the second economy, the vast penumbra of moonlighting, private plots, unlicensed repairmen, and favours traded through networks of acquaintance, which supplied much of what the official economy promised and which every citizen navigated daily, so that the system's subjects lived in two economies at once and knew the official one to be, in part, a theatre. There was the special provision for the apparatus itself, the closed shops and clinics and holiday facilities whose existence was universally known and officially unmentionable, the standing refutation, at the level of the shopping basket, of the doctrine of equality. And there was the peculiar moral atmosphere the dissident literature named with precision: the greengrocer who places the slogan in his window not from belief but from prudence, in Václav Havel's famous parable of 1978, performing obedience in exchange for being left alone, and thereby becoming, in Havel's analysis, both victim and pillar of the order that humiliated him.

Havel was not a Marxist, and his presence in this article requires a sentence of justification, which the taxonomy of the internal critique supplies. By the 1970s the Marxist critics inside the bloc no longer stood alone; around them had formed a broader dissent that spoke the language of rights, legality, and living in truth rather than the language of class and surplus. Charter 77, the Czechoslovak initiative of January 1977 that took the regime's own signature on international human rights covenants as its charter and asked only that the laws be obeyed, gathered ex-communist reformers of 1968, Catholics, Protestants, and independent socialists into a single civic front. The Marxist critics and the rights dissidents disagreed about foundations and about remedies, sometimes sharply; Kołakowski's trajectory from Warsaw Marxism to a sceptical liberalism marks one pole of the disagreement. What they shared was the predicament this article has documented and a common finding about it: that the system's first demand of its subjects was not belief but performance, and that the refusal to perform, by a philosopher declining to affirm a resolution or a worker declining to applaud, was the elementary act from which every larger opposition grew.

### What did Charter 77 ask for, and why does it sit beside the Marxist critics in this account?

It asked that the Czechoslovak state honour the rights its own constitution and its signature on the Helsinki accords already proclaimed, no more and no less. It sits beside the Marxist critics because both camps had arrived, by different roads, at the same discovery: that the gap between the order's professions and its practice was the order's defining feature.

## Work, women, and the nations inside the bloc

Three further dimensions of the lived record belong in any honest survey, each of them a field in which the internal critics and the broader dissent found material the official doctrine could not process. The first is work itself. The systems guaranteed employment and abolished the fear of destitution that disciplines labour under capitalism, achievements their subjects valued and remembered; the same guarantee, joined to chronic shortage of labour and the impossibility of dismissal, dissolved the connection between effort and reward inside the enterprise, producing the tacit bargain the era's jokes summarised and its sociologists documented, in which the state pretended to pay and the workers pretended to work, and in which overstaffed plants hoarded labour against the plan's storms while consumers queued for want of hands in the services nobody planned. The second dimension is the position of women, whom the systems mobilised into employment at rates the West did not match, educated in large numbers, and supplied with childcare and maternity provision of real substance, while leaving the domestic division of labour essentially untouched and the commanding heights of every institution in male hands, so that the bloc's characteristic female figure became the exhausted professional carrying the double burden the feminist critics of the region, writing mostly in samizdat or abroad, named as the system's unkept promise in its most intimate form. The third dimension is nationality. The bloc's states were formally committed to the friendship of peoples and practically organised as national states in an alliance that froze every border question by decree; beneath the freeze the national grievances accumulated, Slovak against Czech inside the federation, Hungarian communities across the Romanian and Slovak borders, the German question suspended over the continent's centre, and the whole structure held together by the alliance's guarantee that no grievance would be permitted a political form. When the guarantee lapsed in 1989 the grievances were the readiest material to hand, which is part of the explanation for what followed the bloc and a reason this article, whose subject ends in 1989, records their accumulation without following them further.

## The environment as an internal witness

The series has met the pattern of this article before, in the environmental record of the socialist states, and the parallel is worth one paragraph because it corroborates the internal critique from an unexpected direction. The bloc's environmental degradation, the poisoned rivers of the chemical districts, the forests dying under the emissions of lignite power stations, the great lake and sea catastrophes of the Soviet south, was documented most precisely not by foreign observers but by scientists and citizens inside the systems, working through the few legal openings the order allowed: academies, nature protection societies, the occasional journal permitted an ecological brief. Their findings were obstructed, delayed, and classified in the familiar pattern, because a system that has abolished the conflict of interests in principle cannot process evidence that its own decisions are damaging its own citizens in fact. The environmental case matters here because it removes the suspicion that the internal critique was merely the ideology of disappointed intellectuals. The chemists measuring the Elbe and the hydrologists measuring the Aral Sea were not competing for power and had no programme to sell. They were measuring, and the apparatus treated measurement as opposition for the same reason it treated dialectics as opposition: the monopoly claimed jurisdiction over the description of reality, and every independent description, of a river or of a class structure, was a small secession from that jurisdiction.

## Solidarity: the critique enacted by a class

The Polish union of 1980 deserves treatment as more than the last of the risings, because it was the internal critique translated into an organisation of millions. The Gdańsk agreement's twenty one demands joined the classic liberties, free unions independent of the party and the employers, the right to strike, freedom of expression and publication, access of the church to the media, to demands drawn straight from the critics' analysis: that the economy be reformed, that privilege in allocation be abolished, that the apparatus's special shops and clinics be closed, that appointments be made by qualification rather than by party card. The union's advisers included veterans of the open letter's generation; its chaplains came from the church the regime had failed to break; its membership reached deep into the ruling party's own membership, so that for sixteen months Poland contained the spectacle, unique in the bloc's history, of a ruling party negotiating with an independent organisation of the class it claimed to be. Martial law in December 1981 interned the union's leadership and drove the organisation underground, where it survived on the infrastructure the defence committees had pioneered: printing, distribution, funds, and the habit of trust. The underground decade is the bridge between the internal critique and 1989, because the men and women who emerged in 1989 to negotiate the bloc's first peaceful transfer had been educated in it, and the round table at which they sat was, among other things, the internal critique finally granted a room and a microphone instead of a cell.

## The long stagnation and the turn in Moscow

The bloc's last decade opened with the Polish crisis suppressed and closed with the whole structure gone, and the interval is best understood as a stagnation that every participant could feel and none could remedy. The economies had exhausted the extensive model, growth by adding labour and capital to known technologies, and could not cross to the intensive model, growth by innovation and efficiency, because innovation requires three freedoms, to fail, to inform, and to displace, that the monopoly could not concede. The debts contracted in the 1970s to purchase calm came due in a harder currency of austerity. The generation that had built the systems died in office, and their successors inherited the rituals without the memories that had once made the rituals plausible. The decisive change came in Moscow, where a new leadership from 1985 attempted to revive the Soviet system by loosening it, proclaiming openness and restructuring, and, most consequentially for Europe, renouncing the doctrine under which allied armies had guaranteed every allied regime. The renunciation was communicated privately before it was understood publicly, and its meaning was tested capital by capital. The companion article on [soviet collapse marxist explanations](/2019/05/01/soviet-collapse-marxist-explanations/) examines the explanations of the Soviet ending itself; here the point is the European consequence. Every regime in the bloc had rested, in the last instance, on a pledge of rescue it did not control. When the guarantee was withdrawn, the regimes discovered, one after another, how much of their authority had been borrowed, and the internal critics, who had argued for decades that the apparatus ruled without the society's consent, watched the proposition submitted to experimental test.

## The sequence of the ending

The ending, when it came, moved through the region in an order that inverted the bloc's hierarchy of strength. Poland went first and slowest, by negotiation: round table talks in the spring, elections in June in which the opposition won every seat it was permitted to contest, and by late summer a government led by the opposition's nominee, the first in the bloc, formed while the old party still held the presidency and the ministries of force. Hungary followed by internal dissolution, its reform communists dismantling the party's monopoly from inside, opening the border fence in the spring and thereby opening the hole through which East Germans poured westward all summer. East Germany, the bloc's hardest state, collapsed by exit and by street together: the exodus through Hungary and the embassies, the Monday demonstrations in Leipzig growing week by week under the slogan that the people were present, the leadership's paralysis in the face of a guarantor who would not shoot, and on a November evening a spokesman misspoke at a press conference and the Berlin Wall, the system's most concrete sentence, was opened by the crowd before midnight. Czechoslovakia's turn came in November, ten days of demonstrations and a general strike converting the theatre of the Velvet Revolution into a government. Bulgaria's leadership changed itself in a palace adjustment that the street then overtook. Romania alone ended in blood, its ruler having built the bloc's most personal and least institutional regime, and the exception confirms the analysis: where no institution existed through which an ending could be negotiated, the ending took the form of an explosion.

### What made the ending move at such speed once it began?

Each regime's fall withdrew the others' remaining assets: the demonstration that negotiation was survivable, the spectacle of crowds unshot, the guarantor's visible indifference, the border opened by a neighbour. Authority in the bloc had been partly a collective performance sustained by the belief that it was permanent, and performances collapse at the speed of belief rather than at the speed of force.

Two features of the sequence matter for this article's argument, and both are frequently misread. The crowds of 1989 did not, in the main, march under the banners of the internal Marxist critics, nor did they demand the critics' programmes of councils and self-management; they demanded, variously, honest elections, open borders, the end of the party's monopoly, and a normal life, a phrase whose content was deliberately unspecified. The internal critique had supplied the diagnosis that made the regimes indefensible in argument, and the dissidents of the rights tradition supplied much of the organisation; but the ending itself was a rejection of the existing arrangements rather than an endorsement of any doctrine, a distinction the article's closing inventory of misreadings insists on because the alternative reading, that 1989 was a referendum won by one economic doctrine over another, flatters the winners and falsifies the crowds. The second feature is the conduct of the apparatuses themselves. With the exception of Romania, the men who commanded the police and the armies of the bloc declined, at the decisive moments, to use them at the scale their predecessors had used against the risings. Explanations differ: the guarantor's example, the elites' own loss of belief, the calculation that a negotiated exit preserved more than a defended wall. For the internal critique the fact itself is the datum. A ruling stratum that will not defend its order when challenged has delivered its own verdict on the order's title to rule, and the critics who had spent decades arguing that the title was fictitious could be forgiven for hearing, in the quiet of 1989, the sound of a very large concession.

## The strongest objection, stated at full strength

Every serious tradition must be met by its strongest opposition, and the internal critique's strongest opposition is not the official apologetics it dismantled but an objection developed by critics of the systems who shared much of the critics' own evidence and rejected their hope. The objection, in the form given it by János Kornai's anatomy of the classical socialist system and echoed by historians of the reform era, runs as follows. The internal Marxist critics were arguing for a better version of a system that had no better version. The concentration of economic and political authority in a single apparatus, which the critics treated as a deformation that a truer socialism would correct, was not a deformation at all but the system's operating principle, the feature that made the whole machine function: the plan required a commander, the commander required a monopoly, the monopoly required the suppression of independent organisation, and each element the critics wished to remove was load bearing for the elements they wished to keep. A socialism of councils, free expression, and market signals among autonomous producers was therefore not a reformed version of the thing that existed but a different thing that had never existed, and the proof is in the record the critics themselves cite. Their programmes were never implemented anywhere, and they were never implemented not because wicked men blocked them but because they could not be: every attempt to install a component of the programme, Hungarian prices, Czechoslovak enterprise autonomy, Yugoslav councils, either was absorbed and neutralised by the surrounding monopoly or began dissolving the monopoly and was therefore terminated by it, from within or from without. On this reading the internal critics were the system's finest products and its most poignant illusion, diagnosticians of genius whose prescriptions described a country that the laws of institutional motion do not permit, and the kindest verdict on their tradition is that it served as the bloc's conscience while the bloc's logic ran on without it.

The objection gains strength from the biographies in the critics table, read unsentimentally. Bahro's league of communists remained a phrase. The open letter's republic of councils was never attempted by its authors when, decades later, one of them held ministerial office in a free Poland and administered a market transition instead. The Budapest School's dictatorship over needs yielded no institutional design at all, only an ever finer description of the cage. Even the Yugoslav experiment, the objection continues, proves the rule rather than the exception, for self-management survived precisely by being confined to the enterprise while the monopoly stood guard over everything that mattered, and the moment the federation's politics opened, the system did not democratise into associated producers but disintegrated along the national seams its designers had frozen. The critics, on this account, mistook a system's essence for its betrayal because they needed the essence to be noble; a colder analysis would have begun from the premise that the concentration they deplored was the system working as designed, and that the choice the century actually offered was never between the apparatus and the councils but between the apparatus and its dissolution.

## The Yugoslav answer, and what must be conceded

The answer available to the internal critique, and the one this article gives, begins with the Yugoslav case because it is the objection's hardest fact. A programme drawn from the critics' own shelf, workers councils disposing of socially owned enterprises, was not merely drafted but enacted, in a European state, for four decades, and the results are a matter of record rather than of speculation. Life under self-management differed measurably from life under the command model in the respects the critics predicted: the enterprise's insiders held real rights, consumption and travel and argument were freer, the economy's information flowed through prices that bore some relation to scarcity, and the system's characteristic pathologies were correspondingly different, group egoism and regional divergence rather than universal shortage and universal pretence. The objection's assertion that no programme from the critics' shelf could ever have been put into practice is therefore, taken literally, false. One of them was implemented, at scale, and the sky did not fall; what happened was more interesting than either camp's prophecy, a functioning order with its own virtues and its own characteristic diseases, which is what usually happens when a programme meets the world.

The concession that honesty then requires must be stated without varnish, because the internal critique's credibility rests on the same standard it applied to the regimes. No critic's programme was implemented at scale as a whole. The Yugoslav councils operated inside a party monopoly that the Praxis philosophers, the experiment's own internal critics, identified as the limit that falsified the experiment's promise, and no society in the bloc or outside it ever combined council control of production with free political organisation and a free press, the combination the open letter demanded and the Prague programme approached. And the objection's central claim, that the concentration of authority was functional rather than accidental, stands unanswered in the form in which Kornai stated it. The critics showed that the concentration existed, that it contradicted the doctrine, and that it corrupted everything it touched; they did not show, because the century did not show, an institutional design in which social ownership at national scale coexisted durably with the dispersal of the authority the concentration supplied. The separation the student must learn, between implementing a programme and refuting an analysis, governs the final verdict. The objection refutes, or gravely burdens, the critics' programmes. It does not touch their analyses, which the regimes' own conduct kept confirming; and a tradition can be right about what a system is and wrong about what can replace it, a combination the twentieth century made familiar in more doctrines than this one.

## The untranslated shelf

A reader who wishes to pursue the internal critics in English will discover the imbalance that explains why this tradition remains half known, and the discovery is itself part of the article's subject. Much of the central literature is available: Bahro's book appeared in English within a year; the open letter of Kuroń and Modzelewski circulated in translation from the 1960s; the Budapest School's major volume was published in English by its exiled authors; Djilas was a bestseller; Kołakowski's histories are standard. The Yugoslav school is partly served, with Marković and Petrović available in translation and the journal's international edition held by research libraries, though the full run of Praxis in its Croatian edition, and the Korčula proceedings, reward only readers of the language. Below that summit the shelf thins sharply. Kosík's essays after the great book, the Czech discussion of the 1960s in the literary journals, the Polish revisionist literature of 1956 to 1968 in its periodicals, the Hungarian sociological work on the second economy, the East German samizdat of the 1980s, exist in English in fragments, in anthologies, or not at all, and the Romanian and Bulgarian records are thinner still. The imbalance is not a conspiracy; it is the ordinary economics of translation acting on literatures whose home presses were forbidden to export them. Its effect, however, is structural: the Anglophone picture of Eastern Europe was formed disproportionately by the critics who wrote for export or arrived in exile, and the internal critique's full weight, its journals and seminars and local controversies, remains legible chiefly to those who read the region's languages. Students should hold that caution over everything written in English on the subject, this article included.

## Comparing the incomparable: the basis of judgement

Where this article has compared the bloc's societies with Western Europe it has tried to observe the discipline the comparison requires, and the discipline should be made explicit because its violation is the commonest defect in the literature. The eastern states were, with the partial exceptions of Czechoslovakia and East Germany, substantially poorer in 1945 than the western states they are habitually measured against; several had been battlefields and all had been looted or bled by the war and the occupation that preceded the bloc's formation. A comparison that ignores the starting point is not a comparison but a verdict in disguise, and the rule applies in both directions: the regimes' own propaganda compared their achievements with the misery of their prewar pasts, chosen as the baseline that flattered them most, while hostile accounts compared them with the richest societies on earth at the century's end. The honest comparisons are narrower and harder. Compare each society with its own plausible alternatives at its own starting point; compare matched pairs where history supplied them; and state, for every verdict, which countries, at which dates, from what level of prior development, are being weighed.

### What does the divided German case control for in comparisons of the two systems?

Language, culture, prewar development, and national institutions were held roughly constant by the division of one country into two states in 1949, so that the divergence of the two Germanies across forty years isolates the effect of the systems themselves more cleanly than any other comparison the century offers, which is why both sides argued about it so bitterly.

The German control is not perfect, and the imperfections should be recorded with the same care: the eastern state paid reparations the western did not, lost a larger share of its industrial plant to dismantling, and was excluded from the Marshall aid that primed the western recovery, while enjoying, in compensation, the captive markets of the bloc. Even discounted for all of that, the comparison retains its force because the direction of human movement across the border, sustained for decades and ended only by a wall built to stop it, constitutes a revealed preference of a population with full information about both systems, and no statistical dispute has ever dissolved that datum. For the economic outcomes themselves, the series article on the [socialist states economic record](/2019/06/15/socialist-states-economic-record/) assembles the measurement controversies, the rival national accounting systems, and the independent reconstructions, and the reader who wants the numbers argued rather than asserted should go there; this article has observed the figures protocol appropriate to its subject and stated no output or living standard figures of its own.

## The model behind the systems, and the charge the critics pressed

Two of the article's required connections can be discharged together, because the internal critics themselves joined them. The systems of the bloc were built on the Soviet model, whose codification as doctrine is the subject of the series guide to [soviet marxism complete guide](/2019/03/01/soviet-marxism-complete-guide/): the party's leading role, democratic centralism inside it, the plan, the monopoly of organisation, all arrived in Eastern Europe as a package whose blueprints had been drawn in Moscow between the wars. The critics' originality was not to reject the package from outside but to show that the package, faithfully installed, generated the apparatus as its necessary manager, and that the model's own theory of itself, in which the party embodies the producers, had no procedure for the case in which the embodiment and the producers disagreed. That is the point at which the internal critique joins the broader argument about democracy inside the Marxist tradition, which the series treats in [marxism and democracy critique](/2013/03/15/marxism-and-democracy-critique/): the critics were, nearly all of them, democrats of a specific kind, holding that the socialisation of production without the democratisation of power was a contradiction that would resolve itself in favour of power, as it did. The reform economists' question, whether coordination by market among social owners could out-perform the plan, is taken up with the models and the evidence in [can planning beat the market](/2019/10/15/can-planning-beat-the-market/), where the Hungarian and Czechoslovak designs reappear as test cases rather than as history.

## Studying the internal critics

The critics table repays being used as an instrument rather than merely read. Students who copy it into a structured notebook, one row per critic with the analysis, the year, and the consequence, and who then add the rows this article could not include, acquire the map of the whole subject in an afternoon; the study notes companion at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is built for exactly that exercise and holds the table in a form designed for revision. Candidates preparing for examinations in which this cluster appears will find the practice questions and the previous years' papers organised by topic at [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html), where the Eastern European material can be drilled against the questions examiners have actually set on this material. The habit that both tools exist to serve is the one the internal critics themselves practised: return to the primary text, state its argument in its own terms, and only then judge it.

## Precursors: the critique before the bloc

The internal critique did not begin in 1945, and its European authors knew their ancestry. In the 1930s, from exile, Leon Trotsky had analysed the Soviet Union in The Revolution Betrayed as a degenerated workers state: the socialised property forms survived, he argued, while a parasitic bureaucracy had usurped political power, and the remedy was not the restoration of capitalism but a political revolution of the workers against the caste that ruled them. Victor Serge, who had served the revolution and been expelled by it, wrote the same anatomy from inside the experience of the camps and the opposition. Christian Rakovsky, an old Bolshevik writing from exile in 1928, had already named the mechanism with a precision the later critics inherited: the professional functionaries of party and state, he argued, were becoming a distinct social stratum whose interests diverged from those of the class in whose name they administered. These texts circulated in the bloc, when they circulated at all, in the original languages of repression: seized in searches, quoted in indictments, known by reputation to men and women who had never held them. Their importance for this article is genealogical. When Kuroń and Modzelewski computed the bureaucracy as a class, when Bahro anatomised the apparatus, when the Budapest philosophers described a formation that was neither capitalism restored nor socialism achieved, they were extending an argument the movement had been having with itself since the 1920s, and the regimes that punished them were punishing, among other things, the memory of that argument. The official doctrine handled the ancestry by deletion: the precursors were unpersons or fascist agents in the textbooks, and a student in Warsaw or Prague could complete a degree in Marxism-Leninism without learning that the tradition contained a critique of the bureaucracy older than the bureaucracy's own European empire.

The ancestry also explains a feature of the internal critique that puzzles readers who meet it fresh: its confidence that the categories of the doctrine, rigorously applied, were sufficient to condemn the systems that claimed the doctrine's authority. The critics did not borrow their instruments from liberalism because they did not need to. Class analysis applied to the apparatus, the theory of alienation applied to administered life, the withering away of the state applied to a state that grew for forty years, the criterion of associated producers applied to producers who associated only by permission: the founding texts, read without the official commentary, supplied an indictment on every page. This is why the regimes' censorship lists always included, alongside the critics' own works, unexpurgated editions of the classics and the early writings of the founders, and why samizdat in the bloc so often took the form not of new doctrine but of old texts, retyped. The internal critique was, in one of its aspects, simply the tradition's memory refusing to be administered.

## The thaw and the secret speech in Europe

The death of Stalin in 1953 and the secret speech of 1956, in which his successor denounced his crimes to a closed session of the Soviet party, detonated differently in each European capital, and the detonations set the pattern for everything that followed. In Poland and Hungary the speech licensed a reckoning that escaped its authors' control within months, ending in the Polish October and the Hungarian revolution. In Czechoslovakia and East Germany the leaderships contained the speech, read it to cadres in controlled form, and changed as little as they could, storing the reckoning for 1968 and 1989 respectively. In the parties' intellectual strata the speech had a subtler effect that outlasted the crises. A generation of Marxist intellectuals, the revisionists as the apparatuses learned to call them, concluded that if the crimes had been possible within the doctrine's house, the house required rebuilding from its foundations: the young Marx against the catechism, humanism against the dialectics of nature, the critique of bureaucracy against the apology for the plan. Leszek Kołakowski in Warsaw, the Praxis circle in Zagreb, the Budapest seminars around Lukács, the Prague philosophers around Kosík, were all, in their different idioms, children of the thaw, formed by the discovery that the system's own supreme authority had confessed to a fraction of what its critics alleged. The regimes spent the following decades trying to withdraw the licence the speech had issued, and the internal critique can be dated, as a continuous tradition, from the moment the licence proved irrevocable.

## Leszek Kolakowski: from revisionist to coroner

The most consequential single career among the internal critics is the Polish philosopher's, because it traversed the whole arc from belief to burial and documented each stage. Leszek Kołakowski began as a brilliant young orthodox scholar, turned in the thaw to a revisionist Marxism of humanist and existential colouring, and wrote in 1959 the essay that gave the era its emblem, the priest and the jester: the priest guards the absolute, the jester doubts it in its own house, and a culture needs the jester's office even, especially, inside a church. His lectures at Warsaw drew the generation of 1968; the regime expelled him from the party in 1966 for an address that measured the system's decade against its promises, dismissed him from the university in 1968 amid the antisemitic campaign, and drove him into the exile from which he composed Main Currents of Marxism, the three volume history that concluded that the doctrine's utopian core and its totalitarian issue were joined at the root. The later Kołakowski is often recruited against the earlier, as if the coroner refuted the jester. The record supports a more interesting reading. His trajectory is the internal critique's own logic pursued without stopping: each stage's questions, asked honestly inside the tradition, generated the next stage's departure from it, and the regime's punishments marked the stages like milestones. Poland expelled its greatest living philosopher for the crime of continuing to think in public, and the expulsion, not any Western review, remains the authoritative assessment of what his thinking was worth to the system that had educated him.

## Adam Schaff and the revisionism of the thaw

Adam Schaff, the Polish party's chief philosopher in the Stalin years and a member of its central committee, illustrates a quieter variant of the internal turn: the apparatchik who educated himself out of the catechism he had administered. Schaff's post-thaw work on alienation argued, against the official line that alienation was a capitalist survival, that it flourished under socialist administration wherever the individual confronted the state and the plan as alien powers, and his later writings on the individual and on the communist movement's future moved steadily toward a socialism of civil liberties he was never permitted to build. He was marginalised in 1968, retained a tolerated eminence, and ended as a critic of both the old order and the manner of its successor's construction. His case matters to the taxonomy because it shows the critique arising not only among the persecuted but inside the apparatus's own philosophical general staff, among men whose loyalty was not in question and whose doubts were therefore unanswerable by the standard charge of hostile intent. The system's philosophical establishment, in Warsaw and Prague and Budapest alike, kept producing its own gravediggers out of its best students, which is what a monopoly of doctrine, rigorously taught, will do in any century: the catechism contained the questions, and the ablest pupils asked them.

## Ernst Bloch and the Leipzig experiment

East Germany's philosophical exception was Ernst Bloch, the philosopher of hope, who accepted a chair at Leipzig in 1949 as a returning exile and attempted to teach Marxism as an open, speculative, unfinished philosophy inside the bloc's most finished state. His seminars became a refuge for students who wanted the doctrine with its questions left in; his journal published discussion the party press would not; and his assistants, among them the young Wolfgang Harich, carried his influence into the party's own institutions. The regime tolerated him while his prestige abroad was useful and moved against his school after 1956, forcing his institute into line and his pupils into prison or silence. Bloch, visiting the West when the Berlin Wall was built in 1961, did not return, accepting a chair at Tübingen and a second career as the Federal Republic's philosopher of utopia. The episode is a parable of the bloc's intellectual economy. A state that had imported one of the century's original Marxist thinkers, and given him a university, could find no use for him except as an ornament, and preferred the ornament abroad to the thinker at home. Leipzig kept the building; Tübingen got the seminars; and the students who had crossed the republic to hear him carried the memory of an unadministered Marxism through the decades of the administered kind, a deposit on which the East German opposition of the 1980s would eventually draw.

## The party as an institution: nomenklatura and the ladder

The internal critics' analyses converge on an institution that deserves description in its own right, since it was the mechanism by which the apparatus reproduced itself. The nomenklatura was the list of posts, in the state, the economy, the universities, the press, the unions, and the cultural institutions, whose occupants were appointed or confirmed by the corresponding level of the party, together with the list of persons eligible to fill them. Through the nomenklatura the party's monopoly ceased to be a slogan and became a personnel system: every directorship, editorship, professorship, and managership in the society was a rung on a single ladder, and advancement on the ladder depended on the confidence of the rung above. The system's subjects understood the ladder intimately, since it governed their own biographies, and the critics' contribution was to name its sociological meaning. A society in which all significant positions are allocated by one hierarchy, and in which the hierarchy recruits by loyalty tested over time, will generate a stratum with a common interest quite distinct from any doctrine it recites: the interest in the ladder itself, in the posts, the access, the special provision, and the immunity that the ladder confers. Djilas called the stratum a class, others a caste or an estate, and the terminological dispute among the critics, though real, should not obscure the shared finding: the ladder was the constitution that mattered, and the written constitutions, with their rights and their councils, were the constitution that did not.

The ladder also explains the texture of the bloc's intellectual life, which the internal critics inhabited and described. A philosopher's book required a publisher, whose director sat on the ladder; a journal's editor, a university's rector, a translator's commission, a paper allocation, all were rungs or favours of rungs. Censorship in such a system is not primarily a man with a stamp, though the bloc had those too, organised in offices whose very names were state secrets in the Polish case. It is the anticipatory prudence of every rung-holder who knows that a mistake costs the rung, so that the system's characteristic product is not the banned book but the unwritten one, not the closed journal but the article its editor never commissioned. The internal critics are the exceptions who wrote anyway, and their exceptional status is part of the evidence: a system confident of its intellectual title does not make its ablest minds choose between silence and ruin as a routine condition of employment.

## The show trials: terror as the founding instrument in Europe

The bloc's founding decade included an instrument imported with the model and applied in each capital in turn: the political trial of the movement's own leaders, staged to demonstrate that the enemy had penetrated the summit and that vigilance justified the apparatus's growth. László Rajk, Hungary's former interior minister, was tried in Budapest in 1949 on charges of conspiring with Yugoslav and Western intelligence and was executed; Traicho Kostov in Bulgaria and Rudolf Slánský, the general secretary of the Czechoslovak party, followed in trials whose scripts were supervised from Moscow and whose defendants confessed to careers of treason that had somehow escaped the notice of the security organs they themselves had directed. The trials' European function was disciplinary in a precise sense. They taught every cadre in the bloc that no biography protected its holder, that yesterday's prosecutor was tomorrow's accused, and that survival lay in the demonstration of loyalty at exactly the pitch the centre demanded. They also taught the intellectuals who would become the internal critics a lesson they never forgot: that the apparatus, when it required a victim, manufactured the evidence from the same materials as its history books. Several of the thaw's reckonings began with the rehabilitation of the trial victims, and the Hungarian revolution's first public act was the reburial of Rajk before a vast silent crowd, a ceremony at which the system's subjects understood themselves to be burying more than a man. The record of the trials is stated here without figures, in accordance with this article's protocol, and without rhetorical heightening, in accordance with its subject's deserving better than heightening.

## The churches: an institution the template could not digest

Everywhere in the bloc the template met the churches, and everywhere the meeting produced a long war of attrition whose outcome differed by country in ways that shaped the opposition's later form. The general strategy was uniform: seize the schools, dissolve the orders, tax and infiltrate the hierarchies, imprison the recalcitrant, Cardinal Mindszenty in Hungary tried and sentenced in 1949 in the founding terror's ecclesiastical counterpart, and sponsor compliant clergy as a counterfeit of loyalty. In most of the region the strategy succeeded in reducing the churches to administered survivals. In Poland it failed, and the failure is one of the hinges of the century. The Polish church under Cardinal Wyszyński, imprisoned in the 1950s and released in the October of 1956, retained its parishes, its seminaries, its primate's authority, and the allegiance of a peasantry and a working class for whom the faith and the nation were braided; it negotiated with the regime from strength, sheltered the culture the regime censored, and in 1978 supplied the conclave with the first Polish pope, whose pilgrimage to his homeland the following year assembled crowds whose size taught the watching opposition the arithmetic of its own potential. The Polish opposition's distinctive character, its fusion of workers, intellectuals, and church, its discipline of nonviolence learned partly in the parishes, and its survival through martial law in networks the church's buildings sheltered, cannot be explained without this institutional exception. The internal Marxist critics, many of them atheists of conviction, learned to work beside an institution their doctrine had scheduled for extinction, and the alliance revised both parties: the critics acquired a respect for institutions independent of the state that their tradition had lacked, and the church acquired, in its own house, a generation of lay intellectuals formed by the encounter.

## Wolf Biermann and the cultural dissent of the later GDR

The East German state's cultural history offers the internal critique in its most intimate register, because the GDR, alone in the bloc, faced a linguistic and cultural alternative of itself across a border its citizens could watch on television. Wolf Biermann, a singer and poet who had emigrated from the West as a convinced young communist, son of a communist worker murdered by the Nazi regime, became through the 1960s the state's most beloved and most forbidden performer: banned from public appearance after 1965 for songs that measured the republic against its promises, published and celebrated in the West, tolerated as a safety valve and a reproach. In 1976, permitted a concert tour in the West, he was stripped of his citizenship in absentia, and the expatriation detonated the East German cultural world: the country's leading writers, including figures of impeccable loyalty, signed a protest that began the exodus of the GDR's literary intelligence to the West and the formation, among those who stayed, of a dissent that gathered in the 1980s under the roofs of the Protestant churches, the one institution the state had failed to fully digest. The peace circles, the environmental libraries, the blues masses and the Monday prayers at Leipzig's Nikolaikirche, from which the demonstrations of 1989 directly descended, were the GDR's version of the Polish pattern in miniature: an independent institution sheltering the society the party claimed to be. Biermann's songs, meanwhile, had done what songs do and memoranda cannot: they had given the internal critique a vernacular, so that the distance between the promise and the republic could be hummed.

## The exceptions that map the rule: Romania, Bulgaria, Albania

The map of the internal critique has blank spaces, and the blanks are data. Romania under Nicolae Ceaușescu developed the bloc's most personalist regime, a national communism that combined defiance of Moscow abroad with a suffocating dynastic control at home, and produced a correspondingly thin internal literature: the novelist Paul Goma, expelled in 1977 after attempting a Romanian Charter, the poet Ana Blandiana, harassed and banned in episodes, a scattering of letters and protests rather than a tradition. The thinness is explained by the regime's method, which atomised more completely than its neighbours and fused the security apparatus with the national myth so that criticism could be processed as treason to the nation and not merely to the party. Bulgaria, the bloc's most loyal member, produced almost no internal critique of standing until the very end, when ecological protest over the poisoned city of Ruse supplied the first independent organisation; the Bulgarian case demonstrates that the critique's flourishing required not only grievances, which were universal, but institutions and memories, a university tradition, a thaw generation, a church or a journal, from which critics could be formed. Albania, which left the Soviet alliance in the 1960s for the Chinese connection and then left that too for an autarkic isolation unique in Europe, sealed its borders against the very possibility of an internal literature reaching any reader, and its writers' fates, imprisonment and execution among them, belong to the darkest page of the region's record. The three cases discipline the article's central claim rather than embarrass it. The internal critique flourished where the systems had educated most and repressed with some admixture of hesitation; where repression was total and education servile, the critique was strangled in the cradle, which is a fact about the conditions of thought and not about the truth of the thoughts that elsewhere survived.

## The Helsinki lever: law as the opposition's instrument

In 1975 the bloc's governments signed the Helsinki Final Act, accepting, in exchange for the West's recognition of Europe's postwar borders, a set of undertakings on human rights and the free movement of information that the signatories regarded as decorative. The internal opposition of the following decade treated the undertakings as a lever, and the lever moved more than the decorations' authors expected. Charter 77 grounded itself explicitly in the covenants and in Helsinki; the Moscow and Warsaw Helsinki groups documented violations in the juridical language the regimes had themselves subscribed; the Polish defence committee's practice of publishing names, addresses, and case files converted repression from an atmosphere into a record that could be checked. The significance for this article is the shift of terrain the lever accomplished. The internal Marxist critics had argued about the nature of the formation; the Helsinki generation argued about the formation's own laws, and discovered that a regime which legitimises itself as lawfulness incarnate is peculiarly vulnerable to being read its own statutes. The two generations overlapped and intermarried intellectually: the men and women of Charter 77 included the reform communists of 1968, and the Polish opposition of 1980 counted both the open letter's veterans and the Helsinki lawyers among its founders. The internal critique's later history is therefore not a replacement of Marxist dissent by liberal dissent but a sedimentation, each layer preserving the earlier one's networks and adding its own instrument, until by 1989 the opposition in the northern tier of the bloc disposed of a complete alternative civic infrastructure: presses, funds, advisers, chaplains, and a tested doctrine of self-limitation.

## Eurocommunism: the verdict of the Western parties

The bloc's systems were also judged, with consequences for their legitimacy, by the communist parties of Western Europe, and the judgement belongs in this article because it was delivered from inside the same tradition and because the regimes felt it as such. Through the 1970s the Italian, Spanish, and, more hesitantly, French parties developed the position the press named Eurocommunism: an insistence that socialism in the developed West must be built through democracy and pluralism, joined to an increasingly open criticism of the Soviet model as a road not to be repeated. Santiago Carrillo's book of 1977, whose title announced Eurocommunism and the state as alternatives, argued that the state apparatus of the Eastern type had to be democratised rather than merely captured, a thesis aimed at Madrid that landed in Moscow and Prague as an attack on the model itself. The Italian party under Enrico Berlinguer carried the distance furthest, treating the exhaustion of the October tradition's propulsive force as a fact to be stated aloud. The regimes answered through their theoretical journals with the vocabulary reserved for heresy, and the answers convinced no one they needed to convince. For the internal critics of the East the Western parties' evolution was a complicated gift. It confirmed, from the tradition's democratic wing, the critics' central contention that the model and democracy were alternatives; it also threatened to strand the Eastern critics between a Western left embarrassed by them and a Western right that wished to use them, a stranding many of them remarked on with some bitterness. The episode closes a circle this article has drawn repeatedly. The critique of actually existing socialism was never the property of one camp. It was produced wherever the tradition's own standards were applied to the tradition's own states: in Warsaw seminar rooms, in Zagreb journals, in Madrid party schools, and in the cells and boiler rooms to which the states consigned the appliers.

## The critics abroad: exile, translation, and the Western ear

Exile was the bloc's involuntary gift to Western scholarship, and the exchange deserves a clear-eyed account. The exiled critics, Kołakowski at Oxford, Brus at Oxford and later in Scotland, Heller and Fehér in Australia and New York, Márkus in Sydney, Šik in St Gallen, Modzelewski's and Kuroń's texts travelling ahead of their authors, Kolakowski's compatriot the historian of ideas and the sociologists of the Polish school, re-founded Eastern European studies in the universities that received them and wrote the works through which the Anglophone world chiefly knows the region's internal life. The transaction had costs that the exiles themselves recorded. The Western ear selected: it amplified the critics who confirmed its own argument and neglected those, the council socialists and market socialists and Christian socialists, whose conclusions were inconvenient to both camps of the Cold War, so that the received picture of Eastern dissent in the West is systematically more liberal and less socialist than the dissent itself was. Translation lagged and distorted; the journals in which the internal debate actually lived, the Warsaw and Prague and Budapest periodicals of the thaws, remain largely untranslated, as the survey of untranslated literature in this article records. And exile changed the exiles, as it must: a critic writing for the seminar rooms of Oxford or the readers of the New York Review is performing a different office from a critic writing for the boiler rooms of Prague, and the later works of several figures in the critics table bear the marks of the new audience in their shift from programme to retrospection. None of this diminishes the achievement. It specifies it, and the specification is the historian's ordinary duty toward evidence that arrived through a filter.

## What the critics agreed on, and where they split

The internal critics are easily mistaken for a school, and the mistake should be corrected by mapping their disagreements, which were as instructive as their consensus. They agreed on the diagnosis's core: the apparatus's monopoly of disposal and organisation, the falsity of the official self-description, the necessity of independent institutions through which society could speak. They split, first, on the formation's name and nature. Djilas and the open letter's authors said a new ruling class; Trotsky's heirs said a degenerated workers state in which the property forms remained a conquest to be defended against the bureaucracy and against restoration alike; the Budapest philosophers said a distinct formation, a dictatorship over needs, irreducible to the older categories; Bahro said a proto-socialism arrested before its own threshold. They split, second, on the remedy's agent. The council tradition trusted the producers organised in their workplaces; the revisionist philosophers trusted the party's reforming wing until the wing was clipped, and then ceased to trust it; the market socialists trusted the price mechanism disciplined by law; the Helsinki generation trusted the citizen armed with the statute book. They split, third, on the attitude to the West, some regarding Western democracy as the available model of the liberties they lacked, others regarding it as a different organisation of unfreedom whose adoption would betray the point of their critique, which had been to demand more than the West offered, not merely the same. The splits matter because they explain the sequel: when the systems fell, the critics did not inherit them, because the critics had never agreed on an inheritance, and the vacuum at the moment of victory was filled by programmes, advisers, and certainties imported or improvised. A tradition that had spent forty years learning what the old order was had spent correspondingly less time agreeing on what should replace it, and that asymmetry, which the strongest objection presses, is the internal critique's genuine tragedy rather than its refutation.

## The critics and the class in whose name they wrote

One further tension runs through the whole tradition and should be faced directly, since the regimes' propaganda exploited it and honest readers notice it. The internal critics were, with few exceptions, intellectuals: professors, writers, editors, students. The class in whose name they claimed to speak was the industrial working class, and the claim was open to the retort, delivered in every trial's indictment, that the accused were a privileged stratum mistaking its own frustrations for the people's will. The record supplies the answer, and it is the risings. In 1953, in 1956 in both its Polish and Hungarian theatres, in 1970 and 1976 and overwhelmingly in 1980, the industrial workers of the bloc acted on the critics' central propositions without waiting for the critics' permission: they struck against the state that employed them, demanded independent unions and councils, and named the apparatus's privilege as their grievance. The relation between the two movements was reciprocal education. The workers' defeats taught the intellectuals the limits of spontaneity and the need for organisation and law; the intellectuals' analyses gave the workers' committees a language in which a wage claim could be understood as a constitutional demand. The defence committee of 1976 was the institutional form of the reciprocity, and Solidarity its mass form. Where the reciprocity failed, as in Czechoslovakia after 1968, where the workers accepted normalisation's bargain of quiet consumption and the intellectuals were left to sign charters in heroic isolation, the opposition remained a conscience rather than a movement, which is why the Czechoslovak ending, when it came, was made by students and theatre people in ten days of carnival rather than by a decade of organisation. The internal critique's authority, in the end, rests on this double foundation: it was written by the systems' own intellectuals and ratified, at intervals across forty years, by the systems' own workers, in the only referendums the bloc ever permitted itself, conducted with feet and with folded arms.

## The founding decade: pluralism's brief season and the closing of the doors

The bloc was not born in its final shape, and the brief season of 1945 to 1947 deserves recovery because the internal critics' later bitterness is unintelligible without the memory of what had been possible. The governments formed after the liberation were coalitions in fact as well as in name: communist, socialist, peasant, and liberal parties sharing ministries, newspapers of genuinely different allegiances appearing in the capitals, elections in several countries whose results the communists accepted when they lost districts and manipulated when they could. Land reforms broke the great estates and were popular in the countryside in a way nothing the regimes later did ever matched. Nationalisation of the commanding heights commanded broad assent in societies where the prewar owners were remembered as collaborators or foreigners or both. The closing of the doors proceeded by the salami method its Hungarian practitioner named with a candour history has preserved: slice by slice, a coalition partner's newspaper suppressed for an offence, a minister arrested on a conspiracy charge manufactured by the security organs, a party congress stage managed into a merger, until by 1948 the pluralism survived only as the coalition's letterhead. The significance for the internal critique is generational. The men and women who became the critics of the 1950s and 1960s had in many cases been the young believers of 1945, who had joined the movement when it still promised the coalition's programme and who measured the mature system against the memory of its own spring. Djilas had written the wartime hymns; Kuroń and Modzelewski were children of the founding; Bahro was a schoolboy of the new republic. The critique's emotional register, betrayal rather than enmity, is the founding decade's bequest, and a reader who skips the brief season will mistake the critics for the system's lifelong opponents when they were, in truth, its disappointed heirs.

## Education: the achievement that manufactured the critics

No account of these societies is balanced that omits their educational achievement, and none is honest that omits what the achievement produced. The systems schooled everyone: they liquidated the illiteracy that still disfigured the region's south and east at the founding, built universities in provincial cities that had never possessed them, opened the professions to the children of peasants and workers by quota and by stipend, and made the teacher and the engineer into the era's folk heroes. The first generation of beneficiaries filled the institutes and ministries with a gratitude that was entirely genuine, and the regimes counted, reasonably, on the gratitude's permanence. What they had not counted on was the curriculum's content. A system that teaches historical materialism as a science equips its pupils with a method for analysing the system; a system that makes the founding texts compulsory reading issues every student a licence to compare the text with the street. The surplus consciousness Bahro named was manufactured in these classrooms by the million, and the bloc's universities became, in each thaw, the first institutions to defect: Warsaw and Budapest in 1956, Prague and Belgrade in 1968, the student strikes that opened the Polish crises and the Czechoslovak ending. The regimes understood the mechanism and periodically purged the universities, expelling the students of 1968 in Warsaw by the faculty, screening the staffs of Prague after the invasion, but the purge could only ever be temporary, because the system needed the graduates it feared. An economy of engineers requires engineering faculties; engineering faculties require the discussion of real problems; real problems, in a shortage economy administered by a monopoly, lead by a short road to the discussion of the monopoly. The classroom was the internal critique's seminary, and the diploma its ordination, and no purge in the record ever solved the contradiction because the contradiction was the system's own success returning to examine it.

## The arts under the monopoly: film as the thaw's thermometer

The cultural history of the bloc can be read as a thermometer of its politics, and nowhere more sensitively than in film, the art the regimes prized most and therefore watched closest. The Stalinist years imposed socialist realism as a universal prescription, the positive hero, the optimistic ending, the enemy unmasked, and produced in every national cinema a stratum of films their own audiences stayed away from in droves. Each thaw released a stored generation. The Polish film school of the 1950s, Andrzej Wajda's war trilogy above all, subjected the national myths to an irony the official culture could not have licensed five years earlier, and Wajda's later Man of Marble, in 1977, performed the internal critique in images, exhuming the Stalinist bricklayer hero of the founding propaganda and interviewing his ruins. The Czechoslovak new wave of the 1960s, Miloš Forman, Jiří Menzel, Věra Chytilová and their colleagues, perfected a comedy of the administered life, the firemen's ball as the whole system in miniature, theft universal, authority pompous and bewildered, the queue and the committee rendered with a tenderness that made the indictment unanswerable; after 1968 the wave was broken, its directors exiled or shelved, its films locked in the vaults their makers joked the state had built as their most reliable distributor. Hungary's Miklós Jancsó turned the history of the plains into a ballet of power and humiliation that censors passed as folklore. The pattern across all three cinemas is the article's pattern in another medium. The talent was the system's own product, trained in its film schools at its expense; the work measured the system against its promises; the answer was the shelf, the ban, the exile visa. A regime that must lock its own prize films in a vault has filed the same assessment of its arguments as the regime that imprisons its philosophers, and the vaults of Prague and Warsaw, when they were opened after 1989, disgorged a national cinema's worth of evidence for the prosecution.

## The security apparatus as an institution

The internal critics wrote about the political police with a restraint born of experience, and the institution deserves a factual paragraph in any survey of the systems. Each state maintained a security service charged with the surveillance of society, staffed by professionals and extended by networks of informers recruited through pressure, payment, and conviction, whose reports filled archives that, in the East German case, survived to become a historical source of terrible completeness. The service's mandate was defined by the doctrine's central anxiety: since opposition could not, in theory, arise from the socialist order itself, its appearance in fact had to be the work of hostile centres abroad, and the service existed to find the centres' local instruments, a mandate that guaranteed it would find them whether or not they existed. The critics' analyses treated the police not as an excrescence but as a structural necessity of a monopoly that must know what society thinks because it cannot permit society to say. Their fates were correspondingly entangled with the institution: its officers compiled the files that filled the trials, its informers sat in the seminars and the editorial meetings, and the discovery after 1989 of how many of the era's conversations had been transcribed gave the region a second, archival wave of reckoning with the internal critique's world. The point for the analyst is the ordering of trust noticed earlier in this article. The security service was the one institution in these states that was never subjected to the economy drives, the staffing cuts, or the doctrinal revisions applied to every other, and an institution's budget across forty years is a more reliable statement of a regime's theory of itself than its constitution.

## The border and the wall: exit as referendum

The bloc's borders were guarded in one direction, and the direction is the datum. Citizens of the Western states required visas and faced searches entering the East; citizens of the Eastern states required permission, rarely granted to the young and the skilled, to leave at all, and those who left without it committed a crime the East German code named flight from the republic. The Berlin Wall, built in August 1961 after the loss of a substantial share of the republic's population through the open sector border, was the system's most honest monument: a state that had claimed the allegiance of the working class walled the working class in, and defended the wall in its official nomenclature as a protective rampart against the enemy its citizens were fleeing toward. The internal critics made the border a standard exhibit, because it converted the abstract question of consent into an engineering fact. A social order may survive its subjects' grumbling, its queues, even its strikes; an order that must prevent its subjects from leaving has answered the question of its own legitimacy in concrete and wire, and the answer was legible from space. The exodus of 1989, when the Hungarians cut their fence and the East Germans walked out through a neighbour's garden, was therefore not a prelude to the collapse but the collapse itself in its first form, the referendum the bloc had spent forty years preventing finally held at the border crossings, with the result its governments had always predicted and always denied.

## The language of the systems: officialese as evidence

The critics were connoisseurs of the regime's language, because language was the medium in which the gap they documented was daily performed. Official speech across the bloc developed a recognisable dialect: a wooden vocabulary of nominalisations and passive constructions in which decisions were implemented, tasks were fulfilled, and shortcomings were noted without any person deciding, fulfilling, or falling short; a calendar of ritual affirmations in which unanimous meetings greeted reports no one had read; a taxonomy of enemies graded from the hostile centre abroad down to the wavering element at the next desk. The dissident literature returned to this dialect obsessively. Michnik wrote of the language of the lie as the regime's native tongue; Havel's greengrocer hangs his slogan as a linguistic act, a sentence whose meaning is its display; the Polish satirists and the Prague humorists mined the officialese for the comedy its speakers could not hear. The linguistic evidence matters analytically because it corroborates the internal critique at the level of daily practice. A doctrine confident of its correspondence to reality develops a vocabulary for new facts; the bloc's dialect developed instead a machinery for preventing facts from achieving verbal form, so that a harvest failure became the nonfulfilment of procurement tasks and a riot became an incident provoked by hostile elements. The critics' counter practice was lexical before it was political: to call the queue a queue, the privilege a privilege, the bureaucracy a class, the invasion an invasion. Normalisation after 1968 understood this perfectly and policed vocabulary as sedition, insisting that the armies of August be named fraternal assistance in every printed line, so that a whole society was required, as the price of print, to repeat a description it knew to be false, and the requirement, repeated daily for twenty years, was itself the system's most efficient lesson in what it was.

## The two springs of revolt

The year 1968 belongs to both Europes, and the comparison disciplines any romantic account of either. In the West the year's movements were of students and the young against universities, war, and the administered affluence of their parents' settlement; in the East the year's movements were of a whole society in Czechoslovakia attempting reform through its own government, of students in Warsaw and Belgrade demanding the liberties their constitutions proclaimed, and of workers in Poland's coastal cities two years later completing the sequence. The Eastern 1968 was the more consequential and the more severely answered. Its Warsaw chapter opened with the banning of a classical play whose anti tsarist lines the audience applauded as contemporary, proceeded through student demonstrations beaten by police auxiliaries, and closed with the regime's antisemitic campaign, in which the security apparatus and its press allies attributed the unrest to a Zionist conspiracy and purged the universities, the press, and the party of citizens of Jewish origin, driving into exile a generation of Polish intellectuals that included some of the internal critique's most distinguished names. Zygmunt Bauman, the Warsaw sociologist whose work on class and on the party's sociology had made him a target of the nationalists inside the apparatus, lost his chair and left in 1968 for Israel and then for Leeds, where his later work on modernity and its discontents carried the Eastern experience into the centre of Western social theory. Bronisław Baczko, the historian of utopia, left for Geneva. The March events completed the Polish revisionists' education: the regime they had hoped to reform had shown it would rather rule through the oldest hatred in the region's cupboard than permit its own children a theatre. The lesson was absorbed by the generation that built the defence committee eight years later, which understood from the start that the opposition had to be organised in society, beyond the party's reach, because the party had demonstrated in 1968 that it would burn its own house to keep the keys.

## Miklos Haraszti and the worker in the workers state

Hungary contributed to the internal critique the single most damaging piece of reportage the bloc produced, and its author's method deserves attention because it answered the charge that the critics were intellectuals describing a class they did not know. Miklós Haraszti, a young writer who had been expelled from university for his poems, took a job as a milling machine operator in the Red Star Tractor Factory outside Budapest and wrote, from the bench, an account of piece work under the New Economic Mechanism that was published abroad in 1977 as A Worker in a Worker's State. The book follows the making of a single component through a shift: the norms negotiated and cheated, the foreman's favours, the scrap hidden and the hours stolen, the workers' intricate private economy of tricks by which a man whose pay depends on pieces learns to manufacture time. Its argument is carried entirely by the detail. The worker in the book is not exploited by an owner; he is administered by a hierarchy whose interest in his effort is as alien to him as any owner's, and his resistance takes the same forms, slowdown, pilfering, the hoarded trick of the trade, that the classical literature described under capitalism. Haraszti was tried in 1973 for the manuscript, before its foreign publication, and received a suspended sentence after an international campaign; he became a central figure of the Hungarian democratic opposition and of its samizdat journal. The book's place in this article is as the internal critique's ethnographic proof: the analysis that the professors had elaborated in the vocabulary of class and needs, verified at a milling machine by a poet working a shift, in a factory named for the revolution's own star.

## Andras Hegedus: the prime minister who became a sociologist

The Hungarian record holds one more career that maps the tradition's possibilities, that of András Hegedűs, who had been prime minister of Hungary in 1955 and 1956, a signatory in the revolution's crisis of the appeal that brought the Soviet army back, and who then underwent the most improbable second life in the bloc's history. Removed from power, he turned to sociology, founded and directed the Academy's sociological institute, and spent the 1960s building a discipline that measured the society he had governed: its stratification, its bureaucracy, its actual distribution of power and goods as against the official picture. His essays on the bureaucratic organisation of socialist society applied to the apparatus the instruments of empirical sociology and reached conclusions parallel to the philosophers'; he was expelled from the party in 1973 and from his institute's direction, and spent his remaining decades as a tolerated elder of the opposition he had once ordered arrested. The career resists every simple moral. Hegedűs's hands were not clean, and he did not claim they were; his sociology can be read as a long act of expiation conducted by other means. For the analyst his importance is evidentiary. The internal critique was not the hobby of the excluded. It recruited, across the decades, a former prime minister, a vice president, the party's own philosophers and publishers and professors, the very summit of the systems it indicted, and a movement whose defectors are drawn from the summit is telling the historian something about the summit's view of the valley.

## The Croatian Spring and Yugoslavia's national question

Yugoslavia's difference did not exempt it from the bloc's underlying laws, and its crisis of 1971 demonstrates the federation's version of the pattern. The Croatian Spring was a movement inside the republic's own communist leadership and cultural institutions for greater republican autonomy, economic control over the tourist coast's earnings, and recognition of the Croatian language and culture within the federation; it gathered students, writers, and the republic's party organisation into a national mobilisation that Belgrade read, correctly, as a challenge to the federation's settlement. Tito broke the movement in December 1971 by the method the system knew: the Croatian leadership was forced to resign, the cultural institutions were purged, editors and student leaders were tried and imprisoned, and the purge then extended to the liberal leaderships of Serbia and the other republics, clearing the federation of the generation that might have reformed it. The episode earns its place in this account because it shows the internal critique's Yugoslav limit from the other side. Where criticism in the federation took a national form, it mobilised masses as the philosophers never could, and it was answered with the same instruments as in Warsaw or Prague, because the monopoly recognised in the national movements the one force that could outbid it for the allegiance of its subjects. The federation's last two decades were governed by the settlement of 1971 and by the constitution of 1974, the work of Edvard Kardelj, the regime's house theorist, which dispersed authority among republics and self managing bodies so elaborately that the federation's centre retained the party and the army as its only working organs. Kardelj's theory of associated labour, the system's official philosophy in its final form, attempted to answer the internal critics by constitutional engineering: bureaucracy would be dissolved by delegating every decision to the associated producers in their communities of labour. The critics of the Praxis generation, silenced in the same years, observed that the engineering multiplied committees while the monopoly that appointed them stood untouched, and the federation's subsequent history, debt, inflation, republican vetoes, and a centre that could neither command nor persuade, is the record on which the reader may judge between the house theory and its critics, always within this article's discipline of ending the account at the decade's close.

## The second circulation: samizdat as an institution

Between the official press and silence the bloc's societies built a third thing, and its construction is one of the era's genuine institutional inventions. Samizdat, the self publication named in Russian and adopted across the region, began as the retyping of banned texts through carbon paper, a copy producing copies in chains whose arithmetic every dissident household understood. By the 1970s and 1980s it had become, in Poland above all, a parallel publishing industry: independent houses with editors, print runs on smuggled and improvised presses, distribution networks, and catalogues that included the internal critics' works, the classics in unexpurgated editions, history the textbooks falsified, and the opposition's own journals, issued on schedule through martial law itself. Czechoslovakia's typists produced editions of banned novelists bound by hand; Hungary's samizdat journal Beszélő carried the democratic opposition's debates; the East German churches sheltered cyclostyled environmental and peace bulletins. The institution matters to this article for two reasons. It was the material base on which the internal critique survived its authors' silencing: a text banned in 1965 could be in its fifth retyping in 1980, read by students who had never heard its author's name spoken aloud. And it was a school of the very capacities the system's end would require, editing, printing, distributing, accounting, trusting, so that the opposition of 1989 disposed, in the northern tier, of a civil society in embryo that had been built one carbon copy at a time. The regimes fought the second circulation with searches, confiscations, and sentences for the possession of paper and ink, and the disproportion of the response, detectives assigned to typewriters, is the taxonomy of this article operating at the level of stationery.

## The consumer's decade and the bargain of quiet

The 1970s in the bloc rested on a bargain whose terms the internal critics were the first to state plainly. After the traumas of the founding and the risings, the regimes of the northern tier, and Kádár's Hungary as the bargain's pioneer, offered their subjects a settlement: rising consumption, toleration of the second economy and of private life, travel within limits, in exchange for the renunciation of public politics. The bargain was financed in part by Western credit, as Poland's and Hungary's and Romania's borrowing mounted through the decade, converting the détente of the governments into the mortgages of the governed. For a time the settlement delivered its side. The shops of Budapest and, more precariously, of Warsaw and East Berlin filled with goods the founding generation could not have imagined; the private car and the weekend cottage entered the region's aspirations; the queues shortened from catastrophes to inconveniences. The critics' analysis of the bargain was unsparing and, in retrospect, exact. Consumption without rights, they argued, was the dictatorship over needs in its most comfortable form: the apparatus deciding which satisfactions would be permitted to substitute for citizenship, and the substitution's terms set unilaterally and revocable at will, as the Polish price rises of the decade kept demonstrating whenever the bills came due. The bargain also corrupted the bargainers, binding the apparatus's own members into the second economy's networks and the West's credit into the system's survival, so that by the 1980s the regimes were administering a stagnation they could neither afford to end nor afford to continue. The decade's quiet is sometimes remembered, by those who lived it, with an ambivalence the historian should respect: it was the most bearable decade the bloc produced, and it was purchased by the deferral of every question this article has examined, at interest.

## Generations: the children of the builders

The bloc's history can be written as a succession of generations, each formed by a different founding experience, and the internal critique changed its character as the generations succeeded one another. The first generation, the founders, had made the systems out of war and illegality and carried the authority of sacrifice; their critics, Djilas and Harich and the young Kołakowski, were of the same formation, which is why the early critique speaks the language of betrayed promise. The second generation, formed by the thaw and by 1968, had not built the system and owed it nothing but an education; its critics, Bahro and Haraszti and the Charter signatories, speak the language of diagnosis, cooler, more institutional, less interested in the promise than in the mechanism. The third generation, formed in the 1970s bargain and the Helsinki decade, encountered the system as an inheritance of queues and rituals whose original justifications they had to look up; its opposition was correspondingly civic rather than doctrinal, organised around rights, ecology, peace, and the refusal of the lie, and its members often found the Marxist critics' vocabulary as dated as the official one. The succession explains a paradox of the ending. When the systems fell, the internal Marxist critique, which had done more than any other body of thought to make them indefensible, supplied few of the ending's leaders and almost none of its programme, because the generation that made the ending had been formed after the critique's classical period and spoke its successor language. Traditions of criticism, like the systems they criticise, have their life cycles, and the internal critique's historical work was complete when its central propositions, that the apparatus ruled in its own interest and that society had never consented, had passed from heresy into the common sense of the crowds of 1989, most of whom had never read a line of Bahro or the Budapest School and did not need to.

## The archives and the reading of the record since

The opening of the bloc's archives after 1989 transformed the study of the systems, and a word about the transformation is included here because it bears on the internal critique's standing as evidence. The files of the parties and the security services, where they survived and were opened, have allowed historians to test the critics' analyses against the apparatus's own secret record, and the test has run, in the main, in the critics' favour. The apparatus's internal reports describe the privilege the critics alleged, in the apparatus's own distribution lists; the security files document the surveillance and the scripted trials the critics inferred; the economic ministries' confidential papers record the falsification of the figures the critics suspected, each level inflating the level below's numbers in the manner the reform economists described from inside the process. Historians have also corrected the critics where correction was due: the archives complicate the picture of a monolithic apparatus, showing factions, hesitations, reformers inside the machine, and societies more various in their accommodations than the dissident literature, written from the opposition's vantage, sometimes allowed. The mature historiography that has resulted, social histories of everyday life, institutional histories of the party state, the biographies of the critics themselves, treats the internal critique neither as gospel nor as grievance but as what this article has argued it is: the testimony of the best placed witnesses, to be cross examined like all testimony and credited where the record confirms it, which the record, on the core claim concerning the ruling apparatus's character, has done.

## The internal critique as a method

The series pattern this article instantiates, met earlier in this series, in the treatment of the socialist states' environmental record, generalises into a method for reading any closed system, and the method is worth stating as the article approaches its close. First, attend to the critics a system produces from within itself, because they alone combine the knowledge of insiders with the motive to publish what the knowledge shows. Second, attend to the system's response to those critics as evidence about the system, since an institution reveals its theory of itself in what it cannot afford to hear. Third, separate, in every critic, the analysis from the programme, crediting each on its own merits, since the history of the bloc shows that the two can diverge to the point of independence. Fourth, state the sharpest counter to the critics in its strongest form, as this article has done with the objection from the system's coherence, and let the verdict carry the objection's residue openly rather than pretend it away. The method is not a celebration of dissidence. The internal critics were sometimes wrong, in their economics, in their estimates of their own societies, in their hopes; the method credits them not with innocence but with position, the position of the witness inside the room. Applied beyond its original case, the method instructs the reader of any order that claims a monopoly of truth, including orders built on doctrines the reader may share, to ask the bloc's questions in the bloc's order: who controls disposal, who takes the decisions, who is permitted to organise, and what befalls the person who asks aloud.

## The guarantee and its price: the alliance as a domestic institution

The bloc's international architecture was also a domestic one, and the internal critics understood it as such. The Warsaw Pact and the council for mutual economic assistance bound the member states to the Soviet Union in a division of labour whose terms were set in Moscow: raw materials and energy flowed westward at prices administered for stability, manufactures flowed eastward into a market that could not refuse them, and each regime's plan was negotiated in the shadow of the alliance's requirements. The political clause of the arrangement was stated publicly only once, in the doctrine that bears the Soviet leader's name, articulated after the invasion of Czechoslovakia: the sovereignty of a socialist state was limited by the interests of the socialist commonwealth, as defined by its strongest member, and a threat to socialism in one country was the common concern of all, to be met in the last instance by their armies. The doctrine's candour was its service to clarity. Every government in the bloc thereafter ruled with a known reservation: its tenure depended on its remaining within a corridor whose walls were guarded from outside, and its subjects knew the corridor's width from the experiments of 1956 and 1968. The internal critics drew the conclusion in both directions. A regime that requires an external guarantee has confessed the insufficiency of its domestic title; a critic who appeals against the regime appeals, in the end, against the guarantor, which is why the European opposition's strategy evolved, from Helsinki onward, toward raising the price of intervention in the guarantor's own diplomacy rather than confronting the guarantee directly. When the guarantor finally renounced the doctrine, in the late 1980s, the renunciation was received in the bloc's capitals with the incredulity of men watching a load bearing wall announce its retirement, and the speed of 1989 is in part the sound of structures discovering which of their walls had been decorative.

## Collectivisation and the land: the bloc's first social transformation

The transformation of the countryside was the founding decade's deepest social intervention and the one whose results varied most instructively across the region. The land reforms of 1945 had created a continent of small proprietors; the collectivisation drives that followed, modelled on the Soviet precedent and pressed hardest between 1949 and 1953, sought to abolish that proprietorship in favour of collective and state farms, and met the peasantry's resistance in the forms available to it: slaughtered livestock, concealed harvests, flight to the industrial towns, and a sullen non cooperation that the production figures of the 1950s silently record. The outcomes map the region's variety. In most of the bloc collectivisation was completed, at the cost of an agricultural sector that never recovered the smallholder's diligence and that the states subsidised and imported food to disguise. In Poland the drive was abandoned after 1956, the collectives dissolved by their members in a matter of weeks when compulsion relaxed, and the countryside remained in private hands for the rest of the period, a standing exception that the regime explained as a national peculiarity and the opposition treasured as a proof that retreat was possible. In Hungary the cooperatives were rebuilt after 1956 with a pragmatism that permitted household plots and market sales, producing the bloc's most successful agriculture and its most prosperous villages. The internal critics, urban almost to a man, wrote less about the land than about the factory, a limitation of their tradition that the record should note: the peasantry's experience of the system, expropriation resisted and routine endured, was the bloc's largest single social fact, and the countryside's verdict on the system, delivered in Poland in 1956 by the simple act of dissolving the collectives when the police looked away, is among the most eloquent documents the period produced, written in the only medium its authors commanded.

## Housing, health, and the social wage: the systems' genuine provision

The internal critique loses credibility whenever it is mistaken for a denial of the systems' social achievements, and the critics themselves, to their credit, rarely made the mistake. The bloc's states provided, as a matter of structure rather than of charity, a social wage of real dimensions: employment guaranteed and with it an income floor, housing at rents frozen to nominal levels, healthcare free at the point of use and organised to reach the whole population, pensions, maternity provision, subsidised food and transport and culture, and education without fees at every level. The provision was uneven, administered through the workplace and the ladder in ways that made it an instrument of dependence as well as of security, and its quality lagged its coverage, the polyclinic's queue and the hospital's informal payments being as characteristic as the entitlement itself. Housing, the scarcest of the goods, was allocated by waiting lists measured in years and by the favour of the allocating institutions, so that the flat became the era's universal object of striving and intrigue, the subject of its best comedies and its bitterest memoirs. But the floor was real, and the memory of the floor survived the systems that built it, complicating every subsequent judgement. The critics' point about the provision was never that it was worthless. It was that the provision was administered as a gift of the apparatus rather than held as a right of the citizen, distributed through the same hierarchy that distributed posts and permissions, so that the worker's flat, clinic, and holiday were held at the pleasure of the order they might otherwise have criticised. Security as a technique of rule, the Budapest philosophers called the arrangement in effect, and the phrase captures what the statistics of coverage cannot: that a benefit one may lose for speaking is not only a benefit.

## The Plastic People and the trial that made a charter

Czechoslovakia's normalisation produced, in 1976, the episode that converted the country's scattered dissent into an organised civic initiative, and the episode's improbability is its significance. The Plastic People of the Universe were a rock band, formed in the afterglow of 1968, licensed and then delicensed as the cultural freeze deepened, playing thereafter at private events to audiences of the young and the disaffected. In 1976 the state tried the band's members and associates for organised disturbance of the peace, in proceedings whose transcripts, smuggled to the philosopher Jan Patočka and to the playwright Václav Havel, persuaded a circle of Prague intellectuals that the trial of an apolitical band was the purest available demonstration of the order's character: young people imprisoned not for opposing the system but for declining to participate in its rituals of taste and obedience. The response, Charter 77, took the form this article has described, a petition grounded in the state's own legal undertakings, whose first signatories spanned the whole spectrum of the silenced, reform communists of 1968, independent socialists, Catholics, Protestants, and the band's own defenders. Patočka, the Charter's first spokesman and its philosophical conscience, died in 1977 after days of police interrogation, and his death gave the initiative its martyr and its measure of the regime's answer. The sequence from a rock band's trial to a civic charter is the internal critique's Czechoslovak variant: where the Marxist tradition had been broken by normalisation's purges, the critique of the system reassembled itself around the defence of the unpolitical, and discovered in the defence a politics more durable than the one it replaced.

## Honecker's republic: consumer socialism in the hardest state

East Germany in the 1970s and 1980s under Erich Honecker offers the bloc's most complete attempt to stabilise the system by provision, and its failure is therefore the most instructive. The programme, announced as the unity of economic and social policy, concentrated the state's resources on housing construction, consumer supply, and social benefits, financed increasingly by credits from the Federal Republic whose terms included humanitarian concessions, the ransoming of political prisoners and the easing of travel for pensioners, in a traffic that made the hardest state in the bloc financially dependent on its capitalist twin. The republic's citizens lived, by the bloc's standards, well: supplied shops, secure tenancies, a welfare provision of genuine breadth, and the most thorough surveillance in the region to keep the account. The internal opposition of the 1980s gathered, as this article has noted, under the Protestant churches' roofs, in peace and environmental circles whose demands were framed in the regime's own vocabulary of peace and protection, and whose members were disproportionately the young whom the provision was meant to satisfy. The East German case tests the bargain of quiet at its strongest form and records its limit. Provision purchased compliance for a generation that remembered worse; it could not purchase belief from a generation that compared the republic not with its past but with the present visible nightly on Western television, broadcasting into most of the state's territory the other Germany's shop windows and parliamentary scandals alike. A legitimacy that must compete with a televised alternative of the same nation, in the same language, is in a competition no ministry of supply can win, and the critics who had argued that the system's foundation was the monopoly rather than the standard of living found in the GDR their cleanest confirmation: the best provided state in the bloc was the one whose citizens, offered the chance in 1989, dissolved it entirely into its neighbour.

## Poland and the road to the round table

The bloc's ending began, fittingly, in the country whose opposition had the longest education, and its opening moves deserve recording because they show the internal critique's generation conducting the negotiation it had theorised for a decade. The Polish strikes of 1988, in the shipyards and mines, were led by a generation younger than Solidarity's founders and impatient with the underground's patience; the regime, bankrupt in currency and in ideas, chose negotiation over a third suppression, calculating that a controlled sharing of responsibility for austerity would divide the opposition and legitimate the pain. The round table that convened in February 1989 brought the apparatus face to face with the men and women it had interned in 1981, Kuroń among the opposition's advisers, the open letter's author now the theorist of the self limiting revolution applied to a transfer of power. The agreement's terms, partially free elections, a new senate, a presidency reserved for the old order's general, were designed as a compromise the regime believed it could survive. The elections of June returned the verdict the regime had spent forty years preventing its subjects from delivering at a ballot box: the opposition won every contestable seat, and the compromise's arithmetic collapsed into a transfer the negotiators had not scheduled. The Polish sequence from the shipyard gate of 1970 to the round table of 1989 is the bloc's longest argument conducted in acts rather than texts, and the internal critique's place in it is not decorative. The opposition that sat down at the table had been educated in the critics' school, in the defence committee's practice and the underground's presses, and the self limitation it practised at the moment of victory, honouring the agreement's letter while its spirit dissolved, was the critics' strategic doctrine surviving contact with power.

## Hungary's opposition and the meeting at Lakitelek

Hungary's road to 1989 ran through the opposition's own institutions and through the ruling party's disintegration, a combination unique in the bloc. The democratic opposition, formed around the samizdat journal Beszélő from 1981 and the Monday free university that met in private flats, numbered among its figures the Budapest School's younger associates and the tractor factory's ethnographer, Haraszti, and it maintained through the decade a running seminar on the system's nature that doubled as a government in waiting's education. The populist writers' wing of the opposition, rooted in the villages and in the national tradition, met in 1987 at the village of Lakitelek with the sponsorship of a reformist party secretary, founding the movement that became the largest party of the transition. Inside the ruling party itself, the reformers around Imre Pozsgay, a minister who had patronised the populists, moved through 1988 to declare the events of 1956 a popular uprising rather than a counter revolution, a revision of the founding verdict that dissolved the regime's title in a single formula, since a state born of the suppression of a popular uprising could not survive the admission in the form its founders had given it. The Hungarian case completes the article's taxonomy of endings. Poland negotiated its way out under the pressure of a mass movement; Hungary's apparatus negotiated with itself, its reform wing calculating that a managed transformation preserved more than a defended monopoly; and in both cases the intellectual preparation, the decades of internal analysis that had deprived the old order of its self belief, determined the form of the exit. Regimes do not fall when they are attacked. They fall when their own servants cease to believe the account of the world that justified their service, and the internal critique's deepest achievement was to have spent forty years making that belief untenable, in the seminar rooms of Budapest as faithfully as in the shipyards of Gdańsk.

## Yugoslavia's last decade: the federation after its founder

Yugoslavia's 1980s, the decade after Tito's death in 1980, test the self management settlement against the conditions its theorists had hoped to avoid, and the record belongs in this survey because it completes the experiment's evidence. The collective presidency that succeeded the founder rotated the federation's headship among its republics and provinces by the year, a mechanism that embodied the system's distrust of its own centre. The foreign debt accumulated in the 1970s came due in a decade of stabilisation programmes negotiated with foreign creditors, inflation that the self managing enterprises passed to their members' incomes, and strikes that multiplied as the incomes fell, conducted by workers whose councils discovered that self management in a contracting economy meant administering one another's sacrifices. The federation's republics, each with its party, its bank, its academy, and its grievances, bargained over the common burden in the vocabulary of national accounting, and the Kosovo protests of 1981, in the federation's poorest province, announced the national question's return to the centre of Yugoslav politics from which the founding settlement had banished it. The Praxis generation, ageing and unrehabilitated, watched a system they had criticised for its bureaucracy drift toward a crisis of a kind their criticism had not foreseen, in which the danger came not from the centre's strength but from the federation's dissolution into its national components, each mobilising the language of self determination the federation itself had taught. The Yugoslav evidence, taken whole, therefore cuts in two directions for this article's argument, and the honesty the internal critique practised requires recording both. Self management had given Yugoslav workers and citizens real rights and a real sphere of initiative that the bloc's other systems denied, and the federation's crisis arrived not through the councils' failure but through the party monopoly's final form, a confederation of national monopolies, which reproduced at the level of the republics the structure the critics had indicted at the level of the federation. The experiment's lesson is thus narrower and harder than either its celebrants or its dismissers supposed: the dispersal of economic authority without the dispersal of political authority leaves the decisive monopoly intact, and the monopoly, denied a federal form, will find a national one.

## The intelligentsia's bargain

Between the apparatus and the workers stood the stratum that produced the critics and also produced the system's daily functioning, the intelligentsia, and its collective bargain with the regimes deserves a section because the internal critique was, among other things, the record of that bargain's breakdown. The systems needed the educated: engineers for the plants, teachers for the schools, doctors for the clinics, journalists for the papers, and a cultural stratum to furnish the civilisation the doctrine promised. In exchange for service the stratum received the era's scarcest goods in relative abundance, flats, travel, books, the second economy's best networks, and a status in societies that honoured learning with an intensity the West's markets rarely matched. The price was the performance this article has described: the signature on the resolution, the slogan in the window, the colleague unmentioned after his fall, the book review written to the required conclusion. The stratum paid the price in instalments so small that each seemed negligible, and the internal critics, who were the stratum's conscience in the literal sense of the members who kept its accounts, recorded the cumulative cost in the coin of self contempt, the era's characteristic intellectual emotion, documented in its novels and its diaries more faithfully than in any statistics. The bargain's breakdown came when the price rose or the goods failed: in 1968 in Prague, when the stratum was asked to affirm an invasion; in 1976 in Poland, when it was asked to watch workers beaten for a price rise; in the 1980s everywhere, when the goods themselves, the flat, the book, the journey, receded beyond the reach of the young graduates the bargain had been designed to purchase. A regime that buys its intelligentsia must keep paying in a currency of provision and prestige; a regime in stagnation pays in neither, and discovers that the stratum's loyalty, having been a transaction, expires with the means of payment, leaving the apparatus alone with its police in a country full of educated people who have stopped performing. The Monday demonstrations in Leipzig were staffed by the graduates of the republic's own universities, and the men who declined to shoot at them had been educated in the same lecture halls.

## Sport, spectacle, and the prestige economy

A lighter institution completes the survey of the systems' self presentation, and the internal critics noticed it too. The bloc's states invested in competitive sport with an intensity out of proportion to its place in any plan of human welfare, building institutes of athletic science, recruiting children through talent systems, and fielding teams whose victories were reported as the social order's report card. The investment made sense within the system's logic. Medals were a currency in which a shortage economy could be rich, a proof of superiority that could be displayed without the inconvenience of a comparison of shop windows, and the athlete's body, trained by the state's institutes and fed from the state's stores, was the planned economy's most photogenic product. The critics' treatment of the spectacle was characteristically double. They recorded the genuine pride the victories gave populations starved of collective achievement, and they recorded the system's need of the pride, the doping programmes that the archives later documented in the hardest state's files being only the most literal form of the general requirement that the performance continue. The prestige economy's function in this survey is a parable of the whole. An order that must demonstrate its superiority in the arenas it can control, the stadium, the chess board, the space programme's European echoes, while walling its subjects away from the arenas it cannot, has stated its own estimate of the open competition, and the estimate agrees with the internal critique's.

## Martial law: the army as the apparatus's last form

Poland's martial law of December 1981 deserves analysis as an institutional event and not only as a repression, because the form the repression took completed the internal critique's argument about where power in these systems finally resided. The Polish party, exhausted by sixteen months of dual power, its membership haemorrhaging to the union it confronted, could no longer rule through itself; the instrument that replaced it was the army, in the person of the defence minister who was also prime minister and party leader, governing through a military council of national salvation. The substitution is the datum. A political order whose civilian apparatus has lost the capacity to command falls back on the one hierarchy whose obedience is professional and whose legitimacy rests on the national rather than the doctrinal claim, and the general who addressed the nation in uniform, against a backdrop of the flag, spoke of saving the state rather than of building socialism, a change of vocabulary that conceded the doctrine's exhaustion more plainly than any dissident pamphlet. Martial law achieved its immediate objects: the union was interned and driven underground, the factories were militarised, the streets were cleared. It achieved nothing else. The economy it was partly declared to save continued its decline under military administration; the opposition it suppressed re emerged with its networks intact and its strategy matured; and the regime that had ruled through the army discovered that an army can hold a country but cannot legitimate a party, so that the seven years between martial law and the round table were a long negotiation with the inevitable conducted by men who had already, in December 1981, delivered the internal critique's verdict on their own system by the choice of the instrument with which they saved it.

## Christa Wolf and the writers who stayed

The internal critique's most ambiguous register belongs to the writers who remained inside the hardest states and attempted to criticise from within the terms of a loyalty they continued to feel, and Christa Wolf, the East German novelist, is its central figure. Wolf had joined the party as a young believer, served its cultural institutions, and written in her novel of 1968, and in the work that followed, the interior history of a generation's accommodation: the divided heaven of the republic's founding hopes, the self censorship anatomised in the very act of narration. Her position was impossible by design and she knew it. She was published and decorated by the state she criticised, surveilled by its police for decades as the files later showed, protected by her fame abroad and compromised, in the eyes of the younger dissidents, by the protection. Her novella written in the summer of 1989 and published as the state collapsed, recording a day spent under surveillance and the moral corrosion of being watched by a system one has served, became the occasion of a bitter literary quarrel in the reunified Germany over whether such belated candour was honesty or evasion. The quarrel deserves its place here because it states, in the cultural register, the question the whole tradition poses. What does criticism owe, and when, and from where? The exiles had one answer, the charter signatories another, the boiler room philosophers a third. Wolf's career, with its genuine achievements and its genuine compromises inseparably woven, is the record of the fourth answer, the attempt to remain and to tell the truth in instalments the censor would pass, and the judgement of that attempt, severe in the quarrels of the 1990s, has softened as the historians have taken over from the participants: her novels remain the best evidence we have of what the bargain of quiet felt like from inside a conscience.

## Time: the hidden tax of the shortage economy

The internal critics and the era's memoirists return so often to the subject of time that the historian is obliged to treat it as one of the systems' defining media. A shortage economy taxes its subjects in hours. The queue for the delivery that might come, the afternoon spent repairing through acquaintances what the services would not repair, the years on the housing list, the decade on the list for a car or a telephone, the working day itself padded with the meetings and the waiting for materials that the plan had failed to deliver: the citizen's scarcest resource was consumed by the system's friction in quantities that no statistic of income captures. The tax fell regressively and instructively. The apparatus's own members were exempted by their special provision, their drivers and their closed distribution, so that the men who administered the shortage did not stand in it, a daily, visible exemption that did more to instruct the queue in the system's sociology than any samizdat treatise. Time also shaped the opposition's peculiar resources. The dissident's wealth was time volunteered: the typist's nights, the courier's journeys, the seminar in the private flat, the underground's whole economy ran on hours its members donated from lives the official economy had already taxed to the margin, and the state's counter measure, the short detention, the summons that consumed a working day, the search that consumed a night, was a tax levied in the same currency. When the end came, it came partly as a refusal to go on paying: the Monday demonstrators in Leipzig were, among other things, citizens declining to spend another evening performing an assent they did not feel, and the phrase the crowds used, that they were the people, asserted a claim on the collective time the state had spent in their name.

## How the West read the bloc: two models and the critics' testimony

Western scholarship on the bloc organised itself for decades around two models, and the internal critique's testimony bears on the quarrel between them in ways that outlasted the bloc itself. The older model, formed in the 1950s, described the systems as totalitarian: an ideology, a single party, a terroristic police, monopolies of communication, weapons, and economic direction, fused into an instrument of total control that atomised society and left no room for internal change. The revisionist social history that challenged the model from the 1970s recovered the societies inside the systems: negotiation, evasion, informal networks, genuine belief and genuine welfare, generations for whom the system was not a prison but a biography. The internal critics, read carefully, testify against the simplicities of both. Against the totalitarian model's atomised society they offer the risings, the councils, the churches and the second circulation, a society that never stopped organising itself in the interstices and periodically organised itself in the open. Against the revisionist softening they offer the taxonomy of response, the prison and the exile and the vault of banned films, and the observation, which the archives have sustained, that the interstices existed on licence and the licence was revocable at will. The mature position the critics' testimony supports is the one the best historiography has reached: these were systems of real domination exercised over real societies, whose domination was never total because totality is beyond any apparatus, and whose subjects were neither the heroes of the dissident memoir nor the contented beneficiaries of the official statistics, but populations making lives inside structures they had not chosen and periodically, at the cost of everything, declining to go on making them on the offered terms.

## The balance sheet of the internal critique

The article has stated the strongest objection and answered it as far as the Yugoslav evidence permits; a final assessment can now gather what the internal critique established, what it failed to establish, and what it left behind, in the spirit of its own method. It established, first, the location of power in the Soviet type systems with a precision the subsequent archives have confirmed: in the apparatus's exclusive grip on disposal across the socialised economy and on organisation across society, exercised through the personnel ladder and defended, in the last instance, by the security organs and the external guarantee. It established, second, the evidentiary principle that a system's treatment of its internal critics is a privileged source about the system, since the response discriminates between criticism the system can answer and criticism it can only punish. It established, third, a body of concepts that outlived the systems and remain in the working vocabulary of the social sciences: the dictatorship over needs, the soft budget constraint that the reform milieu produced, the surplus consciousness, the second economy and the second circulation, the self limiting revolution. Its failures are the objection's territory and need only be summarised. It produced no institutional design, tested anywhere, that combined social ownership at scale with political freedom, and its programmes, councils, markets among social owners, associated labour, remain a shelf of brilliant sketches beside the one partial construction in Yugoslavia whose results were mixed and whose political precondition, the monopoly, contradicted the sketches' intent. It underestimated, as a tradition, the force of the national question, which its universalist vocabulary handled awkwardly and which inherited the region when the systems fell. And it overestimated, in its classical period, the working class's desire for the councils the critics designed for it, an overestimate the Polish workers of 1980 both confirmed in form and corrected in content, demanding independent unions and free expression first and the reorganisation of property a long way second, if at all.

What it left behind is not a doctrine but a discipline, and the discipline is this article's final claim for its subject. The internal critics demonstrated that the most consequential knowledge of a closed system is generated inside it, by the people the system has educated and cannot uneducate; that such knowledge, to survive, requires institutions the system does not control, a church, a union, a journal, a kitchen table with a typewriter; and that the knowledge's fate is a diagnostic instrument in its own right, since the map of what a system punishes is the map of what it fears, and the map of what it fears is the beginning of the map of what it is. The reader who has followed the argument from the phrase's official birth through the critics table to the quiet endings of 1989 holds, in the internal critique, both a chapter of European history and an instrument for reading the present of any order, provided the instrument is used with the separation its own history teaches: credit the analysis where the record confirms it, test the programme where the world has tried it, and concede, as the critics' best heirs conceded, what remains unanswered.

## The phrase in the scholarship

A closing word on the phrase itself, whose career did not end with the systems it named. Historians of the period have retained actually existing socialism, in English and in its German form, as the standard designation for the European systems between 1945 and 1989, precisely because the alternatives carry worse freight: communism confuses the systems with the doctrine they claimed, Soviet bloc subordinates the region's variety to its alliance, and state socialism begs the question the systems' own critics spent their lives arguing. The retention is a small posthumous victory for the critics who took the phrase over, since the scholarly usage preserves their irony in amber: the term of art for the systems is the term that concedes the gap between the systems and the idea. Students meeting the phrase in the literature should therefore handle it as this article has handled it, as evidence and not merely as a label, a fossil of the moment when the rulers of half a continent, reaching for a defence, handed their critics the words in which the indictment would be written, and the critics, being the best educated generation the systems ever produced, wrote it.

## The Petofi Circle and the writers of the Polish October

The thaw's intellectual detonations had addresses, and two of them deserve recording because they show how a debate in a hall becomes a revolution's prologue. In Budapest in the spring and summer of 1956 the Petőfi Circle, a debating forum of the communist youth organisation, held a series of meetings on history, economics, philosophy, and the press that grew from hundreds to thousands of participants, spilling into the streets outside the hall, where loudspeakers were rigged for the overflow. The speakers were the party's own intellectuals; the subject, nominally the lessons of the secret speech, became in session after session an audit of the regime's whole record conducted in the regime's own vocabulary, with the audience's applause functioning as the first free votes Hungary had cast in a decade. The circle's press debate, in which journalists demanded the right to report the country they lived in, is the direct ancestor of the revolution's first demands four months later. In Warsaw the equivalent institution was a journal, Po prostu, the weekly of the young intelligentsia that after the Polish October of 1956 became the revisionist generation's platform, publishing the sociology and the reportage the official press would not touch, until the leadership closed it in 1957 and its staff and readers learned the thaw's first lesson in reverse: that the licence to criticise, granted from above, could be withdrawn from above, and that anything built on the licence alone was a tenant's improvement on the apparatus's property. The internal critique's strategic evolution over the following decades, from petitioning the party to building institutions the party did not own, is the working out of that lesson, and the defence committee of 1976, with its own funds and its own presses, is Po prostu's answer composed twenty years later by its surviving readers.

## The neighbour's wallpaper: the old regimes confront Moscow's reform

The bloc's final crisis had an overture that the internal critics savoured, because it reversed the direction of forty years of discipline. When the Soviet leadership after 1985 began to proclaim openness and restructuring, the oldest regimes of the northern tier found themselves in the position of their own dissidents: custodians of an orthodoxy the centre no longer professed, obliged to explain to their subjects why the model they had imposed as eternal was being dismantled at its source. The East German leadership's discomfort produced the era's most quoted official sentence, delivered by its chief ideologue in 1987 in answer to a question about the Soviet example: a man does not repaper his flat merely because his neighbour is repapering his. The sentence was intended as a dismissal and survives as a confession, since it conceded that the republic's arrangements were a decoration applied to walls it dared not examine. Czechoslovakia's normalised leadership, installed by the invasion that had ended the last reform, received Moscow's new course as a threat aimed at its own title, and the Prague press's handling of the Soviet reforms, delayed, abridged, and surrounded with warnings, taught its readers to follow the Moscow papers for the news of their own future. The internal opposition needed to do very little in these years beyond translation and distribution. The centre was making the critics' argument in the critics' absence, demonstrating weekly that the system the satellites had been forbidden to reform was reformable in principle and indefensible in the form they had been required to keep, and the satellites' rulers, who had justified every domestic severity by the centre's requirements, discovered that the centre's requirements had changed and theirs had not, a discovery from which none of them recovered.

## Minorities inside the fraternal states

The bloc's official doctrine held the national question to have been solved by the abolition of exploitation, and the condition of the region's minorities is the standing refutation the internal record supplies. The Roma of the bloc, settled by force where they were nomadic, dispersed from their settlements in Czechoslovakia under assimilation programmes that treated their culture as a residue to be planned away, sterilised in documented programmes that the dissident press exposed and the federation's charter signatories adopted as a cause, lived the system's promise of equality as an administration of their difference by officials who had decided in advance what their emancipation required. The Jewish communities that remained after the Holocaust and the postwar emigrations faced the doctrine's other face in the campaigns of 1968 in Poland and in the anti cosmopolitan purges of the founding years, in which the vocabulary of class was used to conduct an ethnic purge and the community's remnant was driven to the airports with one way documents. The Hungarian minorities in Romania and Slovakia, the Turks of Bulgaria subjected in the 1980s to a forced assimilation campaign of renaming that drove a mass flight in the bloc's last summer, the Germans of Romania ransomed to the Federal Republic by the head across two decades: the catalogue is long, and its lesson is uniform. A doctrine that has solved the national question in theory cannot process its persistence in fact except as sabotage, and the minorities' experience of the systems, as objects of engineering rather than as citizens with claims, is the internal critique's corroboration from the populations with the least access to print. The critics themselves were slow, as a tradition, to take up the minorities' causes, the Charter's defence of the Roma being an honourable exception, and the slowness is recorded here as part of the tradition's limits, the same universalist blind spot that left the national question to inherit the region.

## What held the systems up so long

An article that has documented the risings, the critics, and the endings owes the reader a direct answer to the question the sceptic is entitled to ask: if the diagnosis was so widely shared and the consent so thin, why did the structures stand for four decades? The supports were several, and they were load bearing in combination. Coercion stood first, not as daily terror after the founding decade but as a known reserve, the demonstrated willingness of 1953 and 1956 and 1968 and 1981, which taught each generation the price of the open act and made the price's memory do the work of the act's repetition. The external guarantee stood behind the coercion, removing from every opposition the hope that persistence alone could prevail. Provision stood third: the social wage, the secure tenancy, the subsidised loaf, which gave the majority a stake in quiet that the critics' programmes could not outbid in the short term, and which the regimes defended as their title long after the ideology had faded. Performance stood fourth, the greengrocer's slogan generalised into a social technology, in which the universal pretence of assent deprived every potential dissenter of the knowledge of how many shared the dissent, so that the systems rested partly on a plural ignorance their own rituals manufactured. Genuine belief stood fifth and should not be omitted by a tradition of analysis that prides itself on fairness: the founding generation's faith, the gratitude of the schooled, the real achievements in literacy, health, and industrial capacity, supplied a residue of conviction that decayed by generational succession rather than by refutation. And habit stood last, the sheer institutional momentum of arrangements that organised every biography from the nursery to the pension, so that imagining their absence required an effort that the events of 1989, not any argument, finally supplied to millions at once. The internal critique's role in the eventual outcome was to work, decade by decade, on the fourth and fifth supports, dissolving the pretence by naming it and the belief by auditing it, so that when the first three supports were withdrawn or exhausted, there was nothing left to hold the structure up. That is a slower and less heroic account of how ideas end regimes than the regimes' enemies sometimes claim, and it has the advantage of fitting the dates.

## The poverty the statistics could not see

Hungary's reform milieu produced, alongside its economics, a sociology whose findings were as unwelcome as any philosophy, and the fate of István Kemény marks the limit of the tolerable in the bloc's most permissive state. Kemény, a sociologist formed in the Lukács circle's orbit, directed in the late 1960s the first serious empirical studies of poverty in a socialist country, documenting in the official categories' despite a stratum of the poor, disproportionately Roma, unskilled, and rural, whose existence the doctrine's account of the society had no room for, since poverty under socialism was by definition a survival in the course of disappearance. The studies' offence was methodological innocence: they counted. Kemény was driven from his institute, his work on the Roma suppressed, and in 1977 he left for Paris, where his research continued in exile. The episode generalises across the bloc's social sciences. Each system maintained institutes of sociology, restored after the Stalin years in which the discipline had been abolished as a bourgeois pseudo science, and each institute oscillated between its two functions: to measure the society for the apparatus's use, and to describe it for the record. The measurements kept escaping into descriptions, because a survey honestly conducted in a shortage economy returns data on privilege, on the second economy's true extent, on the population's actual beliefs, that no amount of editorial care could render harmless, and the bloc's sociology therefore lived in a rhythm of commission and suppression, its ablest practitioners accumulating the knowledge that made them, in the end, unemployable at home. The internal critique's evidentiary base rests substantially on this stratum's work, and the critics table's philosophers should be imagined with the sociologists standing behind them, holding the questionnaires.

## The boiler room university

Normalisation in Czechoslovakia invented, inadvertently, one of the era's emblematic institutions: the purged intellectual employed as a manual worker, in the boiler rooms, window cleaning brigades, breweries, and night watch posts to which the screenings of 1969 and 1970 consigned a generation of historians, philosophers, economists, and journalists. The punishment was designed as erasure, and it functioned, in the manner of the system's other designs, as its opposite. The boiler rooms became a university in exile inside the country: the stokers read, wrote, translated, and taught one another, held seminars in the flats to which the state had reduced them, and produced through the 1970s and 1980s a literature in typescript whose authors' day jobs were the regime's own commentary on their standing. The phenomenon was not confined to Prague. Poland's dismissed academics drove taxis and corrected proofs; Hungary's expelled sociologists surveyed for market research firms at the economy's margin; East Germany's disciplined pastors and teachers staffed the church institutions that became the opposition's shelter. The internal critique's sociology is incomplete without the boiler rooms, because they solved the tradition's material problem: a critic deprived of a post is a critic with time, a grievance, and a network of the similarly deprived, and a system that manufactures such persons by the thousand, in the name of punishing them, has built its opposition a campus and endowed it with leisure. When the Charter and its successors spoke with an authority out of proportion to their numbers, part of the explanation is that the signatories had had twenty years of enforced study in which to become formidable, at the state's expense and in the state's own heating plants.

## The underground church in Czechoslovakia

Czechoslovakia's Catholics supplied the bloc with its most clandestine institution, and its history corrects any impression that the region's religious resistance was a Polish monopoly. The Czechoslovak regime's assault on the church in the founding years was the bloc's most thorough outside Albania: the orders dissolved in a single night operation in 1950, the bishops imprisoned or interned, the seminaries throttled, the Greek Catholic church abolished by decree and its faithful assigned to another obedience. The response, across four decades, was a church that learned to live without buildings: priests ordained in secret, some of them married men ordained for the underground in a discipline Rome tolerated for the emergency, women religious teaching children in private flats, a samizdat of catechisms and theology that paralleled the secular second circulation and shared its couriers. Cardinal František Tomášek, appointed to Prague's see late and initially regarded by the opposition as the regime's acceptable candidate, grew in office into the normalisation era's most inconvenient churchman, lending his palace to the Charter's defence, receiving the crowds of 1988 who petitioned for religious freedom in the largest demonstrations the country had seen since 1969, and standing in November 1989 before the multitudes as the settlement his church had survived to see. The underground church's role in this account is the Czechoslovak form of the general law the Polish case stated at scale: the systems could administer every institution they created, but the institutions they merely failed to destroy became the shelters of everything they feared, and the map of the bloc's oppositions is, in large part, the map of the survivals.

## The exported unemployed: Yugoslavia's safety valve

Yugoslavia's distinctiveness included one institution no other European socialist state possessed at scale, and its effects ran through the federation's whole social history. From the 1960s the federation permitted, and then encouraged, the emigration of its workers to the factories of Western Europe, above all to the Federal Republic of Germany, under bilateral agreements that made Yugoslavia the only socialist country to export its unemployment openly. At the system's height the workers abroad numbered in the hundreds of thousands by any count and their remittances became a pillar of the federation's balance of payments and of its villages' housing stock, the half finished houses of the returnees standing in every landscape as the migration's architecture. The valve's political function was understood by all parties. It drained the pressure that the self managed economy's refusal to hire would otherwise have accumulated, it supplied the hard currency the federation's deficits required, and it gave a generation of Yugoslav workers the comparative knowledge of the two Europes that no propaganda could thereafter take from them, since a man who has worked in Stuttgart and in Zagreb holds the comparison of the systems in his own biography. The internal critics treated the migration as the experiment's confession: a workers state that exports its workers to capitalism has located the boundary of its model's employment, whatever its constitutions say. The Federal Republic's recruitment stop of 1973 closed the valve's widest opening, and the return of workers to an economy that could not absorb them belongs to the prehistory of the federation's last decade, in which the safety valve's closure and the debt's maturity arrived together.

## The party card: membership as career and as creed

The ruling parties of the bloc counted their members in the millions, and the meaning of the card varied so widely across the membership that the internal critics treated the variation itself as evidence. At one pole stood the believers, a diminishing company after the founding generation, for whom membership remained a creed and the party's failures a grief. At the other stood the pure careerists, for whom the card was the ladder's passport, without which the directorships, the foreign postings, the flats in the better districts were simply not applications that could be filed. Between the poles stood the era's characteristic figure, the member of neither faith nor ambition in particular, who had joined as one acquires a driving licence, because the institution expected it and the absence would be noticed. The critics' analyses handled the mass membership with more care than hostile propaganda did. Bahro, who had held the card and worked in the apparatus, insisted that the party contained the system's best potential reformers as well as its jailers, since it monopolised not only power but political education; the Polish opposition's strategists distinguished, in their appeals, between the apparatus and the rank and file, many of whom joined the union in 1980 in the dual membership the regime at first tolerated and then could not forgive. The membership's behaviour in the endings is the final datum. In 1989 the cards were returned, allowed to lapse, or quietly pocketed by the million, with a speed that measured the proportion of the membership for whom the card had been a creed all along. Institutions learn what they are made of at the moment of dissolution, and the parties of the bloc learned that they had been, for the most part, employment exchanges with anthems, a discovery their internal critics had announced, to general indifference, decades in advance.

## Favours, gifts, and the lubricant of shortage

The shortage economy generated a moral economy of its own, which the bloc's sociologists mapped and its satirists immortalised, and which belongs in this survey because it shaped the population's daily experience of the doctrine's claims. Where goods are allocated by queue and by list, access becomes a currency, and the currency circulates through networks of reciprocal favour: the butcher who keeps the cut back, the clerk who finds the form, the doctor's acquaintance, the mechanic's weekend, each obligation recorded in a ledger no one writes down and everyone balances. The networks were not corruption in the simple sense the official campaigns denounced, though the campaigns were periodic and the denunciations sincere in their way. They were the system's circulatory system, the means by which the plan's failures were repaired at the level of the household, and they taught their participants a practical sociology at odds with every official proposition: that the formal economy was a façade behind which the real one ran on connection, that equality before the counter was a fiction the counter's other side disproved daily, and that the apparatus's members, whose access was structural rather than occasional, stood at the network's centre as its bankers. The internal critics drew on this education in their readers. When the Budapest philosophers wrote of the dictatorship over needs, their audience did not need the concept explained, because the audience had spent the morning acquiring, through a cousin at the warehouse, the shoes the shops did not stock. A doctrine may survive its philosophers' refutation. It is in greater danger from the daily, unarguable instruction of its own counters, and the bloc's regimes, which understood the danger, alternated between campaigns against the networks and a tacit reliance on them to keep the population provisioned and quiet, an alternation their subjects learned to read as the seasons of a climate rather than as the policies of a government.

## Bulgaria's ending and the education of a loyalist

Bulgaria's path through 1989 completes the map of the endings and returns the article to the blank spaces in the critics table, because the bloc's most loyal member state produced its opposition last and its transition in the most managed form. Todor Zhivkov had ruled since the 1950s, a survivor whose longevity was itself a technique, and his Bulgaria had followed the Soviet line with a fidelity the other capitals found embarrassing in its completeness. The first independent organisation of the late period arose, as the taxonomy of this article would predict, where a measurable fact could not be administered away: the chlorine emissions drifting across the Danube from a Romanian chemical complex into the Bulgarian city of Ruse, whose mothers organised in 1987 and 1988 the first public protests of the Zhivkov era under the unanswerable slogan of air for their children. The ecological committee that grew from the protests supplied the nucleus around which, in the autumn of 1989, a Bulgarian opposition assembled, weeks before the palace removed Zhivkov himself in November, in a preemptive adjustment by his own Politburo that the street then converted into a negotiation. The Bulgarian sequence confirms the article's argument from its weakest case. Even where the internal critique had been thinnest, the pattern reasserted itself: an independent description of reality, the poisoned air of Ruse measured by its victims, became the first organisation the monopoly had not licensed, and the monopoly's own servants, reading the region's handwriting, chose to manage the ending rather than to defend the indefensible. The difference between Sofia and Bucharest in that season is the difference between an apparatus that retained the institutional capacity to dismiss its own leader and one in which the leader had consumed the institution, and the consequences of the difference were measured, in December, in the two capitals' streets.

## The novel as evidence: fiction inside the monopoly

The bloc's fiction writers performed, in the imagination, the same audit the philosophers performed in concepts, and their books reached readers the treatises never could. Milan Kundera's first novel, published in Prague in 1967, turned on a student joke, a postcard's ironic slogan reported to the authorities, that unmade its sender's life through the machinery of meetings, votes, and expulsions; the book was read across Czechoslovakia as a report on the moral physics of the administered world, and after the invasion its author was expelled from the party, stripped of his teaching post, and driven to the French exile from which his later work anatomised the regime of organised forgetting he had escaped. Ludvík Vaculík, a novelist who had joined the movement as a believer, wrote the era's most consequential piece of occasional prose, the manifesto of June 1968 that called, in two thousand words, for citizens to reclaim the institutions nominally theirs; the text was denounced by name in Moscow and Prague and signed by workers and scientists in numbers that announced a public the censors had insisted did not exist. György Konrád's novel of the welfare office rendered the dictatorship over needs as a caseworker's daily round, the state's kindness and the state's dossier bound in one folder. Bohumil Hrabal, whose books were banned and unbanned in rhythm with the thaws, perfected the monologue of the little man whose talk, digressive and unstoppable, constituted a republic the police could not enter. Georgi Markov, the Bulgarian writer who defected and broadcast his portraits of the Sofia court to the country he had left, was murdered in London in 1978 by a method involving a poisoned pellet, in an operation attributed to the Bulgarian security service; his posthumous sketches of the regime's summit remain the Bulgarian record's closest approach to an insider literature, produced at the price the taxonomy of this article reserves for those who address their own public directly.

Fiction's evidentiary status requires a sentence of care. A novel is not a document, and the historian who quotes Kundera as he would quote a ministry file commits a category error the critics themselves avoided. What the novels document is not events but the moral weather in which events were lived: the weight of the meeting, the price of the signature, the comedy of the queue, the exact tone in which a foreman or a caseworker wielded a small power absolutely. That weather is a historical fact of the first order, and for the bloc's societies it is recoverable from almost no other source, since the official record was written in the wooden language this article has described and the private record, diaries and letters, survived only in fragments. The regimes understood the novels' force better than some of their defenders did. They banned them in the original and hunted their typescripts, they expelled their authors from the writers unions that controlled access to print, and they thereby classified fiction, correctly by their own lights, as a branch of the internal critique: the branch that worked on the imagination, where a monopoly is hardest to police because its contraband is a way of seeing.

## The armed forces: the bloc's silent institution

Every system in the region maintained armed forces of a size that consumed a significant share of its resources, and the armies' political character followed the model's logic with one instructive variation in each country. The officer corps was selected and promoted through the same ladder as every other elite, party membership was the normal condition of advancement, and political officers paralleled the chain of command to ensure that the instrument of last resort understood itself as the party's instrument. Conscription, meanwhile, made the barracks a school of the regime for each male generation, an experience remembered across the region with a uniformity that memoirs record: the boredom, the bullying of juniors by seniors that several armies institutionalised, the political instruction whose formulae no participant was expected to believe and every participant was required to repeat. The armies' conduct at the system's moments of truth constitutes their contribution to this article's evidence. In Hungary in 1956 the army dissolved as a political instrument, units fraternising, handing over weapons, or standing aside while a minority fought; the revolution's suppression was therefore an entirely imported operation. In Czechoslovakia in 1968 the army was ordered to its barracks by its own government and obeyed, a discipline that spared the country a civil war and confirmed that the armed forces of a bloc state were not designed, and not permitted, to defend it against its allies. In Poland in 1981 the army stepped forward as the regime itself, the one case in which the institution of last resort governed openly. In Romania in December 1989 the army, after initial obedience, changed sides in the middle of the revolution and decided it, the conscripts and their generals making opposite calculations within the same forty eight hours. Yugoslavia's People's Army, the federation's proudest common institution and the partisan myth's living custodian, stood apart from the self management experiment as a centralised hierarchy exempt from the councils' logic, a reservation its theorists justified by the nature of war and its critics noted as the experiment's most eloquent boundary: associated labour governed the factory, and the barracks, like the party and the police, remained a zone where association stopped.

The variation across the five cases supports a general finding the internal critics stated in their own vocabularies. The armies of the bloc were reliable exactly in proportion to the foreignness of the enemy they were asked to fight. Against an external invader they would have fought, in several countries with conviction; against their own populations they were instruments of uncertain temper, which is why the regimes preferred the police for domestic work and the ally's divisions for the decisive occasions, and why the endings of 1989 turned so often on a single question asked in a single room: whether the men with the weapons would use them on the crowd outside. In Warsaw and Budapest the question was never put, because negotiation had already answered it. In Berlin it was put and answered in the negative by default, the order never given. In Bucharest it was answered both ways in three days. The systems had spent four decades building armies to guarantee their survival, and the guarantee, like the larger one that stood behind it, proved at the moment of testing to be a belief held by the guarantors' commanders, which evaporated with the belief that had sustained the whole.

## The working day: labour under the plan

The texture of labour in the bloc deserves a fuller account than the risings alone provide, because the workplace was where most citizens met the system most days, and where its promises and its practice kept their daily appointment. The formal order was uniform: employment assigned or chosen within a planned structure, wages set by tariff grids negotiated far above the shop floor, the trade union committee administering the social funds, the holiday vouchers, the sanatorium places, and the condolences, a welfare office wearing a union's name. Over this order lay the informal one every worker mastered: the brigade's internal bargains, the norm negotiated down by a plausible tale of broken tooling, the end of month storming when the plan's shortfall was chased in overtime and the quarter's figures were rescued by exertions the rest of the month had declined to supply. The rhythm of storm and slack was the plan's true calendar, and it taught a lesson no textbook could counteract: that output was a number to be produced for a report at least as much as a good to be produced for a user, and that everyone's effort was calibrated to the report's requirements. Absenteeism, the second shift in the private plot or the moonlight workshop, the long lunch and the early drift, were the workforce's counter calibration, an unorganised and unideological withdrawal of effort that the regimes denounced as indiscipline and relied upon as a safety valve, since a workforce permitted to steal time steals less dangerously than one that organises.

The moral economy of the shop floor had its own heroes and its own memory. The shock worker cults of the founding years, the bricklayers and miners whose records were staged as the new man's portrait, survived into folklore and then into irony; Wajda's bricklayer, exhumed by a journalist in the film this article has mentioned, became the era's emblem precisely because his trajectory, from poster to alcoholic ruin guarded by the apparatus that had made him, condensed the working class's official biography into one life. Against the staged record stood the real solidarities: the brigade that covered for its drunk member, the old hands who taught the apprentices the tricks by which the norm could be met without ruin, the foreman suspended between the office's demands and the floor's opinion, belonging fully to neither. The internal critics, most of them strangers to this world by biography, understood its importance imperfectly and said so; Haraszti's season at the milling machine was famous partly because it was exceptional, an intellectual apprenticing himself to the class his tradition claimed to speak for and reporting back that the class's own critique of the system, conducted in slowdowns and jokes and the hoarding of tricks, was as coherent as any seminar's, and considerably older.

## Nowa Huta and the panel estates: the built environment of the experiment

The systems built on a scale that still organises the region's geography, and the built environment is a document the historian can read without an archive's permission. The founding years raised whole cities as manifestos: Nowa Huta beside Kraków, laid out from 1949 as the model socialist town, its steelworks staffed by a young peasant workforce housed in avenues of a grandeur the regime intended as the future's portrait; Eisenhüttenstadt on the Oder, Sztálinváros on the Danube, and their sisters across the bloc, each announced as the proof that planning could compose a society as it composed a skyline. The sequel in Nowa Huta became the bloc's most quoted irony. The town built to manufacture a proletariat loyal to the system became a fortress of the opposition: its steelworkers struck in 1980 and again in 1988, its parishes, including the church the founders had omitted from the plan and the inhabitants fought a decade to build, sheltered the union's networks, and its Lenin statue was ritually abused by crowds who understood exactly which symbol they were addressing. The founders had not been wrong that a built environment forms its inhabitants. They had been wrong about the direction of the formation, having assumed that gratitude flows from provision, where the record shows that provision without voice forms critics as reliably as it forms clients.

The later decades built differently: the panel estates, prefabricated housing raised in every city from Leipzig to Sofia, which solved the housing famine of the postwar generation at the price of a monotony the era's humour processed relentlessly. The estates deserve a fairer reading than the jokes alone give. For millions they were the first indoor plumbing, the first kitchen of one's own, the first door that locked against the communal flat's enforced intimacies, and their inhabitants defended them against later condescension with some justice. Their sociology, however, cut against the design. The estates housed a cross section rather than a class, engineer and fitter and teacher on one staircase, and their courtyards and stairwells became the neighbourhoods in which the population compared notes on the system's performance, flat by flat and queue by queue. A regime that had intended housing as the social wage's most binding instalment discovered that it had also built, at continental scale, the dormitories of its own opposition, in which the educated and the manual strata, whom the doctrine assigned to different histories, discovered their common one over the shared boiler's failures.

## The demographic wager: the state as matchmaker and midwife

No field displays the reach of these systems' ambitions, or the ingenuity with which their subjects evaded them, more intimately than the politics of reproduction. The states needed births: for the armies, for the labour plans, for the national arithmetic by which several of them measured their standing against their neighbours and their minorities. They pursued births with the instruments at hand, and the instruments reveal the systems in cross section. There were the incentives, medals and titles for mothers of many children, priority in the housing queue for young couples, loans forgiven by instalments at each child, ceremonies in which the state presented itself as the family's senior partner. There were the restrictions, graded by country and decade, on contraception and on abortion, which the founding years had in several states liberalised as part of the emancipation of women and which the demographic anxiety of the later decades reversed. And there was, at the extreme, the Romanian decree of 1966, which prohibited abortion and contraception for most women at a stroke, policed the prohibition through the workplace and the clinic, and filled the country's orphanages with the generation the decree had mandated and the economy could not rear, an outcome documented by the regime's own institutions and by the foreign cameras admitted after its fall. The decree's Romania is the limiting case of the dictatorship over needs applied to the body: a state that had claimed jurisdiction over the wants its citizens might have extended the jurisdiction to the children they might bear, and discovered that even here the administered society produced its evasions, its networks, its tragedies, at a scale the plan had not provided for.

The other states mostly governed reproduction by the softer instruments, and their records supply the comparison that isolates the variable. Hungary, anxious about its falling birth rate across the whole period, built the bloc's most elaborate family policy of allowances, housing priority, and paid childcare leave, and permitted its demographers and sociologists to debate the causes in print, a debate in which the shortage of housing and the exhaustion of the double burdened mother recurred as explanations the official optimism could not dissolve. Poland's church made the family its defended province, and the regime's attempts to liberalise or restrict by turns were fought in a parliament of pulpits the state did not control. East Germany, competing with its twin for the allegiance of the young, assembled a package of marriage loans, housing preference, and childcare provision that demographers credited with real effects and that the republic advertised as socialism's answer to the nursery. The internal critics wrote less about this field than about any other of comparable weight, a silence the feminist writers of the region's samizdat noted and supplied, arguing that a tradition which could anatomise the factory and the ministry had left the household's division of labour and the state's claim on the cradle comparatively unexamined, because its theorists, like its rulers, were mostly men for whom the nursery's economics were somebody else's shift. The criticism stands in the record as the internal critique's own internal critique, and the article registers it in the same spirit as the others: the sharpest correction of a tradition, once again, composed by the people the tradition had itself educated.

## The telephone and the car: two queues that measured the system

Two waiting lists ran through the bloc's decades like a pair of thermometers, and the memoir literature reads them so consistently that the historian may treat them as instruments. The first was the list for a telephone. In economies where the network was a state monopoly and investment followed the plan's priorities, a private line was a good allocated by category and connection: the official's residence connected as a condition of office, the doctor and the journalist by professional claim, the ordinary applicant by patience measured in years and sometimes in decades, so that the telephone became a precise marker of a household's altitude in the administered order, and its absence a daily lesson in the distance between the citizen and the switchboard of power. The second list was for a car. The bloc's motor industries produced vehicles that their own comedians processed into folklore, and the lists for them, again measured in years, created a market in places, in patience, and in the nearly new machine sold at a premium by the man whose turn had come and whose need had gone, a market the state alternately prosecuted and tolerated as it tolerated every mechanism that repaired the plan's omissions. Around the two lists grew a penumbra of practices the sociologists catalogued: the application renewed, the official cultivated, the enterprise's allocation begged as a favour, the relative in the ministry remembered at the right season. The critics' use of the lists was characteristic. They did not argue that a society is condemned by making its citizens wait for a telephone; wealthier orders have their own queues, priced in money rather than in years. They argued that a society which allocates by status while preaching equality will be read, by its own subjects, in the allocation, and that the reading, repeated at every counter and on every list, was the most effective political education the systems provided, delivered free of charge to every household in the region, in instalments, for forty years.

The two lists also explain a feature of the endings that the political narrative alone leaves mysterious: the relief. When the crowds of 1989 demanded a normal life, the phrase carried a freight of telephones unconnected, cars undelivered, flats unallocated, and journeys unpermitted, the accumulated small change of forty years of administered scarcity, and the demand's vagueness was its accuracy, since what was being refused was not a policy but a civilisation of the queue. The internal critics had always insisted that the system's essence was visible in its smallest transactions, that a social order reveals its constitution at the counter more honestly than in its congresses. The crowds, in the end, agreed with them, and voted with their feet for the counter's abolition, which is why the article that began with a phrase ends with a waiting list: the distance between the two is the distance the whole argument has travelled, from the rulers' description of their world to the world's description of its rulers, written by the people who had stood in line inside both.

A reader who doubts that small goods carry large meanings should consider the passport, the third document of the administered life, kept not by its holder but by the authorities in several of these states and surrendered for each journey like a schoolchild's permission slip. To hold one's own passport, as a matter of routine and of right, was among the first demands raised in every capital in 1989, and among the first granted, because everyone understood what the document's custody had said about the custody of everything else.

## Misreadings this article has corrected

Five misreadings recur in the standard accounts, and the article has addressed each in its place; they are gathered here for the reader's convenience. The bloc was not uniform: the template was shared, the societies were not, and the variation decided how each ending went. The phrase actually existing socialism was not coined by critics: it started as the rulers' own description of themselves and was borrowed, with the emphasis reversed, by the people it was meant to silence. Marxist criticism inside these states was not absent or marginal: it was the most penetrating literature the systems produced, and its authors' fates are the evidence. Yugoslav self management was not cosmetic: the councils held real rights and the experiment's failures were the failures of a real construction, not of a façade. And 1989 was not a referendum in which the crowds voted for a rival doctrine: it was a refusal of the arrangements that existed, conducted by populations whose demands ran from honest elections to open borders to a normal life, and treating it as a doctrinal verdict flatters the victors and falsifies the crowds.

## For students and exam candidates

Hold the article's architecture and the answers will build themselves. The governing distinction is between a critic's analysis and a critic's programme: the analyses of Bahro, of Kuroń and Modzelewski, of the Budapest School and the Praxis philosophers were confirmed by the systems' own conduct, while their programmes were implemented nowhere whole, with Yugoslavia as the one partial construction. Learn the critics table as a chronology, name the work and the year and the consequence, and use the divided German case whenever a question invites comparison, stating your starting points before your verdict. The phrase's origin is the highest return single fact: an official self description borrowed by its critics. Practise the topic against the questions actually set on it through the ReportMedic explorer linked in the study section, and keep the figures discipline this article observes: the risings and the repressions are argued from the record, not from numbers no candidate can source.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The Yugoslav experiment is the most concrete institutional alternative in the whole record of the socialist states, and it teaches better than any thought experiment because it ran for four decades and left evidence. Set the self management law of 1950 beside the Praxis critique of it and let the seminar discover that the experiment's sharpest critics were its own philosophers, which opens the article's central pattern from a second door. The critics table works as a one session syllabus: assign each row to a student, require the text, its date, and its author's fate, and then ask the room what the final column, read down, proves. The strongest objection deserves a full session of its own, with Kornai's coherence argument stated by its ablest defender in the room before the Yugoslav answer is attempted, and the concession recorded on the board where it cannot be quietly withdrawn. End the unit by returning to the phrase itself, whose two lives summarise the whole subject in a single vocabulary item.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this subject is linguistic before it is archival. The untranslated shelf section of this article maps the imbalance: the Polish and Czech periodical literatures of the thaws, the journal's complete run in its home edition, the Hungarian sociological record, and the East German samizdat remain accessible only in their languages, and the Anglophone synthesis built without them should be cited with that caution attached. The archives, where open, have so far confirmed the internal critics on the central claim about the apparatus while complicating their picture of its unity, and the productive questions now concern the interstices: the second economy's true extent, the ladder's local operation, the boiler room generation's networks. Journalists covering any order that claims a monopoly of description will find the method section's four questions portable: who controls disposal, who takes the decisions, who is permitted to organise, and what befalls the person who asks aloud. The bloc's record rewards that portability, because its documents were written by people who learned the questions at first hand.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was actually existing socialism?

The name covers the states of Eastern and Central Europe ruled by communist parties from the settlement after the Second World War until 1989: Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, the German Democratic Republic, Romania, and Bulgaria inside the Soviet alliance, Yugoslavia outside it after 1948, and Albania at its margin. Legally the means of production were socialised, the economy was steered by central plans, and one party, fused with the state apparatus, held a monopoly of organised political life. Whether such a formation merits the word socialism is the very question the bloc's own Marxist dissenters argued over.
### Q: Where does the phrase actually existing socialism come from?

The wording started life as the regimes' own label for themselves, adopted in the early 1970s when the leaderships of the bloc presented permanence as maturity: whatever the ideal might promise, here was the thing as built. Writers in opposition took the label over and turned its stress around, treating the adjective as a confession that a distance separated the doctrine from the reality. That double origin is why the article treats the phrase as evidence and not only as a name: the defence coined the indictment's heading.
### Q: Who were the internal Marxist critics in Eastern Europe?

Begin with the Yugoslav vice president Milovan Djilas, whose book of 1957 named the bureaucracy a new class. Add the East Germans Wolfgang Harich, Robert Havemann, and Rudolf Bahro; the Poles Jacek Kuroń and Karol Modzelewski, whose open letter of 1965 remains the tradition's most systematic statement, and the philosopher Leszek Kołakowski; the Czech dialectician Karel Kosík; the Zagreb and Belgrade philosophers of the Praxis journal; and the Budapest circle of Ágnes Heller, Ferenc Fehér, and György Márkus. The reform economists, Šik, Brus, and the Hungarians of 1968, form the tradition's second wing, and the sociologists its quiet third.
### Q: What happened to those critics?

Removal came first: loss of the institute post, the university chair, the journal, the party card, imposed by resolutions whose administrative wording fooled nobody. Prohibition followed, the book unobtainable in its author's language while editions circulated abroad. Exile was the third instrument, sometimes a one way journey granted and then annulled, sometimes expulsion outright. Prison was reserved for those who organised, published without licence, or spoke to workers over the apparatus's head: Harich, Djilas, Bahro, Kuroń, and Modzelewski all served terms. In none of the central cases did the regimes attempt a printed refutation, although they owned every printing press in their territories.
### Q: What was Yugoslav workers self-management?

From June 1950 Yugoslav law vested the running of state enterprises in their workforces. An elected council for each firm picked the director, argued the plan, and split the net income among personal incomes, investment funds, and collective purposes. Jurists recast the underlying ownership as social rather than state property, and in time the arrangement spread to schools, hospitals, and housing. Its boundaries were set elsewhere: credit came from banks the federation influenced, prices and laws framed the choices, and the League of Communists surrendered nothing of its hold on political life, which is the combination that defines the experiment.
### Q: What did the Yugoslav Praxis philosophers criticise?

Their target was bureaucracy as a form of rule, at home no less than in the Soviet Union. Reading the early Marx on alienation, the philosophers of the Praxis circle held that transferring legal title to society changed little while an unaccountable apparatus still disposed of the product and of people's working lives; estrangement survived the expropriation of the expropriators. Applied to Yugoslavia, the standard indicted official privilege, the distance between council rights on paper and managerial power in practice, and the party's untouchable position. The authorities replied by starving the journal of funds, closing the Korčula school, and legislating the Belgrade professors out of their lecture halls in 1975.
### Q: What economic reforms were attempted in the bloc?

The Czechoslovak design associated with Ota Šik, phased in from 1965, would have let socially owned firms answer to prices and results while planners kept only the macroeconomic frame. Hungary's mechanism of 1968 went furthest in practice: compulsory output instructions disappeared for most firms, many prices moved within supervised bands, and bonuses followed profit, though the centre repeatedly reined the experiment back. Polish economists, Brus foremost among them, supplied the theory of a decentralised order steered by economic instruments, which their country debated for decades and adopted only in fragments. Each design stalled where it touched the apparatus's power to allocate.
### Q: What was the Prague Spring economically?

In economic terms the Prague Spring meant exchanging instruction for signals. Šik's model freed enterprises to sell what buyers wanted at prices that registered scarcity, broke up monopolies, tied incomes to performance, and gave councils of employees a genuine voice, while the plan shrank to setting proportions, investment priorities, and the rules of the game. The party's Action Programme wrote that design into an official platform in April 1968. Because allocation by command was the lever through which the apparatus governed daily life, a reform of the mechanism was unavoidably a reform of the regime, which is why August's invasion settled an economic question by military means.
### Q: Was Yugoslavia different from the rest of the bloc?

In two registers the answer splits. Around the enterprise and across everyday existence the contrast with the command economies was plain: councils with enforceable rights, shops that stocked goods, passports that opened borders, workers who could strike and be reported striking. Around the summit nothing had moved: a single party held the state, the police, and the last word, and used the last word against its own philosophers in 1975. Analysts therefore treat Yugoslavia as the bloc's nearest approach to a controlled comparison, one variable released and the decisive one held fast, with the outcomes sorted accordingly.
### Q: Why did the bloc collapse so quickly?

Speed followed structure. Every regime in the region rested, in the final analysis, on a pledge of rescue from Moscow, and the pledge was allowed to lapse in the late 1980s; every economy had run through the growth obtainable by adding inputs and could not obtain the other kind without freedoms the monopoly forbade; every treasury carried the previous decade's borrowing. Once Poland negotiated and Hungary opened its fence, the belief that the order was permanent, the belief on which daily obedience had rested, gave way everywhere at once, and institutions held up by belief fall at belief's speed.
### Q: How does the internal critique grade the systems' response to their critics?

It grades the response as testimony. On the internal critique's reading, an analysis is corroborated when the institution it describes behaves as the analysis predicts, and these institutions, possessing unchallenged command of print, broadcasting, and academic appointment, nonetheless reached for the cell, the expulsion order, and the withdrawn passport when their own Marxists described them. Bahro's book was not reviewed; its author was sentenced. The open letter was not debated; its authors were jailed before delivery. The critique therefore counts the manner of the answer as part of the answer, and finds in it the clearest confirmation that the monopoly, not any doctrine, was what had to be defended.
### Q: What should a student separate between a critic's analysis and a critic's programme?

Because a thinker can be right about what a thing is and wrong about what would work better, and the bloc's critics supply the century's cleanest examples. Their descriptions, of the apparatus's monopoly, of needs administered from above, of ownership without control, have been borne out by the regimes' behaviour and by the archives opened since. Their constructions, council republics, leagues of communists, markets disciplined by socialist law, were adopted nowhere in full and only in Yugoslavia in part. Mark the two performances separately, state the evidence for each, and resist the lazy inference in both directions: a failed prescription does not acquit the diagnosis, and a true diagnosis does not validate the cure.
### Q: Why were the most penetrating analyses written from inside the systems?

Proximity was the method. A foreign scholar of the bloc read statistics the ministries had cooked and met citizens the ministries had rehearsed; the insider had sat through the meeting where the announced decision turned out to have been settled in another room, had watched an issue of a journal vanish, had queued, had been processed. Formation mattered as much as observation: educated in the tradition's texts, often decorated servants of the movement, the critics could hold the founding promises in one hand and the administered reality in the other and compute the difference with instruments the regime itself had issued and could not dismiss as alien.
### Q: How does the critics table order the schools?

By date of publication, and the dates tell a story. The sequence opens in the 1950s with a serving vice president and a party publisher, men at the summit of the founding generation; it passes through the professors and editors of the 1960s; it closes with exiled philosophers and a Hungarian school publishing abroad in the 1980s. Read across the rows, the analyses diversify, humanist, economic, sociological, while the consequences column repeats its short vocabulary of dismissal, prohibition, exile, and prison, and that contrast between a widening literature and a monotonous reply is the table's argument in miniature.
### Q: What separates implementing a critic's programme from refuting a critic's analysis?

Implementation tests a prescription; refutation tests a description, and the two tests have different objects. Yugoslavia implemented workers councils at national scale, which bears on whether the council programme works, with mixed results this article records. It does nothing to refute the analysis that the apparatus monopolised disposal and organisation, which is tested against the record of how the systems behaved, including how they treated the implementers and the analysts. The strongest objection to the critics succeeds against parts of their programmes. Against their analyses it has still to be made.
