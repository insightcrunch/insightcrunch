The Commercial Courts Act is the statute that decides which business disputes in India are taken out of the ordinary civil queue and placed on a faster, stricter track, and its central lesson is that the difference between the two tracks is not the subject matter but the consequence of delay. A dispute qualifies by falling inside an enumerated statutory definition and by crossing a monetary threshold. Once inside, the suit is governed by a rewritten version of the Code of Civil Procedure in which pleadings must be verified by a statement of truth, documents must be disclosed at the threshold, mediation must be attempted before filing unless urgent interim relief is genuinely contemplated, and a defendant who allows one hundred and twenty days to pass from the service of summons loses the right to file a defence at all. Ordinary civil procedure in India treats most time limits as directory and extends them almost as a matter of course. The commercial regime was designed, deliberately and visibly, to do the opposite.

![The Commercial Courts Act 2015 explained, covering commercial disputes, the specified value threshold and strict commercial procedure - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-11.webp)

The Act rewards a reader who separates three questions that public discussion habitually merges. The first is a question of classification: what makes a dispute commercial, and who decides. The second is a question of forum: which court or division hears the suit, and where an appeal from it goes. The third is a question of procedure: what the amended Code demands at each step, and what is lost when a step is missed. This article takes the three questions in that order, states the contested points on mediation and on the forfeiture of the defence at full strength on both sides, and reaches a verdict on the regime as a whole. The verdict, stated at the outset so that the reasoning can be tested against it, is that the statute is best understood as a law about the enforceability of deadlines, and that its successes and its harshness flow from the same single design choice.

## Why Parliament built a separate track for business disputes

The statute was not a spontaneous invention. Its immediate ancestor was the 253rd Report of the Law Commission of India, which examined the proposal for commercial divisions in the High Courts and recommended a dedicated structure for high value commercial litigation, with judges experienced in commercial matters and a procedure designed for speed. Behind the Report stood a longer dissatisfaction, recorded in the Statement of Objects and Reasons, with the time taken to enforce contracts in India and with the signal that delay sent to investors. The Act was associated from its birth with the international assessment of business regulation published by the World Bank, in which the enforcement of contracts was a measured indicator and India performed poorly. That association explains features of the design that would otherwise look eccentric: the emphasis on measurable timelines, on case management by the court rather than drift by the parties, and on costs that follow the event so that unsuccessful litigation carries a real price.

The legislative route was hurried. The regime began as an Ordinance promulgated on 23 October 2015, and the Act that replaced it, the Commercial Courts, Commercial Division and Commercial Appellate Division of High Courts Act, 2015, received the assent of the President on 31 December 2015 as Act 4 of 2016. Section 1(3) deemed it to have come into force on 23 October 2015, the date of the Ordinance, so that nothing done under the Ordinance was disturbed. The same device of an October 2015 Ordinance followed by a year end Act appears elsewhere in the dispute resolution cluster of that period, and the appellate architecture described later in this article has to be read alongside the arbitration statute treated in the guide to the [Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996](/2021/05/01/arbitration-conciliation-act-1996-guide/), because orders under that Act form one of the two permitted streams of commercial appeal.

### What was the Act originally called?

The Act began life under a long descriptive name, the Commercial Courts, Commercial Division and Commercial Appellate Division of High Courts Act, 2015, which recited the institutions it created. The amending Act of 2018 shortened the short title to the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the name in general use.

The longer name still identifies the statute in older reports and pleadings, and it covered the Commercial Division and the Commercial Appellate Division of the High Courts together with the district level Commercial Courts.

The naming history matters for a practical reason. Research material written before the 2018 amendment uses the long title and the original monetary threshold, and material written after it uses the short title and the reduced threshold. A reader who does not fix which version of the statute a source describes will import the wrong threshold and, in some accounts, a wrong description of the appellate structure, because the 2018 amendment also created appellate courts at the district level and adjusted the appeal provisions. The statute is therefore best read as two layers: the 2015 layer, which built the forums and rewrote the procedure, and the 2018 layer, which widened the gate, added compulsory mediation before institution, and completed the appellate map.

The deeper purpose can be stated in one sentence, and the Statement of Objects and Reasons supports the statement. Commercial disputes were to be decided expeditiously, fairly and at reasonable cost, because the credibility of contract enforcement was treated as economic infrastructure, in the same class as roads or ports, rather than as a private service the courts happened to supply. That framing drove a further choice: the procedure was not left to be developed by rule committees and practice directions over time. Parliament wrote the procedure into a Schedule that amended the Code of Civil Procedure in its application to commercial disputes, and provided that the amended Code prevails over inconsistent High Court rules and over the unamended Code itself. The design was therefore centralised and textual from the start, which is one reason the courts have felt able to enforce it strictly: a judge applying the 120 day rule is applying words Parliament chose, not a practice the court developed and might relax.

## What counts as a commercial dispute

Section 2(1)(c) of the Act defines a commercial dispute by enumeration. The definition opens with disputes arising out of the ordinary transactions of merchants, bankers, financiers and traders, and then lists category after category: agreements relating to the export or import of merchandise or services; issues relating to admiralty and maritime law; transactions relating to aircraft and aircraft engines and helicopters; carriage of goods; construction and infrastructure contracts, including those relating to tenders; agreements relating to immovable property used exclusively in trade or commerce; franchising agreements; distribution and licensing agreements; management and consultancy agreements; joint venture agreements; shareholders agreements; subscription and investment agreements pertaining to the services industry, including outsourcing services and financial services; mercantile agency and mercantile usage; partnership agreements; technology development agreements; intellectual property rights relating to registered and unregistered trademarks, copyright, patent, design, domain names, geographical indications and semiconductor integrated circuits; agreements for the sale of goods or the provision of services; exploitation of oil and gas reserves or other natural resources, including electromagnetic spectrum; insurance and re-insurance; contracts of agency relating to any of the foregoing; and such other commercial disputes as the Central Government may notify. A dispute does not stop being commercial because it also involves the recovery of immovable property, or the realisation of money out of property given as security, or because one of the contracting parties is the State, a state agency or a private body carrying out public functions.

Two features of this drafting deserve emphasis. The first is that the list is a list of transaction types, not a description of the parties. A dispute between two individuals can be commercial if it arises out of a partnership agreement or a mercantile transaction, and a dispute between two large companies can fall outside the Act if it arises out of none of the listed categories. Classification follows the character of the transaction from which the dispute arises, and the plaint must be drafted with the relevant clause identified, because the court tests maintainability against the clause and not against the general impression that the litigation feels commercial. The second feature is the residuary power to notify other disputes, which makes the definition expandable by executive action without further amendment, a flexibility Parliament has used sparingly but which keeps the list from freezing at its 2015 content.

The intellectual property clause is the one with the widest practical reach, because it sweeps registered and unregistered rights alike into the regime, with consequences for forum, for mediation and for interim relief that practitioners in that field had to absorb quickly; the wider map of that subject is drawn in the guide to [intellectual property law in India](/2019/08/01/intellectual-property-law-india-guide/), to which this article defers on the substantive rights while retaining the procedural treatment here. The immovable property clause is the one that has generated the most classification litigation, and it is governed by a Supreme Court decision that repays close attention because it shows the judicial attitude to the whole definition.

In Ambalal Sarabhai Enterprises Limited v K.S. Infraspace LLP, decided on 4 October 2019 and reported at (2020) 15 SCC 585, the Supreme Court considered whether a dispute arising out of an agreement relating to land that the parties intended to develop commercially fell within the clause covering agreements relating to immovable property used exclusively in trade or commerce. The Court held that the word used must be read strictly, as meaning actually used or being used in trade or commerce. Property that was merely likely to be used for commerce, or that had been acquired with a commercial intention, did not qualify. The reasoning was purposive in the disciplined sense: the provisions of the Act, with their strict timelines and their forfeitures, were enacted for a defined class of disputes, and a loose reading of the definition would flood the special track with ordinary land litigation and defeat the object of speedy disposal for genuine commercial cases. The Gujarat High Court decision in Vasu Healthcare Private Limited v Gujarat Akruti TCG Biotech Limited, which had taken the same strict view, was approved in substance.

### Does a dispute between two companies always qualify as commercial?

No. The identity and size of the parties are not the test. A dispute between two companies qualifies only when it arises out of one of the transaction categories enumerated in Section 2(1)(c), such as a shareholders agreement, a distribution agreement or an ordinary transaction of merchants and traders.

A claim between companies that arises out of none of the listed categories proceeds as an ordinary civil suit, however large the parties or the amount in dispute may be.

Ambalal also carried a second lesson, about the specified value and the object of the Act read together. The Court treated the Statement of Objects and Reasons and the amendments to the Code as a single scheme directed at expeditious and fair disposal of commercial disputes at reasonable cost, and read the definitional provisions in light of that scheme. That approach, strict on the words of the definition and purposive on the scheme, recurs across the case law of the Act, and it explains results that can look inconsistent to a reader who expects either literalism or purposivism throughout. On entry conditions the courts are strict, because entry conditions allocate a scarce resource, namely the fast track. On the scheme itself they are purposive, because the scheme is the reason the entry conditions exist.

Classification disputes have a further practical edge. A plaintiff who files in the commercial track and fails on classification faces transfer or return of the plaint and the loss of the time spent, while a plaintiff who files an ordinary suit that ought to have been commercial faces objection at the threshold and the same disruption in reverse. The prudent course, reflected in the better drafted plaints, is to plead the clause of Section 2(1)(c) relied upon, the facts that bring the transaction within it, and the computation of the specified value, as distinct and numbered parts of the pleading, so that the classification can be tested and upheld on the face of the document rather than reconstructed in argument.


## The specified value: the monetary gate and its reduction in 2018

Entry to the commercial track requires two keys, and the definition supplies only the first. The second is the specified value, defined in Section 2(1)(i) as the value of the subject matter of the suit as determined in accordance with Section 12, which must not be less than the statutory floor or such higher value as the Central Government may notify. Section 12 directs attention to the relief claimed: where the relief is for money, the amount sought to be recovered; where it relates to movable or immovable property or to a right in such property, the market value; and, for intangible rights, the market value as estimated by the plaintiff, with the court fee valuation and the jurisdictional valuation working together under the applicable court fees law. The figure is therefore a pleaded and computable figure, not an impression, and a plaint that does not disclose its computation invites objection at the first hearing.

As enacted, the floor was one crore rupees. The figure was a deliberate policy choice: the new forums were to begin with genuinely high value disputes, the class in which delay was costliest and in which the case for specialised judges was strongest. The 253rd Report had contemplated a high threshold, and the Ordinance and the Act adopted it. The consequence, visible within the first two years of operation, was that the regime governed a relatively small number of very large suits, while the great mass of commercial litigation between smaller firms, traders and service providers continued under the ordinary procedure whose delays the Act had been passed to escape.

The Commercial Courts, Commercial Division and Commercial Appellate Division of High Courts (Amendment) Act, 2018, Act 28 of 2018, which received assent on 20 August 2018 and was deemed to have come into force on 3 May 2018, reduced the floor from one crore rupees to three lakh rupees. The reduction was the single most consequential change in the amending Act, larger in practical effect even than the insertion of compulsory mediation, because it multiplied the population of suits inside the regime. Disputes of a size routine in district level commerce, a supply contract gone wrong, an unpaid invoice chain, a dealership terminated, a consultancy fee withheld, became commercial disputes of a specified value, triable only under the amended Code with its statement of truth, its disclosure discipline and its 120 day defence deadline. Material written before the amendment that states the threshold as one crore rupees is wrong for any suit governed by the amended Act, and the persistence of the old figure in secondary writing is the most common factual error in the literature of this statute.

### What is the minimum specified value for a commercial suit?

The minimum specified value is three lakh rupees. Section 2(1)(i) fixes the floor at that figure, reduced from one crore rupees by the amending Act of 2018, and the Central Government may notify a higher value, which then operates as the floor in place of the three lakh figure.

A commercial dispute valued below the floor cannot be tried by a Commercial Court or a Commercial Division, whatever the character of the transaction, and proceeds instead under the ordinary civil procedure.

The reduction deserves a balanced statement, because it is defended and criticised in terms that are both coherent. The case for the reduction, made by the Government when the amendment was moved, is that the disciplines of the Act are not a luxury for large litigants but a remedy for a systemic disease, and that a trader suing for ten lakh rupees suffers from a five year suit in exactly the way a company suing for ten crores does, with fewer resources to endure it. On that view, restricting the fast track to crore level disputes reserved the cure for those least in need of protection. The case against, made by practitioners and by commentators on district court capacity, is that the 2018 amendment widened the gate without a matching widening of the forums: the same district judiciary, redesignated rather than reinforced in many States, was asked to run a far larger docket under far stricter timelines, and the predictable result in under resourced districts is either strain or quiet non enforcement of the timelines, which corrodes the statute more than a narrower gate would have done. Both accounts identify a real variable, judicial capacity, and differ on whether Parliament should have waited for it. The assessment section of this article returns to the point with a verdict.

One further consequence of the reduction is jurisdictional layering inside the district judiciary. Commercial Courts at the district level are constituted at the level of the District Judge and, following the 2018 amendment, courts below that level may also be constituted for lower value disputes in territories where the High Court exercises ordinary original civil jurisdiction, with Commercial Appellate Courts at the District Judge level hearing appeals from them. The specified value therefore does more than open or close a gate; it routes the suit within the commercial structure itself, and the routing differs between a territory whose High Court has ordinary original civil jurisdiction and one whose High Court does not, which is the subject of the next section.

## The forum map: courts, divisions and appellate tiers

The institutional structure has to be described territory by territory, because the Act builds on a constitutional peculiarity: a small number of High Courts, among them Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta, Madras and Himachal Pradesh, exercise ordinary original civil jurisdiction, trying civil suits directly, while in the rest of India original civil jurisdiction sits in the district courts. The Act respects that geography rather than overriding it. Section 3 empowers the State Government, after consultation with the High Court, to constitute Commercial Courts at the district level for areas where the High Court has no ordinary original civil jurisdiction, and, after the 2018 amendment, also for areas within a High Court's original jurisdiction, so that lower value commercial disputes in those territories have a district level forum below the Commercial Division. Section 3A, inserted in 2018, provides for Commercial Appellate Courts at the District Judge level to hear appeals from Commercial Courts below that level. Section 4 provides for a Commercial Division in every High Court exercising ordinary original civil jurisdiction, constituted by the Chief Justice, with judges nominated from among the High Court judges and expected to bring experience of commercial disputes. Section 5 provides for a Commercial Appellate Division, with one or more Division Benches, in every High Court, to hear appeals from Commercial Courts and Commercial Divisions alike.

The result is a structure with two trial tiers and two appellate tiers, aligned by value and territory. A commercial dispute of a specified value arising in a district without an original side High Court goes to the Commercial Court. A dispute arising within the original civil jurisdiction of a High Court goes to its Commercial Division if it satisfies that court's pecuniary jurisdiction, and to a Commercial Court below it if it does not. Appeals travel upward inside the commercial structure rather than into the general appellate stream, and the appellate provisions are examined in their own section below. The wider architecture of courts within which these forums sit, from the Supreme Court and the High Courts to the district judiciary and the tribunal layer, is mapped in the guide to [the judicial system and its governing laws](/2021/04/01/judicial-system-laws-india-guide/), which this article treats as the field reference and does not repeat.

Pending litigation was swept into the structure by Section 15, which required the transfer of suits and applications relating to a commercial dispute of a specified value, including applications under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, from ordinary civil courts and High Courts to the Commercial Court or Commercial Division once constituted, with two protections: a case in which final judgment had been reserved was not to be transferred, and the receiving court could hold case management hearings and set fresh timelines for the steps that remained, including, in its discretion, a fresh period for a written statement in a transferred suit to which the ordinary proviso would otherwise have applied awkwardly. The transfer provision is easily underestimated. It converted the Act from a statute for new filings into a statute for the existing docket in a single stroke, and much of the early case law on the amended Code arose in transferred suits, where the courts had to decide which procedural steps already taken survived and which had to be redone under the stricter regime.

### Which forum tries a commercial dispute in a city whose High Court has no original civil jurisdiction?

The Commercial Court at the district level tries it. Section 3 provides for Commercial Courts in territories where the High Court does not exercise ordinary original civil jurisdiction, and such a dispute never reaches the High Court at the trial stage at all.

Its appeal travels instead to the Commercial Appellate Division of the High Court, or, where the trial court sits below the level of a District Judge, first to the Commercial Appellate Court designated at that level.

Staffing and support were addressed, though more thinly than the 253rd Report had urged. The Act contemplates nomination of judges with commercial experience to the Commercial Division, and obliges governments to provide infrastructure and to arrange training for judges, officers and staff of the Commercial Courts and Divisions. The Report had proposed a more ambitious settlement, with demonstrable expertise as an appointment criterion and structured training through the judicial academies. The gap between the Report's institutional ambition and the Act's thinner provisions is the seed of most serious criticism of the regime, because a procedural code can be enacted in a session while a bench of experienced commercial judges cannot. That gap does not weaken the textual analysis in the sections that follow, but it conditions the assessment at the end: the statute's promises are procedural, and procedure is only as strong as the forum ordered to keep its timetable.


## Pre-institution mediation: the compulsory first step

The 2018 amendment inserted Chapter IIIA and Section 12A into the Act, and with them the most debated procedural gate in Indian civil litigation. Section 12A(1) provides that a suit which does not contemplate any urgent interim relief shall not be instituted unless the plaintiff has exhausted the remedy of pre-institution mediation, conducted in the manner prescribed by rules made by the Central Government, which are the Commercial Courts (Pre-Institution Mediation and Settlement) Rules, 2018. Section 12A(2) empowers the Central Government to authorise, by notification, the authorities constituted under the [Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/) for the conduct of that mediation, and in practice the District Legal Services Authorities receive and process the applications. Section 12A(3) requires the authorised authority to complete the mediation within three months from the date of the plaintiff's application, permits an extension of a further two months with the consent of the parties, and directs that the period spent in the mediation shall not be counted toward limitation under the Limitation Act, 1963. Section 12A(4) requires a settlement to be reduced to writing and signed by the parties and the mediator, and Section 12A(5) gives such a settlement the same status and effect as an arbitral award on agreed terms under Section 30(4) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which makes it enforceable as a decree without a fresh suit. Where the opposite party does not join the process, the authority closes the attempt with a report recording that the mediation did not start because of non participation, and the plaintiff, having exhausted the remedy the section demands, may then institute the suit. The general mediation statute that later reorganised the field is treated in the article on the [Mediation Act 2023](/2021/07/01/mediation-act-2023-explained/), which owns that subject; the treatment here is confined to Section 12A as a gate on the institution of commercial suits.

The design has an obvious internal tension, and the brief for the statute requires both sides to be stated at strength. The case for compulsion is made by the provision's defenders in the language of the provision itself. A settlement attempt before filing costs a fraction of a suit; the limitation clock is stopped for the duration, so a plaintiff loses nothing by attempting it; the settlement, if reached, carries the force of an award, so nothing is wasted; and experience under voluntary schemes showed that an option which parties may decline at no cost is declined almost always, so that mediation without compulsion was a facility that existed on paper and settled almost nothing. On that account, compulsion is not an obstacle placed before the court door but the activation condition of a remedy that voluntariness had left dormant, and the three month period is short against the life of the suit it may replace.

The case against is made with equal force by commercial practitioners who run the process. A plaintiff facing a defendant who has no intention of settling, a debtor using every procedural day as free credit, is required to spend up to three months, and five with an extension the defendant's consent can be used to spin out, on a formality whose outcome is known in advance. The defendant's rational strategy inside a compulsory process is delay, because delay is the asset the process hands him. Worse, the urgency exception invites distortion: plaintiffs who wish to file at once are tempted to plead urgent interim relief that the suit does not genuinely need, so the requirement deforms pleadings across the whole docket, manufacturing urgency in cases that have none and forcing courts to police the boundary. Both accounts are accounts of incentives, and they are not reconcilable by more careful drafting of the period or the form, because they disagree about the behaviour the gate produces.

The Supreme Court resolved the threshold legal question, whether the requirement is mandatory at all, in Patil Automation Private Limited v Rakheja Engineers Private Limited, decided on 17 August 2022 and reported at (2022) 10 SCC 1. High Courts had divided on whether Section 12A was a mandatory precondition or a directory step whose omission could be cured. The Court held that the section is mandatory: the design of the Act as amended in 2018, the use of the words shall not be instituted, and the object of diverting commercial disputes to settlement before they harden into suits, together left no room for a directory reading. A suit instituted in violation of the mandate is liable to have its plaint rejected under Order VII Rule 11 of the Code, and the court may exercise that power of its own motion. Mindful that mediation infrastructure had been uneven across the country and that litigants had filed in reliance on the divided case law, the Court directed that the rejection consequence operate from 20 August 2022, so that suits instituted before that date were not destroyed by a declaration they could not have anticipated. In Dhanbad Fuels Private Limited v Union of India, the Court later reaffirmed both the mandatory character of the section and the prospective operation of the rejection consequence, approving an approach that kept an earlier suit in abeyance and directed time bound mediation rather than rejecting a plaint filed before the Patil Automation date.

### Can a defendant force rejection of the plaint for skipped mediation?

Yes, where the suit was instituted on or after 20 August 2022 without urgent interim relief genuinely contemplated and without the mediation having been exhausted. The defendant may apply under Order VII Rule 11 for rejection of the plaint, and the court may also act of its own motion.

That is the declaration made in Patil Automation, and it is the reason the mediation application, or a properly founded claim of urgency, has become a standard part of the commercial plaint file.

The second question, what counts as urgent interim relief, was left open in Patil Automation and answered in Yamini Manohar v T.K.D. Keerthi, decided on 13 October 2023 and reported at (2024) 5 SCC 815. The Court held that the words contemplate any urgent interim relief require the court to examine the plaint, the documents and the circumstances of the suit as a whole, from the standpoint of the plaintiff, and to ask whether the need for urgent interim intervention is genuinely disclosed. A prayer for interim relief inserted as a device to circumvent Section 12A is to be seen through, and deception or falsity on the point, where apparent or established, defeats the exemption. At the same time, the Court was careful to protect the plaintiff whose urgency was real when the suit was filed: the refusal of interim relief at the ad interim stage does not by itself establish that no urgency was contemplated, and does not justify rejection of the plaint. Urgency is judged at institution, on the material the plaintiff then had, and not with the benefit of the hearing's outcome. A continuing infringement, a threatened dissipation of the very assets from which a decree would be satisfied, or an ongoing breach that compounds daily are the classic instances in which the exemption holds; a stale claim dressed with an injunction prayer, after a long and unexplained silence following a notice, is the classic instance in which it fails.

The two decisions together produce a defensible settlement of the debate, and it is worth saying plainly why. The mandatory rule in Patil Automation answers the dormancy problem identified by the provision's defenders: mediation is attempted in every non urgent case, because the price of skipping it is the plaint. The scrutiny rule in Yamini Manohar answers the distortion problem identified by the critics: the urgency plea is examined on its substance, so the escape route is open to genuine urgency and closed to drafting. What neither decision can answer is the delay objection in the individual hard case, the creditor who knows the debtor will not settle. For that plaintiff the three months are a real cost, imposed by a legislature that judged the aggregate gain in settlements and docket relief to be worth the individual loss. That is a legislative judgment of a familiar kind, and the courts, having enforced it, have at least confined it to its statutory measure: the period is fixed, the extension needs consent, the limitation clock is stopped, and a non participating defendant produces a prompt closure rather than an open ended wait.


## Pleadings discipline: the statement of truth and the disclosure regime

The amended Code begins its discipline at the moment of filing, before any question of defence arises. Order VI Rule 15A, inserted by the Schedule to the Act, requires every pleading in a commercial dispute to be verified by a statement of truth, in which the party, or a person authorised to act for the party, declares that the statements in the pleading are true to the declarant's knowledge, information or belief, distinguishing between what is known and what is believed, and acknowledges that consequences follow from false statements. A pleading not verified in accordance with the rule is liable not to be taken on record, and a party who has not verified cannot rely on the pleading as evidence or speak from it at trial in the ordinary way. The device is borrowed from English practice, where the statement of truth disciplines pleading by attaching a named individual's responsibility to the document, and its function in the Indian regime is identical: it converts the plaint from a lawyer's instrument into a document a responsible officer has owned, and it makes tactical overstatement a personal risk rather than a free option.

Disclosure is the second threshold discipline, and the more consequential of the two. Order XI of the Code, as substituted in its application to commercial disputes, requires the plaintiff to file, with the plaint, a declaration on oath disclosing and producing all documents in the plaintiff's power, possession, control or custody pertaining to the suit, and to state which of them are relied upon, which relate only to a matter of background, and which the plaintiff declines to produce and on what ground. The defendant owes the corresponding duty with the written statement, and each side must then inspect the other's documents and file an affidavit of admission and denial, accepting or disputing the existence, authenticity and correctness of each document disclosed. The sanction is the heart of the scheme. A party is not permitted to rely on a document that was in its power, possession, control or custody and was not disclosed, except with the leave of the court, and leave is not a formality: the party must establish a reasonable cause for the non disclosure, a standard the courts apply with the same strictness they apply to the written statement deadline, because a generous leave practice would dissolve the rule just as surely as a generous extension practice would dissolve the deadline. Urgent filings are given a narrow window to complete disclosure shortly after institution, but the exception is confined to the case of urgency that justified the filing and does not reopen the general duty.

The practical effect is to move the centre of gravity of a commercial suit to its first weeks. In ordinary procedure, documents surface gradually, often late, sometimes at the evidence stage, and adjournments to deal with surprises are part of the rhythm of the litigation. In the commercial regime the documentary record is substantially fixed at the pleadings stage, the admission and denial process identifies what is genuinely disputed, and the case management hearing that follows can therefore plan a trial on a known record rather than a guessed one. Counsel's working method has changed accordingly, and the assembly of the disclosure bundle, indexed, paginated and cross referenced to the paragraphs of the pleading, is treated in better run chambers as the principal drafting task of the suit; a practitioner reviewing such a bundle for a hearing will often keep the indexed set open in a [PowerPoint viewer for paginated case materials](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) so that each document can be called up in the order in which the pleading cites it. That working detail is worth recording because it captures what the regime demands in practice: the case is built once, at the start, in documentary form, and everything later is argument about a record that already exists.

## The written statement: thirty days, one hundred and twenty days, forfeiture

The provision around which the whole regime turns is the substituted proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 of the Code, mirrored in Order V Rule 1. A defendant in a commercial dispute must file the written statement within thirty days from the date of service of summons. If the defendant fails to file within those thirty days, the court may allow filing on another day, for reasons recorded in writing and on payment of such costs as the court deems fit, but not later than one hundred and twenty days from the date of service of summons. On the expiry of that period, the defendant forfeits the right to file the written statement, and the court shall not allow a written statement to be taken on record. A further proviso to Order VIII Rule 10 removes any residual doubt by providing that no court shall make an order extending the time provided for filing the written statement. The drafting is a closed system: a period, a grace period with conditions, a hard stop, an express bar on extension, and a stated consequence. Every element that the courts had used in ordinary suits to soften the unamended rule, discretion, inherent power, the interests of justice, is addressed and excluded by a corresponding element of the text.

The Supreme Court read the text as it was written in SCG Contracts India Private Limited v K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure Private Limited, decided on 12 February 2019 and reported at AIR 2019 SC 2691. The defendant there had been served on 14 July 2017 and filed its written statement on 15 December 2017, beyond the one hundred and twenty days, after the trial court had granted a short further period on costs. The Supreme Court held that the amended provisions are mandatory, that the right to file is forfeited on the expiry of one hundred and twenty days from service, that the court has no power to take a later written statement on record, and that the inherent powers under Section 151 of the Code cannot be used to override the express bar, because inherent power exists to supplement the Code and not to contradict it. The judgment addressed the older authorities on the unamended Order VIII Rule 1, in which the period had been held directory, and confined them to the unamended text: those cases construed a provision without a forfeiture clause and without a bar on extension, and their reasoning could not survive the insertion of both. A three judge bench affirmed the position in Desh Raj v Balkishan, decided on 20 January 2020, holding that the amended provisions apply with their mandatory force to commercial disputes, while suits outside the commercial regime continue to be governed by the unamended Code and its more forgiving case law. The line the two decisions draw is jurisdictional in character: the same Code contains two written statement regimes, and which one applies depends on the classification and value questions settled at the start of this article.

### Why can the court not extend the written statement period on grounds of fairness?

Because Parliament removed the power. The proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 fixes one hundred and twenty days from service of summons as the outer limit and states the forfeiture in terms, and the proviso to Order VIII Rule 10 forbids any order extending the time.

In SCG Contracts the Supreme Court held that Section 151 inherent powers cannot be invoked against an express statutory bar, so fairness arguments must be addressed to the legislature rather than to the court.

The forfeiture provision attracts the strongest counter reading in the statute, and the stance of this article requires it to be put at full strength. The case against strictness is that a defence is not a favour but the substance of adjudication, and that a defendant may miss the period for reasons outside its control: summons served at a registered office during a change of management, records held by a third party or a government office, counsel's default, illness, a strike, a portal failure on the final day. In ordinary procedure the court's discretion exists precisely for these cases, and a rule that converts every failure into a lost defence will, in some fraction of cases, produce decrees that do not reflect the merits and that a later generation may regard as the regime's disgrace. The hardship is aggravated because the consequence is not a costs order or a procedural disadvantage but the loss of the right to be heard on the facts at all, leaving only cross examination and legal argument on the plaintiff's case.

The case for strictness, which the courts have accepted, is that the discretion was tried and measured. Under the unamended Code the written statement period was the single most exploited delay device in civil litigation, extensions were routine to the point of being expected, and a suit could spend years at the pleadings stage while the defendant's position improved with time. A deadline that yields to a good reason will yield, in practice, to a well drafted reason, and the trial court deciding extension applications has neither the time nor the information to separate the deserving from the tactical at the scale the docket requires. Parliament's answer was to give a generous envelope, thirty days as the ordinary entitlement and ninety more on recorded reasons and costs, which is four months from service for a document whose contents the defendant largely knows, and to make the boundary of the envelope final. The Supreme Court's reasoning in SCG Contracts is best read as deference to that calibration rather than as enthusiasm for forfeiture: the balance between expedition and indulgence had been struck by the legislature in express words, and judicial softening would not improve the balance but merely relocate it to a forum that had already demonstrated it could not hold it.

The verdict of this article on the point is that the strict reading is correct and should be maintained, with one qualification that the case law already contains. Strictness is defensible because the envelope is long, the trigger is service rather than filing, and the obligation is known in advance to every commercial litigant; it would cease to be defensible if service itself were defective or if the period were short, and the courts rightly continue to examine service with care, since the entire forfeiture machinery runs from the date of service of summons. Within those limits, a discretionary revival power would not be a safety valve but a repeal, and the experience of the unamended Code is the evidence.


## Case management, trial and the ninety day judgment rule

Once pleadings and disclosure are complete, Order XVA of the Code, an Order inserted by the Act, moves the suit into active judicial management. The court holds a case management hearing, the first of which is to take place within four weeks of the filing of the affidavits of admission and denial of documents by all parties. At the hearing the court examines the pleadings and documents, frames or refines the issues, fixes dates for the stages that remain, including the filing of evidence affidavits, the cross examination of witnesses and the hearing of arguments, and records the schedule in an order that binds the parties. The Order arms the court with sanctions for non compliance with the schedule, ranging from foreclosing a party's right to file documents or evidence, to imposing costs, to deciding the suit on the material available, and it directs the court to conduct the trial, so far as practicable, on a day to day basis once evidence begins. Parties may seek modification of the schedule, but the premise of the Order is that the timetable belongs to the court and not to the convenience of counsel, a premise ordinary Indian civil practice had inverted for generations.

The closing discipline of the trial stage is the judgment rule. Order XX Rule 1, as applied to commercial disputes, requires the Commercial Court, the Commercial Division or the Commercial Appellate Division, after the hearing concludes, to pronounce judgment within ninety days of the conclusion of arguments, and the registry is to furnish copies of the judgment to the parties by electronic mail or otherwise as the provision directs. A reserved judgment in a commercial suit is therefore not an open ended state. The ninety day rule matters beyond its own terms because it completes the statute's theory of time: every stage from institution to judgment carries a stated period and a consequence, mediation before filing, thirty and one hundred and twenty days for the defence, disclosure at the threshold, a court fixed schedule for trial, sixty days for appeal, and ninety days for the judgment itself. A litigant can map the entire life of a commercial suit on a single page, which is precisely the artifact this article sets out below, and the fact that the map can be drawn at all is the clearest contrast with ordinary procedure, where no equivalent page exists because the periods are nominal and the consequences are negotiable.

## Summary judgment under Order XIIIA

Order XIIIA, inserted by the Act, gives the commercial courts a power ordinary Indian procedure had never possessed in general form: the power to decide a claim without recording oral evidence. An application may be made by either party at any time after summons has been served on the defendant, provided it is made before the court has framed the issues in the suit, and the Order does not apply to a suit originally filed as a summary suit under Order XXXVII. The test in Rule 3 is in two cumulative parts. The court must consider that the plaintiff has no real prospect of succeeding on the claim, or that the defendant has no real prospect of successfully defending the claim, as the case may be, and that there is no other compelling reason why the claim should not be disposed of before the recording of oral evidence. The court may then give judgment for or against a party, dismiss the application, strike out an issue, or make a conditional order, for example requiring a party to deposit money or furnish security as the price of continuing to trial, where the defence is shadowy but not hopeless.

The phrase no real prospect is the operative standard, and it is deliberately set between two familiar poles. It is more than an arguable case, because a defence that is merely stateable but bound to fail on the documents does not have a real prospect. It is less than a probability, because the court is not conducting a mini trial on affidavits and must not resolve genuine contests of fact or credibility that oral evidence exists to test. The Delhi High Court's treatment of the Order, in decisions examining admitted invoices, acknowledged guarantees and documentary defences, has worked out the standard's practical content: summary judgment thrives where the record is documentary and undisputed and withers where the outcome turns on contested fact. The Supreme Court's discussion of the scheme in Ambalal Sarabhai, though concerned with classification, supplies the purposive frame, namely that the procedural innovations of the Act are to be understood in a manner that facilitates expeditious and fair disposal at reasonable cost, and a reading of Order XIIIA that shrank it into a duplicate of the summary suit would fail that direction.

### When should a party apply for summary judgment rather than wait for trial?

The application belongs to the case whose outcome is written in its documents: the admitted invoice unpaid, the guarantee whose execution is not denied, the defence that contradicts the defendant's own correspondence. It must be moved after service of summons and before issues are framed.

It should be accompanied by the disclosed record rather than by fresh material. Where a genuine factual contest needs oral evidence, the application wastes the very time the Order exists to save, and courts mark the failure in costs.

Commentators agree, though they differ on the remedy, that summary judgment remains under used relative to its design. Part of the explanation is professional habit: counsel trained in a system without the device reach for it late or not at all. Part is judicial caution, since a summary judgment reversed on appeal costs more time than a trial would have done, and trial courts know it. And part is the conditional order, which offers a middle course so attractive that it absorbs cases that might otherwise have ended at the Order XIIIA stage. The device still performs a function even when it fails, because an application forces the respondent to state its real case on affidavit at an early stage, and that statement then frames the issues and the trial. Measured by closures alone the Order looks modest; measured by its effect on the cases it touches, it is one of the regime's working parts.

## Costs: the event, the actual bill and the duty to give reasons

Costs in ordinary Indian civil litigation were, for most of the Code's life, nominal, token amounts bearing no relation to the expense of the suit, so that a successful party recovered a judgment and a fraction of the cost of obtaining it, and an unsuccessful party risked little beyond its own expenditure. The Act replaced that settlement for commercial disputes by substituting Section 35 of the Code in its application to them. Under the substituted provision the court has discretion to determine whether costs are payable by one party to another, the quantum of costs and when they are to be paid, and costs include the fees and expenses of witnesses, legal fees and expenses, and any other expenses incurred in connection with the proceedings. Section 35(3) lists the considerations the court must weigh, among them the conduct of the parties, whether a party succeeded on part of its case, whether a reasonable offer to settle was made and refused, and whether the proceedings were frivolous or vexatious in whole or part. The general rule the provision embodies is that costs follow the event, meaning the unsuccessful party pays, and a court that departs from an award of costs against the unsuccessful party must record its reasons for doing so.

The change is behavioural before it is financial. A costs regime in which the loser pays the winner's actual, assessed expenditure alters the arithmetic of speculative claims and of tactical defences alike, because both are priced. It reinforces the rest of the scheme at every point: the party who pleads urgency falsely, who withholds documents and seeks leave, who lets the written statement period run and litigates the forfeiture, or who resists a summary judgment application on a hopeless defence, does so in a forum that can charge the episode to the party responsible for it. Costs orders in the commercial divisions have accordingly become a real subject of advocacy, with parties filing statements of costs and courts assessing them, a practice the older nominal regime never generated. The provision also completes the answer to the objection that strict deadlines are unjust: a regime that prices misconduct and delay has less need to excuse them, because the party injured by an opponent's default is compensated in money even where the procedural consequence falls on the defaulter.

## Appeals: sixty days, two lists and a closed door

Section 13 governs appeals, and its structure mirrors the rest of the Act: a defined route, a short period and a closed list. As substituted by the 2018 amendment, Section 13(1) allows a person aggrieved by the decision of a Commercial Court below the level of a District Judge to appeal to the Commercial Appellate Court, and Section 13(1A) allows a person aggrieved by the judgment or order of a Commercial Court at the level of a District Judge exercising original civil jurisdiction, or of a Commercial Division of a High Court, to appeal to the Commercial Appellate Division of that High Court, in each case within sixty days from the date of the judgment or order. The proviso to Section 13(1A) then confines appeals from orders to those specifically enumerated in Order XLIII of the Code, as amended by the Act, together with orders appealable under Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, the second of the two permitted streams noted at the start of this article. Section 13(2) closes the door behind the list: notwithstanding any other law or the Letters Patent of a High Court, no appeal lies from any order or decree of a Commercial Court or Commercial Division otherwise than in accordance with the Act. Section 14 adds the appellate timetable, directing the Commercial Appellate Division to endeavour to dispose of the appeal within six months of its filing. Section 8 bars any civil revision application or petition against an interlocutory order of a Commercial Court, including an order on jurisdiction, and directs that such a challenge be raised, subject to Section 13, only in an appeal against the decree. The High Courts have held that Section 8 does not extinguish the supervisory jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 227 of the Constitution, which cannot be removed by statute, but that the jurisdiction survives in a narrowed form and is to be exercised sparingly and only in exceptional circumstances in commercial matters, the position taken by the Delhi High Court in Black Diamond Trackparts Private Limited v Black Diamond Motors Private Limited.

The Supreme Court construed the closed list strictly in Kandla Export Corporation v OCI Corporation, reported at (2018) 14 SCC 715. The question was whether an appeal that Section 50 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 did not permit could still be maintained under Section 13 of the Commercial Courts Act. The Court held that it could not: Section 13 is in two parts, a main provision conferring the appeal and a proviso carving the permitted orders down to the two enumerations, and the Commercial Courts Act could not be used to enlarge the appellate rights given by the arbitration statute that governed the order. The reasoning is the appellate counterpart of SCG Contracts: where Parliament has written a closed list and a bar in express words, the court applies them, and grievances about the narrowness of appellate review in commercial matters are grievances to take to the legislature.

The practical consequence is a litigation culture in which the trial is the main event. Interlocutory skirmishing, the ordinary suit's principal industry, is starved from three directions at once: the disclosure regime fixes the record early, the case management order fixes the schedule, and the appeal provisions deny most interlocutory orders an audience until the final decree, by which time the point is usually spent. A party that loses a procedural battle in a commercial suit must ordinarily win the war or fold the point into the appeal from the decree, and that single fact changes settlement behaviour more than any exhortation to expedition could have done.


## Jurisdiction, the statutory bars and the arbitration interface

Classification and value open the gate; the jurisdiction provisions decide what happens on either side of it. Section 6 gives the Commercial Court jurisdiction to try all suits and applications relating to a commercial dispute of a specified value arising within its territorial limits, and Section 7 performs the corresponding function for the Commercial Division in a High Court with ordinary original civil jurisdiction, extending to the whole territory over which that jurisdiction runs. Section 10 routes arbitration matters into the same structure: applications and appeals under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 arising out of a commercial dispute of a specified value are filed before the Commercial Court at the district level or, where the subject matter is an international commercial arbitration or arises within an original side territory, before the Commercial Division, so that the court supervising an arbitration and the court trying a commercial suit are the same specialised forum. Section 11 states the outer boundary: the Commercial Court and the Commercial Division are barred from entertaining a suit or proceeding relating to a commercial dispute in respect of which the jurisdiction of the civil court is expressly or impliedly barred under another law, or which another body is empowered to determine. That bar is what keeps bank recovery proceedings governed by the debt recovery legislation, matters committed to the company law tribunal, and disputes committed to sectoral regulators and specialised tribunals in their own forums, even where the underlying transaction would otherwise answer the description in Section 2(1)(c). The definition is wide, but it operates inside a legal system that had already assigned many commercial subjects elsewhere, and Section 11 is the provision that respects those assignments.

A counterclaim inside a commercial suit follows the suit into the regime. The original Act contained a transfer provision for counterclaims that the 2018 amendment omitted as unnecessary, and the settled position is that a counterclaim relating to a commercial dispute is tried by the forum seized of the suit, valued on its own subject matter for court fee and specified value purposes, and governed by the amended Code, including the disclosure discipline and the written statement timetable applied to the pleading that answers it. Mediation doctrine has had to work out the corresponding Section 12A question, and the Delhi High Court has held that a counterclaim in a commercial dispute is in substance a fresh suit for procedural purposes, so that the mediation obligation can attach to it as it would to a plaint, subject always to a genuine contemplation of urgent interim relief.

The jurisdiction provisions also explain a recurring source of confusion in practice, the difference between a suit that is commercial in character and a suit that is merely filed in a commercial court. The forum's authority depends on both keys turning, the enumerated character of the dispute and the specified value, and an order deciding jurisdiction either way is an interlocutory order within the meaning of Section 8, challengeable not by revision but in the appeal against the decree or, where the order is within the permitted enumerations, by the appeal Section 13 allows. Parties therefore plead classification and value with the care described earlier, because an error does not merely send the file to another courtroom; it can unwind months of procedure conducted under the wrong code.

## How the specified value is determined

Section 12 repays reading in full because it converts the threshold from a slogan into a computation. Where the relief sought is recovery of money, the specified value is the money sought to be recovered. Where the relief relates to movable property or to a right in it, the value is the market value of the property on the date of the suit. Where the relief relates to immovable property or a right in it, the market value again governs. Where the right sought to be enforced is an intangible right, the value is the market value of the right as estimated by the plaintiff. A counterclaim is valued by the same heads, applied to its own subject matter. The plaintiff's estimate is not beyond control: the court fee payable follows the applicable court fees legislation on the same valuation in the ordinary case, a plaintiff who undervalues to enter or to avoid a forum invites objection and correction, and the court is not bound to accept a figure that the plaint's own contents contradict.

Three practical consequences follow. The first is that valuation is a drafting act with jurisdictional consequences, and the computation belongs on the face of the plaint, head by head where more than one relief is claimed. The second is that interest, damages and pendente lite claims are framed with the threshold in mind, because the amount sought to be recovered is the amount the plaint seeks, stated in the prayer and supported in the body. The third is that the three lakh floor, low as it is, still excludes a real class of disputes, the very small trade claims that fill the ordinary district docket, and those claims proceed under the unamended Code even where the transaction is plainly commercial in character. The gate is character plus value, and either key failing is enough to keep a suit outside.

## The English inheritance: where the procedural devices came from

The brief for this statute records that its procedural amendments were drawn from practice in commercial courts elsewhere, and the debt is specific enough to be traced device by device. The Commercial Court in London, sitting within the Business and Property Courts, had operated for generations with active case management, statements of case verified by statements of truth under Part 22 of the English Civil Procedure Rules, and a summary judgment jurisdiction under Part 24 of those Rules, exercisable where a claimant or a defendant has no real prospect of succeeding and there is no other compelling reason for a trial. The Indian Order XIIIA reproduces the Part 24 test in substance, including the compelling reason limb and the power to make conditional orders, and Order VI Rule 15A reproduces the statement of truth with its discipline of personal responsibility for the pleading. Case management hearings on the Indian model answer to the same philosophy as the English case management conference: the court, rather than the parties, owns the timetable.

The comparison is instructive in both directions. England pairs those devices with a costs regime in which the loser pays assessed costs as a matter of course, and the Indian Act adopted that pairing too, which is why the substituted Section 35 belongs in any account of the borrowing. England also staffs its Commercial Court with a small cadre of specialist judges and a compact, high value docket, and that is the part of the model the Indian statute could gesture at only through nomination and training provisions, for the demographic reasons the assessment section discusses. A borrowed procedure travels more easily than a borrowed bench, and a reader who understands which parts of the English settlement India took, and which it could not, has the key to the regime's strengths and to the location of its strains.

## Inside the mediation process: how Section 12A runs in practice

The Rules of 2018 give the section its working form. The plaintiff applies to the authorised authority, in practice the District Legal Services Authority, in the prescribed form, and the authority issues notice to the opposite party inviting it to join the mediation and to indicate its willingness within the time the notice allows. Where the opposite party consents, the authority assigns the dispute to a mediator and the sessions proceed; where the opposite party refuses, fails to respond or does not appear, the authority records the outcome in a report that the mediation was a non starter, and that report is the plaintiff's passport to the court, evidence that the remedy the section demands was exhausted by being attempted and declined. Where the parties settle, the settlement is drawn up, signed by the parties and the mediator, and acquires under Section 12A(5) the status and effect of an arbitral award on agreed terms, enforceable in the manner of a decree. Where they do not settle after participation, the process closes with a report to that effect and the suit may be filed, with the mediation period excluded from the limitation computation in either case.

Two features of the practice deserve notice because they shape how the section is experienced. The first is that the process is administered by the legal services machinery rather than by the court that would try the suit, so the gate is kept by an institution whose culture is settlement rather than adjudication, and whose officers process the application as an intake function. The second is that the quality and speed of the intake vary between districts in a way the statute cannot control, and the Supreme Court's decision in Patil Automation to run the rejection consequence from 20 August 2022 was in part a recognition of that variation, giving the system a date from which the infrastructure could fairly be held to be in place. For the plaintiff's adviser, the practice points are unglamorous and decisive: file the mediation application early enough that its three month course does not press against limitation on the main claim, keep the proof of application and of the closing report with the plaint papers, and, where urgency is the ground for going straight to court, plead the facts of urgency in the plaint itself, because Yamini Manohar directs the court to test the contemplation of urgent relief on the plaint, the documents and the circumstances of the case, and not on assertion alone.

## A commercial suit worked through: the unpaid supply contract

A single worked example gathers the provisions into the sequence a litigant actually meets. A components manufacturer supplies goods over a year to an equipment maker under a written supply agreement, invoices are raised and part paid, and a balance of forty lakh rupees remains unpaid despite two demand notices. The claim arises out of an agreement for the sale of goods and out of ordinary transactions of merchants and traders, so the character key turns under Section 2(1)(c). The amount sought to be recovered is forty lakh rupees, comfortably over the three lakh floor, so the value key turns as well. No urgent interim relief is genuinely contemplated, since the goods have been consumed in manufacture and there is no asset whose dissipation an injunction would prevent that the plaint can identify, so counsel does not plead urgency it cannot sustain. The manufacturer applies to the District Legal Services Authority for pre-institution mediation. The equipment maker attends two sessions and offers a fraction in full settlement, which is refused, and the process closes without settlement inside the three month period. The plaint is then filed in the Commercial Court with a statement of truth signed by the manufacturer's authorised officer, the supply agreement, the invoices, the ledger extracts and the correspondence disclosed and produced with it, and a declaration on oath covering the documents in the manufacturer's possession, including two internal emails that help the other side on a quality complaint, which must be disclosed because the duty covers documents pertaining to the suit and not only documents that assist the discloser.

Summons is served, and the equipment maker's thirty days begin. Its defence, that some consignments were defective and that a set off arises, depends on inspection reports held by its quality department and on a debit note correspondence, all of which must be marshalled inside the period; on recorded reasons and payment of costs the court allows filing up to the one hundred and twentieth day, and the written statement arrives on the one hundred and ninth, accompanied by its own disclosure. Had it arrived on the one hundred and twenty first, the analysis in SCG Contracts would have applied in full: the right forfeited, the document refused, and the suit proceeding on the manufacturer's evidence with the equipment maker confined to cross examination and argument. Inspection and admission or denial follow, and two invoices are admitted while the quality reports are denied, which identifies the real contest. At the case management hearing the court frames issues on liability for the balance, the defect allegation and the set off, fixes the evidence calendar, and records that trial will run on consecutive days. The manufacturer, holding the admitted invoices, applies under Order XIIIA for summary judgment on the admitted portion, arguing that the defence to that portion has no real prospect; the court gives judgment for the admitted sum and directs trial of the balance, a split outcome the Order expressly permits. Judgment on the remainder follows the trial and is pronounced inside ninety days of the close of arguments. The equipment maker, aggrieved on the set off, appeals within sixty days to the Commercial Appellate Division, and the manufacturer recovers, in addition to the decree, costs assessed under the substituted Section 35 that bear a real relation to what the suit cost, because the event has been priced throughout. Every feature of that narrative that differs from an ordinary money suit, the mediation gate, the officer's signature on the truth of the pleading, the disclosure of the damaging emails, the forfeiture that nearly was, the judgment for the admitted part without a trial, the costs, is a feature Parliament wrote into the text, and none of them depends on the goodwill of the forum.

## The supporting machinery: practice directions, data, training and overriding effect

Four quieter provisions hold the structure together and are easy to miss. Section 16 gives the amendments made by the Act to the Code of Civil Procedure overriding effect in their application to commercial disputes, so that where a High Court rule or another provision of the Code conflicts with the amended text, the amended text prevails; the litigant's first port of call is therefore the Code as the Schedule rewrites it, and not the local rule book. Section 18 empowers the High Court to issue practice directions to supplement the Act and the amended Code for the conduct of commercial matters, and the original side High Courts have used the power to regulate filing, electronic service and case management in detail, which is why practice differs at the margins between commercial divisions while the skeleton remains uniform. Section 17 requires the maintenance and publication of data on the commercial courts' working, a provision whose modesty conceals its importance, because a regime justified by measurable delay must consent to be measured. Sections 19 and 20 oblige the State Government to provide infrastructure and to arrange training for the judges, officers and staff of the commercial forums, the statutory hook on which judicial academy programmes for commercial law hang. Finally, Section 21 gives the Act overriding effect over other laws to the extent of inconsistency, and Section 21A, inserted in 2018, empowers the Central Government to make rules for carrying out the Act, the power under which the mediation Rules were made. None of these provisions decides a case by itself; together they explain the regime's texture, national in its rules, locally administered in its details, and dependent for its daily reality on institutions the statute can direct but cannot conjure.

## The 2018 amendment taken as a whole

The amending Act of 2018 deserves treatment as a unit, because its changes are usually described piecemeal and their combined direction is missed. It began, like the parent Act, as an Ordinance, promulgated on 3 May 2018, and the Act, Act 28 of 2018, was deemed to have come into force on that date, so the Ordinance period and the Act period run without a seam. Its first change was nominal and substantive at once: the long short title was replaced by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, a renaming that signalled the statute's graduation from a High Court experiment to a general commercial code for the country. Its second change was the threshold reduction examined earlier, from one crore rupees to three lakh. Its third was structural completion at the district level: provision for Commercial Courts below the level of a District Judge in territories where the High Court exercises ordinary original civil jurisdiction, so that the lower band of commercial disputes in those cities would not crowd the Commercial Division, and for Commercial Appellate Courts at the District Judge level to hear their appeals, inserted as Section 3A with the appeal route restated in Section 13. Its fourth change was the insertion of Chapter IIIA and Section 12A, the mediation gate. Its fifth was a tidying of the appellate and transfer scheme, including the omission of the original Section 9, which had dealt with the transfer of counterclaims and was no longer needed once the general transfer provision was understood to cover them. Its sixth was the insertion of Section 21A, conferring rule making power on the Central Government, under which the mediation Rules of 2018 were framed.

Read together, the changes describe a legislature correcting its own first draft in light of two years of operation. The 2015 Act had built excellent forums for a narrow class and left the mass of commercial litigation outside; the 2018 Act widened the class, completed the appellate staircase at the bottom, and added a settlement gate at the entrance. The direction of travel, from showcase to system, is the single most important fact about the amendment, and it is why accounts of the Act that stop at the 2015 text misdescribe the law in force on every point that matters to a litigant: the name, the threshold, the mediation duty and the appeal route.

## Transferred suits and the reach of the amended Code in time

Section 15's transfer obligation generated a distinct body of early litigation, because a transferred suit arrives in the commercial forum with a procedural history, pleadings filed, issues perhaps framed, evidence perhaps begun, under a code the Act has rewritten. The section itself supplies the governing principles. Suits and applications relating to a commercial dispute of a specified value, including applications under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, pending in a High Court where a Commercial Division has been constituted, stand transferred to the Division, and those pending in a civil court in a district for which a Commercial Court has been constituted stand transferred to that Court. A case in which final judgment has been reserved is not transferred. The receiving forum may hold case management hearings in a transferred matter, prescribe fresh timelines and issue the directions needed for speedy disposal under Order XVA, and the bar in the written statement proviso does not apply mechanically to a transferred suit, the court having discretion to set a fresh period for the written statement where the stage of the suit makes the ordinary computation unjust or impossible. Where a qualifying case has somehow not been transferred, the Commercial Appellate Division may, on a party's application, withdraw it and transfer it, and that order is final.

Beneath those principles sit two questions of temporal reach that the courts have had to settle across the cluster. The first is which suits the amended Code governs at all: the scheme the Supreme Court has endorsed is that the amendments apply to commercial suits instituted after the Act's commencement and, through the transfer mechanism and the receiving court's discretion, to transferred suits in respect of steps not yet completed, while steps validly completed under the old procedure are not reopened merely because the forum has changed. The second is the interaction with the written statement forfeiture in suits where summons was served long before transfer: it is here that the Section 15 discretion does its work, and courts have used it to fix a fresh and final date rather than to visit a forfeiture computed from a service that occurred in a different procedural world. The pattern is consistent with the statute's general jurisprudence. The text is enforced without apology inside its proper field, and the field's edges, transfer, transition and classification, are where judicial judgment operates.

## Disclosure in depth: what Order XI demands and what it forbids

Because the disclosure regime does so much of the regime's work, its mechanics merit a fuller account than a summary allows. The plaintiff's duty arises with the plaint itself: a declaration on oath listing all documents in the plaintiff's power, possession, control or custody pertaining to the subject matter of the suit and to the issues that may arise, identifying the documents relied upon, the documents that relate to a matter of background or are otherwise relevant, and any documents the plaintiff declines to produce, with the ground of decline, privilege being the standard instance. The defendant's corresponding declaration accompanies the written statement. Discovery by interrogatories survives in the Order in a controlled form, available with the court's leave and directed at matters in issue. Inspection follows disclosure, each party inspecting the documents disclosed by the other, and the admission and denial affidavit then records, document by document, whether existence, authenticity and correctness are admitted or denied, with reasons where a document is denied, a requirement that prevents the tactical blanket denial by which parties in ordinary suits kept every document in play until the evidence stage. Electronic records are dealt with expressly: a party relying on electronic records must disclose the prescribed particulars and, where a printout is produced, support it with the declaration the Order requires as to the device, the manner of production and the integrity of the record. Production of a document not disclosed is governed by the leave standard already described, reasonable cause for the non disclosure, and the courts have treated a party's own tactical withholding as the opposite of reasonable cause, while accepting genuine after discovered documents, for example a record created or obtained later in the ordinary course, where the explanation holds together.

The cumulative effect is a reversal of the ordinary suit's information economy. In ordinary procedure, information is a private asset to be deployed for advantage at the moment of maximum surprise. In the commercial regime, information about documents is a common asset created at the start, and private advantage survives only in argument about what the common record means. Litigants who find the regime harsh usually locate the harshness in the written statement forfeiture, but experienced commercial counsel locate the daily discipline in Order XI, because it is the provision that cannot be negotiated with at all: a document either was disclosed or it was not, and the record of that fact is created before any dispute about it can arise.

## The settlement offer and the pricing of conduct

One element of the substituted Section 35 deserves separate notice because it imports a settlement technology that Indian procedure had lacked. Among the considerations the court weighs in awarding costs is whether a party made a reasonable offer to settle the dispute and the offer was refused by the party that later did no better at trial. The provision gives a settlement offer financial teeth without the machinery of a formal payment into court: a defendant who offers a realistic sum early and is refused, and a plaintiff who demands a realistic sum and is refused, each acquires a costs argument that survives victory and defeat alike, because the question becomes not only who won but who made the litigation longer than it needed to be. Combined with the mediation gate at the entrance and the summary judgment device in the middle, the costs rule on refused offers completes a settlement architecture that presses the parties toward resolution at three separate points in the suit's life, before filing, after disclosure and continuously through the trial, and prices resistance at each point. Commentators who describe the Act as merely a fast track for trials understate it: it is at least as much a statute for making trials unnecessary, with the trial timetable serving, in part, as the credible threat that gives the settlement points their force.

## What the Act leaves alone, and why the boundaries matter

A statute is defined as much by its silences as by its commands, and three silences in this Act are structural. The first is execution: the Act does not rewrite the Code's provisions for executing decrees, so a commercial decree is executed through the ordinary machinery, with its own objections and appeals, and the discipline of the trial does not automatically continue into the recovery. Practitioners identify execution, rather than trial, as the stage at which delay re enters the commercial dispute, and reform proposals return to the point regularly. The second silence is the appellate restraint already described: by confining interlocutory appeals to two enumerations and barring revision, the Act accepts that some erroneous interlocutory orders will stand until the decree, a deliberate purchase of speed at the price of mid course correction. The third silence is subject matter at the boundary of Section 11: whole fields of commercial life, banking recovery, insolvency, company oppression and mismanagement, consumer claims, are governed by their own statutes and forums, and the Act neither absorbs nor coordinates them beyond the bar itself, so that a business dispute with several strands may still travel to several forums, each with its own clock. A researcher who grasps the silences will not make the standard error of treating the Commercial Courts Act as a complete code of commercial dispute resolution. It is a code for the commercial suit, complete within that territory and deliberately incomplete outside it.

## How to read the statute: a note for the researcher and the aspirant

The Act rewards a particular reading order, and students of the series' other procedural statutes will recognise the discipline. Begin with the definitions in Section 2, and within them with the pair on which everything turns, the commercial dispute in clause (c) and the specified value in clause (i), and note that the definition clause also carries the Explanation preserving a dispute's commercial character despite incidental property relief or a state party. Move then to the forum sections, Sections 3 to 5, and to the jurisdiction and bar provisions, Sections 6 to 11, holding the territory question and the Section 11 boundary in view together. Read Section 12 for the computation of value, and Chapter IIIA for the mediation gate. Only then turn to the Schedule, where the Code is amended, and read the amended Orders in the sequence of a suit: Order V and Order VIII for summons and defence, Order VI for the statement of truth, Order XI for disclosure, Order XIIIA for summary judgment, Order XVA for case management and Order XX for judgment, with Section 35 of the Code for costs held alongside. Finish with the appellate provisions, Sections 13 and 14, and the bar in Section 8. Read in that order, the statute explains itself: each later provision presupposes the classification and value decisions of the earlier ones, and the case law, Ambalal on entry, Patil Automation on the gate, SCG Contracts on the defence, Kandla on the exit, falls into place as commentary on the four corners of the design.

## Interim relief inside the commercial suit

Interim relief occupies a double position in the regime, as a remedy inside the suit and as the key that unlocks the mediation gate before it. Inside the suit, the governing provisions are those of the Code that the Act left substantially intact, the temporary injunction jurisdiction under Order XXXIX, attachment before judgment under Order XXXVIII, and the appointment of receivers under Order XL, applied with the tempo the case management regime imposes. The commercial context changes the practice around those provisions more than their text. An injunction application in a commercial suit is heard on a record that disclosure is about to fix, by a court that will set the whole calendar at the case management hearing, and interlocutory orders on such applications are, with narrow exceptions, outside the permitted appeals unless they fall within Order XLIII as amended, so the first hearing of an interim application carries a finality in practice that it lacks in ordinary litigation. Courts have responded by hearing interim applications fully and once, rather than in the instalments ordinary practice tolerated.

Before the suit, interim relief performs its second function. The plaintiff who contemplates urgent interim relief may institute without exhausting mediation, and the case law from Yamini Manohar has made the plaint's own showing the test of that contemplation. The drafting consequence is that the urgency case must be built into the plaint as facts, the date of discovery of the infringement, the continuing character of the wrong, the market in which the damage compounds, the inadequacy of a remedy delayed by three months of mediation, rather than asserted as a conclusion. In intellectual property matters, where the wrong is characteristically continuing and each day of infringement is a fresh injury, the exemption has real work to do, and the courts have accepted continuing infringement as the paradigm of contemplated urgency while remaining alert to the stale claim dressed in urgent language, the notice sent and slept upon, the application filed long after the facts were known. The discipline is symmetrical with the rest of the Act: a word in the text, urgent, is given a test, the test is applied to the document, and the document's author bears the consequence of the answer.

## Judges for commerce: nomination, experience and the training obligation

The 253rd Report placed judicial quality at the centre of its design, proposing that commercial forums be manned by judges with demonstrable experience of commercial litigation and that structured training accompany appointment. The Act adopted the direction in a lighter form. The Chief Justice constitutes the Commercial Division and nominates its judges from among the judges of the High Court, and the corresponding expectation at the district level is that Commercial Courts be presided over by officers of appropriate seniority and experience. Sections 19 and 20 then place the supporting duties on the State Government, infrastructure on the one hand and training of judges, officers and staff on the other. The lighter form was a concession to a real constraint: a High Court cannot manufacture commercial experience by nomination where the Bar and the Bench have handled commercial work in the ordinary stream for decades, and the district judiciary's seniority based postings resist specialisation of the kind a cadre would require.

Experience since commencement suggests the concession has cost less at the High Court level than critics feared and more at the district level. The original side High Courts built genuine specialisation quickly, because their commercial rosters could be kept stable and their bars specialised alongside them, and the Commercial Divisions of Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta and Madras developed recognisable bodies of practice within a few years. At the district level, where the Commercial Court is frequently an additional charge of a District Judge carrying a general docket, the specialisation the Report imagined has been thinner, and the enlarged post 2018 jurisdiction presses hardest exactly there. The lesson is one the series has met in other institution building statutes: a legislature can command a forum into existence and prescribe its procedure in a single enactment, but the human institution accumulates at the speed of careers, and the interval between the two is where the statute's reputation is decided.

## The counterclaim and the set off in commercial practice

A defendant in a commercial suit is rarely a pure defender, and the regime's handling of the defendant's own claims tests whether its disciplines are symmetrical. A counterclaim under Order VIII Rule 6A is, in the settled phrase, treated as a plaint in a cross suit: it is valued on its own subject matter for specified value and court fee purposes, it must answer the description of a commercial dispute to remain in the forum, and it is governed by the amended Code, so that the plaintiff in the main suit becomes, for the counterclaim, a defendant under the same obligations, including a written statement to the counterclaim inside the same discipline of periods and the same disclosure duty in respect of the counterclaim's documents. The mediation question noted earlier belongs here: a counterclaim that raises a genuinely new commercial dispute has been held by the Delhi High Court to attract the Section 12A discipline as a fresh suit would, a conclusion that follows from the cross suit character of the device and that defendants ignore at the price of the counterclaim's rejection. A set off, legal or equitable, stands differently: it is a defence to the extent of the sum claimed rather than a cross suit, it travels with the written statement, and it lives or dies inside the one hundred and twenty days with the rest of the defence, which is why the pleading of a set off cannot be held back as a second line for later deployment. The symmetry the statute achieves is worth naming: every discipline described in this article as a burden on the plaintiff is, one pleading later, a burden on the defendant as claimant, and the regime's fairness, such as it is, rests substantially on that rotation.

## Electronic filing, service and the registry in the commercial divisions

The Act's text is largely silent on technology, and the digital layer of the regime has been built under Section 18 practice directions and the general e-filing frameworks of the High Courts. In the larger Commercial Divisions, electronic filing of commercial suits is the working norm, summons and notices move electronically where the rules allow, cause lists and orders are published online, and the case management hearing is prepared through written timelines exchanged in advance. The significance for the statute's design is that the calendar the Act mandates is administrable only with a registry that can track it: a court that must hold the first case management hearing within four weeks of the admission and denial affidavits, reserve judgment for no more than ninety days after arguments, and move appeals toward a six month endeavour, needs systems that flag dates rather than files that wait to be noticed. Where those systems exist, the Act's periods operate as the working rhythm of the court; where they do not, the same periods depend on the memory and energy of individual registries, and slippage concentrates there. Technology is therefore not an ornament on this statute but part of its machinery, and the unevenness of the machinery between States is one of the quieter explanations of the unevenness of the regime itself.

## Why some commercial suits still take longer than the calendar promises

The calendar is a design, and designs meet dockets. Three frictions account for most of the distance between the table below and the experience of litigants in the slower forums. The first is volume against judges: the post 2018 docket includes every trade dispute over three lakh rupees in districts where one officer may hold the Commercial Court as an additional charge, and a case management system multiplies hearings per suit even as it shortens the suit's life, so the front of the process is heavier by design. The second friction is interlocutory mass: the Act narrowed appeals and barred revision, but parties continue to file the applications the Code allows, appointment and removal of parties, amendment, discovery disputes, leave for late documents, and each contested application consumes the hearing time the schedule reserved for evidence. The third is the evidence stage itself, where cross examination of witnesses in document heavy disputes resists compression, and where the day to day direction of Order XVA meets the reality of counsel engaged in several courts. None of these frictions is an argument that the calendar is fictitious. Suits in the well resourced divisions do run to their schedules in a manner unknown to the ordinary stream, and even in the slower forums the pleadings and disclosure stages, where the forfeiture and the bar do their work automatically, close on time. The frictions mark where the frontier of reform has moved to: having disciplined the parties, the system must next discipline its own supply, and that is a budgetary and administrative task rather than a legislative one.

## The same facts in an ordinary suit: a comparison the reader can test

The cleanest way to see the Act is to run the worked example of the unpaid supply contract through the ordinary stream in imagination. Without the Act, the manufacturer files a regular suit on the same documents, with no mediation gate and no statement of truth in the commercial form. Summons is served; the written statement, nominally due in thirty days and extendable by ninety, arrives after a series of extensions spread over a year, accompanied by few documents, the damaging correspondence being nobody's duty to disclose. A replication follows, then issues, then a plaintiff's evidence phase spread across many short hearings, with documents proved and objected to one by one and surprises met by adjournments. Interim applications punctuate the suit, each with its own appeal or revision audience in the higher court, and the suit's fifth year finds it in cross examination. Costs at the end are nominal. Nothing in that narrative requires bad faith from anyone; it is the equilibrium of a system in which every period is negotiable and every interlocutory order has a forum above it. The commercial regime is the same narrative with each negotiable point made final, and the comparison, rather than any single provision, is the statute's true content. A reader who holds both narratives in mind understands why the courts defend the forfeitures that look, in isolation, severe: each forfeiture is the load bearing member of a structure whose alternative has been observed at length.

## Drafting the commercial plaint: where suits are won before they begin

Because the record is fixed early and the classification is tested on documents, the commercial plaint is drafted to a different standard from the ordinary one, and the standard can be stated as a set of working rules that experienced chambers apply. The plaint pleads the clause of Section 2(1)(c) under which the dispute is commercial, and pleads the facts that satisfy the clause, rather than leaving the characterisation to inference. It computes the specified value under Section 12 in a distinct paragraph, relief by relief. Where mediation has been exhausted, it pleads the application, the process and the closing report, so that the Section 12A compliance appears on the face of the filing; where urgency is relied on, it pleads the facts of urgency with dates, as Yamini Manohar requires. The statement of truth is signed by an officer who has read the pleading and the documents, not as a courier formality, because the verification is the point at which the client's organisation takes responsibility for the case it is asking the court to fast track. The disclosure declaration is compiled from a genuine search of the client's records, including the records that hurt, since the bar on undisclosed documents makes a partial search the single most expensive economy available to a plaintiff. And the prayer is framed with the summary judgment and costs provisions in view, claiming interest and costs in the manner the Code allows, so that the suit's later stages have the foundations the plaint is supposed to lay. A plaint drafted on those rules does not guarantee success on the merits, but it guarantees that the suit will be decided on the merits rather than on the plaintiff's own procedural defaults, which in this regime is the first half of the contest.

## The defence side: the first one hundred and twenty days from the defendant's chair

The defendant's experience of the regime is the mirror image, compressed into the period between service and the outer date. The summons fixes the clock, and the first task is verification of service itself, its date, its mode and the documents that accompanied it, because every later computation runs from that date and because SCG Contracts makes the date effectively unarguable once established. The second task is the assembly of the defence record at a speed ordinary practice never demanded: the contracts section, the accounts section and the operational staff who hold the correspondence must be searched within weeks, not because disclosure is a courtesy but because the written statement and its accompanying declaration must speak for the whole documentary position, and documents omitted then are documents surrendered. The third task is the pleading decision itself, including whether a counterclaim or a set off is to be raised, knowing that both must be complete within the same window and that the counterclaim carries its own disclosure and, on the Delhi view, its own mediation question. The fourth is the extension application, if the first thirty days will not suffice, moved in time, supported by recorded reasons and an offer of costs, since a court asked at day one hundred and ten for the balance of the period is a court being told the truth about the defendant's diligence, while a court asked at day one hundred and nineteen is being asked to share a risk the statute assigns to the defendant alone. Chambers that defend commercial suits well describe the period as the case in miniature: the defence that cannot be assembled in one hundred and twenty days is usually the defence that was never going to survive disclosure, and the forfeiture, when it falls, falls most often on parties who treated the summons as the beginning of a negotiation with the court's patience rather than as the start of a fixed count.

## The business regulation assessment and its afterlife

The association between the Act and the World Bank's business regulation assessment deserves a closing word, because it is the part of the statute's origin story that time has overtaken. India's poor showing on the enforcement of contracts indicator, measured through a standardised commercial dispute in the country's two largest business cities, was the specific embarrassment the 2015 reforms were designed to answer, and the design choices traced in this article, specialised courts, timed stages, priced delay, read as a direct transcription of the indicator's methodology into domestic law: the hypothetical case in the assessment proceeded through filing, service, trial and enforcement, and the Act rebuilt each of those stages in the real system. The assessment series itself was discontinued by the Bank in 2021 after questions about data integrity in its later editions, and the indicator no longer exists to be improved. The statute, however, does not stand or fall with its originating metric. The case for enforceable deadlines in commercial litigation rests on the domestic costs of delay, on credit priced against slow recovery, on working capital locked in receivables, on the signal a slow court sends to every party deciding whether to perform a contract voluntarily, and those costs are measurable in the Indian economy without reference to any external league table. The episode leaves a durable lesson in legislative design: a reform adopted to satisfy an external measure survives, if it survives, only when the measure happened to point at a real domestic defect, as in this instance it did.

## Interest, and the money consequences that accompany the decree

The decree in a commercial suit carries money consequences beyond the principal, and two of them complete the account of the regime's pricing of time. Interest under Section 34 of the Code lies in the court's discretion for the period before the suit, governed substantially by the contract and by the law of interest, and for the period from the suit to the decree and from the decree to realisation, where the court awards rates it considers reasonable on the principal sum adjudged, with the statutory recognition that a liability arising out of a commercial transaction may bear a rate exceeding the ordinary ceiling, though not exceeding the contractual rate where one governs the transaction. The provision matters to commercial litigation out of proportion to its length, because in a dispute over an unpaid price the interest claim over years of litigation can rival a substantial fraction of the principal, and the court's discretion over its rate and periods is one of the variables settlement negotiations price from the first exchange of letters. Read with the costs rule, the interest discretion gives the commercial court a coherent pair of instruments: costs price the parties' conduct of the litigation, and interest prices the defendant's use of the plaintiff's money during it, so that delay is charged to the party who benefits from it at both ends of the account. That coherence is not accidental, and it is the financial expression of the same design that the calendar expresses in time.

## Limitation at the mediation gate: the exclusion and its edges

The second proviso to Section 12A(3), directing that the period spent in pre-institution mediation shall not be computed for the purpose of limitation, is a small clause carrying a heavy load, because without it the compulsory gate would consume the plaintiff's limitation period and a defendant could defeat a claim by spinning out a process the plaintiff was forced to enter. The exclusion covers the period during which the parties remained occupied with the mediation, running from the application to the authority until the process closes by settlement, by a report of failure or by a non starter report, and it operates in addition to the general law of limitation rather than in substitution for it, so that a plaintiff computes the limitation period for the suit as if the mediation interval had not elapsed. Two edges of the clause generate the practice questions. The first is the plaintiff who applies for mediation near the end of limitation: the exclusion protects the mediation interval itself, but it does not revive a claim whose period had already expired before the application was made, and such a plaintiff has spent the interval purchasing nothing, which is why the drafting guidance earlier in this article insists on applying early. The second edge is the suit filed in breach and rejected after Patil Automation, where the plaintiff must begin again with a mediation application and a fresh plaint, and whether the time spent in the rejected suit earns protection under the general principles governing proceedings prosecuted in good faith in a wrong forum becomes a live and fact sensitive question on which a plaintiff should never have needed an answer. The clause, in short, is generous to the compliant and silent to the non compliant, and that asymmetry is of a piece with the statute's general method of rewarding the party who follows the calendar in the text rather than in argument.

## Settlement inside the suit: the doors that remain open after filing

Exhausting mediation before filing does not exhaust settlement within the regime, and the case management structure assumes that suits may end by agreement at several points after institution. The court's powers at the case management hearing include exploring whether the dispute can be resolved without a full trial, and the parties remain free throughout to record a compromise under Order XXIII of the Code, whereupon the court passes a decree in accordance with the lawful terms agreed, a decree that carries the same enforceability as one delivered after contest. A settlement reached after disclosure has a different quality from one reached before it, because both parties negotiate with the documentary record open, and practitioners of the commercial divisions credit the disclosure stage, more than any exhortation, with the settlements the regime produces in its middle phase: the party whose documents are weak learns it early, in private, with time to price a settlement against a costs regime that will charge the full trial to the loser. The architecture is therefore best understood as a sequence of settlement opportunities of increasing information and increasing pressure, the mediation gate before filing, the disclosure mirror after the pleadings, the summary judgment test before the issues, and the costs and interest pricing throughout, with the trial reserved for the residue of disputes in which each side, having seen the record and the prices, still prefers judgment. That sequencing, and not speed alone, is the mature account of what the Act was built to do.

## Four decisions, one jurisprudence

The case law of the Act is unusually legible, because four Supreme Court decisions mark its four corners and point in the same direction. At the entry corner stands Ambalal Sarabhai Enterprises Limited v K.S. Infraspace LLP of 4 October 2019, holding the definition to a strict reading so that the special track is reserved for the class Parliament described. At the gate stands Patil Automation Private Limited v Rakheja Engineers Private Limited of 17 August 2022, holding the mediation precondition mandatory and its breach fatal to the plaint, tempered by a prospective date that protected those who had filed while the law was unsettled. At the defence stands SCG Contracts India Private Limited v K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure Private Limited of 12 February 2019, confirmed by the three judge bench in Desh Raj v Balkishan of 20 January 2020, holding the one hundred and twenty days to be a boundary of the court's power and not a guideline for its discretion. At the exit stands Kandla Export Corporation v OCI Corporation, reported at (2018) 14 SCC 715, holding the appellate lists closed against enlargement from any other statute. Yamini Manohar v T.K.D. Keerthi of 13 October 2023 and the later reaffirmation in Dhanbad Fuels Private Limited v Union of India fill the one space the four corners left open, the testing of asserted urgency, and they fill it in the same spirit: the exemption is real, it is judged on the plaint and the circumstances, and it is not a drafting facility. A student who reads the six decisions in that order has read the statute's judicial history entire, and a practitioner who cites them has the answer to nearly every threshold question the regime generates, which is a condition few procedural statutes in India have ever reached.

## Valuation disputes as threshold litigation

Because the specified value routes the suit, it is litigated, and the pattern of that litigation is predictable enough to describe. A defendant who prefers the ordinary stream, or a higher or lower forum within the commercial structure, objects that the plaint overvalues an intangible right, understates the market value of property, or aggregates reliefs that Section 12 values separately, and asks for the plaint's return or the suit's transfer. A plaintiff who has valued on the plaint's own documents is difficult to move, which is why the better practice treats the valuation paragraph as a small proof, identifying the head of Section 12 relied on and the material supporting the figure. The court deciding the objection is deciding forum and procedure in one order, since a change of value band can move the suit between the Commercial Court, the Commercial Appellate Court's trial side at the district level and the Commercial Division, with the amended Code following the suit into whichever forum receives it. The episode illustrates a proposition that runs through the whole Act and that a reader should carry into the assessment: in this regime the preliminary questions are not preliminaries to the real dispute in the sense of being lesser than it, because each of them, character, value, mediation, service, determines the rules under which the real dispute will be decided, and several of them determine whether it will be decided at all.

## The commercial suit calendar: every step, its period and its price

The table below is the findable artifact of this article. It sets out the life of a commercial suit as the Act and the amended Code arrange it, step by step, with the governing period at each step and the consequence of missing it. The consequence column is the point of the artifact and of the statute: in ordinary civil procedure most of its entries would read extension on costs, while in the commercial regime the characteristic entries are rejection, forfeiture and foreclosure.

| Step | Period allowed | Consequence of missing the step |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-institution mediation application to the authority under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 | Before institution, where no urgent interim relief is contemplated | Suit cannot be instituted; a plaint filed without it is liable to rejection under Order VII Rule 11 |
| Completion of pre-institution mediation | Three months from the application, extendable by two months with the consent of the parties; the period is excluded from limitation | Process closes; settlement, if reached, has the effect of an arbitral award on agreed terms; if the defendant does not participate, a non starter report issues and the plaintiff may sue |
| Filing of the plaint with statement of truth and full disclosure of documents | At institution | Unverified pleading liable not to be taken on record; undisclosed documents barred from later reliance except with leave on reasonable cause shown |
| Written statement | Thirty days from service of summons, extendable on recorded reasons and costs up to one hundred and twenty days from service | On expiry of one hundred and twenty days the right to file is forfeited and the court shall not take the written statement on record; no extension is permitted |
| Disclosure, inspection and admission or denial of documents | Within the periods fixed by Order XI as applied to commercial disputes, following the pleadings | Documents not disclosed are lost to the disclosing party except with leave; admissions and denials recorded at this stage bind the trial |
| First case management hearing | Within four weeks of the filing of the affidavits of admission and denial by all parties | Court fixes a binding schedule; non compliance with the schedule may lead to foreclosure of steps, costs or decision on the available material |

## Assessment: a statute about deadlines, and what the deadlines have done

The series of which this article forms a part has returned repeatedly to a single finding: the enforceability of a deadline determines a statute's effect. Provisions that announce periods without consequences are read by the system they govern as aspirations, and the litigation system, staffed by rational actors, prices them accordingly. The Commercial Courts Act is the counter example that proves the finding. Here a legislature decided, in express words and across the whole procedural field, that periods would have consequences, and the courts, from the Supreme Court downward, have held the legislature to its decision with unusual consistency: strict on classification in Ambalal Sarabhai, mandatory on mediation in Patil Automation, unsparing on forfeiture in SCG Contracts and Desh Raj, closed on appeals in Kandla Export. Read together, those four lines of authority are not four decisions but one jurisprudence, and its content is that the text of this statute means what it says because the statute's entire object would dissolve if it did not.

The honest assessment must then separate three questions that commentary often runs together. The first is whether the design is coherent, and on that the answer is plainly yes: classification, threshold, mediation, pleadings, disclosure, case management, summary judgment, costs and appeals interlock, each compensating for the pressure the others create. The second question is whether the design is administered with the capacity it assumes, and on that the answer is mixed and regional. In the Commercial Divisions of the large original side High Courts, with experienced judges, electronic filing and a practising bar that has reorganised itself around the calendar, the regime has visibly shortened the life of commercial suits and changed settlement behaviour. In district Commercial Courts staffed by redesignation, carrying the enlarged post 2018 docket of three lakh rupee disputes on the infrastructure of the ordinary district judiciary, the calendar is harder to hold, and the strictness of the text presses on the litigants of the smallest suits, which is where the forfeiture cases that trouble commentators tend to arise. The third question is whether the remaining unfairness is a reason to soften the rules or to resource the forums, and the position taken in this article is the latter. Every proposal to soften, a discretion to extend the written statement period for sufficient cause, a power to cure a skipped mediation, a wider appeal from interlocutory orders, recreates in miniature the ordinary procedure whose failure caused the Act, and would be used at scale in exactly the way the unamended Code was used. The defects of the regime are defects of capacity, and capacity is cured by judges, courts and registries, not by discretion.

Two genuine limitations should be recorded without flinching. The first is that the Act disciplines the stages of a suit more successfully than it disciplines the supply of judicial time: a case management order can fix dates, but it cannot create the judge hours to keep them when a court carries several hundred commercial matters, and slippage at the trial stage remains the weakest link in the calendar. The second is that the threshold reduction of 2018, defensible as access policy, sits uneasily with the severity of the sanctions: a three lakh rupee dispute between small traders carries the same forfeiture, the same disclosure bar and the same mediation gate as a three hundred crore dispute between corporations, and the smaller litigant is the one least likely to be advised in time. A graduated response, in which the smallest commercial suits attracted the timetable but a moderated set of consequences, would answer that objection without surrendering the design, and it is the reform most consistent with the statute's own logic, since the Act already graduates its forums by value. Until Parliament takes it up, the practical burden falls where this article has tried to place it throughout: on knowing the calendar before the suit begins, because in a commercial dispute the calendar is not administration. It is the case.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What counts as a commercial dispute?

A commercial dispute is a dispute arising out of one of the transaction categories enumerated in Section 2(1)(c) of the Act. The list covers the ordinary transactions of merchants, bankers, financiers and traders; export and import of merchandise or services; admiralty and maritime matters; carriage of goods; construction and infrastructure contracts including tenders; agreements relating to immovable property used exclusively in trade or commerce; franchising, distribution, licensing, management and consultancy agreements; joint venture and shareholders agreements; partnership agreements; technology development agreements; intellectual property rights in trademarks, copyright, patent, design, domain names, geographical indications and semiconductor integrated circuits; agreements for the sale of goods or the provision of services; exploitation of natural resources including electromagnetic spectrum; insurance and re-insurance; and agency contracts relating to any of these, together with any further category the Central Government notifies.

### Q: What is the specified value and how did it change?

The specified value is the monetary value of the subject matter of a commercial dispute, determined under Section 12 of the Act, and it is the second key to the commercial forum: the dispute must answer the statutory definition and its value must reach the statutory floor. As enacted in 2015 the floor was one crore rupees, confining the regime to high value litigation. The amending Act of 2018, Act 28 of 2018, reduced the floor to three lakh rupees, bringing the ordinary run of trade disputes, unpaid invoices, dealership and supply claims of modest size, inside the commercial track with its mediation gate, disclosure discipline and strict deadlines. The Central Government may notify a higher value than the floor stated in Section 2(1)(i).

### Q: Is pre-institution mediation compulsory?

Yes, for a suit that does not contemplate urgent interim relief. Section 12A, inserted by the amending Act of 2018, provides that such a suit shall not be instituted unless the plaintiff has exhausted pre-institution mediation before the authorities constituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. The mediation is to be completed within three months of the application, extendable by two months with the consent of the parties, and the period is excluded from limitation. In Patil Automation Private Limited v Rakheja Engineers Private Limited the Supreme Court held the requirement mandatory, with a plaint filed in breach liable to rejection under Order VII Rule 11, and directed that the rejection consequence operate for suits instituted from 20 August 2022.

### Q: How does commercial court procedure differ from ordinary civil procedure?

The differences are written into the Code of Civil Procedure as the Schedule to the Act amends it for commercial disputes. Pleadings are verified by a statement of truth. Documents in a party's power, possession, control or custody must be disclosed with the pleading, and undisclosed documents cannot be relied on later except with leave on reasonable cause shown. A suit without contemplated urgent interim relief must first exhaust pre-institution mediation. The written statement is forfeited after one hundred and twenty days from service of summons. The court manages the case through case management hearings under Order XVA, may give summary judgment under Order XIIIA without oral evidence, must pronounce judgment within ninety days of the close of arguments, and awards costs that follow the event on a realistic basis under the substituted Section 35.

### Q: When can a court give summary judgment?

A commercial court may give summary judgment under Order XIIIA of the Code, on the application of either party, made after summons has been served on the defendant and before the court has framed the issues in the suit. The applicant must satisfy the court of two things together: that the plaintiff has no real prospect of succeeding on the claim, or that the defendant has no real prospect of successfully defending it, as the case may be, and that there is no other compelling reason why the claim should not be disposed of before oral evidence is recorded. The court may give judgment, dismiss the application, or make a conditional order, for example requiring a deposit or security, where the case is shadowy rather than hopeless. The Order does not apply to a suit originally filed as a summary suit under Order XXXVII.

### Q: What happens if a written statement is filed late?

Filing is governed by the substituted proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 of the Code. The written statement is due within thirty days of service of summons. The court may allow filing on a later day, for reasons recorded in writing and on payment of costs, but not beyond one hundred and twenty days from service. On the expiry of one hundred and twenty days the defendant forfeits the right to file a written statement and the court shall not allow it to be taken on record, and a further proviso to Order VIII Rule 10 forbids any order extending the period. In SCG Contracts India Private Limited v K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure Private Limited the Supreme Court held the limit mandatory and refused to save a late filing through the court's inherent powers, a position a three judge bench affirmed in Desh Raj v Balkishan.

### Q: Where does an appeal from a commercial court go?

Section 13 of the Act routes the appeal inside the commercial structure. A person aggrieved by the decision of a Commercial Court below the level of a District Judge appeals to the Commercial Appellate Court designated at the District Judge level. A person aggrieved by the judgment or order of a Commercial Court at the level of a District Judge, or of a Commercial Division of a High Court, appeals to the Commercial Appellate Division of that High Court. The period is sixty days from the date of the judgment or order in each case. Appeals from orders are confined to those enumerated in Order XLIII of the Code and Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, and Section 14 directs the appellate division to endeavour to dispose of the appeal within six months of filing.

### Q: How are costs awarded in a commercial court suit?

Costs are governed by Section 35 of the Code as substituted in its application to commercial disputes. The court has discretion to decide whether costs are payable by one party to another, their quantum and when they must be paid, and costs include witness expenses, legal fees and other expenses incurred in connection with the proceedings. In exercising the discretion the court weighs the conduct of the parties, whether a party succeeded on part of its case, whether frivolous claims or counterclaims caused delay, and whether a reasonable offer to settle was made and refused. The governing approach is that costs follow the event, meaning the unsuccessful party ordinarily pays, and a court departing from that course must record its reasons, so awards in commercial suits bear a real relation to the expenditure the litigation caused.

### Q: What is a statement of truth in a commercial suit?

A statement of truth is the verification that Order VI Rule 15A of the Code requires for every pleading in a commercial dispute. The party, or a person authorised to act for the party, declares which statements in the pleading are true to the declarant's own knowledge, which are based on information received and believed to be true, and which are matters of legal submission, and accepts that the pleading is made under the discipline the rule imposes. The device, borrowed from English practice, attaches a named individual's responsibility to the document. A pleading that is not verified in accordance with the rule is liable not to be taken on record, and the party cannot rely on it as evidence in the ordinary way, which makes proper verification a condition of the pleading's life rather than a formality attached to it.

### Q: Which documents must be disclosed at the start of a commercial suit?

Under Order XI of the Code as applied to commercial disputes, the plaintiff must disclose on oath, with the plaint, all documents in the plaintiff's power, possession, control or custody pertaining to the subject matter of the suit and the issues that may arise, and must produce the documents relied upon, identify those relied on for background, and state which documents are withheld and on what ground, privilege being the standard ground. The defendant owes the same duty with the written statement. Each side then inspects the other's documents and files an affidavit of admission and denial. A document that was within a party's power or possession and was not disclosed cannot be relied upon later except with the leave of the court, granted only where reasonable cause for the non disclosure is established.

### Q: What happens at a case management hearing in a commercial suit?

The case management hearing is the court's instrument of control under Order XVA of the Code, and the first hearing is to be held within four weeks of the filing of the affidavits of admission and denial of documents by all parties. The court examines the pleadings and the documents, frames or refines the issues that require trial, and fixes the calendar for the stages that remain, including the filing of evidence affidavits, the recording of cross examination and the hearing of arguments, recording the schedule in an order binding on the parties. The court may also explore settlement, decide pending procedural applications, and warn of the sanctions the Order provides for default, which include foreclosing a party's right to take a step, imposing costs, or proceeding to decide on the material available where a party's default makes a fair schedule impossible.

### Q: How is the specified value of a commercial suit calculated?

Section 12 of the Act supplies the computation. Where the relief sought is recovery of money, the specified value is the amount sought to be recovered. Where the relief relates to movable property or a right in it, the market value of the property on the date of the suit governs. Where the relief relates to immovable property or a right in it, the market value again governs. Where the right sought to be enforced is an intangible right, the value is the market value of that right as estimated by the plaintiff. A counterclaim is valued under the same heads on its own subject matter. The valuation is stated in the plaint, works with the court fee valuation under the applicable court fees law, and decides whether the suit crosses the three lakh floor and which tier of the commercial structure tries it.

### Q: Can a pending ordinary suit be transferred to a Commercial Court?

Yes. Section 15 of the Act requires all suits and applications relating to a commercial dispute of a specified value, including applications under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, pending in a High Court where a Commercial Division has been constituted, to be transferred to the Commercial Division, and those pending in a civil court in a district for which a Commercial Court has been constituted, to be transferred to that Court. A case in which final judgment has been reserved is excepted from transfer. The receiving forum may hold case management hearings in the transferred matter, set fresh timelines for the steps that remain, and, in its discretion, fix a fresh period for a written statement where the ordinary computation would not fit the transferred stage. If a qualifying case is not transferred, the Commercial Appellate Division may withdraw and transfer it on a party's application.

### Q: Can urgent interim relief excuse a plaintiff from pre-institution mediation?

Yes, where the urgency is genuine and disclosed by the suit itself. Section 12A applies only to a suit which does not contemplate any urgent interim relief, so a plaintiff who genuinely contemplates such relief may institute the suit directly. In Yamini Manohar v T.K.D. Keerthi the Supreme Court directed the court to examine the plaint, the documents and the cause of action as a whole, from the plaintiff's standpoint, and to see through a prayer inserted merely as a device to bypass mediation, while holding that the later refusal of interim relief does not by itself prove that no urgency was contemplated when the suit was filed. A continuing infringement or a threatened dissipation of assets is the characteristic case in which the exemption holds; a stale claim with an injunction prayer added is the characteristic case in which it fails.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in SCG Contracts about the written statement limit?

In SCG Contracts India Private Limited v K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure Private Limited, decided on 12 February 2019, the Supreme Court held that the amended Order VIII Rule 1 is mandatory in a commercial suit. The defendant there had been served on 14 July 2017 and filed its written statement on 15 December 2017, after the trial court had granted a short extension on costs, and the Court held that the filing came too late: on the expiry of one hundred and twenty days from service the right to file stands forfeited, the court cannot take the written statement on record, and the inherent powers under Section 151 of the Code cannot be used to override the express bar. The earlier authorities treating the unamended period as directory were confined to the unamended text, which contained neither a forfeiture clause nor a prohibition on extension.

### Q: Why did the Supreme Court insist on actual use of property in Ambalal Sarabhai?

In Ambalal Sarabhai Enterprises Limited v K.S. Infraspace LLP, decided on 4 October 2019, the question was whether a dispute over land intended for commercial development arose out of an agreement relating to immovable property used exclusively in trade or commerce under Section 2(1)(c)(vii). The Supreme Court held that used means actually used or being used, so that property merely likely to be used for commerce, or acquired with a commercial intention, does not qualify. The reasoning was that the Act's strict timelines and forfeitures were enacted for a defined class of disputes, and a loose reading of the entry condition would draw ordinary land litigation into the fast track and defeat the object of expeditious disposal for genuine commercial cases. The Court accordingly read the definition strictly while reading the Act's scheme purposively, a combination that has shaped the classification case law since.

### Q: What is the difference between a Commercial Court and a Commercial Division?

The two are trial forums of the same regime at different levels of the judiciary, allocated by territory and value. A Commercial Court is a court at the district level, constituted by the State Government under Section 3 after consultation with the High Court, and it tries commercial disputes of a specified value arising in districts where the High Court does not exercise ordinary original civil jurisdiction, as well as the lower band of disputes within an original side territory. A Commercial Division is a division of a High Court exercising ordinary original civil jurisdiction, constituted by the Chief Justice under Section 4 and staffed by judges of that High Court nominated for commercial work, and it tries the commercial disputes that fall within that High Court's original civil jurisdiction and pecuniary limits. Appeals from both travel to the appellate tiers of the same structure.

### Q: How soon must a commercial court deliver judgment after arguments conclude?

Within ninety days of the conclusion of arguments. Order XX Rule 1 of the Code, as applied to commercial disputes, requires the Commercial Court, the Commercial Division and the Commercial Appellate Division to pronounce judgment within that period after the hearing concludes, and copies of the judgment are to be furnished to the parties by electronic mail or otherwise as the provision directs. The rule completes the Act's chain of stated periods, running from the three months of pre-institution mediation through the one hundred and twenty days for the written statement and the sixty days for appeal to the ninety days for judgment, and it means that a reserved judgment in a commercial matter is a state with a statutory end rather than an open ended wait.

### Q: Which orders of a commercial court cannot be appealed?

Every order that is not within the enumerations Section 13 permits. An appeal against an order lies only where the order is specifically enumerated in Order XLIII of the Code of Civil Procedure, as amended by the Act, or is appealable under Section 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, and Section 13(2) bars any other appeal notwithstanding any other law or the Letters Patent of the High Court. Section 8 adds that no civil revision application or petition lies against an interlocutory order of a Commercial Court, including an order on jurisdiction, and that the challenge must be raised in the appeal against the decree, subject to Section 13. In Kandla Export Corporation v OCI Corporation the Supreme Court held the list closed, refusing to enlarge arbitration appeal rights through the Commercial Courts Act. The High Courts have preserved a narrow and sparingly exercised supervisory jurisdiction under Article 227 of the Constitution alongside the bar.

### Q: Does a settlement reached in pre-institution mediation bind like a decree?

A settlement that satisfies Section 12A has the status and effect of an arbitral award on agreed terms. Section 12A(4) requires the settlement to be reduced to writing and signed by the parties to the dispute and the mediator, and Section 12A(5) provides that it shall have the same status and effect as an arbitral award on agreed terms under Section 30(4) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. An award on agreed terms is enforceable in the same manner as a decree of the court under the arbitration statute, so the parties do not need a fresh suit to enforce what they agreed, and the settlement concludes the dispute with the authority of an adjudicated outcome while sparing both sides the suit that the mediation was designed to prevent.
