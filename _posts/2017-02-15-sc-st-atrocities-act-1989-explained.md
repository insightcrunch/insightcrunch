The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 is usually debated as if it were one law doing one thing, and that single habit explains most of the confusion around it. It is two statutes wearing one short title. The first creates a set of offences that exist nowhere else in Indian criminal law, wrongs defined by what they do to the dignity, land, labour and civic standing of a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe: conduct such as garlanding a person with footwear, tonsuring a head by force, dedicating a woman to a temple against her will, or imposing a social and economic boycott on a family. The second statute does something conceptually different. It takes offences that the general penal code already punishes, murder, rape, arson, hurt, kidnapping, and raises their consequences when the victim belongs to a listed community and the offender does not. A question about defining new wrongs and a question about sentencing familiar wrongs differently attract different arguments, different evidence and different objections, and an assessment of this Act that does not separate them will misjudge both.

![The SC and ST Atrocities Act 1989, showing its two limbs of new offences and enhanced punishments and the special court machinery that tries them - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-26.webp)

Around those two limbs Parliament built a procedural machine that departs from ordinary criminal process at almost every point. Trials sit in Special Courts and, after the amendment of 2015, in Exclusive Special Courts that hear nothing else. Investigation is reserved to officers of a stated rank and is time bound. The ordinary route to anticipatory bail is closed by the statute itself. Victims and witnesses hold a chapter of rights of their own, inserted in 2015, which includes being heard at the bail stage, protection from intimidation, and payment of travelling and maintenance expenses. Monetary relief is payable to victims under a schedule in the rules, in stages as the case moves, and it does not wait for a conviction. A public servant who wilfully neglects the duties the Act assigns commits an offence by that neglect alone. None of these features is a detail. Together they are the statute's theory of why ordinary criminal law failed the people it protects: cases were not registered, investigations were casual, trials drifted, witnesses were exposed, and the complainant bore costs the state should have carried. The Act answers each failure with a mechanism, and judging the Act means judging whether each mechanism answers the failure it was aimed at.

This article treats the Act as a statute pillar should. It explains why a separate law was needed when a civil rights statute already existed, sets out the constitutional footing, works through both limbs and the machinery at the level of the statutory category, states what the courts have required the prosecution to prove, presents the enforcement record under a strict discipline about denominators, and gives both the case for the Act and the case against its design in their proponents' own strongest terms. It describes offence categories as categories. It names no victim and retells no incident, because the statute's operation can be understood completely from its text, its rules and the reported record.

## Why a statute against atrocities when exclusion was already an offence

The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 was already on the statute book for more than three decades when Parliament enacted the Act of 1989, and the relationship between the two statutes is the first thing to understand, because they were built against different harms. The Act of 1955 gives effect to Article 17 of the Constitution, which abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. Its subject is exclusion: enforcing religious disabilities such as preventing a person from entering a place of public worship or drawing water from a public source, enforcing social disabilities in access to shops, restaurants, hospitals and occupations, refusing to sell goods or render services, and molesting or boycotting a person for having exercised a right the statute secures. Its punishments were fixed in the middle of the twentieth century and are short, running in months rather than years, and by the 1980s the statute had fallen into sparse use.

The harm that produced the Act of 1989 was of a different order. Successive official reports and the record placed before Parliament described violence, humiliation and dispossession directed at members of the listed communities: land occupied or harvested by others, labour extracted under bondage, women subjected to degrading treatment, voters obstructed, complainants pursued through false cases, and complainants and witnesses exposed to retaliation when they did invoke the law. The general penal code punished the physical acts within that pattern, but it punished them as ordinary assaults, ordinary trespasses and ordinary intimidation, indifferent to the element that made them instruments of subordination, and the institutional record of registration, investigation and conviction under the ordinary law was the record the new statute was designed to change. The distinction between the two statutes is therefore functional, and [the companion treatment of the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955](/2017/06/01/protection-of-civil-rights-act-1955/) develops it: the statute of 1955 polices the boundary of access, and the statute of 1989 polices violence and humiliation inflicted across that boundary, with machinery strong enough, in design, to make a complaint survivable for the person who files it.

The Act received the assent of the President on 11 September 1989, was published in the Gazette the following day, and was brought into force on 30 January 1990. It is Act 33 of 1989. Its formal title states its three purposes together: to prevent the commission of offences of atrocities against members of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, to provide for Special Courts for the trial of such offences, and to provide for the relief and rehabilitation of victims. It originally extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, a limitation that fell away when the constitutional position of that State changed in 2019. The rules under it, the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Rules, 1995, carry the administrative detail: the identification of atrocity prone areas, the relief schedule, the vigilance machinery and the duties of district officers. The date of the rules is itself a datum the pillar should not pass over. The Act commenced in January 1990 and its rules were notified only on 31 March 1995, so that for the first five years of its life the statute operated without the relief schedule, the vigilance structure and the investigation rules that give its design administrative content; the offences and the courts existed, and the machinery that was supposed to make complaining survivable did not. The pattern, an Act in force and its rules in gestation, recurs across Indian protective legislation, and in this statute's case it fixed an early reputation for ineffectuality that the subsequent record has never entirely displaced. The amendment of 2015, enacted as Act 1 of 2016 and brought into force on 26 January 2016, is the Act's second founding: it expanded the offence list, rebuilt the court provisions, inserted the chapter of victim and witness rights, strengthened the public servant provision, and added presumptions that change how trials are argued. Any account of the Act that stops at the 1989 text describes a statute that no longer exists.

### Why was the civil rights statute of 1955 not enough on its own?

The statute of 1955 addressed exclusion from temples, wells, shops and services, and punished it with short terms fixed in that era. It contained no answer to violence, land dispossession, forced labour or organised humiliation, no special courts, no victim rights and no relief schedule, so a separate statute was enacted in 1989 against the different harm of atrocity.

## How the Act was made: the record of 1989 and the second founding of 2015

The Statement of Objects and Reasons that accompanied the Bill of 1989 is the shortest reliable account of why Parliament acted, and its reasoning tracks the distinction this article has drawn between the two older bodies of law. It recorded that the Acts then in force, naming the civil rights statute among them, had proved ineffective in preventing offences against members of the two communities, that such offences continued to be committed in considerable numbers notwithstanding the prohibition of untouchability, and that the existing law's penalties and machinery were inadequate to the gravity and organisation of the conduct concerned. The remedy the Statement proposed had three parts, and the enacted Act contains all three: deterrent punishment for a defined catalogue of offences, special courts for speedy trial, and relief and rehabilitation for victims as a statutory function rather than an act of grace. The Bill passed both Houses in the monsoon session of 1989 with support that crossed the governing and opposition benches, a fact worth recording because the statute's later controversies have sometimes been written as if its enactment were a partisan act; the argument of 1989 was about machinery and quantum, and the principle of a special statute was common ground.

The path to the second founding was longer and more contested than the path to the first. A full-scale amendment was first attempted by ordinance in March 2014, which introduced the exclusive court structure, the expanded offence list and the rights chapter in substantially the form later enacted, and lapsed without being replaced within the constitutional window. The amendment Bill was then taken through Parliament in 2015, passing the Lok Sabha in August of that year and the Rajya Sabha in December, and received assent at the end of December 2015 as Act 1 of 2016, commencing on 26 January 2016. The debate on the Bill is instructive for the same reason the debate of 1989 is: members divided over the relief amounts, the completeness of the offence list and the court structure, and the proposition that the statute required strengthening rather than dilution carried the House. The rules were then amended in April 2016 to give the amended Act its administrative content: the enhanced relief scale, the seven day payment standard and the sixty day investigation and charge sheet standard all date from that notification. A statute pillar that treats 1989 as the only relevant year therefore misdescribes its subject twice over. The Act a complainant invokes, an officer administers and a court applies is the composite of 1989, of January 2016 and of April 2016, and the analysis that follows is of that composite.

## The constitutional footing

The Act stands on four constitutional supports, and an examination candidate or an advocate who can name them holds the statute's legitimacy argument in outline. The first is Article 17, which abolishes untouchability, forbids its practice in any form, and provides that the enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. Article 17 is one of the very few provisions of the Constitution that operates against private persons and not only against the state, and it is the direct source of Parliament's competence to criminalise conduct between citizens in this field. The second support is Article 15, which prohibits discrimination on grounds including caste and then, in its fourth clause, expressly permits the state to make special provision for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. A statute that singles out two groups of citizens for special criminal protection is a special provision of exactly this kind, and the enabling clause answers the objection that protection distributed by group membership is itself discriminatory. The third support is Article 21, the right to life and personal liberty, which the courts have read to include life with dignity; the Act's offences of humiliation are framed as attacks on dignity rather than only on body or property, and the constitutional reading explains why humiliation inflicted in public view occupies the centre of the offence list. The fourth support is Article 46, a directive principle obliging the state to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, and in particular of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, and to protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation. A directive principle cannot be enforced by a court, but it states the constitutional instruction the Act executes.

The courts gave Article 17 a correspondingly wide reading when the civil rights statute came before them. In State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale, the Supreme Court treated untouchability as a practice to be understood by its social operation rather than by any literal or religious test, and sustained the penal machinery Parliament had built on the Article. That reading matters to the Act of 1989 because the atrocities statute inherits the premise: the constitutional wrong is the enforcement of subordination, and the criminal law is a permitted instrument against it. The dignity line under Article 21 developed in parallel. Where a statute's central offences are humiliations (public stripping of dignity rather than injury to the body), the constitutional language of a life with dignity is what allows the law to grade those offences as grave, and the courts' acceptance of that grading is visible in the way the validity of the Act's provisions, when challenged, has been assessed against the protective purpose rather than against a demand for symmetry with ordinary crime.

Beneath the footing sits the listing mechanism that determines who is protected. Under Articles 341 and 342, the President, after consultation with the Governor of a State, specifies by public notification the castes, races, tribes or groups deemed to be Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes in relation to that State, and Parliament alone may by law include a community in the list or exclude one from it. The lists are therefore state specific: a community listed in one State is not, by that fact, listed in another, and the protection of the Act follows the lists. The Act's own definitions lean on the constitutional ones. A member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe means a member of a caste or tribe specified in the relevant list, and the word atrocity is defined by reference to the offences the Act itself creates, so that the statutory label and the statutory offence list are the same thing. The definitional circle matters in litigation, because a recurring question is whether conduct that falls outside the offence list can still be prosecuted under the Act. It cannot. Conduct outside Section 3 must be prosecuted, if at all, under the general law, and the special machinery of this Act follows only its own offences and the enhanced limb described below.

### Which constitutional provisions stand behind the Atrocities Act?

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and authorises punishment of its enforcement, Article 15(4) permits special provision for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, Article 21 supplies the dignity reading behind the humiliation offences, and Article 46 directs the state to protect these communities from social injustice and exploitation.

## The shape of the statute: two limbs and a machine

The Act as it stands after the amendment of 2015 runs to a compact set of chapters. The opening provisions give the title, extent, commencement and definitions. Section 3 contains the offences and their punishments, and Sections 4 to 13 deal respectively with neglect of duty by public servants, enhanced punishment for a subsequent conviction, the application of parts of the general codes, forfeiture of property on conviction, presumptions, powers conferred on district officers, removal of a person likely to commit an offence from a specified area, the consequences of such removal, and measures to be taken on a second removal. Section 14 creates the courts and Section 14A the appeal, and Section 15 the prosecutors. A new chapter inserted in 2015, Chapter IVA containing Section 15A, states the rights of victims and witnesses. Sections 16 and 17 concern the power to impose a collective fine and preventive action by the district administration. Section 18 closes the anticipatory bail route. Section 19 denies probation to an adult convicted under the Act, Section 20 gives the Act overriding effect over inconsistent laws, Section 21 states the duty of the government to secure effective implementation, and Section 23 confers the rule making power under which the Rules of 1995 were made.

Two features of the offence provisions frame everything that follows. The first is the identity condition on the accused. Section 3 speaks throughout of a person who is not a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe. The Act therefore does not apply to an offence committed by one member of the listed communities against another, and it does not apply to an offence by a member of any other community against a person outside the lists. Its field is the asymmetric case: an offender from outside the protected communities and a victim within them. The second feature is the knowledge and ground condition on the aggravated forms of the offences, examined in the judicial section below: the courts have required the prosecution to establish a connection between the act and the victim's identity, and that requirement, more than any other single doctrine, decides outcomes. A reader who holds the identity condition, the ground condition, the two limbs and the machinery in mind at once has the whole statute in a usable form, which is why [the social justice guide that maps this field of statutes](/2017/02/01/social-justice-laws-india-guide/) treats this Act as the criminal layer's centre of weight.

### What are the two distinct things the Atrocities Act does?

It creates offences that exist nowhere else, directed at humiliation, dispossession, coerced labour and civic obstruction against members of the listed communities, and it separately raises the punishment for ordinary penal code offences, grave ones directly and scheduled ones by adoption, when committed against such a member by a person outside the communities.

## The definitions that quietly govern every case

A pillar should spend a section on definitions, because under this Act the definitions are operative law and not vocabulary. The definition of atrocity is purely referential: it means an offence punishable under Section 3. The consequence, already noted, is that no residuary atrocity exists; a prosecutor who cannot place conduct within a clause of Section 3 cannot invoke the Act at all, and the definitional sentence is therefore the first wall every complaint must clear. The definitions of the two protected identities incorporate the constitutional lists by reference, so the operative document in a contested case is not the Act but the Presidential Order for the relevant State, and a charge sheet lives or dies by the entry in that Order. The amendment of 2015 added definitions that changed the working of the trial: victim, defined to include the person against whom an offence was committed and, where that person is dead or incapacitated, dependents and legal heirs for the purposes the Act specifies; dependent; public servant; and the paired definitions of social boycott and economic boycott that give the new boycott offence its content, the first describing a refusal to permit a person to render or receive customary services or to abide by the community's social relations, the second a refusal to deal with, work for hire for, or do business with the person, extending to denial of opportunities and services. The definitions of Special Court and Exclusive Special Court, also elaborated in 2015, fix the forum question at the level of the definition itself, so that the identity of the trial court is not a matter of administrative convenience.

Two further definitions deserve a sentence each because they appear in argument constantly. Economic boycott and social boycott are defined by refusals (to deal, to hire, to permit services), which makes the offence one of coordinated exclusion; a single shopkeeper's rudeness is not within the clause, and the prosecution must show the refusal operating as the community instrument the definition describes. And the definition of victim carries the Chapter IVA rights with it: the rights of notice, protection and hearing belong to the defined victim and dependents, which is why disputes about who counts as a victim in a case with several affected family members are resolved by returning to the definition rather than to sympathy. A reader who has absorbed this section can predict, correctly, that a striking share of the litigation under a statute famous for its punishments is in fact litigation about its second section.

## The first limb: offences that exist nowhere else

Section 3(1) is the heart of the first limb, and its method deserves attention before its content. The general penal code is organised around harm to body, property and reputation in the abstract. Section 3(1) is organised around subordination. Almost every clause describes an act whose wrong consists not only in what is done but in the social meaning of doing it to a person because of the community that person belongs to, and in the audience before whom it is done. The punishment for an offence under Section 3(1) runs from imprisonment of six months to five years, with fine. The categories, stated at the level of the statutory category as this series requires, fall into families.

The family of degradation offences addresses attacks on dignity. It covers forcing a person to consume an inedible or obnoxious substance; dumping excreta, waste, carcasses or other obnoxious material in or near the person's premises or neighbourhood; garlanding with footwear or parading a person naked or semi naked; forcibly removing a person's clothes, tonsuring the head, removing a moustache or painting the face or body; and intentional insult or intimidation with intent to humiliate, committed in a place within public view. The 2015 amendment enlarged this family considerably. It added, among others, the imposition of a social or economic boycott on a person or family, the dedication of a woman belonging to a listed community to a deity, idol or temple, or the performance of a similar practice, and a group of offences directed at women: assault or criminal force with intent to dishonour or outrage modesty, sexual touching and words, acts or gestures of a sexual nature without consent, and exploitation of a woman's sexual person by a person in a position of dominance. The boycott offence repays attention because it criminalises a collective instrument rather than an individual act: a village or trade group that resolves to deny a family work, custom, access or services, and enforces the resolution, commits an offence under the Act even though no blow is struck. The relief schedule attaches a stated sum to that offence, which converts an often invisible form of coercion into a compensable and prosecutable wrong.

The family of land offences addresses the material base of subordination in an agrarian society. It covers wrongful occupation or cultivation of land owned by, allotted to, or notified for allotment to a member of a listed community; wrongful dispossession from land or premises, or interference with the enjoyment of rights over land, premises or water; destruction of crops and taking of produce from such land; and compelling a person to dispose of or remove carcasses or to carry out similar degrading manual tasks, together with obstruction of the use of common property resources, public passages, burial grounds and cremation grounds, and of access to places of public resort and sources of water. Land is the point at which caste and livelihood meet, and the pattern these clauses describe (occupation, dispossession, crop destruction, denial of water and of common resources) is the pattern of economic coercion that a charge of ordinary criminal trespass describes very inadequately.

The family of labour offences covers forced or bonded labour other than the compulsory public service the Constitution permits, compelling a person to dispose of a carcass or to dig graves, and employing or permitting the employment of a person in manual scavenging. The manual scavenging clause interacts with the separate prohibition statute of 2013, and its presence in this Act means that the same conduct can attract the atrocities machinery when the victim belongs to a listed community.

The family of civic and process offences is the least discussed and among the most revealing. It covers preventing a person from voting, from filing a nomination as a candidate, or from proposing or seconding a candidate; compelling a holder of an elected office to act or not to act; instituting false, malicious or vexatious legal proceedings; giving false or frivolous information to a public servant and thereby causing that servant to use lawful power to injure or annoy; and causing injury, insult or annoyance by a public process set in motion dishonestly. These clauses exist because litigation and administration are themselves instruments by which a locally powerful person can exhaust a weaker one, and the Act treats the abuse of lawful forms, when directed at a listed victim for the statutory reason, as an atrocity rather than as sharp practice.

Across the families, two drafting choices should be noted. The Act enumerates rather than generalises, so that conduct falling outside the list is outside the Act however ugly it is; the 2015 expansion, which roughly doubled the catalogue, was Parliament's answer to gaps that acquittals had exposed. And the list is expressly an offence list against a person not of the listed communities, which keeps the identity condition visible on the face of the charge sheet.

Within the first limb, the clauses addressed to women form a drafting project of their own, and their history tracks the general law's. The original Act of 1989 addressed the sexual dimension of atrocity thinly, principally through the enhanced limb's application to rape and through general clauses on dishonouring women. The amendment of 2015 inserted the dedicated clauses: assault or use of criminal force with intent to dishonour or outrage the modesty of a woman of a listed community, intentional touching of a sexual nature without consent, words, acts or gestures of a sexual nature, and sexual exploitation by a person in a position to dominate the woman's will, together with the dedication clause addressed to the devadasi practice. The drafting choice mirrors the one the general criminal law made in 2013, when the sexual offences were rewritten as a graded set of distinct wrongs rather than a single offence of outraging modesty, and it has the same rationale: the older vocabulary had forced grave conduct into a trivial sounding charge. Under this Act the clauses do additional work, because they carry the special forum, the presumptions, the Chapter IVA rights and the relief schedule with them, and because the relief rules, as amended in 2016, provide for relief in these cases without insisting on the medical preconditions that had operated as a practical bar, and for payment of the admissible relief on the conclusion of the trial in the grievous cases even where the trial does not end in conviction. A complainant in such a case therefore has two statutory vocabularies available for the same facts, the general sexual offences and the atrocities clauses, and the choice between them, or the combination of both, is made on the identity element: where the evidence will carry the proposition that the act was directed at the victim as a member of her community, the atrocities clauses state the wrong as the victim experienced it and bring the machinery with them.

## The second limb: ordinary offences, extraordinary consequences

The second limb sits inside Section 3(2) and works by reference rather than by definition. Its most severe clause provides that a person outside the listed communities who commits an offence under the Indian Penal Code punishable with imprisonment of ten years or more against a person or property, knowing that the person belongs to a listed community or that the property belongs to such a person, is punishable with imprisonment for life and with fine. Murder, rape, dacoity, arson of a dwelling and the gravest hurt offences fall on the penal code side of that line. The Act does not redefine them; it attaches a consequence to the identity of the victim, and the consequence begins at life imprisonment where the penal code itself often provides a range below that. A second clause deals with false evidence: fabricating or giving false evidence intending to procure the conviction of a listed person for a capital offence carries imprisonment for life, and if an innocent person is convicted and executed in consequence, the fabricator is liable to the death penalty; false evidence aimed at offences punishable with seven years or more carries a term of six months to seven years. Further clauses address mischief by fire or explosive substance against property, denial of customary rights of passage to places of public resort, and the screening of an offender from legal punishment; a clause inserted in 2015 extends the limb to a schedule of penal code offences carrying lesser terms, offences such as hurt, grievous hurt, criminal intimidation, theft in a dwelling and similar offences listed in a schedule to the Act, which are punished under this Act with the punishment the penal code provides for them. A final clause raises the floor for a public servant who commits an offence under the same provisions: the term may not be less than one year.

Two companion provisions complete the architecture of consequence. Section 5 enhances punishment on a subsequent conviction: a person convicted of an offence under the offences chapter who is convicted again is punishable with imprisonment of not less than one year, extendable to the punishment provided for that offence. Section 7 empowers the court, on conviction, to declare property used in the commission of the offence forfeited to the government. The second limb therefore changes outcomes at three points (the sentence for the grave offence itself, the exposure of a repeat offender, and the property consequence), and the argument about it is correspondingly different from the argument about the first limb. Defenders of the limb argue that a crime used to enforce a hierarchy is a graver wrong than the same act between equals, because its target is also a message to every member of the victim's community, and the sentence should say so. Critics of the limb argue that criminal law punishes acts and intentions, that identity based enhancement duplicates what sentencing discretion can already do, and that the knowledge condition makes the clause either redundant where knowledge is obvious or unprovable where it is not. The article returns to that exchange at the assessment; the textual point to hold is that the enhancement is triggered by knowledge of identity, and the courts' treatment of knowledge and ground, taken up below, is what gives the limb its working shape.

The reader comparing codes should register how the Act interacts with the successor criminal codes. The Act's references to the Indian Penal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure are read, after the code replacement, as references to the corresponding provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and [the comparison of the Indian Penal Code with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/) sets out the mapping on which that reading rests. The Act itself was not repealed or re enacted in the replacement; it continues as a special statute standing beside the general codes, which is the usual Indian pattern for legislation of this kind.

## Sentencing under the Act: floors, fines and the repeat offender

The Act's sentencing design departs from the general pattern of Indian penal drafting in one respect that deserves emphasis: it legislates floors. The ordinary penal code typically prescribes a maximum and leaves the term to the court's discretion, which in practice produces wide dispersion for identical offences. Section 3(1) prescribes both a floor of six months and a ceiling of five years; the false evidence and fire clauses of Section 3(2) carry floors of six months and, for the dwelling fire clause, of one year; the public servant clause carries a floor of one year; Section 4 carries a floor of six months; and Section 5 raises the floor for a second conviction to one year. A floor is a legislative judgment that the sentencing discretion of the trial court, exercised within local society, was itself part of the failure the Act addresses, and it removes the option of the nominal sentence that converts a conviction into a formality. The ceiling structure is equally deliberate. The single greatest exposure, imprisonment for life, is reserved for the enhanced limb's grave offences and for the false evidence clause aimed at a capital conviction, so that the statute's severity concentrates where the underlying conduct is independently among the gravest in the penal code. Fines accompany the imprisonment throughout, and a court may direct a realised fine toward the victim under the general provisions of the procedure code, which operate in addition to, and independently of, the administrative relief under the rules; the two streams are not set off against each other, because they answer different purposes, the fine punishing the offender and the relief repairing the household. Sentencing remarks in the reported cases under the Act tend to track the same two variables the elements isolate: the publicity of the humiliation and the deliberateness of the identity targeting, which is a further indication that the courts have absorbed the statute's own theory of the wrong into the discretion the floors leave them.

## How the offence families operate on facts

Statutes of this kind are best understood by watching their elements engage a set of facts, and three of the families repay the exercise because each turns on a different kind of proof. Take the land clauses first. A family belonging to a listed community holds land allotted to it under a state land programme, recorded in its name in the revenue record, and at harvest the standing crop is cut and taken by others, who also begin ploughing the plot. Under the general law the complaint is theft of crop and criminal trespass, and its fate usually turns on possession disputes that revenue litigation can keep alive for a generation. Under the Act the elements are different in kind: the prosecution must show the victim's membership of a listed community, the allotment or ownership as the revenue record states it, the act of wrongful occupation or taking of produce, and the identity condition on the accused. Title is not tried; the record speaks, and the offence is complete on the acts. The design choice is visible: Parliament converted the commonest material conflict in the field into an offence whose proof sits in documents the state itself maintains, so that the case need not be re fought as a civil dispute in criminal dress.

Take the boycott offence next, because its proof is the opposite of documentary. A family files a complaint under the Act, and in the weeks that follow the village's traders stop selling to it, the tractor owners stop hiring out, the labour contractors stop engaging its members, and a message circulates that dealings will resume when the complaint is withdrawn. No single act in that sequence is violent, and each participant can describe his own refusal as a private choice. The offence the 2015 amendment created is aimed exactly at the coordination: the definitions speak of refusal to deal, refusal to hire and refusal to permit customary services, and the prosecution's work is to prove the pattern and its connection to the protected identity, typically through the testimony of those who were told of the resolution and of those who enforced it. The clause's arrival in 2015 closed the Act's most obvious strategic gap, since retaliation for complaining had previously been answerable mainly through the witness protection provisions rather than as a substantive offence, and its presence changes the advice given to a complainant's family from the first week.

Take, finally, the process offences, where the abuse consists of using the state's own forms. A complaint is filed against a listed person alleging an offence the complainant knows did not occur, arrests and proceedings follow, and the listed person spends money, time and standing on a defence before the falsity surfaces. The general law's remedies for that pattern, prosecution for false information or a civil claim for malicious prosecution, are slow and rarely set in motion by the person wronged. Section 3(1) makes the institution of false, malicious or vexatious proceedings against a listed victim, and the giving of false information to a public servant to set the law against such a victim, offences under the Act itself, tried in its own courts with its own machinery. The three exercises together state the first limb's method more plainly than any catalogue: in each family Parliament identified the point at which the general law's elements failed to match the wrong (possession instead of record, private choice instead of coordination, remedy instead of offence) and redrafted the element to fit.

## The public servant who neglects the Act commits an offence

Section 4 is the provision that most sharply distinguishes this statute from ordinary penal law, because it criminalises the administrator rather than only the assailant. Whoever, being a public servant but not a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, wilfully neglects the duties required to be performed under the Act and the rules is punishable with imprisonment for a term of not less than six months and up to one year. The amendment of 2015 rewrote the section to spell out the duties whose neglect is punishable, and the list reads as a catalogue of the points at which cases had historically died: reading the written information over to the informant before signature, registering the complaint under the correct provisions, recording the victim's and witnesses' statements, conducting and completing the investigation and filing the charge sheet in time, registering a complaint of an offence under the Act rather than under a lesser provision, preparing the spot inspection and damage assessment, and giving effect to the preventive and relief duties the rules assign. The drafting choice is instructive. Parliament did not content itself with a general command to implement the Act; it converted each known administrative failure into a specified duty and attached a criminal sanction to its wilful breach.

The provision's practical record is its weakness, and honesty requires the point to be made in the pillar rather than left to the critics. Prosecutions under Section 4 require the machinery of the state to proceed against its own officers, sanction and institutional reluctance stand in the way, and convictions are rare enough that the section functions more as a statement of standard than as a working deterrent. Defenders of the design answer that a stated standard has disciplinary and appellate value even where criminal prosecution is uncommon, since it defines the conduct for which an officer may be proceeded against departmentally and gives courts and vigilance committees a text against which to measure an investigation. Both things are true, and the gap between the text and its enforcement is the single clearest instance of the general pattern this article keeps returning to: the Act's design failures are fewer than its enforcement failures.

The section's position inside the larger scheme is worth one more observation, because it explains the drafting of the duties list. Each duty named in the amended Section 4 corresponds to a filtration point at which the older system had quietly disposed of complaints: a statement recorded in diluted form, a case registered under the civil rights statute or the penal code instead of under this Act, an investigation left to drift past the point at which witnesses could be found, a charge sheet filed after the relief stages had stalled. By enumerating the duties, the amendment converted what had been a general accusation of administrative indifference into a set of auditable acts, each capable of being checked against the case diary and the file. The vigilance and monitoring committees under the rules, which review registration and investigation in their periodic meetings, audit against exactly this list, and a complainant's representative who knows the list can frame a grievance as the neglect of a named duty rather than as a complaint about attitude, which is the difference between a grievance the system must answer and one it can absorb. The criminal sanction may be rare; the audit standard operates every week.

## Presumptions: how the Act reallocates proof

Section 8, recast by the amendment of 2015, inserts three presumptions into a trial under the Act, and they matter because the offences characteristically occur in settings where direct proof of motive and of shared intention is hard to obtain. Where the prosecution proves that the accused rendered financial assistance to a person accused of, or reasonably suspected of, an offence under the Act, the court shall presume, unless the contrary is proved, that the accused abetted the offence. Where an offence is committed against a listed victim by a group of persons, and the court finds the offence to be in furtherance of a common intention or a common object, the presumption operates accordingly. The third presumption is the one the brief for this article flags and the one litigated most: where the accused had personal knowledge of the victim or the victim's family, the court shall presume that the accused was aware of the caste or tribal identity of the victim, unless the contrary is proved. The rationale is a fact about Indian social life that no evidence manual could state more plainly: in the village and small town settings where most of these cases arise, the parties ordinarily know one another, and caste identity within a locality is a matter of general social knowledge rather than of documents. The presumption does not relieve the prosecution of proving the act, or of proving acquaintance; it prevents the trial from collapsing into an artificial demand that the prosecution prove, by separate evidence, a fact that the setting makes obvious and that only the accused's own denial puts in issue. The presumption is rebuttable, and an accused who shows genuine absence of acquaintance or of the means of knowledge displaces it. Presumptions of this kind are a recurring Indian legislative device in statutes addressing offences committed in private or within relationships of power, and their constitutional treatment is settled in the direction of permitting rebuttable presumptions that rest on a rational connection between the proved fact and the presumed one. The discipline the courts impose, developed in the reverse burden cases under the narcotics and anti corruption statutes, including Noor Aga v. State of Punjab, is twofold: the prosecution must first prove the foundation facts beyond reasonable doubt, and the accused's rebuttal burden is the civil standard of preponderance of probabilities rather than proof beyond reasonable doubt. Section 8 conforms to that discipline on its face. The foundation fact for the identity presumption is acquaintance, which the prosecution must prove; the presumed fact is only awareness of identity; and the accused may displace it on the lower standard. A challenge to the presumption as reversing the burden on an essential element therefore meets the answer the courts have given in the cognate statutes, that an evidentiary presumption tied to a proved foundation and open to rebuttal allocates the burden of going forward without relieving the prosecution of the burden of persuasion on the offence as a whole.

## The procedural machine: rank, speed and a closed bail route

The Act and its rules assign the investigation to an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, appointed with regard to past experience and sense of ability and justice, and the rules require the investigation to be completed on top priority. The amendment of the rules in April 2016 fixed the working standard that completes the sequence: investigation and the filing of the charge sheet within sixty days. Reserving investigation to a senior officer answers a specific historical complaint, that complaints at the station level were recorded under provisions that carried no special consequence, or not recorded at all, and that the officer recording them often belonged to the same local structure as the accused. The seniority rule does not guarantee a good investigation, but it moves the decision about whether a case exists from the station to an officer answerable at district level, and Section 4 makes a failure at that level a punishable neglect of a stated duty.

Section 14, substituted in full by the amendment of 2015, creates the trial forum. For speedy trial, the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, establishes an Exclusive Special Court for one or more districts, a court that tries offences under this Act and nothing else. In districts where fewer cases are recorded, the government instead specifies an existing Court of Session as a Special Court. Both forms of court may take cognizance of offences under the Act directly, without the committal proceedings that ordinarily move a sessions case from a magistrate to the sessions court, and both sit at sessions level, which is why the gravest sentences, including life imprisonment and the death penalty in the false evidence case, can be imposed by them. The section then states the timeline that the brief for this article singles out: it is the duty of the State Government to establish an adequate number of courts to ensure that cases are disposed of within a period of two months, as far as possible, proceedings are to continue from day to day until the witnesses in attendance have been examined, and a trial under the Act is, as far as possible, to be completed within a period of two months from the date of filing of the charge sheet. The doubled qualification, as far as possible, is doing real work in the text. Parliament did not make the two months a condition of the trial's validity, breach of which would free the accused, and no Indian statute could run a sessions trial on that fuse; what it did was write the expectation into the statute so that delay in these cases is measured against a stated legislative standard rather than against the ambient pace of the sessions docket. Measured against that standard, the record is examined below.

Section 14A, also inserted in 2015, routes appeals from any judgment, sentence or order of a Special Court or Exclusive Special Court to the High Court, on facts and on law, notwithstanding the ordinary appeal provisions of the procedure code, with a limitation period of ninety days and a power in the High Court to entertain an appeal up to one hundred and eighty days for sufficient cause. Section 15 provides for a Special Public Prosecutor for each Special Court, and an Exclusive Special Public Prosecutor for each Exclusive Special Court: the state either specifies a public prosecutor or appoints an advocate of not less than seven years' practice. The prosecutor provisions complete the design logic. A statute that distrusts the ordinary chain at the complaint stage (senior investigator, punishable neglect) and at the trial stage (dedicated court, day to day hearing) would be incoherent if it left the prosecution to whoever happened to hold the district brief, so it creates a prosecutor of defined standing answerable for these cases alone.

Section 18 states, in one sentence, that nothing in Section 438 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the anticipatory bail provision, applies to a case involving the arrest of a person accused of an offence under this Act. The bar is a feature of the 1989 design rather than of the 2015 amendments, and the Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality in State of Madhya Pradesh v. Ram Krishna Balothia, reasoning that the bar operates on a defined class of offences and does not extinguish the court's power over arrest and regular bail under the other provisions of the law. The bar's rationale within the statute's own theory is that the period between complaint and arrest is the period of maximum exposure for a complainant and for witnesses in a locality where the accused's side is usually the locally stronger one, and a pre arrest order obtained on notice would convert that period into a structured opportunity for pressure. Whether the bar should survive in its original breadth, and what happened when the Court revisited the working of the Act in 2018 and Parliament answered, is the controversy that belongs to the companion article, and [the account of the 2018 amendment and the Court](/2017/03/01/sc-st-act-2018-amendment-explained/) owns that sequence. This pillar states the bar as enacted, notes the verdict that sustained it, and takes the analysis no further, because the legal sequence that followed belongs to that article's questions.

### Can the administration act against a person likely to commit an atrocity before any offence occurs?

Yes. The Act arms the district administration with preventive powers. Officers may be given magisterial and police powers for prevention and investigation. A person likely to commit an offence in a specified area may be directed to remove himself from it for up to two years, and preventive action may follow a report that an atrocity is likely.

## The duties the Act lays on government itself, and its overriding force

Two closing provisions of the Act state its relationship to the rest of the legal system, and a pillar should not leave them unexamined. Section 20 gives the Act overriding effect: its provisions operate notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law, in the procedure code, or in any custom or usage, subject to the Act's own terms. The clause is what resolves collisions in the Act's favour where they genuinely arise, for example where a general provision would route a case to an ordinary court or a lesser sentence, and its express reference to custom and usage is a deliberate echo of the constitutional footing, directed at the argument, occasionally heard in the earlier decades, that conduct sanctioned by local custom stood outside ordinary legal judgment. Section 19 works from the other end: it removes the benefit of probation for an adult convicted under the Act, so that the sentence the court passes is a sentence the offender serves, closing the route by which the probation statutes converted convictions under protective legislation into releases on good conduct. Read together, the two sections mark the statute's refusal to be absorbed into the general system's softening devices, a refusal consistent with the floors in its punishments and with the bail bar examined in the machinery section.

Between those provisions sits one of the Act's least used and most distinctive powers. Section 16 empowers the state government, where it is satisfied that the inhabitants of an area are concerned in, or abetting, the commission of offences under the Act, or harbouring persons engaged in them, to impose a collective fine on the inhabitants of that area, apportioned and recovered under the rules the section contemplates. The power belongs to an older family of Indian collective responsibility provisions, and its theory is the inverse of the boycott offence's: where a community closes ranks around offenders, the statute makes the community bear a collective cost for the shelter it provides, reasoning that silence in an identified area is a contribution to the offence rather than a neutral stance. Successive administrations have used the power sparingly, and its constitutional proportionality has been debated where it has been invoked, since a fine apportioned across inhabitants reaches persons against whom nothing individual is proved. Its presence in the statute is part of the design's honesty about its theatre of operation: the Act assumes that the obstacle to enforcement is often not the unknown offender but the protected one, and Section 16 is the provision that addresses the protection directly.

Section 21 then turns the statute's demands inward, onto government. It makes it the duty of the state government to ensure the Act's effective implementation, and it enumerates what that duty includes: the provision of legal aid to victims so that justice is not denied for economic or other disability, travelling and maintenance expenses to victims and witnesses during investigation and trial, the economic and social rehabilitation of victims, and the appointment of officers charged with initiating and supervising prosecutions. The enumeration matters because it converts the machine's components from administrative grace into statutory duty, and because it supplies the standard against which the vigilance committees, the commissions and the courts in writ proceedings measure a district's performance. The relief schedule's entitlements, the legal aid the complainant's adviser checks for, and the supervisory officers whose existence the monitoring structure presupposes all trace to this section and to the rules made under Section 23 in pursuance of it. The Act's framers, in other words, wrote the implementing state into the statute as a duty bearer with named obligations, and the enforcement record set out below is the record of performance against duties the statute itself specified, which is why the assessment at this article's close judges the machine against the Act's own text and not against an external ideal.

## The rights of victims and witnesses

Chapter IVA, inserted by the amendment of 2015 and consisting of Section 15A, is the part of the Act that readers inside the system consistently describe as its most advanced, and the part least known outside it. Before 2015 the victim of an atrocity occupied the ordinary position of a witness in the state's case: entitled to be informed of little, protected by no specific provision, and dependent on the prosecutor's diligence for any participation. Section 15A replaced that position with a catalogue of entitlements that attaches to the victim, to dependents and to witnesses alike.

The catalogue, stated at the level of its heads, runs as follows. The state bears a duty to make arrangements for the protection of victims, dependents and witnesses against intimidation, coercion, inducement, violence and threats of violence. The victim is entitled to be treated with fairness, respect and dignity, and special care is required where the victim is a woman, a child, a person with disability or a person above sixty. The victim and witnesses are entitled to reasonable, accurate and timely notice of court proceedings, including proceedings on bail. The victim is entitled to information, including about the entitlement to relief, legal aid and the stages of the case. Travelling and maintenance expenses during investigation and trial are payable. Socio economic relief, in the form the section describes, is to be provided within a stated short period. The victim or a dependent is entitled to be heard at the stages of bail, discharge, release, parole, conviction and sentence, and to file written submissions on conviction and sentence. Legal services are to be made available through the district legal services machinery. And the state is required to prepare and publicise a charter of these rights, so that the entitlements do not depend on a complainant finding a lawyer who happens to know the chapter exists. A victim pursuing a complaint under this Act is assembling exactly the kind of paper file, certificates, statements, medical records and payment receipts, on which claims later turn, and keeping that file in a form that can be opened, searched and shared with counsel is a practical necessity; a reader assembling case papers in spreadsheets and documents can [open and read the office files in a case bundle with the ReportMedic office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which handles the Excel, Word and presentation formats such bundles arrive in.

Two features of the chapter deserve analytic emphasis rather than summary. The right to be heard at the bail stage converts the victim from a spectator of the decision that most affects personal safety into a participant in it, and it interacts with the bar on anticipatory bail to produce a consistent position: the accused's liberty applications under this Act are contested in the presence of the person the Act protects, not resolved in chambers on the police papers alone. And the notice and information rights are, in substance, an answer to the commonest practical grievance in these cases, which is not hostility but silence: a complainant who hears nothing for two years concludes that nothing is happening, while a charge sheet, a bail order and a hearing date were all moving through a system that never told them. General statements about a case are no substitute for advice on an individual matter, and anyone applying this chapter to a live dispute should take advice on their own facts, because limitation periods and forum rules in criminal process are unforgiving. The chapter's existence, however, changes what a complainant is entitled to ask for, and an entitlement that is never asked for is the standard failure mode of Indian remedial legislation.

## Relief and rehabilitation: money that does not wait for a verdict

Distinct from compensation awarded by a court on conviction, the Act operates a relief scheme through its rules, and the distinction is the first thing a complainant's adviser needs. Rule 12 of the Rules of 1995 obliges the district administration, on receiving information of an atrocity, to arrange inspection of the place of occurrence, assessment of damage, and payment of relief in cash or in kind or both to the victim, family members and dependents. The amounts are set by a schedule annexed to the rules, organised by offence, and the amendment of the rules notified on 14 April 2016 set the scale that defines the scheme's present shape: relief ranging from Rs 85,000 for the least grave scheduled offences to Rs 8,25,000 at the top of the scale, the figure attached to murder and to gang rape, with rape at Rs 5,00,000 and the imposition of a social or economic boycott at Rs 1,00,000, the whole scale linked to the Consumer Price Index for Industrial Workers so that it does not silently erode. The same amendment rationalised the phasing of payment: the amount for an offence is released in stages as the case progresses, a share on the registration of the case, a further share when the charge sheet is filed, and the balance at the conclusion stages the schedule specifies, and the admissible relief is to be paid within seven days of the stage that triggers it. For certain offences of a grievous nature against women, relief is payable on the conclusion of the trial even where the trial does not end in conviction, a rule that recognises that the harm the relief addresses is not contingent on the prosecution's success.

The design deserves to be recognised for what it is. An ordinary criminal case offers a victim nothing until sentence, and usually nothing then beyond the satisfaction of conviction, because fine realisations are small and compensation orders discretionary and hard to execute. The relief scheme inverts the sequence: the state pays early, in defined amounts, on administrative findings rather than on proof beyond reasonable doubt, because the economic function of the payment is to keep a victim's household functioning and to reduce the dependence on the accused's community that makes withdrawal of complaints rational. That is also why the staged structure matters as policy and not only as accounting. A payment released in full on registration would create a visible incentive problem that critics of the Act already allege in general terms; a payment released only on conviction would fail the household at the moment of maximum need. The staged release threads the two objections, and the awareness failure documented in the brief's discourse angles, that the entitlement is widely unclaimed because it is widely unknown, is an administrative failure of publicity rather than a defect of the schedule's drafting.

For the gravest offences the schedule's norms reach beyond a cash figure, and a complainant's family should know it. The norms annexed to the rules provide, in the cases at the top of the scale such as murder, for the reconstruction of the household the offence destroyed: pension to the widow or dependents, employment for a member of the family, agricultural land or a house site and a constructed house where the family has been rendered shelterless, and the cost of the children's education and maintenance borne under the scheme. These entitlements are administrative, claimed from the district administration on the strength of the registered case and the assessment the rules require, and they are independent of the trial's outcome in timing even where the final stage of the cash component is linked to it. Their presence in the scheme completes the relief design's logic. A cash payment addresses weeks; a pension, a job and a house address the structural consequence of the offence, which in the gravest cases is the removal of the family's earner or its expulsion from its place. The annual reporting's recurring finding that relief in full is claimed in only part of the eligible cases is therefore not a footnote to the scheme but its central administrative failure, since the households least able to pursue an unpublicised entitlement through a district office are the households the schedule was written for.

## What the complainant's adviser assembles first

The practical payload of this pillar can be stated as a sequence of checks, in the order in which they decide cases. The first check is membership: the victim's community must appear in the Presidential Order for the State, and the caste or tribe certificate, or the revenue and school records that evidence membership, should be secured before the complaint is framed, because the identity element will be tested and a defect here is not curable later. The second check is clause selection: the facts must be fitted to the clauses of Section 3 they actually satisfy, distinguishing the created offences from the enhanced limb and from the scheduled penal code offences, since a charge sheet that reaches for the gravest clause on facts that support a lesser one invites the acquittal on which critics of the Act then rely. The third check is the identity evidence: the facts from which the accused's knowledge of the victim's identity will be proved or presumed (acquaintance, residence, prior dealings, the words used) should be recorded in the first statements, because they are freshest then and because the presumption in Section 8 operates on proved acquaintance. The fourth check is place, in every case touching the insult and humiliation clauses: the statements should fix where the words were spoken and who could see or hear, since the public view doctrine will be the trial's hinge. The fifth check is administrative: the relief application to the district administration, the claim for travelling and maintenance expenses, the request for protection where intimidation is apprehended, and the diary of hearing dates that the notice rights in Chapter IVA entitle the victim to receive. None of these steps requires anything exotic; all of them are steps the statute's text presupposes, and the cases that fail for want of them are the recurring subject of the appellate record under Section 14A.

## Prevention, monitoring and the district state

The Act is not only a prosecution statute, and its preventive chapters are the part of the design that examination answers and commentary most often omit. Section 9 empowers the state to confer on officers the powers of an executive magistrate and the powers of arrest, investigation and prosecution exercised by a police officer, for the prevention and investigation of offences. Section 10 provides for externment: where the district administration is satisfied that a person is likely to commit an offence under the Act in a specified area, it may direct that person to remove himself beyond the limits of that area for a period not exceeding two years, with consequences for breach and for a person removed twice. Section 16 empowers the imposition of a collective fine on the inhabitants of an area, a power drawn from the older Indian tradition of collective responsibility for communal offences and available where a community shelters offenders. Section 17 authorises preventive action by a law enforcement authority on a report that an offence is likely to be committed. Section 21 then states the duty of governments to take the measures the section lists for effective implementation, including legal aid, travelling and maintenance expenses, economic and social rehabilitation, and the appointment of officers and committees to oversee the whole. Under the rules, the preventive structure is institutionalised: specified areas where atrocities are anticipated are identified, a special officer is appointed for them, nodal officers coordinate at state level, and vigilance and monitoring committees at state, district and sub division level review registration, investigation, relief and prosecution in their meetings. The vigilance committee idea is the statute's answer to the question of who watches the machinery between elections and judgments, and its uneven functioning across states, reviewed in the annual reporting the rules require, is a fair index of the unevenness of the Act itself.

## How a case travels under the Act

The machinery described in pieces above assembles into a sequence, and setting the sequence out shows where the design intends pressure and where practice relieves it. A complaint begins at the police station, where the information is to be recorded, read over to the informant before signature, and registered under the provisions of this Act where the facts disclose them; the 2015 text of Section 4 makes each of those steps a duty whose wilful neglect is punishable, because experience had shown that the first filtration of a case happened at the writing desk. The district administration is informed, the place of occurrence is inspected, the damage assessed, and the first stage of relief set in motion toward payment within seven days of its trigger. Investigation passes to an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, whose investigation is to run on top priority and, under the rules as amended in 2016, to produce a charge sheet within sixty days. The case then stands before the Special Court or the Exclusive Special Court, which may take cognizance directly, proceeds from day to day once witnesses are in attendance, and is directed toward completion within two months of the charge sheet as far as possible. The victim and witnesses move through that sequence not as bystanders but as rights holders under Chapter IVA: notified of proceedings including bail hearings, protected against intimidation, reimbursed for travel and maintenance, and entitled to be heard at bail, discharge, release, parole, conviction and sentence. An appeal lies to the High Court within ninety days. The relief balance moves on its own administrative track in stages, independent of the trial's pace.

One step in that sequence carries a history that explains the 2015 drafting. Under the original Act, the Special Court was a Court of Session, and in Gangula Ashok v. State of Andhra Pradesh the Supreme Court held that, like every Court of Session, it could not take cognizance of an offence unless the case was committed to it by a magistrate, since the Act then contained no provision dispensing with committal. The consequence was a procedural loop the statute had never intended: cases registered for speedy trial were routed first through a magistrate's committal proceedings, with the delay and the additional hearing that committal imports. Karnataka amended the Act for its own territory in 2003 to confer direct cognizance, and the central amendment of 2015 generalised the solution by providing in terms that the courts it creates shall have power to take cognizance directly. The episode is a compact lesson in how special statutes fail at their joints with the general codes: the offence, the court and the timeline were all in the Act from 1989, and the case still spent its first years moving between courts because a single procedural interface had been left to implication.

Every link in that chain has a known weakness, and the honest pillar names them rather than leaving the enumeration to polemic. Registration depends on the station's willingness to apply the Act's provisions rather than lesser ones, which is why Section 4 lists the choice expressly. The sixty day investigation standard meets the ordinary constraints of forensic reports, medical evidence and witness availability. The two month trial standard meets a sessions judiciary that carries, across the country, a pending trial load in these cases alone that the annual reporting counts in lakhs, accumulated year on year. The vigilance committees that are supposed to catch these failures in review meet irregularly in many districts. None of this is an argument that the design is misconceived; it is the observation that the Act legislated for an administrative capacity that has to be built district by district, and that the distance between the text and the district is the statute's biography in every state.

## What the courts have required the prosecution to prove

The judicial reading of the Act has concentrated on three elements, and an advocate preparing or defending a case under it works almost entirely within them. The first is the ground element. In Masumsha Hasanasha Musalman v. State of Maharashtra, the Supreme Court held that the enhanced limb of Section 3(2) is attracted where the offence is committed on the ground that the victim is a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, and that a penal code offence that merely happens to have such a victim, without the identity connection, does not convert itself into an atrocity. The proposition was refined rather than abandoned in the later cases: the Court has continued to insist that the prosecution establish the identity element as a fact, while accepting, in cases such as Khuman Singh v. State of Madhya Pradesh, that knowledge of the victim's identity may be proved from the circumstances of acquaintance, residence and setting, which is precisely the territory the 2015 presumption in Section 8 covers. The acquittal pattern that follows is the one the brief for this article identifies: the commonest reason prosecutions fail is not disbelief of the underlying incident but failure to prove the identity element to the criminal standard, whether because the complaint was drafted without it, the investigation never gathered it, or the evidence at trial did not carry it. In Patan Jamal Vali v. State of Andhra Pradesh, the Court added a dimension the older cases lacked, recognising that where a victim's caste identity operates together with gender and disability in a single course of violence, the identity element should be read with that intersection in view rather than as a single axis inquiry.

The second element is place, and it governs the insult and humiliation clauses. Section 3(1) punishes intentional insult or intimidation with intent to humiliate in any place within public view. In Swaran Singh v. State, the Supreme Court drew the distinction on which the clause turns: a public place is a place the public owns or habitually uses, while a place within public view is any place, including private premises, where members of the public can see or hear what occurs. A caste insult delivered in a courtyard, a street facing room, or any setting exposed to the view of others can satisfy the clause; the same words inside a closed room cannot. Ashabai Machindra Adhagale v. State of Maharashtra applied the distinction to hold that the presence or accessibility of the public, rather than the legal character of the premises, is the test. In Hitesh Verma v. State of Uttarakhand, a three judge bench restated the boundary from the other side: abuses exchanged inside a house, out of the view of any member of the public, do not constitute the offence, and the insult must in any event be directed at the victim on account of the victim's membership of the listed community, not be the vocabulary of an unrelated quarrel. The place doctrine is frequently decisive because humiliation, unlike assault, leaves no injury to exhibit; whether the words were public is often the whole of the contested evidence, and it is a question the investigation must be built to answer from the first day.

The third element is the composition of the charge itself. In Gorige Pentaiah v. State of Andhra Pradesh, the Court held that the basic ingredients of an offence under the Act must appear in the complaint: that the accused is not a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, that the victim is, and that the act falls within a clause of Section 3. A complaint that does not disclose those ingredients does not set the special statute in motion, and proceedings founded on one are liable to be quashed. Read together, the three doctrines describe the courts' settled posture toward the Act. The statute's validity and its machinery have been sustained, including the anticipatory bail bar in Ram Krishna Balothia, but the offences are construed as specific wrongs with specific elements, and the prosecution is held to each element with ordinary criminal rigour. That posture explains a feature of the record that polemic on both sides distorts: a high acquittal count is exactly what a system produces when a statute with demanding elements meets investigations that were not built around those elements, and it is consistent both with cases that should never have been registered and with genuine offences that were badly proved, which is why the acquittal figure cannot, without more, carry the weight either side loads onto it.

## Appeals and the High Court's supervisory role

Before 2015, appeals from the Special Courts travelled under the ordinary provisions of the procedure code, and the fit was imperfect, since the code's appeal structure assumes the ordinary hierarchy of criminal courts. Section 14A regularised the position: an appeal lies to the High Court from any judgment, sentence or order of a Special Court or an Exclusive Special Court, other than an interlocutory order, on facts and on law, and the non obstante opening of the section displaces the code's scheme for these cases. The limitation period is ninety days, and the High Court may entertain an appeal after that period and up to one hundred and eighty days if satisfied the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause. An appeal on facts as well as law is a full rehearing power, which matters under this Act because the decisive questions (whether the place was within public view, whether the identity element was proved, whether the accused's acquaintance was established) are characterisations of evidence rather than pure questions of law, and a facts appeal is the only vehicle that can correct them.

Distinct from appeal is the High Court's inherent power to quash proceedings, and its exercise has shaped the Act's threshold law. The Gorige Pentaiah doctrine, that a complaint must disclose the Act's basic ingredients, operates at the quashing stage: proceedings whose founding complaint omits the identity of the parties in the statutory sense, or places the facts outside every clause of Section 3, are liable to be terminated without a trial. Quashing doctrine is the judiciary's filter on the same problem the elements doctrine addresses at trial, and the two should be read as one system operating at two points in time: a weak case may be stopped at the threshold for what the complaint omits, or fail at the end for what the evidence does not prove, and the distance between those two filters is the space in which most litigation under the Act is conducted. Defence practice under the Act is correspondingly front loaded, and prosecution practice that treats the complaint as a formality misunderstands where its case will be tested first.

## Disputes at the edges: who the Act protects, and where

The identity condition generates its own litigation at the boundaries, and a pillar should map it. Membership of a Scheduled Caste for the Act's purposes follows the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950 and the corresponding Orders for the Scheduled Tribes, as amended by Parliament from time to time. The Scheduled Castes Order carries a condition the Tribes Order does not: a person professing a religion other than the Hindu, Sikh or Buddhist religion is not deemed a member of a Scheduled Caste, a limitation that has survived constitutional challenge and that means the Act's protection, so far as castes are concerned, follows a religion linked definition drawn in 1950 and extended twice since. The lists are also state specific in a way that produces hard cases at migration: a community notified in the State of origin and not in the State of residence leaves its member protected in one jurisdiction and unprotected in another for the purposes of state lists, and the courts have generally held that the list of the State where the offence and the proceedings sit governs the statutory question, since the constitutional notifications are themselves framed in relation to a State. Membership is treated by the courts as a matter of birth within a listed community and of the notification's terms, not of marriage, adoption of customs, or self description; a person who marries into a listed community does not thereby acquire its statutory identity, and a person born into one does not lose it by marriage out of it. These edges matter beyond their doctrine, because a charge sheet that misstates the victim's notified community, or relies on a caste certificate issued under the wrong State's list, hands the defence a ground of failure that has nothing to do with the facts of the offence.

The religion condition in the Scheduled Castes Order has generated the sharpest of these boundary questions, and its leading authority is old and unreversed. In Soosai v. Union of India, the Supreme Court declined to extend Scheduled Caste status to a person who had converted to Christianity, holding that the Order's terms govern and that caste disabilities of the kind the Order addresses were not shown to persist in the same form within the convert's adopted faith community. The holding's critics have argued, in scholarship and in Parliament, that conversion does not in fact dissolve caste in social practice, and proposals to delete the religion condition from the Order recur in the legislative record; the condition stands, and while it stands, a member of a listed caste who professes Christianity or Islam is outside the Act's protection for caste based offences, a boundary of the statute that surprises most readers and that any honest account of its coverage must state plainly. No corresponding condition limits the Scheduled Tribes lists, where membership turns on the tribal community's notification and not on the faith its members profess.

The territorial and personal edges of the accused's side are simpler. The Act extends across India. It binds any person who is not a member of the listed communities, citizen or not, including public servants in their official acts, which is the premise of Section 4 and of the public servant clause within Section 3(2). It does not apply to conduct between members of the listed communities themselves, a deliberate legislative choice that confines the special statute to the asymmetric case it was designed for and leaves intra community offences to the general law, however serious they are. The choice is sometimes criticised from within the communities the Act protects, on the ground that violence by one listed group against another can replicate the same subordination; Parliament's answer, visible on the face of the text, is that the special machinery is justified by the outsider pattern and that extending it inward would dissolve the rationale. The point is recorded here because it is a genuine design boundary and not an oversight.

## The amendment of 2015 read as a diagnosis of the original Act

An amendment of this size is a document in its own right, because each of its parts identifies a failure its drafters found in the working of the original. Read that way, the amendment of 2015 contains a complete official diagnosis of the Act's first twenty five years, and it is worth extracting before the artifact table assembles the whole scheme.

The expansion of the offence list diagnoses under inclusion. The original catalogue had been drawn around the atrocities of the 1980s countryside, and the intervening decades produced acquittals and unchargeable conduct at its edges: humiliation by garlanding with footwear, which the older insult clause reached awkwardly; the boycott, which no clause reached at all; the dedication of women, which the general law touched only through other statutes; obstruction of burial grounds, cremation grounds and common resources, which sat uneasily between the land clauses and the civil rights statute. Parliament's response was to enumerate again, at greater length and with definitions attached, accepting the enumeration method's known cost (the list will age again) in exchange for charges that fit the conduct complainants actually report.

The substitution of Section 14 and the insertion of Section 14A diagnose institutional thinness. The original Act had asked state governments to specify existing sessions courts as Special Courts, and in district after district the specified court was the ordinary sessions court with an additional board outside it, carrying the district's whole sessions load. The exclusive court, trying nothing else, with its own prosecutor and a statutory direction toward two month disposal, is the amendment's admission that a designated court is not a dedicated one. The appeal section diagnoses a quieter defect, the uncertainty of the appellate route, and answers it with a single forum and a stated limitation.

The rights chapter diagnoses the victim's absence from the original design. The Act of 1989 had been written around the offender and the state: its victim appeared as a recipient of relief and as a witness. Chapter IVA writes the victim into the procedure, with notice, hearing and protection rights that operate during the case rather than after it, and its enactment aligns the Act with the direction the general criminal law had itself begun to take through the victim compensation and victim appeal provisions of the procedure code. The rewritten Section 4 and the inserted presumptions diagnose proof failures of the two kinds the enforcement record displayed: cases dying administratively before they began, answered by the enumerated duties, and cases dying evidentially on the identity element, answered by the acquaintance presumption. The schedule of lesser penal code offences adopted into the Act by the new clause of Section 3(2) diagnoses a threshold defect: conduct such as hurt, intimidation and theft against listed victims had fallen between the grave enhanced limb, which required an offence punishable with ten years or more, and the created offences, which did not include them, so that a large middle band of identity driven crime had attracted no special consequence at all.

The diagnosis is coherent, and noticing its coherence is the strongest available evidence for the assessment this article reaches at the end: the amendments of 2015 are not the record of a statute whose theory failed, but of a statute whose theory was progressively specified against the points at which practice defeated it. Whether specification can continue indefinitely, or whether the enumeration method and the district capacity constraint place a ceiling on what amendment can achieve, is the live design question the next amendment, whenever it comes, will have to answer.

## A central statute in state hands: the federal working of the Act

Police and public order are subjects of the State List, and criminal law and procedure are subjects of the Concurrent List, and the Act's daily operation sits exactly on that seam. Parliament enacted the statute, relying on its concurrent competence over criminal law and on the constitutional provisions the footing section described, but every institution that operates it is a state institution: the police who register and investigate, the state government that establishes the courts with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, the prosecutors the state appoints, the district administrations that pay the relief, and the vigilance committees through which the state reviews its own performance. The Centre's instruments are money, reporting and exhortation: central assistance flows to states under a centrally sponsored scheme for the Act's implementation, meeting part of the cost of relief and of the machinery; the rules require states to furnish the material on which the annual report to Parliament is built; and the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment reviews state performance in the committees and conferences the scheme provides. A state may also amend the Act in its application to itself with the President's assent, as Karnataka did in 2003 to confer direct cognizance on its Special Courts before the central amendment of 2015 generalised the power, an instance of the federal laboratory working in the direction of strengthening.

The consequence for assessment is that speaking of the Act's performance in the singular is a category error the annual reporting tempts everyone into. There are as many working versions of the Act as there are state systems operating it, and the concentration of registrations in a handful of states, noted in the record section, means the national aggregates describe those systems in the main. The statute's text is uniform; its institutional reality is federal, and the gap the assessment identifies (between a complete text and a half built machine) is widest precisely where the building was left to the level of government with the least fiscal room and the most immediate exposure to the local power relations the Act exists to override. That structural observation, rather than any charge of bad faith against a particular administration, is the federal reading of the enforcement record, and it predicts, correctly, that the Act's performance improves where a state builds the exclusive courts, staffs the prosecutors' posts and publishes its relief figures, and stagnates where it does not.

## The Act after the replacement of the codes

The replacement of the colonial criminal codes by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam changed the Act's references without changing the Act. Provisions of the Act that speak of offences under the Indian Penal Code are read with the corresponding offences of the new Sanhita; the bar in Section 18, framed against the anticipatory bail provision of the old procedure code, operates against the corresponding provision of the Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, which carries the anticipatory bail jurisdiction in its place; the victim compensation provision of the old code, with which the relief scheme interacts, has its counterpart in the new code's compensation scheme section; and the enhanced limb's threshold test, an offence punishable with ten years or more, is applied to the punishments the new Sanhita prescribes for the corresponding offences. The transition questions that arise in individual cases (which code governs an offence committed before the replacement, a matter the new codes' repeal and savings provisions answer by keeping the old law for accrued liability) are questions for the courts in those cases, and this pillar records only the structural point: a special statute survives a general codification by translation of its references, and nothing in the replacement altered the Act's offences, its machinery or its relief scheme. Readers tracing a provision across the replacement will find the correspondence set out in the comparison of the two penal codes linked earlier in this article.

## What the Act adds to ordinary criminal law

The findable artifact for this statute is the comparison its critics and defenders both need: for each of its working features, what the position would be under ordinary criminal law and what this Act provides instead.

| Feature | Position under ordinary criminal law | What the Atrocities Act provides |
|---|---|---|
| Specific offences | The penal code punishes acts such as assault, trespass and intimidation without regard to the social meaning of the act or the identity of the victim. | Section 3(1) creates offences that exist nowhere else: humiliation in public view, garlanding with footwear, forced tonsuring, land dispossession, coerced labour, civic obstruction, dedication as a devadasi, sexual offences against listed women, and social or economic boycott. |
| Enhanced punishment limb | A penal code offence carries the sentence the code prescribes, whatever the victim's identity and whatever the offender's knowledge of it. | Under Section 3(2), a penal code offence punishable with ten years or more, committed knowing the victim belongs to a listed community, carries imprisonment for life; scheduled lesser offences are adopted into the Act by reference. |
| Public servant duty | Neglect of duty by an official is a disciplinary matter, or an offence only under specific provisions of other statutes. | Section 4 makes wilful neglect of the duties the Act assigns, from registration to charge sheet, an offence punishable with six months to one year of imprisonment. |
| The trial court | Sessions offences are tried by the ordinary Court of Session after committal, in the order the docket reaches them. | Section 14 creates Special Courts and Exclusive Special Courts that try only these offences, take cognizance directly, and sit at sessions level. |
| Trial timeline | No general statutory period governs the completion of a sessions trial; the speedy trial right is enforced case by case. | The statute directs disposal within two months as far as possible, day to day proceedings, and completion within two months of the charge sheet as far as possible. |
| Victim and witness rights | The victim is a witness in the state's case, with compensation dependent on court orders and the general victim compensation schemes. | Chapter IVA gives victims and witnesses rights to protection, notice of proceedings including bail, expenses, information, legal aid and being heard at bail, discharge, release, parole, conviction and sentence. |

| Relief scale | Monetary relief for a victim awaits conviction, a fine, or a discretionary compensation order, and is usually small and slow. | The rules prescribe a scheduled scale, Rs 85,000 to Rs 8,25,000 on the 2016 revision, paid by the district administration in stages from registration onward, without waiting for the verdict. |

## The Act beside its sibling statutes

No special statute operates alone, and this Act's working overlaps three neighbours closely enough that the overlaps decide real cases. The civil rights statute has been treated at the outset; its field is exclusion, and where one course of conduct contains both exclusion and atrocity, charges under both statutes can arise from the same facts, with the atrocities machinery governing the grave part. The prohibition of manual scavenging is governed by the statute of 2013, which creates its own offences and rehabilitation entitlements; the Act of 1989 reaches the same conduct, where the victim belongs to a listed community and the identity condition is met, through its clause on compelling a person to carry out manual scavenging and similar tasks, and the complainant's adviser chooses the charge, or combines them, by comparing the elements and the forums rather than by habit. The bonded labour statute of 1976 abolishes the bonded labour system and extinguishes bonded debts, a civil and restorative scheme; the Act of 1989 criminalises the compelling of forced or bonded labour as an atrocity, so the same arrangement of coercion can produce release and rehabilitation under one statute and prosecution under the other, and the two are complements rather than rivals. A fourth neighbour operates quietly in every trial: the general victim compensation provision of the procedure code, under which a court may recommend compensation and the district legal services authority may award it where the statutory relief does not reach, interacts with the rules' schedule as a residual source rather than an alternative one. The pattern across all four overlaps is the pattern of Indian protective legislation generally: Parliament legislates by accumulation, each new statute assuming the older ones, and the skill the system demands of its users is precisely the skill of selecting the instrument whose elements fit the facts. The Act's pillar position in the cluster rests on that selection being made correctly at the complaint stage, which is why this article has treated clause selection as a first order question rather than a pleading detail.

## The scrutiny ecosystem: commissions, Parliament and the annual report

The Act does not lack overseers, and mapping them completes the institutional picture before the record is examined. The National Commission for Scheduled Castes, a constitutional body under Article 338, and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes under Article 338A, each hold the powers of a civil court for investigating matters within their mandate, which includes monitoring the safeguards the Constitution and the laws provide to the two communities, and the working of this Act stands at the centre of that monitoring. The commissions inquire into specific complaints, review registration and prosecution patterns, and report to the President, whose reports are laid before Parliament with a memorandum of the action taken on their recommendations. Their reports on the Act have returned, across decades and under commissions of varying temperaments, to the same findings the enforcement section of this article sets out: under registration in places, investigation below the standard the statute sets, trials that do not conclude, relief that does not reach, and courts directed but not established. The parliamentary layer adds the annual report on the working of the Act that the Union Ministry lays before the Houses, the questions through which members extract district and state figures from the Ministry, and the standing committee examinations in which the Ministry accounts for the scheme's expenditure and the states' returns. The significance of the ecosystem for the reader is not ceremonial. It means the Act is one of the most continuously reported statutes in Indian law, and that a researcher testing any claim about its working has official series, commission findings and parliamentary answers against which to test it, provided the denominator discipline of the next section is observed. It also means the failures the record shows cannot be described as unknown to the state: they are documented by the state's own instruments, in most years, in terms. The distance between the Act's text and its operation is therefore not a discovery awaiting a researcher; it is a standing admission in the official record, and the argument is only ever about what follows from it.

## The enforcement record, and the discipline it requires

Every figure in this section carries its source, its period and its base, and the reader should treat any figure quoted elsewhere without those three attachments as unusable, whoever quotes it. The National Crime Records Bureau's Crime in India 2022 recorded 57,582 cases registered for crimes against Scheduled Castes and 10,064 cases for crimes against Scheduled Tribes. That head of the Bureau's tables is wider than this Act: it counts offences against members of the two communities under the penal code together with offences under this Act. The Union Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment's annual report on the working of the Act for the same year counts the Act's own cases at 51,656 for victims from the Scheduled Castes and 9,735 for victims from the Scheduled Tribes. Two official counts of atrocity cases for one year therefore differ by several thousand, and neither is wrong; they measure different sets. Anyone who has seen the two figures deployed interchangeably in debate has seen why [the enforcement record articles in this series, including the account of the anti-terror statutes](/2011/07/15/anti-terror-law-enforcement-record/), insist on the denominator before the argument.

The annual reporting supplies the two further figures around which the public argument revolves. On the Ministry's count for 2022, charge sheets were filed in about six in ten of the cases investigated under the Act for victims from the Scheduled Castes, the balance closing as final reports on grounds that include insufficiency of evidence and cases found false. On conviction, the same reporting records a conviction rate of 32.4 per cent for 2022, computed on the cases in which trial was completed during the year, against 39.2 per cent on the same base in 2020. Each of those figures must be read on its base. The conviction rate on completed trials is not the proportion of registered cases that end in conviction in the same year, which is far lower, because the great majority of registered cases do not reach a completed trial within the year at all: the pending trial load under the Act runs into lakhs of cases nationally, and a trial completed in 2022 is usually the product of a registration from years earlier. An acquittal, further, is a finding that the prosecution did not prove its case to the criminal standard, and under this Act the failure is located, by the doctrine set out earlier, most often in the identity elements rather than in the occurrence of the incident; an acquittal is therefore not a finding that the complaint was fabricated, and a final report marked false at the investigation stage is a different event from an acquittal at trial, recorded by a different authority on a different test. The reporting also records the machinery gap in concrete terms: its review counted 194 districts with Special Courts established out of 498 districts across the fourteen states it surveyed, against a statutory direction that adequate courts be established everywhere cases arise.

The geography of the record is as instructive as its totals. The annual report for 2022 records that cases under the Act are heavily concentrated: a small number of states account for the overwhelming share of registrations, with Uttar Pradesh returning the largest count of cases under the Act for victims from the Scheduled Castes at 12,287, followed by Rajasthan at 8,651, Madhya Pradesh at 7,732 and Bihar at 6,799, the first six states together accounting for about four fifths of the national figure, and thirteen states together for nearly the whole of it. Concentration of that order cuts both ways in argument and should be handled with the same discipline as the rates. It may reflect the distribution of the underlying conduct, the distribution of population, the willingness of police in particular states to register under the Act rather than under lesser provisions, or the presence in some states of the organisations that assist complainants; the tables do not discriminate among those explanations, and state rankings quoted without the caution are a staple of the debate's weaker literature. What concentration does establish, without interpretation, is administrative: the Act's national performance figures are in substance the performance figures of a handful of state systems, and reform addressed to the average district in the abstract will miss the districts in which the statute actually operates.

What the record settles, then, is narrower than either side's rhetoric. It settles that registration under the Act happens at a large and rising scale, that investigation converts a substantial majority of investigated cases into charge sheets, that trials complete slowly and in small proportion to registrations, that about one in three completed trials ends in conviction on the annual reporting's computation, and that the specialised court network the statute directs is incomplete years after the direction was given. It does not settle the question the argument is actually about, which is whether the cases that fail were false at birth or true and unproved, because the published categories do not distinguish those two populations, and no honest reading of the tables can make them do so.

## Where cases are actually decided: investigation quality and witness survival

Between registration and judgment sits the variable that neither the offence list nor the court structure controls, and the enforcement argument cannot be closed without it. A charge sheet under this Act must carry, in admissible form, the membership of the victim, the identity condition on the accused, the clause specific act, and, for the aggravated forms, the material from which knowledge and ground will be inferred. Each of those is an investigative product. Membership is documentary and usually available. The clause specific act is testimonial in the dignity offences and documentary in the land offences, which is one reason the land clauses convict at a different rate from the insult clauses in the reported pattern. Knowledge and ground are assembled from the parties' history: prior disputes, prior complaints, the words recorded in the first statements, the composition of any group involved. An investigation that records the incident and stops has gathered the one part of the case that was never going to be the problem, and the acquittal that follows eighteen months later will be entered, accurately, as a failure of proof, from which debaters will then extract whichever moral they brought with them.

Witness survival is the second half of the same variable. The interval between charge sheet and trial in a sessions case is measured in years in most districts, and in these cases the witnesses characteristically live inside the social and economic field of the accused's side: they work in its fields, borrow in its markets and send their children to its schools. Chapter IVA's protection and expenses provisions, the general witness protection scheme adopted nationally in 2018, and the boycott offence are all aimed at this interval, and their combined purpose is to make it possible for a witness's evidence at trial to resemble the statement recorded at investigation. The phenomenon of the hostile witness, pervasive in the appellate record under the Act, is what the failure of that purpose looks like from inside a judgment. Reform proposals in the official record return to these two variables with a regularity that the more theatrical debate about arrest and bail obscures: scrutiny of charge sheets before filing, prosecutors attached from the investigation stage, protection assessed at the first hearing, and trials in the exclusive courts actually run from day to day. A reader who wants to predict the Act's next decade should watch those unglamorous indicators, because the statute's theory stands or falls on them and on nothing else.

## When relief is refused: the writ as the scheme's backstop

The relief scheme's failures generate their own small body of practice, and a pillar should record its shape. Where the district administration does not assess, does not pay within the standard the rules set, or disputes the victim's eligibility, the remedy ordinarily taken is a writ petition to the High Court under Article 226, in which the schedule and the registered case do most of the work: entitlement follows the offence category and the stage reached, and the court's inquiry is into the administrative default rather than into the merits of the prosecution. The pattern of these petitions is itself evidence about the scheme. They are rarely about the schedule's amounts, which are fixed and indexed; they are about delay, about the stage classification of a payment, about a family's exclusion on a membership dispute, and about the non cash entitlements (pension, employment, land, a house) that require several departments to move together and therefore move slowly or not at all. The vigilance and monitoring committees are the intended first forum for exactly these grievances, and the recourse to the writ is, in part, a measure of the committees' unevenness. The point completes the relief section's argument: the scheme is well designed at the level of entitlement and weakly guaranteed at the level of delivery, and the distance between the two is administered, district by district, by the same district state whose performance under Section 4 and Section 14 the rest of this article has measured.

## The two cases, in their proponents' own terms

The case for the statute's necessity is made by its defenders on evidence they regard as structural. Violence and humiliation directed at members of the listed communities is documented in the official data set out in this article and in successive reports of the commissions charged with these communities' welfare, and the conduct the first limb criminalises, dispossession, boycott, forced degrading labour, public humiliation, is conduct the general code describes inadequately or not at all. The ordinary law, on this case, had its opportunity across the decades of the civil rights statute and produced registration failures, investigation failures and conviction failures so consistent that a legislature was entitled to conclude the failure was institutional rather than incidental, and to answer it institutionally: senior investigators, dedicated courts, punishable neglect, protected witnesses and relief that does not depend on the prosecution. Writers in this line, among them the scholar Anand Teltumbde in his analyses of caste violence, argue that the Act is invoked in only a fraction of the incidents its categories describe, so that its working problem is under use against a background of under reporting, and that the acquittal figures reflect the difficulty of proving caste linked offences in localities where witnesses live under the accused's side's influence. The National Campaign on Dalit Human Rights, in its monitoring of the Act's working, has made the complementary administrative argument: relief unclaimed, committees unmet and courts unbuilt are failures of the state, not of the statute's theory. On this side's strongest formulation, repealing or diluting the Act would not restore a neutral ordinary law; it would restore the specific institutional conditions under which the offences were, in practice, free of consequence.

The case made by the Act's critics begins from the procedure rather than the offences, and it is entitled to be stated as strongly as its proponents state it. The offences are cognizable and non bailable; arrest can follow upon registration without any preliminary filter; the anticipatory bail route is closed by Section 18; and the consequences of accusation (loss of employment prospects, suspension for public servants, reputational ruin in a small community) are severe and largely irreversible even where the case ends in acquittal. Critics point to the same annual figures used by defenders and read them the other way: where about two in three completed trials end in acquittal and a substantial share of investigated cases close as final reports, the system is, on their reading, processing a large volume of complaints that cannot be sustained, some of them filed, they argue, as leverage in land disputes, service rivalries and local quarrels that have nothing to do with caste. The criticism found its most prominent institutional expression in a judgment of the Supreme Court authored by Justice A. K. Goel in 2018, which recorded a judicial view that the Act's machinery was being turned to false implication and vented grievances, and imposed safeguards on registration and arrest; the legislative answer to that judgment and the Court's later reconsideration of it are the sequence that the companion article on the 2018 amendment examines, and they are not re-argued here. Defenders answer the critics' reading of the figures with the denominator discipline: acquittal under this Act characteristically follows failure to prove the identity element, which is as consistent with a true offence badly proved as with a false one, and a legal system that cannot distinguish those populations from its published tables should not let either side's certainty stand in for the missing analysis. Critics reply that the burden of the process falls on the accused from the day of registration regardless of which population a case belongs to, and that a statute justified by institutional failure should be especially careful not to reproduce that failure against a different set of people.

The value disagreement beneath the factual one should be named, since it does not yield to evidence. One side holds that criminal law addressed to hierarchy may legitimately distribute its protections and its procedural strength unequally, because the underlying risks are unequal; the other holds that criminal process, of all state instruments, must remain symmetrical between citizen and citizen, because its coercive weight is the same on every shoulder it falls. The Act is Parliament's resolution of that disagreement in favour of the first position, sustained by the courts on validity while being construed strictly on elements, and a reader who sees that the resolution is a choice, and what each side of the choice costs, understands the statute better than a reader who has memorised its sections.

## Assessment

The verdict this pillar defends follows from its opening claim. Judged as one statute, the Act invites a single score, and every single score given to it is an artefact of which limb and which failure the scorer looked at. Judged as two statutes and a machine, the assessment separates cleanly. The first limb is the Act's durable achievement: it named a class of wrongs that the general code could not see, and the 2015 additions (boycott, devadasi dedication, obstruction of common resources, the offences against women) extended the naming to the forms of subordination that the intervening decades had made visible. Its weakness is enumeration itself: a list ages, and conduct outside the clauses is outside the Act. The second limb is the Act's most contestable design choice, and also, on the doctrine the courts have built, its most disciplined: the life sentence consequence is fenced by the knowledge and ground requirements, so that the limb operates in practice as a sentencing rule for offences proved to be identity driven, which is either exactly what its defenders say it should be or an elaborate route to a result sentencing discretion could reach, depending on the reader's position in the value disagreement recorded in this article. The machine is where the Act is most plainly failing on its own terms: the two month trial direction, the district court network, the Section 4 duty and the vigilance reviews are all textually complete and operationally partial, and the honest summary of four decades is that Parliament built a demanding institutional design and the states have built about half of it. A reader who wants the argument that followed when that gap between text and operation reached the Supreme Court in 2018 should continue to the companion article on the amendment and the Court; a reader who wants the older statute this one supplemented should continue to the account of the civil rights statute. The pillar's closing judgment is the one its title promises: two statutes in one cover, and the cover has mattered more to the argument than either statute inside it.

The reader who has followed this pillar to its end has the equipment the article set out to supply, and it is worth stating what the equipment does when applied. Presented with a reported atrocity, the reader can ask, in order, whether the victim is a member of a notified community in the State of occurrence; whether the facts fit a clause of Section 3, or an enhanced or scheduled offence; what the first statements say about knowledge of identity, about place, and about the words used; whether the case stands before the exclusive court it is owed, on a charge sheet filed within the standard the rules set, by an officer of the required rank; what the victim has been paid and what remains due under the schedule's stages; and whether the elements doctrine and the presumptions, rather than the headlines, will decide the trial. Those are not the questions the statute's two camps ask, and that is the point. The camps argue about the shape the law should take, which is the continuing legislative question that the companion articles pursue; the pillar's reader knows the shape the law has, which is the prior question that every argument about its future must survive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the SC and ST Atrocities Act make an offence?

Section 3 of the Act makes an offence of conduct directed at a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe by a person who belongs to neither community. The list covers forced consumption of obnoxious substances, dumping of excreta or carcasses near a victim's premises, forcible removal of clothing, parading and tonsuring, wrongful occupation or dispossession of land, forced or bonded labour, interference with voting and candidature, false legal proceedings and false information to a public servant, intentional insult or intimidation with intent to humiliate in a place within public view, offences concerning water sources and places of public resort, and the categories added in 2015, including garlanding with footwear, dedication of a woman as a devadasi, sexual offences against women of the listed communities and the imposition of a social or economic boycott. The punishment for these offences runs from six months to five years, with fine.

### Q: Who can complain and who can be accused under the Atrocities Act?

A complaint may be made by the victim of an offence, and information of an offence may reach the police from any source, as in ordinary criminal process; the case then proceeds as a state prosecution rather than as a private suit. The person accused must satisfy the Act's identity condition: Section 3 applies to a person who is not a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, and the victim must be a member of one of those communities as notified under Articles 341 and 342 of the Constitution. It follows that an offence by one member of the listed communities against another is not an offence under this Act, however serious it is, and must be prosecuted under the general law. Public servants are within the Act's reach both as potential offenders and, under Section 4, as persons punishable for neglecting the duties it assigns them.

### Q: What duty does the Atrocities Act place on public servants?

Section 4 of the Act makes it an offence, punishable with imprisonment of six months to one year, for a public servant who is not a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe wilfully to neglect the duties the Act and its rules require of him. The amendment of 2015 rewrote the section to enumerate those duties, and they cover the points at which cases had historically failed: reading the informant's statement over before signature, registering the complaint under the correct provisions of the Act, recording statements of the victim and witnesses, conducting the investigation and filing the charge sheet within the prescribed period, preparing the inspection and assessment of damage, and giving effect to the preventive and relief obligations the rules impose. The duty is the statute's answer to administrative filtration of complaints, converting each known failure into a specified obligation backed by a criminal sanction.

### Q: What rights do victims and witnesses have under the Atrocities Act?

Chapter IVA of the Act, inserted by the amendment of 2015 and consisting of Section 15A, gives victims, their dependents and witnesses a catalogue of rights enforceable against the state. It includes the right to be treated with fairness, respect and dignity, with special care for women, children, persons with disability and older persons; protection from intimidation, coercion and violence; reasonable, accurate and timely notice of court proceedings, including bail proceedings; information about the case, about legal aid and about the entitlement to relief; payment of travelling and maintenance expenses during investigation and trial; socio economic relief; legal services through the district legal services machinery; and the right to be heard at the stages of bail, discharge, release, parole, conviction and sentence, including by written submissions. The state is further required to prepare and publicise a charter of these rights so that they do not depend on specialist knowledge to claim.

### Q: Is anticipatory bail available under the Atrocities Act?

No. Section 18 of the Act provides that nothing in Section 438 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the provision under which a person who apprehends arrest may seek bail in advance, applies to a case involving the arrest of a person accused of an offence under this Act. The bar was part of the original design of 1989, and the Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality in State of Madhya Pradesh v. Ram Krishna Balothia, holding that it did not offend the equality or liberty provisions of the Constitution. The bar closes only the anticipatory route: a person arrested under the Act may still seek regular bail from the competent court, at which the victim is entitled to notice and to be heard under Chapter IVA. The controversy that later gathered around the breadth of the bar, and the legislative and judicial sequence of 2018 and after, is examined in the companion article on the 2018 amendment.

### Q: What did the 2015 atrocities amendment add?

The amendment of 2015, enacted as Act 1 of 2016 and brought into force on 26 January 2016, was the largest reconstruction of the Act since its enactment. It substantially expanded the offence list in Section 3(1), adding categories including garlanding with footwear, forcible tonsuring and removal of moustache, dedication of a woman as a devadasi, imposition of a social or economic boycott, obstruction of the use of common property resources, and sexual touching and gestures without consent. It substituted Section 14 to create Exclusive Special Courts alongside specified Special Courts, with direct cognizance and a two month disposal direction, and inserted Section 14A on appeals. It inserted Chapter IVA, Section 15A, stating the rights of victims and witnesses. It rewrote Section 4 to enumerate the duties of public servants, and it inserted the presumption that an accused acquainted with the victim or the victim's family knew the victim's caste or tribal identity unless the contrary is proved.

### Q: What compensation and relief is payable under the Atrocities Act?

Relief under the Act is paid administratively under Rule 12 of the Rules of 1995 and the schedule annexed to them, and it is distinct from any compensation a court may order on conviction. On the revision of the rules notified in April 2016, the scale runs from Rs 85,000 for the least grave scheduled offences to Rs 8,25,000 at the top of the scale, the amount attached to murder and to gang rape, with rape carrying Rs 5,00,000 and imposition of a social or economic boycott Rs 1,00,000. The amount for each offence is released in stages as the case progresses, a share from the registration stage, a further share when the charge sheet is filed and the balance at the later stages the schedule specifies, with payment due within seven days of the stage that triggers it. For certain grievous offences against women, relief is payable on the conclusion of the trial even where the trial does not end in conviction.

### Q: Why is the conviction rate under the Atrocities Act low?

The rate depends on the base it is computed on, and the two bases tell different stories. The Union Ministry's annual report on the Act records a conviction rate of 32.4 per cent for 2022 computed on cases in which trial was completed that year; computed against all cases registered in a year, the proportion ending in conviction is far lower, because most cases do not reach a completed trial within the year and the pending load is large. On the completed trial base, the doctrine explains much of the outcome: the prosecution must prove not only the act but the identity elements, that the accused is outside the listed communities, the victim within them, and, for the aggravated offences, the ground or knowledge connecting the act to that identity. Investigations that do not gather that element, witnesses exposed to local pressure across long trials, and delay that erodes testimony all produce acquittals that the published categories cannot separate into false cases and true cases badly proved.

### Q: Is every crime against a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe an atrocity under this Act?

No, and the distinction is the one most often lost in public discussion. An offence is an atrocity under the Act only if it falls within Section 3: either it is one of the offences the section creates, or it is a penal code offence drawn into the Act by the enhanced limb or the schedule. A crime against a member of a listed community that falls outside those routes, an ordinary theft unconnected with any scheduled clause, for example, is prosecuted under the general law in the ordinary courts, without the Act's special machinery, presumptions or relief schedule. The courts have added a further requirement for the aggravated forms: the prosecution must establish the connection between the act and the victim's identity, the ground or knowledge element, and a case that cannot establish it does not become an atrocity merely because the victim belongs to a listed community.

### Q: What does "in any place within public view" mean under the Atrocities Act?

The phrase qualifies the insult and intimidation offences in Section 3(1), and the Supreme Court's reading of it decides a large share of those prosecutions. In Swaran Singh v. State, the Court distinguished a place within public view from a public place: the question is not who owns the premises but whether members of the public can see or hear what happens there. An insult delivered in a courtyard, a veranda or any space exposed to the view of others can satisfy the clause; words exchanged inside a closed room or within a house, out of public view, cannot, as the three judge bench confirmed in Hitesh Verma v. State of Uttarakhand. The rationale is written into the offence itself: humiliation is a public act, aimed at the victim's standing before others, and words nobody outside the room could witness lack the element that makes the wrong an atrocity rather than a private quarrel in abusive language.

### Q: What happens if the accused claims they did not know the victim's caste or tribe?

Knowledge of the victim's identity is an ingredient of the aggravated offences, so the claim matters, but the accused's bare assertion does not decide it. The amendment of 2015 inserted a presumption in Section 8: where the accused had personal knowledge of the victim or of the victim's family, the court shall presume that the accused was aware of the caste or tribal identity of the victim, unless the contrary is proved. The presumption reflects the social reality of the settings in which these cases arise, where the parties are usually acquainted and identity is a matter of general local knowledge. An accused who genuinely lacked acquaintance or the means of knowledge may rebut it with evidence. Before the presumption was enacted, the Supreme Court in cases such as Khuman Singh v. State of Madhya Pradesh had already accepted that knowledge could be inferred from the circumstances of the parties' relationship and the setting of the offence.

### Q: Which court tries offences under the Atrocities Act?

Offences under the Act are tried by a Special Court or an Exclusive Special Court under Section 14, as substituted by the amendment of 2015. The State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, establishes an Exclusive Special Court for one or more districts, a court that tries only offences under this Act; in districts recording fewer cases, an existing Court of Session is specified as a Special Court instead. Both are courts of session level, able to impose the Act's gravest sentences, and both may take cognizance of an offence directly, without the committal proceedings that ordinarily carry a case from a magistrate to the sessions court. A Special Public Prosecutor, or an Exclusive Special Public Prosecutor, conducts the prosecution, and an appeal from the court's judgment, sentence or order lies to the High Court under Section 14A, within ninety days.

### Q: How quickly is an atrocities trial supposed to be completed?

The statute sets its expectation in Section 14 in deliberately hortatory terms. It is the duty of the State Government to establish an adequate number of courts to ensure that cases under the Act are disposed of within a period of two months, as far as possible; proceedings are to continue from day to day until the witnesses in attendance have been examined; and the trial is, as far as possible, to be completed within a period of two months from the date of filing of the charge sheet. The repeated qualification means the timeline is a legislative standard against which delay is measured, not a condition whose breach ends the prosecution or entitles the accused to discharge. The distance between the standard and the record is the Act's central implementation fact: the annual reporting counts the cases pending trial under the Act in lakhs, and a completed trial is typically the product of a registration from years earlier.

### Q: Who investigates an offence under the Atrocities Act, and how fast must the charge sheet be filed?

Investigation is reserved to an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, appointed under Rule 7 of the Rules of 1995 with regard to the officer's past experience and sense of ability and justice. The reservation to senior rank is a designed safeguard against the station level filtration of complaints that the Act's history documented. The investigation is to be conducted on top priority and completed, with the charge sheet filed in court, within sixty days, the period fixed by the amendment of the rules in 2016. Completing the investigation and filing the charge sheet within the period is also among the duties enumerated in Section 4, so that a wilful failure by the investigating officer is not merely an administrative lapse but a neglect of a statutory duty punishable under that section, quite apart from its consequences for the victim's staged relief, one share of which is triggered by the filing of the charge sheet.

### Q: What is the difference between the Atrocities Act and the Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955?

The two statutes police different harms under the same constitutional promise. The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 gives effect to Article 17 by punishing the enforcement of disabilities arising out of untouchability: exclusion from places of public worship, water sources, shops, services, hospitals and occupations, and boycott of a person for exercising rights under it. Its penalties were fixed in its era and are short. The Atrocities Act of 1989 addresses violence, humiliation, dispossession and coercion: offences that exist nowhere else, enhanced punishment for grave penal code offences against listed victims, special courts, a bar on anticipatory bail, victim and witness rights, and a scheduled relief scheme. Exclusion is the first statute's field and atrocity is the second's, so a refusal of entry and a public humiliation arising from the same dispute may engage both statutes at once, through different offences, different courts and different consequences.

### Q: How does the Atrocities Act treat offences that are also offences under the Penal Code?

It does not re enact them; it attaches consequences to them by reference. Under Section 3(2), a person outside the listed communities who commits an offence under the Indian Penal Code punishable with imprisonment of ten years or more against a member of a listed community or their property, knowing the victim's identity, is punishable with imprisonment for life and with fine. Clauses of the same provision address false evidence intended to procure a wrongful conviction, mischief by fire against property, and the screening of offenders. The amendment of 2015 added a route for lesser penal code offences: a schedule to the Act lists offences such as hurt, grievous hurt and criminal intimidation, and committing a scheduled offence against a listed victim with the requisite knowledge is punishable under this Act with the punishment the penal code provides for it. The penal code offence must still be proved as the code defines it; the Act changes the forum, the sentence and the surrounding machinery.

### Q: What punishments does the Atrocities Act prescribe?

The punishment structure follows the two limbs. Offences created by Section 3(1) carry imprisonment from six months to five years, with fine. Within Section 3(2), giving or fabricating false evidence to procure a conviction for a capital offence carries imprisonment for life, and the death penalty where an innocent person is executed in consequence; false evidence aimed at offences punishable with seven years or more carries six months to seven years; mischief by fire against property carries six months to seven years, rising where a dwelling is intended to be destroyed; and a penal code offence punishable with ten years or more, committed with knowledge of the victim's listed identity, carries imprisonment for life and fine. A public servant committing an offence under Section 3(2) faces a minimum of one year. Section 5 enhances the punishment of a person convicted a second time to a minimum of one year, and Section 7 empowers the court to order forfeiture of property used in the offence. Neglect of duty under Section 4 carries six months to one year.

### Q: What preventive machinery does the Act create in areas where atrocities recur?

The Act's preventive scheme works through the district administration rather than the courts. Section 9 empowers the state to confer magisterial and police powers on officers for prevention and investigation. Section 10 permits externment: a person likely to commit an offence in a specified area may be directed to remove himself from it for up to two years, with further consequences on a second removal. Section 16 empowers the imposition of a collective fine on the inhabitants of an area, and Section 17 authorises preventive action on a report that an offence is likely. Under the rules, areas where atrocities are anticipated are identified, special officers are appointed for them, nodal officers coordinate at the state level, and vigilance and monitoring committees at state, district and sub division level review registration, investigation, relief and the rights of victims and witnesses. Section 21 states the government's duty to take the measures listed in it, including legal aid, rehabilitation and the machinery of oversight, for the Act's effective implementation.

### Q: Who counts as a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe under the Act?

Membership follows the constitutional lists. Under Articles 341 and 342, the President specifies, after consultation with the Governor, the castes and tribes deemed to be Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in relation to each State, and Parliament alone may alter the lists by law. The Act's definitions adopt that mechanism, so a victim's community must appear in the list for the State concerned; a community listed in one State is not, by that fact alone, listed in another. The Scheduled Castes lists carry a further condition inherited from the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950: a person professing a religion other than the Hindu, Sikh or Buddhist religion is not deemed a member of a Scheduled Caste, a limitation that does not apply to the Scheduled Tribes lists. The courts treat membership as a matter of birth within a notified community, so it is not acquired by marriage into the community and not lost by marriage out of it, and the caste or tribe certificate and the list entry are the documents on which the question is ordinarily decided.

### Q: What is the social and economic boycott offence added in 2015?

The amendment of 2015 made it an offence under Section 3(1) to impose or threaten a social or economic boycott on a person belonging to a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, or on their family. A social boycott, as the Act's explanation frames it, is a refusal to permit a person to render or receive customary services, or to abide by the social relations and incidents of the community; an economic boycott is a refusal to deal with, work for hire for, or do business with the person, including the denial of opportunities and services. The offence targets an instrument of collective discipline rather than an individual act of violence: the resolution of a village, a trade body or a dominant group to cut a family off from work, custom, water, grazing or trade, enforced without a blow being struck. It sits among the Section 3(1) offences with the standard punishment of that sub section, and the relief schedule attaches a stated amount to it, Rs 1,00,000 on the 2016 revision, recognising that a boycott's harm is economic as much as physical.
