George Cukor's Romeo and Juliet, released by Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer in August 1936, is the studio system's monument to Shakespeare: the most expensive sound film MGM had made to that date, the most carefully researched, the most reverently cast, and, by the measure of the play it adapts, among the least alive. The verdict of this study is that the picture succeeds almost completely at the task its producer set and fails almost completely at the task the playwright set, and that the distance between those two tasks is the most instructive fact in the screen history of the tragedy. Irving Thalberg wanted a production that no professor could fault and no rival studio could match, a proof that Hollywood craftsmanship had come of age. William Shakespeare wrote a play about two teenagers whose speed, appetite, and heedlessness outrun every adult institution in Verona. Cukor's film honours the first intention in nearly every frame and betrays the second in nearly every casting decision, and it does both so handsomely, with such craft and such evident love of the text, that it remains the essential starting point for any account of the play on film.

The film also carries a distinction that frames everything else in the screen cluster of this series. Silent cameras had filmed the tragedy repeatedly from the first years of the century, in the one-reel and feature versions surveyed in [the earliest Romeo and Juliet films of the 1900s](/2028/08/18/earliest-romeo-juliet-films-1900s/), but those pictures could show the story only in pantomime and intertitle. Cukor's production was the play's first sound-era feature, the first version able to carry the verse itself, and it understood that ability as a solemn trust. Where the silent films had treated the play as a famous plot to be illustrated, MGM treated it as a sacred text to be enshrined. The enshrinement cost just over two million dollars, employed the studio's most decorated craftsmen, consulted professors from Harvard and Cornell, and placed the producer's wife, a star in her mid-thirties, opposite a Romeo of forty-three. Every one of those facts belongs to the same decision, and the decision, rather than any single performance or set, is the film's real subject.

To call the picture the Hollywood studio era's Shakespeare is to make a claim about representativeness, and the claim holds. The studio era, at its zenith in the middle nineteen-thirties, believed that a great factory could manufacture any valued object, a western, a musical, a biography, a classic, by applying capital, research, and contracted genius in sufficient quantity, and it tested the belief most severely on the objects that resist manufacture. Romeo and Juliet was the severest test the system set itself in the decade: the most prestigious author, the most famous love story, the largest budget, the longest preparation. The film that resulted is therefore evidence of a kind no lesser production could supply, since it shows the system operating at maximum strength and reveals, in the pattern of its successes and failures, exactly where the system's strength ended. Its sets, costumes, music, and verse-speaking stand at the summit of what the factory could do. Its lovers stand at the boundary of what the factory could not do, which was to be young on purpose. A history of the play on screen that skipped this film would lack its control experiment, the version against which the variables of age, pace, and reverence were first isolated.

## Why MGM Built a Monument: Thalberg, Mayer, and the Prestige Gamble

The picture existed because one man willed it against the settled judgement of his own studio. Irving Thalberg, MGM's production chief and the most admired producer in Hollywood, pressed for a film of the play across five years, and Louis B. Mayer, the studio's head, resisted for most of them. Mayer's objection was commercial and, on the evidence of the market, reasonable: he held that mass audiences regarded Shakespeare as material over their heads, and the Depression-era ledger punished expensive experiments. Thalberg's counter-argument was never really about the play's box-office prospects. He argued that a classic mounted with full pageantry and ritual would raise the studio's standing in the public eye, that prestige was itself an asset, purchased once and banked for years. When the film was finally approved, the approval came with an instruction to keep the budget down, an instruction the production then honoured in the breach on a scale that became part of Hollywood legend.

The trigger, when it came, was rivalry rather than conversion. Jack L. Warner announced that Warner Bros. would film Max Reinhardt's stage production of A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Mayer, unwilling to be outdone in cultural ambition by the Burbank studio, released Thalberg to proceed. A second encouragement came from the stage: the Broadway revival of the tragedy that opened in December 1934, with Katharine Cornell as Juliet, had demonstrated that the play could still draw fashionable audiences in a serious production, and it supplied the film with one member of its company, since Basil Rathbone, Cornell's Romeo, crossed to the screen version in the altered capacity of Tybalt. Thalberg announced the production in June 1935, with his wife Norma Shearer as Juliet and George Cukor as director. His stated ambition, preserved in the production literature, was "to make the production what Shakespeare would have wanted had he possessed the facilities of cinema", a sentence that deserves attention, because it defines the film's whole method: the cinema's facilities, its money, its research departments, its acres of scenery, were to be placed at the service of an imagined authorial approval.

### Why did Louis B. Mayer resist the film for five years?

Mayer resisted because he believed the mass audience considered Shakespeare beyond its reach and because the Depression ledger punished costly experiments. His production chief argued the opposite case in prestige terms, that a classic mounted with pageantry would raise the studio's public standing, and the project moved only when Warner Bros. announced a rival production and rivalry overcame caution.

The economics that followed exposed the gap between the announced intention and the executed one. The negative cost was first estimated at 800,000 dollars; by the end of a six-month shoot that wrapped in May 1936, it had passed two million, making the picture MGM's most expensive sound production to that point. The studio's own records, preserved in the Eddie Mannix ledger, give the worldwide earnings at 2,075,000 dollars and the final loss at 922,000 dollars, a figure some later accounts round to a million. The arithmetic matters for the argument of this article, because the loss was not the public's verdict on Shakespeare alone. It was the price of a method: of building Verona rather than finding it, of researching every paving stone, of dressing well over a thousand players, of shooting for half a year. A cheaper Cukor version is imaginable; a cheaper Thalberg version is not, because the expenditure was the message. The film was designed to be reported as much as watched, and the trade press of the spring of 1936 duly reported it, in features that counted the yards of cloth and the acres of scenery with the wonder usually reserved for engineering works.

Thalberg's choice of director followed the same logic of insured prestige. Cukor was then among the most literate directors in Hollywood, fresh from successes with literary adaptations, and trusted above all with actresses; he had never directed Shakespeare, a fact that troubled nobody at the studio, because the production's Shakespearean authority was to be supplied by its consultants and its text, while Cukor supplied taste, clarity, and the handling of performers. The assignment of the screenplay to Talbot Jennings, a writer of historical spectacles, completed the pattern. The play was to be arranged for the screen the way the studio arranged a prestige novel: respectfully, intelligently, and at whatever length the arrangement required. That Cukor later looked back on the film with open regret, wishing he had found its Italian warmth and escaped its gloss, is the testimony of the man inside the machine, and his evidence receives its own hearing later in this study. At the moment of production, though, no participant doubted the premise. The premise was that reverence was a method, and that method, given enough money, could not fail.

## Verona on the Backlot: Sets, Costumes, and the Professors

The physical film was built in Culver City, and the building of it remains the most documented act of archaeological devotion in studio history. A design unit travelled to Italy with cameras and returned with 2,769 photographs of Verona and its region; the photographs were converted into fifty-four complete scale models, and from the models grew the settings. The Cathedral Square set covered eight acres. The Capulet ballroom measured 250 by 400 feet, with the garden of the Capulets at about the same dimensions, and the balcony that Romeo reached by a rose trellis stood within a Verona assembled from the paintings of Botticelli, Bellini, Carpaccio, and Gozzoli as much as from the photographs, since the production's visual imagination was frankly pictorial: it sought the Italy of the early Renaissance masters, filtered through the MGM art department. Cedric Gibbons, the studio's supervising art director, carried the screen credit and the eventual Academy Award nomination, shared with Fredric Hope and Edwin B. Willis. Oliver Messel, the English stage designer, was brought across the Atlantic to supervise the design, and the friction between Messel's theatrical sensibility and the Gibbons and Adrian regime became one of the production's internal stories. Cukor, recalling the quarrel decades later, described a tug of war in which Thalberg sat like Solomon and never committed himself, and admitted that he had wanted to go with Messel and had not succeeded. The film's look, stately, symmetrical, and immaculate, is the visible record of which side prevailed.

The costume effort matched the architecture. Period reporting, notably a long account in the Literary Digest of April 1936 published while the film was still in production, counted 1,200 players costumed, 20,000 yards of cloth for the women's dresses against 18,000 for the men's, and nine yards of material in a single cloak for Romeo. Such figures were publicity, certainly, but they were publicity of a particular kind: they invited the public to admire the film before seeing it, as an achievement of research and manufacture. The Digest's summary of the studio's care deserves quotation, because it captures the production's own theory of itself. "The studio has been granitic in its care of Shakespeare's dialog", the magazine reported. "Eighteen versions of the play were studied before the Cambridge Edition version was chosen... There have been no additions; the cuts have been minute." The final claim was false, as the textual record examined below demonstrates: close to half the play is absent, songs were imported from two other plays, and whole sequences were invented. The claim is nevertheless evidence of the first importance, because it shows what the prestige method needed to believe about itself. Reverence required the fiction of an untouched text, and the fiction was maintained by the same publicity machine that counted the cloth.

### What did the academic advisers actually contribute to the production?

Two professors were flown to the set, John Tucker Murray of Harvard and William Strunk Jr. of Cornell, author of The Elements of Style, instructed to criticise the production freely. Their contribution was consultative, a check on detail and diction, but their presence carried the larger meaning: the studio had insured its Shakespeare with academic endorsement attached in advance.

The advisers' presence also deserves a plainer reading. Strunk, the great apostle of brevity and clarity in prose, made an apt patron for a film whose screenplay pursued intelligibility above all, and the production's defenders have linked his influence to the picture's lucid delivery of difficult verse. Yet the professors could certify only what they were shown: the text as arranged, the details as built. No consultant was asked the questions on which the film would eventually be judged, whether teenagers should be played by stars in their thirties and forties, whether a tragedy of haste should move at a processional pace, whether Verona should be a city or a museum. The research apparatus answered every question except the dramatic ones, and it answered them superbly. A production can be faulted for its Verona only by someone prepared to say that accuracy was the wrong goal; the sets were, by the standards the production set itself, irreproachable. They were also, and the two judgements are compatible, the scenery of a pageant rather than the streets of a feud, beautiful in the manner of the paintings they copied and static for the same reason. A fresco does not bleed, and the film's Verona rarely suggests a place where young men kill each other in the heat.

The wardrobe department under Adrian, MGM's chief costume designer and the most influential dresser of actresses in Hollywood, worked to the same brief as the architects. Adrian's standing rested on his gift for translating a star's persona into silhouette, and the gowns built for Shearer, high-waisted, broad-shouldered in the Renaissance manner, and photographed by Daniels to glow against the darker male costumes, turn Juliet into the picture's visual sovereign regardless of the scene's nominal focus. The men's clothing ran to the doublets and hose of the paintings, with Romeo's cloak, the nine-yard cloak of the publicity, cut as a romantic hero's banner. Splendid as the results are, they complete the production's quiet rearrangement of the tragedy's centre of gravity. In the play, the eye belongs to whoever is speaking; in this film it belongs to whoever is dressed, and the dressing is a hierarchy with the producer's wife at its apex. The costumes are, in the strictest sense, the casting made visible: rank rendered in cloth, worn by performers whose authority the audience was asked to accept in place of their characters' youth.

Herbert Stothart's music extended the same philosophy into the score. Rather than commission a wholly new musical argument, Stothart built his score around borrowed classics, drawing on Tchaikovsky's Fantasy Overture on the play and on the stately Pavane from Peter Warlock's Capriol Suite, neither source credited on screen. The choice was of a piece with the whole design: the film wrapped the tragedy in the certified masterpieces of adjacent arts, painting for the eye and concert music for the ear, so that a ticket-buyer was purchasing an anthology of European culture with the play at its centre. William H. Daniels, Garbo's cinematographer and the master of MGM's glamour lighting, photographed the result in a soft, lustrous black and white that flatters every face it touches. Nothing in the picture is ugly, raw, or hurried. Everything in it has been considered. The consideration is the achievement, and it is also the limitation that the rest of this study keeps meeting under different names.

## A Juliet of Thirty-Four and a Romeo of Forty-Three: The Casting Compromise

The text is unusually precise about the heroine's age. Capulet tells Paris that his child "is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2), and the Nurse fixes the date with peasant exactness: "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3). Romeo's age goes unstated, but the source poem describes a youth on whose "tender chin, as yet, no manlike beard there grew", and the whole machinery of the tragedy, the guardianship, the arranged marriage, the banishment endured as a child endures it, presumes adolescents in the keeping of adults. Against this the film set Norma Shearer and Leslie Howard. Howard, born in April 1893, was forty-three when the picture opened. Shearer's age requires a sentence of its own, because the record divides: her birth certificate and baptismal record give 11 August 1902, which made her thirty-four in the month of the premiere, while reference works of an older vintage print 1900, which would make her thirty-six, and studio publicity of her reign preferred arithmetic younger than either. The dispute is itself evidence, since no other casting in the film attracted it. On any arithmetic the fact stands: a woman in her mid-thirties and a man of forty-three played the two most famous teenagers in literature, and the production knew it, and proceeded anyway, because the alternative contradicted the purpose of the enterprise.

That purpose explains the decision better than any failure of perception. A prestige production of this scale could not be entrusted to unknown adolescents, because its economics and its meaning both depended on star value. Shearer was not merely the studio's leading lady; she was the producer's wife, the first lady of MGM, an Academy Award winner whose name on a marquee guaranteed the respectable audience Thalberg courted. Howard was the screen's established embodiment of sensitive, literate masculinity, an English gentleman whose Romeo carried associations of refinement that the production prized. The casting was therefore not an accident of vanity, though vanity had its share, but the logical outcome of the prestige method applied to personnel: just as the sets had to be the most accurate and the advisers the most eminent, the lovers had to be the most distinguished. The series thesis of this screen cluster holds that the cliché imagines young lovers and that Cukor's mature stars restore the studio era's compromise, and the compromise can be stated exactly. The studio era could sell youth or it could sell rank. It could not afford to sell both in the same picture, and it chose rank.

### Why did MGM entrust the lovers to its two most adult stars?

The production's economics and meaning both depended on rank. A two-million-dollar prestige picture required names that guaranteed the respectable audience, and Shearer was the producer's wife and the studio's first lady, while Howard embodied the literate gentlemanliness the enterprise prized. Unknown adolescents would have served the play's ages and defeated the film's purpose, which was institutional proof rather than verisimilitude.

The performances themselves were prepared with a diligence that mirrors the sets. Shearer had no stage training and had never performed a full Shakespeare play; her entire prior experience of the dramatist consisted of the balcony scene played opposite John Gilbert in The Hollywood Revue of 1929, a variety film's novelty turn. For the film she worked intensively with coaches brought from the East Coast stage, Constance Collier prominent among them in the accounts, labouring to make the long speeches sound natural in her own mouth, and drama coaches were engaged across a company in which many screen actors, accustomed to naturalism, found themselves giving more stage-like performances under the production's influence. The result, judged fairly, is a Juliet of intelligence, musical phrasing, and genuine feeling in the potion scene, which Cukor staged for her as one long uninterrupted take running from her mother's exit through the soliloquy to the drinking of the vial, trusting her stamina across the tragedy's most demanding solo. What no coaching could supply was the one property the role is built on: the visible fact of being thirteen, with a thirteen-year-old's absolutism, ignorance, and speed. Shearer's Juliet deliberates where the text's Juliet leaps. Howard's Romeo, similarly, is a study in wistful maturity, a performance of great charm and melodic verse-speaking that continually suggests a man remembering first love rather than undergoing it. The balcony scene between them is beautifully spoken and strangely unhurried, a duet for two accomplished recitalists, and the film never recovers from the calm at its centre.

Howard's case deserves its separate sentence, because his Romeo has suffered most from the controversy's shorthand. He was not a matinée idol miscast by a star system that could not tell age from glamour; he was one of the most intelligent actors in films, a stage-trained English performer whose screen persona, the sensitive, literate gentleman of Berkeley Square and The Petrified Forest, had been built on exactly the qualities of inwardness and verbal finesse that Romeo's part seems to invite. The casting was, on paper, an act of criticism, an argument that Romeo is a poet before he is a boy. The performance honours the argument wherever the verse is reflective and fails it wherever the verse is appetite: Howard's Romeo woos, laments, and philosophises beautifully, and never once seems likely to kill a man in the street or scale a wall at midnight, and the tragedy's plot depends on precisely those likelihoods. His early death in 1943, when the civil airliner carrying him was shot down over the Bay of Biscay, fixed the performance in amber as part of a martyred actor's legend, and later audiences have often watched the film as his memorial. The memorial glow flatters the portrait and distorts the evidence, since the Romeo of 1936 was already, in its own moment, an anachronism beautifully sustained.

The supporting cast repeats the pattern at every level, and one member of it concentrates the whole irony. Basil Rathbone, who had played Romeo opposite Katharine Cornell in the 1934 Broadway revival that helped inspire the film, was cast as Tybalt, and Rathbone, born in June 1892, was forty-four, a year older than the film's Romeo, so that Verona's firebrand youth was the senior of its lovesick one. The transfer was dictated by star logic, since Tybalt offered the showier character part, and Rathbone seized it, earning the film's only acting nomination from the Academy with a performance that later scholarship, Stephen Orgel's account in particular, singles out for its unexpected register: where the stage tradition makes Tybalt a hot-headed troublemaker, Rathbone plays him as stuffy and pompous, a fencing-master of etiquette whose menace is social before it is physical. The reading is intelligent and it fits the film's world, in which even violence has been to finishing school. John Barrymore, at fifty-four the company's most experienced Shakespearean and a generation earlier the American stage's defining Hamlet, played Mercutio, and Edna May Oliver, at fifty-two, played the Nurse with a spinsterish precision that turned the part's earthiness into dry comedy. C. Aubrey Smith, seventy-three, presided as Capulet. A spectator adding the ages finds a Verona governed, romanced, and fought over by performers whose combined experience was immense and whose median age belonged to the tragedy's parents. The film did not hide this. It could not. It asked the audience to accept maturity as the price of mastery, and the bargain it offered, verse beautifully spoken by masters in place of youth perilously lived by novices, is the bargain every later version refused.

## The Stage Had Always Cast Adults: Why the Camera Changed the Contract

The ages that scandalised filmgoers would have raised no eyebrow in the theatre, and the difference is the beginning of wisdom about this film. The stage tradition from which the production descended had never cast teenagers as the lovers. Katharine Cornell, whose 1934 Broadway Juliet was the reigning stage interpretation when Thalberg announced the film and whose success helped persuade MGM to proceed, was forty-one years old. Her Romeo in that production, Rathbone, was forty-two, and her Tybalt, in the same staging, was the nineteen-year-old Orson Welles, a distribution of ages that the theatre found entirely natural: maturity in the lyric leads, whose verse the audience had paid to hear perfectly spoken, and youth in the fighting part, where energy outweighed experience. The convention rested on the physics of the auditorium. From the twentieth row, a decade is invisible; what carries is voice, carriage, and authority, and on those measures the adult Juliet defeats the adolescent one almost every night of the year. The theatre's contract with its audience was understood by both parties: the programme said Juliet, the stage showed a tragedienne, and the imagination split the difference.

The cinema abolished that contract without announcing the change. The close-up is a witness that cannot be flattered by distance, and MGM's own publicity machine, which sold the film on the faces of its stars in posters and lobby cards, invited the public to inspect exactly what the stage would have kept at a merciful remove. Daniels's glamour photography worked to restore the mercy, bathing Shearer in the soft light perfected on Garbo, and within any single frame the illusion often holds. Across two hours it cannot, because film is cumulative in a way theatre is not: the camera returns to the face in scene after scene, in the garden, at the window, in the tomb, and each return files another report. The stage Juliet is judged on the evening; the screen Juliet is judged on the evidence. This is why the casting question, which the production treated as a problem of prestige, was in fact a problem of medium, and why the film's defenders and detractors have talked past each other for decades. The defenders describe a stage performance of high finish that happens to have been photographed. The detractors describe a film that fails the elementary test of photographic belief. Both are right, and the film is the monument of their disagreement.

The point can be put as a general law of adaptation, and the successors learned it in sequence. A medium that shows must cast what it shows; a medium that tells may cast what it admires. Castellani, inheriting the problem in 1954, kept the adult casting and softened it with location light and Italian faces, a half-measure that produced a beautiful film with the same fault in milder form. Zeffirelli abolished the fault by abolishing the insurance, casting unknowns of the text's own ages and staking the production on their inexperience, and the gamble's success reordered the expectations of every producer who followed. Luhrmann, two generations on, cast young stars and treated the camera as an accomplice rather than a witness. Each settlement is unintelligible without the first one, the Culver City settlement, in which the most powerful studio in the world examined the problem, understood the stage's answer, and applied it to a medium where it could not work. The failure was not stupidity. It was fidelity to a vanishing order, executed with unlimited resources, and it is precisely the unlimited resources that make the failure permanent evidence rather than a footnote.

## The First Sound of the Play: What the Talkies Inherited From the Silents

The silent cinema had filmed the tragedy for three and a half decades before Cukor, in versions ranging from single-reel condensations to full features, and it had solved the story in the only way available to it: as a sequence of famous pictures. The balcony, the ball, the duel, the tomb formed a strip of tableaux that audiences who knew the plot could follow without a word, and the acting style of those films, broad, gestural, and emblematic, was the style of the pictures themselves. Sound abolished the rationale of that cinema at a stroke and created a problem nobody had budgeted for. A talking Romeo and Juliet had to decide what the talking was for. It could treat the verse as the main event and the camera as its servant, or it could treat the story as the event and the verse as an obstacle to be thinned. Every sound version since has been a negotiation between those positions, and Cukor's film, as the first feature-length negotiation, set the terms so firmly toward the verse that its successors have been reacting against the settlement ever since.

The decision looks inevitable in retrospect and was nothing of the kind. The early talkies were widely suspected of theatricality, photographed plays being the era's standing insult, and a studio seeking prestige might rationally have hidden the play inside spectacle, cutting the text to a thread and letting the built Verona carry the picture. Warner Bros. had taken something closer to that path with its Reinhardt Midsummer, a director's fantasia in which Mendelssohn, fairies, and studio trickery shared the billing with the text. MGM chose the opposite wager. It advertised the dialogue itself as the attraction, counted the eighteen editions consulted as a selling point, and delivered a film in which the camera repeatedly stills itself to let speeches happen. The wager expressed Thalberg's theory of prestige exactly, since prestige attaches to what is difficult, and the dialogue was the most difficult element in the property. It also expressed a shrewd commercial instinct about novelty, for in 1936 the sound of Shakespeare at feature length was itself a sensation, a demonstration of what the talking picture had matured into since the first crude dialogue films of the late nineteen-twenties. Audiences were being sold a technological coming-of-age with a classic as its certificate.

What the talkies inherited from the silents, all the same, is visible in the film's bones. The tableau structure survives intact beneath the dialogue: the procession, the ball, the balcony, the funeral, the tomb succeed one another as a strip of famous pictures, each composed for admiration, and the interpolated pageantry that expands the screenplay belongs to the silent cinema's instinct that a classic must be illustrated to be sold. The performances inherit the same doubleness, since the principals play in the rhetorical manner the silent screen had preserved from the older stage while the camera records them in the intimate manner of the new one, and the friction between the manners is the friction Barrymore's Mercutio makes audible. The film is therefore a palimpsest of the play's whole screen history to its date: silent illustration, stage rhetoric, and studio sound laid one over another, each layer legible. Students of the play on film begin here not because the picture solved the problems but because it was the first to pose them all at once, at full scale, with the industry's best resources, and its failures are correspondingly instructive. A cheap film's failures teach little. This film's failures are the failures of an entire system thinking as hard as it could, and they repay study in proportion.

## What Prestige Bought: The Film as the Studio's Self-Portrait

Prestige, in the Hollywood of the nineteen-thirties, was a genre with conventions as strict as the western's, and the film observes every one of them. The source must be unimpeachable, a classic whose filming is itself an event. The research must be publicised as thoroughly as the stars, so that the audience arrives pre-instructed in the production's seriousness. The cast must insure the investment with names whose own prestige compounds the property's. The running time must announce importance by exceeding the ordinary programme's span. The release must be ceremonial, a Broadway theatre opening in New York and a gala on the West Coast rather than a mere availability in cinemas. Cukor's film satisfies the template in every particular, and understanding it as a prestige picture, rather than as a failed attempt at something else, explains features that otherwise look like errors. The processional pace is not a miscalculation of the tragedy; it is the genre's tempo, the speed at which importance moves. The museum Verona is not a failure to find the city; it is the genre's scenery, built to be admired in the trade papers before it was inhabited on the screen. Even the casting belongs to the template, since a prestige picture does not discover stars but deploys them.

Read this way, the film is MGM's self-portrait at the zenith of the studio system, and its subject is the system itself: centralised, capital-rich, confident that any cultural object could be manufactured to specification given sufficient research, money, and time. Thalberg's career was the proof the industry offered itself that taste could be industrialised, that a producer of genius could run quality the way other men ran volume. The Romeo and Juliet he built is the argument's fullest expression and its refutation in a single object, because the qualities the tragedy most needs, danger, haste, and youth, are precisely the qualities that cannot be manufactured to specification. They can only be risked. The production risked nothing that its systems could insure, and insured everything that its systems could reach, and the result is a film of genuine magnificence with the risk removed. Later Hollywood would learn to manufacture risk as a style. In 1936 it had not yet learned, and the honesty of the film's failure, its refusal to counterfeit the qualities it could not supply, is part of its documentary worth.

The industry context sharpens the portrait. Warner Bros. had fired first with the Reinhardt Midsummer, a prestige Shakespeare imported from the European stage, and MGM's answer asserted the home-grown alternative: no imported theatrical genius, but the studio's own departments, its own stars, its own professors, its own acres. The rivalry was about the ownership of culture, and MGM won it in the only currency the genre recognises, since Cukor's picture outspent, out-researched, and out-nominated the Burbank entry while sharing its commercial fate. Both films lost money; both convinced the industry that Shakespeare was the problem. The conclusion, examined coldly, misreads the evidence in a way this article exists to correct. Two prestige pictures failed in successive years not because audiences rejected the playwright but because the genre's economics required blockbuster returns on museum methods. A modestly budgeted Shakespeare, cast young and shot fast, was never tried by a major studio in the period, because the genre forbade modesty. The playwright was blamed for the template, and the template's authors collected four Academy nominations and moved on.

## What the Film Keeps and What It Cuts: Jennings's Forty-Five Percent

The publicity promised an untouched text, and the screenplay tells a more interesting story. Talbot Jennings retained roughly forty-five percent of the play, a little less than half, shaping the remainder into a running time of about two hours, a span with its own irony, since the Prologue advertises "the two hours' traffic of our stage" and the film, at 125 minutes, overshoots even that generous Elizabethan estimate. The Prologue itself the film keeps, and keeps entire, in the choral frame that announces "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue). The opening brawl is then staged against the backdrop of a religious procession, the first of many expansions in which the film spends its spectacle budget on ceremony. What was surrendered follows the familiar logic of the stage tradition, the second Chorus gone, the comic scene of Peter with the musicians gone, the whole texture thinned toward the lovers and the feud's principals. The screenplay's distinctiveness lies not in the cuts but in the company they keep, because Jennings also added. Three sequences were built around Friar John, the messenger whose failure the play reports in a handful of lines, so that the machinery of the missed letter is shown at a length Shakespeare never gave it. Rosaline, whom the play keeps rigorously unseen, appears in the ball sequence to rebuff Romeo in person. Friar Laurence, by contrast, is much reduced, and Peter the servant is much enlarged, with Andy Devine given lines Shakespeare assigned to other Capulet servants and made the instigator of the opening brawl. Two songs were imported from other plays entirely, "Come Away, Death" from Twelfth Night and "Honour, Riches, Marriage, Blessing" from The Tempest, so that the soundtrack of the most reverent Shakespeare film ever made contains material its author wrote for other works.

### Which parts of the play did the film surrender most readily?

The cuts fell where stage tradition had cut, on the second Chorus, on the comedy of Peter with the musicians, and on Friar Laurence, whose role shrinks in the screenplay. Verse that served ceremony, music, and procession survived at length while argumentative verse thinned, a pattern that shows the screenplay protecting spectacle and pathos ahead of the play's reasoning.

The cutting pattern deserves its critical name. A screenplay that reduces the Friar reduces the play's one adult intelligence, the character who states the tragedy's philosophy in advance: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder" (2.6). A screenplay that expands Friar John's journey and Rosaline's rejection spends its additions on plot mechanics and romantic prelude. The exchange is characteristic of the adaptation's mind throughout: argument out, illustration in. Yet the indictment requires its qualifying clause, and the scholarship supplies it. Patricia Tatspaugh, in her chapter on the tragedy of love on film in The Cambridge Companion to Shakespeare on Film, observes that Jennings retained more of Shakespeare's poetry for the young lovers than any of his big-screen successors managed. The statistic reframes the familiar charge. Later and livelier versions cut the lovers' verse hardest, because verse slows the very scenes their directors wanted to accelerate; Cukor's version, whatever it did to the Friar and the Chorus, protected the arias. Its lovers speak more of the play than Zeffirelli's or Luhrmann's lovers do, and they speak it in long, lucid, beautifully engineered takes, introduced by a presentational device the critic David Sterritt has singled out, in which the principal figures are ushered in at the start with their names, their players' names, and a brief account of their place in the story, as if the film were a well-run museum in which every exhibit carries a legible label. Clarity was the production's house virtue, and in the verse it achieved something real. No spectator leaves this film unsure of what any line means. The question the critics have fought over is what the lines are doing while being so clearly understood, since understanding, in this tragedy, is supposed to arrive too late and at the speed of catastrophe.

Juliet's potion scene shows the virtue and the limit in a single sequence. Cukor held the camera on Shearer through the soliloquy in one unbroken take, and the speech's panic is in the text for any performer to find: "Farewell! God knows when we shall meet again. / I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins / That almost freezes up the heat of life" (4.3). Shearer finds much of it. She does not find, because the film around her has not built it, the terror of a child alone in a house of enemies; the scene reads as a great actress's set piece, applauded as such in 1936 and admired as such since, and its very completeness as a performance works against the fiction. The tragedy's Juliet is not completing anything. She is improvising a desperate gamble at an age when the film's Juliet has been a star for seventeen years. The distinction is not between good and bad acting but between acting and evidence, and it recurs so often in this picture that it becomes the picture's defining tension: everything is performed superbly, and the performances keep testifying against the story they serve.

## The Company Around the Lovers: A Complete Society in Marble

A prestige production casts downward as carefully as upward, and the supporting company of this film repays the attention the stars monopolise, because it explains the picture's peculiar social completeness. Verona in the play is a working city, of servants, watchmen, musicians, apothecaries, and citizens who turn out to protest the brawls, and the film populates its acres with the MGM stock company at full strength, every stratum present and correctly dressed. The effect is of a society entire, and like everything else in the picture it cuts both ways: the crowd scenes have a density no stage could match, and the density is arranged, so that even Verona's disorder arrives in formation. The opening brawl, staged against a religious procession, states the method in its first minutes. The play's street fight is a squalid eruption of servant violence that shames the great houses; the film's is a civic spectacle in which hundreds of costumed citizens move through a cathedral square, and the shame is drowned in the pageantry. A spectator admires the sequence and forgets to deplore it, which is the film's whole relationship to the feud in one image.

At the head of the older generation stands C. Aubrey Smith's Capulet, played at seventy-three with the granite bonhomie that made Smith the screen's embodiment of imperial English authority, an odd and revealing choice for a Veronese patriarch, since his Capulet governs the ball and rages at his daughter with the manner of a club chairman restoring order. Opposite him, Violet Kemble-Cooper, a genuine ornament of the London and New York stage, gives Lady Capulet a chill elegance that the film uses sparingly, and Robert Warwick's Montague and Virginia Hammond's Lady Montague complete the parental frame at the far side of the feud. The casting of these parts from the front rank of stage-trained character actors reinforced the production's central proposition, that Shakespeare was to be played by the profession's aristocracy at every level, and it gives the film's Verona an authority of ensemble that the lovers' scenes, for all their finish, do not equal. Where the principals seem conscious of the monument they inhabit, the character actors simply live in theirs, and several of the picture's most convincing minutes belong to them.

Two performances define the ensemble's limits from opposite directions. Henry Kolker's Friar Laurence suffers from the screenplay rather than from any failure of his own, since the role as Jennings arranged it has lost the long philosophical speeches that make the Friar the play's adult intelligence, and Kolker is left a benign functionary of the plot, blessing and potioning on schedule. The reduction matters beyond one performance, because a tragedy whose counsellor has been trimmed to a mechanism loses its argument about adult responsibility, and the film's Verona comes to seem a place where events happen to the young through nobody's reasoning at all. At the other extreme of enlargement stands Andy Devine's Peter, the Capulet servant rebuilt by the screenplay into the brawl's instigator and a recurring comic presence, played in Devine's unmistakable wheeze. The expansion is pure studio practice, a contracted comedian given material worthy of his salary, and it works on its own terms while quietly reversing the play's social geometry, in which the servants' violence is the feud's shame rather than its entertainment. Between Kolker's diminished sage and Devine's amplified clown, the ensemble tells the story of the adaptation in small: the argument reduced, the diversion enlarged, the society complete and gleaming, and the tragedy proceeding through it like a procession through a city that has been tidied for the occasion.

Completeness of costume, though, is not completeness of voice, and the film's Verona is edited as selectively as its text. The citizens who in the play turn out to deplore the brawls and demand the Prince's justice are present as crowds rather than as speakers, a thousand extras arranged into picturesque indignation. The musicians whose comedy follows the supposed corpse, the play's bleak joke about art's indifference to grief, are cut entirely, removing the one moment at which Verona's ordinary working life comments on the catastrophe in its own idiom. The watch, the apothecary, the servants beyond Peter survive as functions within the pageant rather than as the city's counter-voices. The result is a society that can be seen entire and heard only in its upper register, a Verona of masters, priests, and principals, with the commons present as scenery. The play's city argues with its rulers in prose; the film's city applauds them in formation. No single cut imposes that difference, but the pattern of cuts and expansions, traced through the ensemble, imposes it completely, and it is the social form of the same choice that governed the casting and the cutting of the verse.

## Barrymore's Mercutio and the Performance That Escapes the Marble

Around the marble centre, one performance moves at a different temperature. John Barrymore was fifty-four, a ruin of the most famous American classical actor of his generation, his powers damaged by drink and his discipline unreliable, and Thalberg cast him as Mercutio partly from piety, since Barrymore's name certified the production's theatrical lineage, and partly, the record suggests, because the part's mixture of brilliance and dissolution had become a portrait the actor could fill without acting. The result divides the film's critics as nothing else in it does. Stephen Orgel calls him "an elderly John Barrymore as a stagey Mercutio decades out of date", and the charge is precise: the performance belongs to an older theatre, rhetorical, self-regarding, played at the audience, with the Queen Mab speech delivered as a bravura concert turn rather than as the dangerous improvisation of a young wit. The defence answers that the stage manner is the life of the film, that Barrymore's Mercutio, flirtatious, teasing, and visibly delighted by his own invention, is the single figure in Verona who seems to be enjoying the language rather than curating it, and that his scenes with Howard crackle with a complicity the lovers' scenes never attempt. Both accounts describe the same footage. The disagreement is over whether a Shakespeare film should preserve the theatrical past or abolish it, and Barrymore's Mercutio is the preserved past walking through the picture, gorgeous and anachronistic at once.

The character's own words define what the performance had to carry. Mercutio talks of dreams as "the children of an idle brain, / Begot of nothing but vain fantasy" (1.4), and the play builds him as the tragedy's one sceptical intelligence, the friend whose wit keeps puncturing the lovers' lyricism until the feud punctures him. In a young company he is the cleverest of the boys. In Cukor's company he is the elder statesman of cleverness, and the relationships silently re-form around the fact: his teasing of Romeo reads as an uncle's raillery, his death less as the waste of a contemporary than as the fall of a institution. The duel that kills him is, by the fencing record, a rarity, one of the very few screen fights Basil Rathbone won, and the production stages the street fighting with a choreographed elegance that keeps faith with the film's whole aesthetic, violence as ballet. The ballet of the ball, choreographed by Agnes de Mille, had already established the idiom, building the Capulet dance into a formal court masque in which the lovers' first exchange of a sonnet becomes a ceremony performed inside a larger ceremony. The idiom is coherent and beautiful. It is also a decision about the tragedy, since a Verona where even the brawls are choreography has decided in advance how much disorder it will permit, and the play's Verona permits a great deal.

### Why does Barrymore's Mercutio still divide the film's critics?

The performance preserves a stage rhetoric inside a medium learning naturalism, so the footage reads as embalmed to one school and as its uncurated life to another. Orgel's school hears a turn decades out of date; the defence hears wit enjoying itself while lovers curate their lines, and the division concerns what a Shakespeare film should do with the past.

The Nurse makes the complementary case in a lower key. Edna May Oliver plays her dry, prim, and precise, a performance of expert comic timing that trims the character's bawdy expansiveness to fit the film's taste and the era's censorship, since the Production Code then governing Hollywood required the tragedy's sexuality to be handled by implication, its wedding night rendered as a decorous morning. Oliver's Nurse is funny the way the whole film is funny, in inverted commas, and the performance has worn well as character acting while ceasing to be the Nurse of the text, the woman whose garrulous affection is one of the play's great engines of both comedy and doom. Between Oliver's restraint and Barrymore's excess, the supporting company maps the film's two available modes: taste and theatricality, each admirable, neither dangerous. The lovers, suspended between the modes, achieve a third: beauty. It is not nothing. In 1936 it was very nearly everything the industry knew how to want.

## The Lovers' Scenes Under the Camera: Garden, Ball, Balcony, and Tomb

The production introduces its lovers through opposed emblems, an arrangement the scholarship on the film has documented with care. Romeo appears first in an arcadian landscape, leaning against a ruined building while a shepherd pipes nearby and a dog attends him, a figure composed out of pastoral melancholy before he has spoken a word of consequence. The entrance illustrates the opening scenes' account of a youth in love with love, sighing through oxymorons, "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O any thing, of nothing first create" (1.1), and Howard's performance settles at once into the register the emblem predicts: refined, musical, and contemplative, a Romeo whose passion reads as a condition of sensibility rather than an appetite. Juliet is placed in the contrary element, the formal garden of the Capulets with its decorative fish pond, associated from her first frames with water, ornament, and the ordered beauty of a great house. The antithesis is intelligent and entirely directorial. It is also a warning. A Romeo composed as a pastoral emblem and a Juliet arranged as a garden ornament have been fixed as pictures before they have met as persons, and the film's love story unfolds as the gradual animation of two portraits, exquisite and stately, rather than as the collision the play stages.

The ball sequence is the film's set piece and its aesthetic confession. In the 250-foot ballroom, Agnes de Mille's choreography builds the Capulet dance into a formal court masque, rank upon rank of costumed figures moving in patterns that quote the Renaissance paintings the designers had studied, and the lovers' meeting takes place inside this machinery of ceremony. Shakespeare's scene hazards everything on speed and proximity: a crowded room, a shared sonnet improvised between strangers, a kiss stolen in public. The film renders the same beats as a pageant in which the couple's intimacy must be excavated from the spectacle by the camera, in glances and close framings, while the masque processes around them. Romeo's first sight of Juliet keeps the text's astonishment, "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! / It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night / As a rich jewel in an Ethiop's ear" (1.5), and the verse lands, because Howard speaks it as if discovering the words. What the sequence cannot reproduce is the danger of the room, Tybalt's recognition, Capulet's restraint, the sense of a feud suspended by hospitality for one evening only, since a Verona this ceremonious has no unmanaged spaces in which danger could gather. The masque is the film in miniature: magnificent, scholarly, and cooled.

The balcony scene, the property on which the whole enterprise was sold, shows the method at its most accomplished and its most exposed. Romeo climbs through the garden by a rose trellis to a Juliet framed in architecture copied from the photographs, and the exchange proceeds in Daniels's softest light, the verse beautifully distributed between the two voices. "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2). The lines are allowed their full music, unhurried by cutting, and Shearer's answering meditation on names, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2), is delivered with a thoughtful clarity that any stage would envy. Yet the scene's dramatic content is a negotiation conducted at mortal risk by two children who have known each other for hours, and the film's serenity keeps dissolving the risk. Nothing in the framing suggests that discovery means death, though the text says so repeatedly; the garden is a set, patrolled by nobody, lit like a valentine. Later versions would stage the scene as trespass, with walls scaled and households waking. Cukor stages it as a recital in a beautiful place, and the difference is the difference between the two conceptions of the film entire.

The later movements repeat the pattern with diminishing returns. The wedding is expanded into ceremony, the parting at dawn compressed into decorum by the Production Code's requirements, with Juliet's "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5) surviving as tenderness rather than as the aubade's argument with death, and the funeral is expanded again into procession, the film reaching for its spectacle budget whenever the tragedy offers it a rite. The Mantua material shows the method working on the plot's machinery rather than its poetry. Shakespeare dismisses the exile in two strokes, the news and the purchase, trusting the audience to supply the journey; the film, true to its instinct for illustration, builds the machinery out in full, following Friar John's frustrated errand through the interpolated sequences and returning Romeo to the pastoral register of his first appearance, a melancholy exile among ruins and shepherds, so that the catastrophe assembles itself on screen with every gear visible. The visibility is the point and the problem. A tragedy that depends on the audience's dawning horror at a mechanism it half-noticed cannot survive having the mechanism demonstrated in advance, gear by gear, however beautifully the demonstration is photographed. The tomb sequence gathers the whole method into a single test. Romeo's death and Juliet's waking are played at full emotional value by both performers, and Juliet's final lines, "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3), are spoken with a simplicity that cuts through the surrounding marble. For a few minutes the film achieves the pathos its publicity promised. The pathos arrives, though, as the climax of a pageant about two mature artists rather than as the destruction of two children, and the distinction governs what a spectator can feel. Admiration is the emotion this film manufactures best, and admiration, at a tomb, is a form of distance. The scene readings converge on the verdict the critics reached by other roads: the lovers' scenes are the finest flowers of the prestige method and the clearest demonstration of its limits, since everything in them is achieved except the one thing the scenes are for.

## Sound, Speech, and the Verse: How the Film Taught Hollywood to Speak Shakespeare

The picture arrived in the first decade of talking cinema, when the industry's memory of learning to record dialogue at all was fresh, and its handling of verse deserves recognition as a technical settlement with long consequences. The early fear, voiced whenever a studio classic was proposed, was that mass audiences would not follow heightened speech at feature length. Cukor's production answered the fear with a doctrine of clarity. The dialogue is delivered at a measured pace, plainly articulated, and directed toward intelligibility before brilliance, with the principal figures introduced at the outset by name, player, and function so that no spectator need spend the first act solving the dramatis personae. The doctrine had an institutional patron in Strunk, whose Elements of Style had made a national gospel of lucidity, and whatever the professor's direct interventions amounted to, the film behaves as his book advises: it omits needless obscurity, favours the concrete, and trusts the sentence to carry its meaning without strain. The result refuted the fear in the form the industry best understands, since the quality press singled out exactly this clarity for praise, and Nugent's review is best read as a certificate that Shakespeare could be understood in a cinema.

The doctrine's costs are the other half of the settlement. Verse spoken always for sense tends to lose its weather, the speed, overlap, and recklessness with which the play's young people think aloud, and the film's delivery style, coached and concerted across a company of screen actors unused to speaking in verse at all, settles into a uniform elevation that flattens the text's social range. The play's language is a map of Verona, prose for servants and clowns, rhyme for ceremony, blank verse swelling and breaking as passion dictates, and the production's house style irons the map into a single well-lit surface. Mercutio's prose, the Nurse's garrulity, the servants' bickering all emerge in the same polished register as the lovers' sonnets. What the film gains is dignity of a very high order; what it forfeits is the tragedy's acoustics, in which the audience is meant to hear worlds colliding. Later directors would treat the verse as behaviour, spoken at conversational speed by actors cast for their faces, and would be accused in their turn of sacrificing poetry to pace. The accusation and the counter-accusation both begin here, in the first sound version's decision that the verse must above all be understood. The decision was honourable, influential, and wrong in the way the film is generally wrong: it solved the problem the institution feared and declined the problem the play posed.

The editing extends the doctrine into the film's rhythm. Margaret Booth, MGM's supervising editor and one of the most powerful craftsmen in the studio, presided over a cutting style that favours continuity and ceremony: speeches are held in long takes that respect the verse paragraph, processions are assembled in measured alternation, and the joins between scenes carry the smoothness of a well-run state occasion. The rhythm is the audible form of the prestige method, since haste in the cutting room would have implied haste in the conception, and the conception was dignity. Yet the tragedy's own rhythm is a study in acceleration, comedy curdling into catastrophe at gathering speed, and a film that cuts processionally from first reel to last has declined the play's tempo as firmly as it declined its casting ages. The famous deliberation of the picture, the quality Variety translated as a long sit, is not a failure of energy in any department. It is the sum of a hundred correct decisions, each defensible, each prestigious, each a small refusal of the play's demand for speed.

The exception that proves the doctrine's value is the lovers' verse itself, which the screenplay protected beyond the standard of any successor. Tatspaugh's finding, that Jennings retained more of the poetry for Romeo and Juliet than later and more celebrated versions allowed them, means that the film's central performances carry an unusual density of the text, and Howard and Shearer, whatever their ages, deliver that density with a musicianly care that younger and more photogenic successors rarely matched. A listener who wants the sound of the balcony scene entire, the morning parting entire, the potion speech entire, in voices trained to the studio era's standard of diction, has exactly one sound-era monument to consult, and it is this one. The film's speech is therefore both its anachronism and its archive. It preserves a way of speaking Shakespeare that the stage itself was abandoning, the grand manner at the moment of its perfection, and preserves it in the mouths of the last company assembled to practise it on an imperial budget. Archives are not required to be alive. They are required to be faithful, and by that standard the picture's verse, like its Verona, is beyond reproach and beyond imitation.

## Reverence Against Life: The Critics Then and Since

The received history of the film's reception is that the critics of 1936 recoiled from its arty solemnity and the public stayed away, and the second half of that sentence is true while the first half is largely a later construction. The New York Times greeted the picture with a rave. Frank S. Nugent praised a production "ornate but not garish, extravagant but in perfect taste, expansive but never overwhelming", reflecting "great credit upon its producers and upon the screen as a whole", and concluded that it was "a dignified, sensitive and entirely admirable Shakespearean, not Hollywoodean, production". The Film Daily called it a "superb and important achievement" and "one of the most important contributions to the screen since the inception of talking pictures". Variety, the trade paper whose judgements were commercial before they were aesthetic, admired the faithfulness and the "very beautiful" costuming while entering the two reservations that mattered, that the film was "not too imaginative" and that at over two hours it was "a long sit". John Mosher in The New Yorker granted "a very definite achievement" while finding the whole "somewhat cumbersome", a good and sensible presentation that stopped short of great art. Read together, the notices describe a success of a specific kind: the industry and its quality press congratulating the medium on its manners. The reservations were about pace and imagination, not about competence, and they came wrapped in respect.

The one major dissent of 1936 came from Graham Greene, and it was aimed past the film at the entire enterprise. Greene wrote that he was "less than ever convinced that there is an aesthetic justification for filming Shakespeare at all" and that "the effect of even the best scenes is to distract". The argument deserves its longevity, because it identifies the fault line the film could not cross. Greene's objection was not that Cukor had filmed the play badly but that the camera's wealth of incident, the built Verona, the processions, the thousand properties, competes with the verse for the spectator's attention and wins, so that the poetry becomes an accompaniment to its own illustration. On the lovers specifically, the dissent of the period fixed on the ages with a bluntness the trade papers avoided, and the complaint hardened into the film's permanent nickname in the reference books: a beautiful production with grandparents in the tomb scene. That the complaint coexisted with Nugent's enthusiasm is the point. The film divided its first critics along the exact line it still divides them: whether reverence toward a classic is a form of respect or a form of avoidance.

### Did the critics of 1936 reject the film, as later accounts claim?

The record says otherwise. The Times raved, the Film Daily reached for superlatives, Variety praised the faithfulness and the costumes while doubting the pace, and the New Yorker granted a definite if cumbersome achievement. Greene's dissent was the exception, aimed at filming Shakespeare in principle. The cold-reception story is a later simplification, repeated even in filmographies, that the notices refute.

The later scholarship divided along the same line with better evidence. The prosecution's most quoted sentence belongs to Stephen Orgel, who describes the picture as "largely miscast", with "a preposterously mature pair of lovers in Leslie Howard and Norma Shearer" at its centre. Jack Jorgens, whose Shakespeare on Film set the terms for a generation of screen study, held the play itself to be "an exuberant celebration of young love, its excitement, passion, and lyricism", a standard by which a stately film built on middle-aged stars condemns itself out of its own programme notes. The defence, when it came, came from the film's historians rather than its aesthetes. Kenneth S. Rothwell, the dean of Shakespeare-on-screen bibliography, concluded his filmography's account by ruling that the picture "should be cherished as a masterwork from antiquity: a bit archaic, a little rigid, slightly overdone, but, yes, still withal warm and good". The sentence is a verdict in the form of an embrace, and it relocates the film's value from the dramatic to the documentary: the picture matters as the fullest surviving record of how the classical studio system imagined cultural legitimacy. Tatspaugh's observations cut across both camps, noting that Jennings kept more of the lovers' poetry than any successor and that the successors, learning the lesson of 1936, cast "younger, less experienced but more photogenic actors" in the central roles. David Sterritt, writing for Turner Classic Movies, adds the director's defence, praising Cukor's down-to-earth clarity and his refusal of purist ruts. The adjudication this study reaches is that both schools are correct about different objects. As a rendering of the tragedy of youth, the film fails, and Orgel's sentence stands. As a document of an industry's idea of art, it is unmatched, and Rothwell's sentence stands. The error lies in forcing one object to be the other, an error the film's own publicity committed first.

Cukor's own testimony, given in interviews across the 1970s, sides with the prosecution on the essential point and then complicates it. "It's one picture that if I had to do over again, I'd know how", he said. "I'd get the garlic and the Mediterranean into it." He wished he had given the film more Italian flavour, had scaled it down, made it more intimate; he judged that in 1936 the whole company had been "caught up in production gloss, giving a film a big, stately look", and that the picture had suffered "a bit, maybe more than a bit". Yet he defended the casting without reservation: Norma and Leslie and John Barrymore were wonderful, he insisted, and could not be improved on. The two judgements together are the most exact criticism the film has received, because they locate the failure where it belongs, in the conception rather than the execution. The actors were beyond improvement within the film's terms. The terms were the mistake. A director who sets out to enshrine a text will hire masters and build museums; a director who sets out to film young love in a violent city will hire unknowns and find streets, and the history of the play on screen after 1936 is the history of the second conception displacing the first.

Jorgens supplies the standard by which both conceptions can be measured, since his account of the play insists on the lovers' end as the tragedy's signature: a clear-sighted calm and a sense of inevitability with which the hero and heroine greet their destruction, fully aware of the corrupt world they are leaving. Against that standard, the two great mid-century answers fail in mirror image. Zeffirelli's lovers, in Jorgens' reading, drown the calm in sentiment and pathos, dying as victims of a sorrow they never comprehend. Cukor's lovers preserve the calm and forfeit the tragedy around it, dying with the composure of sitters in a state portrait, their awareness complete and their peril unreal. The calm in the play is terrible because it is young, purchased in five days at the price of everything; in the film it is the natural expression of maturity, the serenity of performers who have survived a thousand evenings and expect to survive this one. The distinction explains why the tomb scene, for all its finish, moves audiences less than its reputation promises, and why the film's admirers so often praise it in the vocabulary of respect rather than of grief. Respect is the correct emotion before a monument. It is not the emotion the play was written to cause.

The division among the first critics also ran along a social seam that later accounts flatten. The quality press, the Times at its head, reviewed the film as an institutional event and judged it by the standards of the undertaking: taste, fidelity, finish. The wits reviewed it as an evening and judged it by the standards of the theatre: pace, heat, belief. Greene's dissent mattered most because it crossed the seam, granting the undertaking its excellence while denying that excellence was the point. The Academy, when its turn came, voted with the quality press, nominating the undertaking in four categories and rewarding, among the performers, only the supporting turn whose theatricality the wits had praised. The pattern has repeated in every decade since, with institutions honouring the film's intentions and working critics reserving their love for livelier versions, and any honest reception history must keep the two electorates separate. Conflating them produces the myth of the cold reception on one side and the myth of the misunderstood masterpiece on the other, and the documentary record sustains neither.

## The Long Afterlife: Library Classic, Classroom Text, and the Rehabilitation

For decades after its commercial failure, the film occupied a curious position in the MGM library, honoured as one of the studio's great classics by courtesy of its intentions while being seldom revived with enthusiasm, a title that catalogues praised and audiences skipped. Its clarity found it a second career in the classroom, where the lucid delivery, the complete story shape, and the legible introductions of its characters made it a serviceable instrument for teachers confronting a class with the play entire, and generations of students met the tragedy first in Cukor's processional version, an influence on the play's popular image out of all proportion to the film's box-office record. The reference tradition kept the honours warm. Leonard Maltin's long-running guide awards the picture three and a half stars, a rating it shares, in one of film criticism's neatest symmetries, with Zeffirelli's 1968 version, as if the ledger of taste had decided that the marble monument and the sunlit correction were worth exactly the same to posterity. The American Film Institute carried the symmetry into the new century by nominating Cukor's film for its 100 Passions list in 2002, two generations after the picture's own public had declined to attend it.

The rehabilitation gathered its most vivid evidence from an unexpected quarter. In 2016 a documentary on Leslie Howard revealed a roll of colour 16mm Kodachrome that Howard himself had shot during the production's opening exterior work, showing the company in costume amid the lights and apparatus of the set. The footage has a force no amount of studio documentation could match, because it shows the monument under construction in colour, informal and sunlit, with the forty-three-year-old Romeo behind the camera behaving like the enthusiast he was. Posterity's image of the production, frozen for decades in Daniels's monochrome formality, loosened at a stroke. The episode belongs in this article because it demonstrates the process by which the film's reputation has been revised: not by new argument but by new evidence, each recovery of the production's human texture warming the object Rothwell asked the world to cherish. A film once dismissed as embalmed keeps being caught, in photograph and home movie and memoir, in the act of having been alive to make.

The scholarly rehabilitation followed the same course. Rothwell's filmography entry of 1990, for all its closing warmth, had repeated the simplified story of a film rejected by critics and public alike, and the archival recovery of the 1936 notices, raves and all, corrected the record on which judgement depends. Tatspaugh's Cambridge Companion chapter supplied the missing half of the textual account, the lovers' poetry retained beyond any successor's measure, and Sterritt's essay for Turner Classic Movies assembled the director's defence in modern form. By the time Cukor's own interviews had been fully absorbed, with their garlic and their Mediterranean and their unrepentant pride in the cast, the film had acquired the one thing it lacked in 1936: an interpretation adequate to its strangeness. It is read, at last, as what it is, the studio system's testament to its own cultural ambitions, with the tragedy audible inside the testament like a voice in a cathedral. The afterlife does not overturn the verdict of the box office or of the stricter critics. It explains why the film survives both, and why a series on the play's performance history must begin its screen cluster here, with the monument, rather than with the livelier films that defined themselves against it.

## The Verdict: A Monument That Preserved the Poem and Missed the Pulse

The consequences arrived quickly and can be measured. The public, as distinct from the press, stayed away in the numbers that mattered; MGM's ledger closed on its 922,000-dollar loss; and Hollywood drew the conclusion the industry always draws, that the author rather than the method had failed. As the production historian Douglas Brode records, the major studios abandoned Shakespeare for over a decade after the twin disappointments of the Reinhardt Midsummer and Cukor's tragedy, and when the play returned to the screen it returned under new rules. Renato Castellani's 1954 film, examined next in this cluster in [Renato Castellani's 1954 film](/2028/07/14/renato-castellani-1954-film/), took the opposite path on the question of place, carrying its cameras to Italian locations and letting a real city's light discipline the verse, though it kept the adult casting and paid for it in the same coin. Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film, the subject of [Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 Romeo and Juliet](/2028/07/21/franco-zeffirelli-1968-film/), completed the correction by casting actual teenagers, Leonard Whiting at seventeen and Olivia Hussey at fifteen, and became the popular success the play's screen history had waited three decades for. The argument over which of these answers deserves the palm is the subject of [the debate over which film Romeo and Juliet is best](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/), and it cannot be conducted honestly without Cukor's film as its first exhibit, because every successor defined itself against the monument: Castellani against its artifice, Zeffirelli against its ages, Luhrmann against its stillness. A film that teaches all its successors what to refuse has not failed in the way its ledger suggests.

The Academy's response completes the contemporary picture. Four nominations, for the picture itself, for Shearer, for Rathbone in the new supporting actor category, and for the art direction, with no wins, is the precise institutional signature of the film's achievement: honoured in every department of craft, crowned in none, admired as an undertaking and passed over as an experience. It is difficult to improve on that distribution as a summary. The picture's craft was and is superb. Its cinematography glows, its design coheres, its verse is lucidly spoken by the most accomplished company the studio system could assemble, and its producer's intentions were honourable in a way Hollywood intentions rarely are. Against all of that stands the play, which is about two children who mistake five days for a lifetime and are right to, since five days is what they get. "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3), the Prince concludes, and the woe depends on the waste, and the waste depends on the youth. Cukor's film preserves everything about the tragedy except the waste.

The double judgement suggests the terms on which the film asks, finally, to be watched. Watched as the tragedy, it disappoints in the ways this study has catalogued, and no rearrangement of sympathy can make a forty-three-year-old's wooing perilous or a procession frightening. Watched as a double document, it fascinates as few films do: a record of the play's verse spoken in the grand manner by the last company the studio system could assemble for the purpose, laid inside a record of that system's cultural ambition at its most confident and most exposed. The two records comment on each other continuously. The verse keeps escaping the monument built to honour it, in a Barrymore aside, in a potion speech that outruns its frame, in lines whose speed rebukes the pace of the procession carrying them; and the monument keeps explaining, by its very completeness, why the verse needed rescuing from the system that loved it. Later filmmakers learned the lesson as technique. The deeper lesson is about institutions and art: that reverence is a resource with a price, that the price is paid in the currency the work itself supplies, and that a tragedy of waste cannot be produced by a method whose first principle is that nothing be wasted. The film demonstrates the theorem at two million dollars, with professors in attendance, and the demonstration has never been repeated at the same scale, because the industry that could afford it drew its own conclusion and declined to try again.

One final fact brackets the enterprise with an appropriateness no screenwriter would dare. The picture opened in New York on 20 August 1936, at the Astor Theatre, to the respectful notices described above. Its West Coast premiere followed at the Carthay Circle Theatre in Los Angeles on 14 September, and on that night Irving Thalberg died, aged thirty-seven, of the heart condition that had shadowed his whole career; some later accounts compress the story into a claim that he died on the night of the premiere, which is true only of the Los Angeles opening, the New York premiere having passed almost a month earlier. The film was the last production he personally supervised entire, and it reads in retrospect as his monument in a double sense, the prestige object he had argued into existence and the tombstone of the system that built it. The studio era's Shakespeare stands in Culver City's Verona still, in the surviving prints: eight acres of researched streets, a ballroom the size of a parade ground, professors on the payroll, masters in the cast, and at its centre two beautiful, accomplished, impossible teenagers of forty-three and thirty-four, speaking the verse better than almost anyone has spoken it on film, and demonstrating by their excellence the one thing the prestige method could never purchase. The monument endures. The pulse had to wait for other films.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Cukor profile, and what does it record about the film?

The InsightCrunch Cukor profile is the table in this article that gathers the production's documented facts in one place: its director and producer, its advisers, its premieres, its running time, its negative cost and worldwide earnings and loss as MGM's own records give them, the proportion of the play's text it retains, the ages of its principal players at release, its cinematographer, composer, designers, and choreographer, its four Academy Award nominations, and the death of its producer on the night of its West Coast premiere. It records the film as a ledger because the film was made as one, an enterprise in which every value was maximised and certified, and because the arguments about its success and failure all turn on those figures rather than on anybody's impressions of them.

### Q: Who directed the 1936 film of Romeo and Juliet, and who produced it?

George Cukor directed the film, and Irving Thalberg produced it, without screen credit. Cukor was then MGM's most literate director of adaptations, trusted above all with actresses, though he had never directed Shakespeare before this assignment. Thalberg, the studio's production chief and the most admired producer in Hollywood, had pressed for the film across five years against the resistance of the studio head, Louis B. Mayer, and designed it as a prestige undertaking whose success would be measured in standing as much as in receipts. The screenplay was arranged by Talbot Jennings. The partnership explains the picture's character: a producer who conceived it as an institutional proof, and a director whose gifts were taste, clarity, and the handling of performers, executing a conception that neither man fully re-examined once the machinery began to move.

### Q: How old were Leslie Howard and Norma Shearer when the film opened?

Leslie Howard was forty-three, and Norma Shearer was thirty-four by her birth record, which gives 11 August 1902, or thirty-six by the older reference books that print her birth year as 1900. Howard's age is undisputed, since he was born in April 1893. Shearer's is genuinely contested, because studio publicity of her era preferred younger arithmetic than either figure, and the dispute itself testifies to how sensitive the production was on the point. The text they were playing against gives Juliet's age exactly, since Capulet states that she has not seen the change of fourteen years and the Nurse fixes her fourteenth birthday at Lammas Eve. The gap between the performers and the parts, roughly twenty years for her and more for him, became the film's defining controversy and the lesson every later version applied.

### Q: Why did Irving Thalberg have to argue for five years to make the film?

Thalberg had to argue because Louis B. Mayer believed the mass audience considered Shakespeare material over its head, and because the Depression-era economics of MGM punished expensive experiments. Mayer's resistance was commercial rather than philistine, and on the evidence of the eventual ledger it was not foolish. Thalberg's counter-case was that a classic mounted with full pageantry and ritual would raise the studio's standing in the public eye, and that prestige was an asset worth buying outright. The argument succeeded only when two external events armed it: Warner Bros. announced Max Reinhardt's A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Mayer would not be outdone in cultural ambition, and the 1934 Broadway revival with Katharine Cornell demonstrated that a serious production of the tragedy could still draw fashionable audiences. Rivalry and evidence together released the money in 1935.

### Q: How much did the film cost, and how much did it lose?

The film cost just over two million dollars, against an early estimate of 800,000, making it MGM's most expensive sound production to that date. The studio's own records, preserved in the Eddie Mannix ledger, give worldwide earnings of 2,075,000 dollars and a final loss of 922,000 dollars, a figure some later accounts round to a million. The overrun came from the method rather than from mismanagement: a six-month shoot, a Verona built across acres of backlot from thousands of photographs and fifty-four scale models, well over a thousand players costumed, and a company of stars and professors retained throughout. The loss was therefore structural. A production designed to demonstrate that no expense had been spared could hardly then spare the expenses its demonstration required, and the deficit is the prestige method's own arithmetic made visible.

### Q: How much of Shakespeare's text survives in the film?

Roughly forty-five percent of the play survives, a little less than half, arranged by Talbot Jennings into a running time of about two hours. The Prologue is retained entire, and the cuts follow the stage tradition's familiar pattern, falling on the second Chorus, on the comedy of Peter with the musicians, and on connective and argumentative verse throughout. Friar Laurence is sharply reduced. Against those losses stands a distinction the scholarship insists on: Patricia Tatspaugh has observed that Jennings retained more of Shakespeare's poetry for the young lovers than any of his big-screen successors managed, so that the film's Romeo and Juliet speak more of the play than the more celebrated versions allowed theirs to speak. The screenplay's economy is therefore double, severe toward the play's reasoning and generous toward its arias, and the combination defines the adaptation's mind.

### Q: Which scenes in the film did Shakespeare never write?

The film interpolates three sequences built around Friar John, the messenger whose failure the play reports in a few lines, so that the machinery of the undelivered letter is dramatised at a length the text never gives it. Rosaline, whom the play keeps rigorously unseen, appears in person at the Capulet ball to rebuff Romeo, embodying a character Shakespeare deliberately withheld. The opening brawl is staged against a religious procession of the film's invention, and the wedding and funeral are expanded into ceremonies far beyond their textual warrant. Two songs are imported from other plays entirely, "Come Away, Death" from Twelfth Night and "Honour, Riches, Marriage, Blessing" from The Tempest. These additions matter because the production's publicity had promised the opposite, assuring the public in the spring of 1936 that there had been no additions and that the cuts had been minute.

### Q: Who played Mercutio, and why is the performance controversial?

John Barrymore played Mercutio, at fifty-four, a generation after he had been the American stage's defining Hamlet. The performance is controversial because it preserves an older theatrical rhetoric inside a medium learning naturalism: rhetorical, self-regarding, and played at the audience, with the Queen Mab speech delivered as a bravura concert turn. Stephen Orgel's verdict, an elderly Barrymore as a stagey Mercutio decades out of date, states the prosecution's case exactly. The defence answers that Barrymore's Mercutio, flirtatious and visibly delighted by his own wit, is the one figure in the film's Verona who enjoys the language rather than curating it, and that his scenes carry a life the lovers' scenes never attempt. Both accounts describe the same footage, and the disagreement is really about whether a Shakespeare film should preserve the theatrical past or abolish it.

### Q: Why does Basil Rathbone play Tybalt in the film after playing Romeo on stage?

Rathbone plays Tybalt because the production's star logic assigned the showier character part to a leading man of his standing, and because his Romeo had served its purpose as prestige. He had played Romeo opposite Katharine Cornell in the 1934 Broadway revival whose success helped persuade MGM to film the play, and he was the only member of that company carried into the screen version, translated from lover to firebrand. The translation produced the film's prettiest irony of ages, since Rathbone, born in June 1892, was forty-four, a year older than Leslie Howard's Romeo, so that Verona's hot youth was the senior of its lovesick one. His performance, played against stage tradition as stuffy and pompous rather than hot-headed, earned the film's only acting nomination from the Academy, in the new supporting actor category.

### Q: What did the film's two academic advisers do on the set?

John Tucker Murray of Harvard and William Strunk Jr. of Cornell were flown to the production with instructions to criticise it freely, and their documented work was consultative: a check on detail, diction, and the text as arranged, rather than a governing hand on casting, pace, or design. Strunk, the author of The Elements of Style, made an apt patron for a screenplay whose ruling virtue was clarity, and the production's lucid delivery of difficult verse is often linked to his influence. Their larger function was insurance. By attaching professors from the two most eminent universities to the enterprise in advance, the studio guaranteed that its Shakespeare arrived with academic endorsement already secured, so that reviewers and educators would receive the film as a certified object. No adviser was asked the dramatic questions on which the film would be judged, about its lovers' ages or its processional tempo.

### Q: How was the film's Verona constructed, and from what sources?

The film's Verona was constructed on the MGM backlot in Culver City from first-hand research: a design unit photographed the real city and its region, returning with 2,769 photographs that were converted into fifty-four complete scale models, from which the settings grew. The Cathedral Square set covered eight acres, the Capulet ballroom measured 250 by 400 feet with the garden at about the same dimensions, and the visual imagination drew as heavily on the paintings of Botticelli, Bellini, Carpaccio, and Gozzoli as on the photographs, so that the city on screen is early Renaissance Italy as its painters composed it. Cedric Gibbons carried the art direction credit, with Oliver Messel, the English stage designer, supervising a design regime whose methods quarrelled with the studio's own, a friction Cukor recalled for the rest of his life. The result is a fresco rather than a city, beautiful and static for the same reason.

### Q: What music did Herbert Stothart borrow for the score?

Stothart built his score around two borrowed classics, neither credited on screen: Tchaikovsky's Fantasy Overture on the play, the concert work that had already fixed the tragedy's musical image for two generations of listeners, and the stately Pavane from Peter Warlock's Capriol Suite, a twentieth-century composition in Renaissance dress. The borrowing was policy rather than economy. Just as the sets quoted the certified masterpieces of Italian painting, the score wrapped the film in the certified masterpieces of the concert hall, so that a ticket purchased an anthology of European culture with the play at its centre. Stothart, MGM's senior composer, integrated the borrowed material with his own lyrical writing in the studio's plushest manner, and the effect is of a piece with the production's whole philosophy: nothing in the picture was to be merely adequate, and nothing in it was to be new when a masterpiece could be borrowed instead.

### Q: What did the New York Times say about the film on its release?

The New York Times raved. Frank S. Nugent, the paper's film critic, praised a production "ornate but not garish, extravagant but in perfect taste, expansive but never overwhelming", reflecting "great credit upon its producers and upon the screen as a whole", and concluded that it was "a dignified, sensitive and entirely admirable Shakespearean, not Hollywoodean, production". The review matters beyond its warmth because it refutes the later commonplace that the critics of 1936 rejected the film. The Film Daily was comparably enthusiastic, reaching for superlatives about the picture's importance to the screen, while Variety admired the faithfulness and the costumes and doubted the pace, and the New Yorker granted a definite if cumbersome achievement. The contemporary failure was commercial, not critical, and the distinction governs any honest account of the film's reception.

### Q: Why did Graham Greene dismiss the film's whole enterprise?

Graham Greene dismissed the enterprise itself rather than the execution, writing that he was "less than ever convinced that there is an aesthetic justification for filming Shakespeare at all" and that "the effect of even the best scenes is to distract". His objection was that the camera's wealth of incident, the built Verona, the processions, the thousand properties of a prestige production, competes with the verse for the spectator's attention and wins, reducing the poetry to an accompaniment of its own illustration. The review has outlasted the raves because it identifies the film's structural problem with a precision the friendlier notices avoided: reverence, applied through a camera, becomes distraction. Greene's dissent was the famous exception among the notices of 1936, and it aimed past Cukor at the entire project of prestige Shakespeare, which is why it still frames the terms in which the film is debated.

### Q: How many Academy Award nominations did the film receive, and how many did it win?

The film received four Academy Award nominations and won none of them. The nominations covered the picture itself, in the person of its producer Irving Thalberg; Norma Shearer as best actress; Basil Rathbone as best supporting actor, in the first year of that category's existence; and the art direction and interior decoration of Cedric Gibbons, Fredric Hope, and Edwin B. Willis. The distribution is the precise institutional signature of the film's achievement, honoured in every department of craft and crowned in none, admired as an undertaking and passed over as an experience. Rathbone's nomination, the company's only acting honour, went to the performer who had been demoted from the stage production's Romeo to the film's Tybalt, a detail that summarises the production's relationship to its own material with an economy no critic has improved.

### Q: What did Cukor later say he would do differently?

Cukor said he would get the garlic and the Mediterranean into it. In interviews across the 1970s he returned to the film with unusual candour, wishing he had given it more Italian flavour, scaled it down, and made it more intimate, and judging that the whole company in 1936 had been "caught up in production gloss, giving a film a big, stately look", so that the picture suffered "a bit, maybe more than a bit". He described the design quarrel between the Gibbons and Adrian regime and Oliver Messel, admitted he had wanted Messel's theatrical approach and lost, and reported that Thalberg sat over the dispute like Solomon and never committed himself. Yet he defended the casting without reservation, insisting that Norma and Leslie and John Barrymore were wonderful and could not be improved on, a combination of judgements that locates the failure in the conception rather than the execution.

### Q: Why did the major studios avoid Shakespeare for a decade after the film?

The major studios avoided Shakespeare because the prestige method had made the author appear uncommercial, after Cukor's film lost 922,000 dollars and Warner Bros.' Reinhardt Midsummer fared comparably in the same seasons. The industry drew its customary conclusion, that the material rather than the method had failed, and the production historian Douglas Brode records the result as an abandonment of the playwright by the major studios for over a decade. The reasoning deserves scrutiny, since no major studio in the period attempted the alternative the evidence suggested, a modestly budgeted version cast young and shot fast, because the prestige genre forbade modesty. Shakespeare was therefore blamed for the economics of the template used to film him, and the play's screen history restarted only when Castellani carried his cameras to Italy in 1954 and Zeffirelli cast teenagers in 1968, each reform defined against the monument of 1936.

### Q: Who choreographed the Capulet ball, and what form does the dance take?

Agnes de Mille choreographed the Capulet ball, and the dance takes the form of a formal court masque, rank upon rank of costumed figures moving in measured patterns drawn from the Renaissance paintings the designers had studied. The sequence fills the 250-foot ballroom with a ceremony inside which the lovers' first meeting must occur, so that their shared sonnet becomes an intimacy excavated from spectacle by glances and close framing while the masque processes around them. The choice is the film in miniature: magnificent, scholarly, and cooled, since a Verona where even the dancing is pageantry has decided in advance how much disorder it will permit. The ball's splendour is inseparable from its dramatic cost, and the scene remains the clearest single demonstration of the prestige method's exchange of danger for design.

### Q: What happened on the night of the film's Los Angeles premiere?

Irving Thalberg died, aged thirty-seven, on the night of the West Coast premiere at the Carthay Circle Theatre on 14 September 1936. The producer had suffered from a congenital heart condition throughout his life, and he died less than a month after the film's New York opening at the Astor Theatre on 20 August. The stars arriving at the Los Angeles theatre were so grief-stricken that the studio's publicist abandoned the customary entrance interviews and simply announced each arrival. Some later accounts compress the story into the claim that Thalberg died on the night of the premiere, which is true only of the Los Angeles opening and false if applied to the New York premiere three weeks earlier. The film was the last production he personally supervised entire, and it stands as his monument in the double sense of a supreme achievement and a memorial.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Cukor profile to argue that reverence preserved the verse at the cost of the play's youth?

An essay should set the profile's two columns against each other: the certification column, of cost, advisers, research, and honours, against the age column, of a Romeo of forty-three, a Juliet in her mid-thirties, a Mercutio of fifty-four, and a Tybalt older than the Romeo he fights. The first column explains why the verse survived better here than in any successor, protected by money, scholarship, and performers of the highest technical finish. The second explains what the protection cost, since the tragedy's engine is the waste of youth and a production that insures its lovers against inexperience insures them against the play. The profile's loss figure then closes the argument commercially, and its four nominations close it institutionally: the industry rewarded every element of the reverence and declined the experience, which is the definition of the monument this film became.
