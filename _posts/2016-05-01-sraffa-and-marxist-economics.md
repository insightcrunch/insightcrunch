Piero Sraffa was a Cambridge economist whose one short book, published in 1960, did two things that are usually discussed in separate rooms. It supplied the technical basis for the most damaging internal critique that mainstream capital theory has ever received, a critique whose central logical point the mainstream side publicly conceded. The same book also supplied the basis for a challenge to Marx, pressed by economists who worked inside Sraffa's own framework, according to which the labour values at the heart of Marx's theory are an unnecessary detour: everything values were supposed to explain, they argued, can be obtained without them. A reader who knows only the second of these facts owns half a portrait, and the missing half changes what the first one means.

The missing half is a life. The framework that was used to declare Marx's value theory redundant was built by a scholar whose life's work was the recovery of the classical surplus tradition: the economist who edited the standard scholarly edition of Ricardo across two decades, who arranged the account through which Antonio Gramsci received books in a fascist prison, and who was thanked by Wittgenstein for the criticism that reshaped a famous philosophy. Sraffa's unpublished papers, opened to researchers late in the twentieth century, have since supported a serious and contested reading on which he understood his own project as a reconstruction of that classical tradition, with Marx inside it, rather than as a refutation of Marx. This article sets out the framework, the challenge it generated, the replies the challenge provoked, and the evidence about its author's intentions, because the neo-Ricardian challenge reads differently once it is recognised as an argument among heirs.

## The man from Turin

Sraffa was born in Turin in 1898, the son of a professor of commercial law, and he took his degree at the University of Turin with a thesis on wartime and postwar inflation supervised by Luigi Einaudi, the economist who later became president of the Italian republic. Turin in those years was the most politically charged industrial city in Italy, and the young Sraffa moved in the circle around Gramsci's journal L'Ordine Nuovo, writing for it and forming the friendship with Gramsci that lasted until Gramsci's death in prison. A visit to London in the early nineteen twenties, where he studied at the London School of Economics, widened his contacts; he met John Maynard Keynes, who became his protector, and published in English on Italian banking. What he published there ended his Italian career. An article on the collapse of a large Italian bank, printed in an English newspaper's reconstruction supplement, offended Mussolini personally, and the demand for a public retraction made his position at home untenable.

### Why did Sraffa leave fascist Italy?

He had written in an English publication about the failure of an Italian bank and the state's part in its rescue, and Mussolini took offence and demanded a retraction. Sraffa would not retract, his future in Italy closed, and in 1927 he accepted Keynes's invitation to settle in Cambridge, which remained his home for the rest of his life.

He taught first at Perugia and then at Cagliari, and the two articles that made his early reputation were already attacks on the theoretical core of the economics of his day. The first, published in Italian in 1925, and the second, published in the Economic Journal in 1926 under the title The Laws of Returns under Competitive Conditions, argued that the Marshallian theory of the supply curve, on which the whole apparatus of partial equilibrium rested, was internally inconsistent: increasing returns could not coexist with competition, and diminishing returns belonged to a different logical shelf than the one Marshall had placed them on. The critique was polite in form and destructive in substance, and it announced the habit of a lifetime, which was to find the load-bearing assumption of an orthodoxy and test it quietly until it cracked.

Cambridge gave him a lectureship in 1927, and in time a fellowship at Trinity College, but he published almost nothing for three decades and became a legend of a peculiar kind: a man whose influence ran through conversation, correspondence, and the work of others. He was a member of the informal discussion group, nicknamed the circus, that worked through Keynes's monetary theory in the early nineteen thirties and helped push it toward the General Theory; the chapter of that book dealing with own rates of interest acknowledges his prompting. In 1932 he published a long review of Friedrich Hayek's Prices and Production in the Economic Journal which anatomised Hayek's concept of a natural rate of interest so effectively that economists still cite it as the model of how a review can dismantle a system. Then, for the most part, silence: the silence of a man who had concluded that his positive work would take decades and who refused to publish it in pieces.

## The Ricardo edition and the recovery of a tradition

The work that filled those decades was, in the first instance, an edition. In 1930 the Royal Economic Society commissioned Sraffa to edit the works and correspondence of David Ricardo, the classical economist whose system had dominated English political economy in the early nineteenth century and whose reputation had since been absorbed into a story in which he figured as a crude forerunner of the marginal theory that replaced him. Sraffa accepted the commission expecting a task of a few years. It occupied him, with the collaboration of Maurice Dobb, for more than two decades: ten volumes appeared between 1951 and 1955, and an eleventh, the general index, completed the set in 1973. The edition is still the standard scholarly Ricardo, the text every serious student of the classical tradition must use.

An edition of that scale is never merely clerical, and Sraffa's was openly an act of recovery. His long introduction to the first volume reconstructed Ricardo's earliest theory of profits, written in 1815 before Ricardo had settled his theory of value, as a self-contained argument in which the rate of profit in agriculture was determined as a ratio between physical quantities of corn, the corn produced and the corn advanced as capital, before any question of prices arose. On Sraffa's reading, Ricardo had begun with a surplus approach: output exceeds the inputs required to produce it, and the theory of distribution is the theory of how that surplus is divided and what the division implies. Ricardo's later struggles with value, on this account, were attempts to find a measure in which that physical reasoning could be generalised to an economy of many commodities. The introduction did for Ricardo what no commentary had done: it made him readable as the founder of a distinct tradition, the classical surplus tradition, running from William Petty through the Physiocrats and Adam Smith to Ricardo, and, Sraffa's readers increasingly concluded, to Marx, whose theory of surplus value claimed exactly that descent.

This editorial labour matters to everything that follows for two reasons. The first is technical: the problems Sraffa solved as Ricardo's editor, above all the problem of an invariable measure of value that would let distribution change without corrupting the ruler used to describe it, became the problems his own book addressed. The second is interpretive: a man who had spent twenty years demonstrating that the classical economists possessed a coherent theory of their own, different from and older than the marginalist theory that displaced it, was unlikely to regard Marx, the classical tradition's most systematic heir, as a simple error. That prior does not settle what Sraffa thought, and the evidence of the papers is examined in its own section below, but it frames the question correctly. The challenge his framework posed to Marx came from inside the house Marx claimed as his ancestry.

## A prelude in ninety pages

Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities appeared in 1960, published by Cambridge University Press, and it is one of the slightest volumes ever to reorganise a discipline: fewer than a hundred pages of text, of which the argument proper occupies still fewer, written in a prose of extreme compression that states results and leaves the reader to supply the connective reasoning. Its subtitle, Prelude to a Critique of Economic Theory, announced that the book was a foundation and not the edifice. The critique it was a prelude to was aimed, as the preface indicates, at the marginal theory of value and distribution, whose concepts of capital and its marginal product Sraffa had reason to believe could not survive a rigorous statement of the price system. The critique itself, as a separate work, never appeared.

### What does the subtitle of the book promise?

The subtitle calls the book a prelude to a critique of economic theory, which tells the reader two things. The slim volume is the groundwork, a rigorous restatement of the classical approach to prices and distribution, and the demolition of the rival marginal theory, for which the groundwork was built, was reserved for a successor work that never came.

The gestation had been extraordinarily long. Sraffa's papers show that by the late nineteen twenties he had already turned away from the Marshallian framework and begun constructing price equations from the physical data of production, and the manuscripts record decades of drafting, abandonment, and resumption while the Ricardo edition consumed his working life. The delay had one consequence that shaped the book's reception: it arrived in 1960 as a finished and sealed object, without the scaffolding of articles and controversies by which academic arguments usually announce themselves, and readers had to decide what it was for with very little guidance from its author. Sraffa's preface is famously unhelpful about applications. It remarks that anyone accustomed to the marginal theory may find the standpoint unfamiliar, and it declines to argue the matter. The book proposes; it does not campaign.

That reticence is the first source of the two Sraffas of this article's claim. One Sraffa is the author of a technical construction, a set of relations among prices, the wage, and the rate of profit, which any economist may take up and use for any purpose, including purposes aimed at Marx. The other Sraffa is the historical figure, the editor of Ricardo, the friend of Gramsci, the correspondent whose manuscripts show sustained engagement with Marx's texts, whose relation to the classical tradition was one of deliberate restoration. The challenge discussed in the next sections was constructed almost entirely from the first Sraffa. The second Sraffa is the one the archive restored to view, and the concluding sections of this article weigh what difference he makes.

## Prices from physical conditions

The construction begins by refusing the starting point of the economics Sraffa had been educated in. Marginal theory begins with demand and supply, with preferences, endowments, and substitution, and it determines prices and distribution together, as twin outputs of one mechanism. Sraffa begins instead with the physical facts of production, the facts an engineer or a factory accountant could in principle record. For a given state of technique, each industry uses up definite quantities of commodities as its means of production and employs definite quantities of labour, and it produces a definite quantity of its own commodity. Write down, industry by industry, what goes in and what comes out, and you have the material of the system: a table of inputs and outputs for the whole economy, in which the same commodities appear on both sides, since commodities are produced by means of commodities. The title of the book is the method.

From that table, and from one further piece of information, prices follow. The reasoning is bookkeeping raised to the level of theory. Each industry must recover, from the sale of its product, the value of the means of production it used up, the wages of the labour it employed, and a profit on the capital advanced, calculated at a rate of profit that competition equalises across industries. If the rate of profit is known, and the wage is known, every price is determined by the requirement that each industry exactly covers its costs so reckoned; and the prices must be consistent, since the price at which an industry sells its product is the price at which other industries buy it as an input. The system is a set of simultaneous conditions, and Sraffa showed that, for an economy producing a surplus over its own replacement needs, the conditions have a consistent solution. Nothing in the derivation consults anyone's preferences. Nothing in it consults labour values either, a point whose significance grew teeth later.

### What is given, and what is determined, in the system?

The physical conditions of production are given: the quantities of each commodity used up in making every other, and the labour each process employs. One distributive magnitude is also given from outside, either the real wage or the rate of profit. Everything else, all prices and the remaining distributive magnitude, is then determined together.

The pivotal feature of the construction is that distribution is not determined inside it. Either the wage or the rate of profit must be supplied from outside the price relations before those relations can be solved, and Sraffa was explicit that this was a deliberate restoration of the classical arrangement. In the classical economists, the wage was settled by social and historical conditions, by subsistence, custom, and the bargaining strength of the parties, and the rate of profit emerged as the share of the surplus left over; distribution was a social fact fed into the theory, not a technical output of it. Marginal theory had abolished that arrangement by deriving both shares from the technology and the supposed scarcity of factors, and Sraffa's construction quietly reinstated the older one. The price system, on his showing, has one degree of freedom, and theory alone cannot close it. Something outside the equations, class relations in the broadest sense, must say where between the possible extremes the actual division of the product falls.

A second distinction does heavy work throughout the book and throughout this article: the distinction between basic and non-basic commodities. A basic commodity is one that enters, directly or indirectly, into the production of every commodity in the system: iron is basic in an economy where iron tools help to make everything, corn is basic where corn feeds every worker and every horse. A non-basic commodity enters only its own production, or the production of a limited family of goods: a luxury perfume, consumed and never used as an input anywhere, is the standard example. The distinction matters because the prices of basics and the rate of profit are determined together, in a self-contained core of the system, while the prices of non-basics are determined afterwards, in terms of the basics, and can wander without disturbing the core. When the rate of profit changes, every basic price is implicated; the perfume merely reprices. It follows that the theory of distribution is a theory of the basic system alone, and that the economy's essential structure can be studied in a reduced form from which everything inessential has been stripped away.

### What makes a commodity basic rather than non-basic?

A commodity is basic when it enters, directly or through the inputs of its inputs, into the production of every commodity, as iron and corn do in classical examples. A commodity that serves only its own production or a narrow family of goods, like perfume, is non-basic, and its price is settled after the core is solved.

Built into the construction is a claim about what kind of theory this is. Sraffa called the quantities he worked with objective and in principle observable: the bushels and tons and hours are the sort of magnitudes that can be recorded without asking anyone what they feel. The contrast he intended was with the magnitudes of marginal theory, above all with the quantity of capital, which, he intended to show, cannot be recorded at all without first knowing the prices and the rate of profit it is supposed to explain. The book's first part restricts itself to single-product industries, where each process makes one commodity, and to economies first at subsistence and then producing a surplus; the second part takes up joint production, fixed capital, and land, the complications that any general theory must eventually face. The architecture is deliberate. The core results are won on the simplest ground first, so that when the complications arrive, the reader can see which results survive them and which must be modified, a discipline that later controversialists on all sides did not always observe.

## The wage and the rate of profit, in words

Strip the system to its distributive skeleton and a single relation carries the whole weight: for a given technique, wages and profits divide a fixed product, so that one share can grow only as the other shrinks. Sraffa expressed the relation in a form of unusual transparency by measuring the wage as a share of the economy's net product rather than as a basket of goods. When the wage is stated as a proportion of the surplus product, the relation between the wage share and the rate of profit becomes a straight line. If wages absorb nothing, the whole surplus goes to profits and the rate of profit stands at its maximum, the ceiling set by the technique itself. If wages absorb the entire surplus, the rate of profit is zero. Between those poles, every increase in the wage share lowers the rate of profit in exact proportion: take a quarter of the net product for wages and the profit rate falls to three quarters of its maximum; take half, and it falls to half. The frontier traced by those points is the wage-profit frontier, and its straightness, under this way of measuring, is one of the book's quiet technical triumphs.

### How do wages and the profit rate trade off for a fixed technique?

For a fixed technique the product available for division is given, and wages and profits are the two claims on it. Measured against the net product, each point given to the wage share takes the same point from the profit share, so the rate of profit falls in proportion as the wage share rises, along a straight descending line.

Two cautions belong with the picture, because later argument turned on both. The first is that the straight line is a property of measuring distribution against the standard net product, a construction explained in the next section; when the wage is instead a specified basket of real goods, the frontier for a technique can bend, since the prices of the wage goods themselves move as distribution changes, and Sraffa's treatment of the real wage case traces those movements rather than assuming them away. The second caution is that the frontier describes one technique in isolation. An economy choosing among techniques has a frontier for each, and the economy's effective frontier is the outer envelope of them all, the boundary formed at each rate of profit by whichever technique is most profitable there. That envelope, and the strange behaviour of the techniques that compose it, is where the book's most explosive result lives, and it is treated in the sections on reswitching below.

What the frontier establishes, even before any controversy, is the shape of the classical theory of distribution. There is a genuine trade-off between the shares, set by technique; there is no mechanism inside the price relations that selects the point on the frontier; and the rate of profit is therefore not the price of a factor in any sense that survives inspection, but a residual whose size depends on where the social settlement of the wage happens to sit. Defenders of the marginal theory could and did reply that the frontier is a constraint and not a theory, that something must still explain the point chosen, and that supply and demand in factor markets remained the candidate explanation. That reply is fair as far as it goes, and Sraffa's construction by itself does not answer it. What answers it, or begins to, is the demonstration that the marginal candidate explanation cannot be stated coherently for capital as a whole, which is the burden of the reswitching result. The frontier is the frame; the explosion happens inside it.

## The standard commodity and the measure problem

Ricardo had tormented himself with a problem he never solved: the search for an invariable measure of value. He wanted a measuring rod for prices and shares that would not itself stretch or shrink when distribution changed, so that when a price moved he could know whether the movement belonged to the commodity measured or to the ruler. No actual commodity qualifies, because every actual commodity's price shifts as the wage and the profit rate shift, and it shifts by an amount that depends on the proportions in which labour and means of production enter its making. A commodity produced with much labour and little capital cheapens as wages rise; one produced with much capital and little labour cheapens as the profit rate rises; each is a ruler made of rubber, and Ricardo died knowing it.

Sraffa's solution is one of the most elegant constructions in the history of economic theory, and it is a construction, not a discovery: he builds the ruler. Take the basic system and rescale its industries, multiplying each industry's scale by a chosen factor, until the collection of industries has a remarkable property: the aggregate means of production and the aggregate net product consist of the same commodities in the same proportions. An economy rearranged in those proportions is Sraffa's standard system, and its composite net product, the bundle of basics left over after replacement, in exactly the proportions of the inputs, is the standard commodity. Because the bundle that the system produces over and above its needs is made of the same stuff, in the same proportions, as the bundle it used up, the ratio between the two bundles is a pure physical ratio, a number that no change in prices or distribution can touch. That ratio delivers the maximum rate of profit directly from the physical data, and it delivers the straight-line frontier described above, since measuring the wage as a fraction of the standard net product gives the measuring rod the invariance Ricardo wanted.

### What problem was the standard commodity built to solve?

It was built to give political economy the invariable measure Ricardo had searched for in vain. When distribution changes, every ordinary price moves, so no ordinary commodity can serve as a fixed ruler. The standard commodity is a composite whose product and means of production have identical composition, which makes it immune to distributional change and fit to measure by.

The standard commodity is frequently confused, by students and occasionally by economists who should know better, with a numéraire, the arbitrary unit in which prices happen to be quoted. The difference is fundamental, and one of this article's extra questions returns to it. A numéraire is a label: declare prices in terms of gold, or wheat, or pounds, and every relative price is untouched. The standard commodity is a physical construction with a theoretical job: it is the one bundle whose value cannot be distorted by the very distributional changes whose effects the theory wants to trace, and it exists only inside the standard system, the rescaled shadow of the actual economy. Sraffa was careful to add that the actual economy's own proportions will generally differ from the standard proportions, so the standard commodity is an analytical instrument and not a description of any real national product. It is better compared to the surveyor's benchmark than to money: a fixed point, established by construction, from which genuine movement elsewhere can be told apart from the trembling of the instrument.

With the standard in place, one more of the book's devices falls into view: the reduction of prices to dated quantities of labour. Any commodity's price can be unwound, step by step, into the wages of the labour that made it directly, plus the wages of the labour that made its means of production, plus the wages of the labour behind those, each layer carrying profit at the going rate for the time it was advanced. The price resolves into a sum of wage payments stretched back through time, weighted by compounding. Sraffa performed this reduction not to revive a labour theory of price, and readers who took it as one missed its purpose: it is the device that exposes why the marginal theory of capital fails. For the weights in the reduction depend on the rate of profit, and the proportions in which capital and labour contribute to a price shift as that rate shifts, which means the quantity of capital embodied in a process is not a datum at all. It is a conclusion that changes with the distribution it is meant to determine. The next sections draw out that consequence.

## Two techniques and a crossing

Now assemble the pieces. An economy that can choose its methods has before it, for every commodity, several known techniques, differing in the inputs they use and in when they use them. At any given rate of profit, a cost-minimising economy adopts, for each line of production, the technique whose product can be sold most cheaply at that rate; the economy's wage-profit frontier is the outer envelope of the individual techniques' frontiers, and as the rate of profit moves, the adopted technique can change at the points where frontiers cross. Everything in that description is common ground. The explosive discovery, stated in the final part of Sraffa's book and then fought over for a decade, concerns the pattern of those crossings: nothing in the logic of the system requires each technique to occupy a single continuous stretch of the envelope. A technique can be the cheapest at a low rate of profit, lose its place to a rival as the rate rises, and then become the cheapest again at a still higher rate, while the rival, enthroned in between, is displaced by the very technique it displaced. That is reswitching.

The result is best met in a concrete case, described in words, without algebra. Consider a commodity that takes time to mature, wine say, or timber, or a machine built over several seasons, and two ways of making it. The first way concentrates its labour early: most of the work is done well before the product is finished, in preparing, planting, and building, and only a little labour is applied near the end. The second way concentrates its labour late: little work at the start, and a heavy application of labour in the final stages before the product is sold. Wages are advanced when the work is done, and profit accrues on the sums advanced for the whole time they are outstanding, so the cost of each way of producing depends on the rate of profit in a way that reflects its labour's timing. When the rate of profit is zero, timing does not matter at all, and the cheaper technique is simply the one using less labour in total. As the rate rises from zero, the technique with the early labour begins to suffer, because its advanced wages carry profit charges for longer; the late-labour technique grows relatively cheaper and takes over. But the compounding that punishes early labour also grows in a curve rather than a line, and the balance between the two cost structures can tilt back: at a sufficiently high rate of profit the first technique, the early-labour one, can undercut its rival again, not because anything about the physical processes has changed, but because the geometry of the two frontiers permits two crossings rather than one.

### Why can the same technique return as the rate of profit rises?

Because techniques differ in the timing of their inputs as well as in their amounts, and the profit charged on advances compounds over time. A method beaten at an intermediate rate, where its early labour charges weigh most heavily against it, can undercut its rival again at a higher rate, so profitability rankings need not move in one direction only.

Sraffa himself presented the possibility with characteristic economy, giving an example in the book's closing part and drawing the moral in a sentence: the same method can be profitable at two separated rates of profit while another method rules between them, and there is no way of ranking methods by their capital intensity that survives the phenomenon. The point sounds like a curiosity. It is a demolition charge, and the next section explains what it was placed under. But one preliminary clarification protects the result from its commonest misstatement. Reswitching is not an empirical claim that real economies often switch back and forth; it is a logical claim about what the theory of production permits. A single possible case, constructed from techniques that violate no assumption of the rival theory, suffices to break a purported law, in exactly the way a single black swan breaks the law that all swans are white. The critics who replied that the phenomenon must be rare in practice were answering a claim nobody had made, and the controversialists on Sraffa's side were careful, at their best, to keep repeating that the issue was coherence, not frequency.

## Capital reversing and the parable of the production function

The marginal theory of distribution, in the form in which it was taught for most of the twentieth century, rests on a parable. Society produces output with two great factors, labour and capital; capital is a measurable quantity, like the acreage of land or the headcount of workers; competition ensures that each factor is paid the value of its marginal product; and because of diminishing returns, a larger quantity of capital per worker is associated with a lower rate of profit, the lower return being precisely the mechanism that reconciles capitalists to the accumulation. The parable delivers an orderly world: techniques can be ranked by their capital intensity, the ranking is the same whatever the rate of profit, and as the profit rate falls the economy slides smoothly toward more capital-intensive methods, as down a staircase whose steps are fixed. Distribution is thereby explained by technology and scarcity together, and the explanation has the moral shape the theory wanted: each share is a price, and the price measures a contribution.

Reswitching breaks the staircase. If the same technique can be the most profitable at a low and at a high rate of profit with a different technique in between, then no distribution-independent ranking of techniques by capital intensity exists, and the association the parable needs, lower profit rate with more capital-intensive technique, can run backwards: a fall in the rate of profit can lead the economy to adopt a technique that is, by any physical reckoning, less capital-intensive, a phenomenon its critics named capital reversing. Worse follows for the measurement itself. The value of a collection of capital goods depends on prices, and prices depend on the rate of profit, so the quantity of capital that is supposed to determine the rate of profit through its marginal product cannot be specified before the rate of profit is known. The parable asks a magnitude to do explanatory work while secretly borrowing that magnitude from the answer. Joan Robinson had opened this line of attack in the early nineteen fifties by asking, with deceptive innocence, in what units the capital in the production function was measured; Sraffa's book supplied the rigorous construction that converted her question from a debating point into a theorem.

It is worth pausing over what the argument does and does not touch, since both sides later blurred the boundary. The argument strikes at aggregate production functions and at any theory that requires capital to be a single measurable factor whose scarcity price is the rate of profit. It does not strike at the use of disaggregated models in which each capital good carries its own price and its own rental, the Walrasian construction in which there is no aggregate capital at all, and the defenders of orthodoxy who retreated to that ground were entitled to point out that the fortress Sraffa had undermined was one particular, if enormously influential, citadel. What could not be salvaged was the parable's explanatory claim: that the rate of profit is determined by the technical scarcity of a measurable thing called capital. A theory may keep its mathematics and surrender its parable, which is, in the judgment this article will defend when it reaches the controversies, approximately what happened.

## The controversy of the two Cambridges

The fight that followed is known, from the geography of its combatants, as the Cambridge capital controversy: Cambridge, England, where Robinson, Sraffa, Pierangelo Garegnani, and Luigi Pasinetti pressed the attack, against Cambridge, Massachusetts, where Paul Samuelson, Robert Solow, and Franco Modigliani at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology defended the neoclassical construction. It began before Sraffa's book, with Robinson's 1953 article asking what the capital in the aggregate production function could possibly denote, and it acquired formal rigour after 1960, when the English side could work with a complete alternative system rather than a list of objections. The Massachusetts side's standard defence was the surrogate production function, an attempt by Samuelson to show that even in a world of many capital goods, the economy could behave as if the parable were true, provided techniques were related in a special way. The defence held only under restrictions that amounted to assuming the conclusion, and the controversy narrowed, year by year, to the question of reswitching: if reswitching was possible in logically admissible cases, the surrogate construction failed as a general defence.

The endgame came in the middle of the nineteen sixties in the pages of the Quarterly Journal of Economics. A claim by David Levhari that reswitching could not occur for an economy as a whole, even if it occurred industry by industry, was answered by Pasinetti with a counterexample, and in 1966 the journal published a symposium on paradoxes in capital theory in which the leading figures stated their final positions. Samuelson's contribution, titled A Summing Up, conceded the point. Reswitching was possible; the simple parable in which a lower rate of profit is reliably associated with a more capital-intensive technique could not be maintained as a general proposition; and he closed with a sentence that has been quoted ever since, remarking that scholars are not born to live an easy existence. The concession was the more remarkable for its source: Samuelson was the most authoritative neoclassical theorist of his generation, the author of the textbook through which the parable had been taught to millions, and he surrendered the logical ground in print, in a journal of his own side.

### What exactly did the summing up concede?

It conceded the logical point: that reswitching and capital reversing are possible in admissible cases, and that the parable tying a lower rate of profit to a more capital-intensive technique, and grounding distribution in the marginal product of aggregate capital, cannot stand as a theory. It did not concede the usefulness of every neoclassical model for every purpose.

Public concession of a logical defeat is rare in any discipline and almost unheard of in economics, a field whose controversies customarily end by attrition, fashion, or the retirement of the combatants rather than by surrender. The episode therefore has an evidentiary weight beyond its content: it certifies that the Sraffian critique, on the narrow ground of the aggregate production function, was not a sectarian Marxist complaint but a result that the strongest available opponent examined and accepted. The historian Geoffrey Harcourt, himself a participant, chronicled the controversy in surveys that remain the standard map of it, and his melancholy conclusion was that the concession changed far less than it should have. The parable continued to be taught, the aggregate production function continued to be estimated, and the textbooks absorbed the controversy, where they mentioned it at all, as a curiosity of the past. Winning the argument and winning the profession turned out to be different events, and the difference between them is one of the standing lessons of the episode.

## What the concession settled, and what it did not

Precision about the concession is a checkpoint of this article, because triumphalist retellings have inflated it into something it was not, and the inflation discredits the genuine result. What was conceded was a proposition in logic: that in multisector models the techniques cannot in general be ordered by capital intensity independently of distribution, and hence that the aggregate production function cannot in general bear the interpretation placed on it. What was not conceded was the whole of neoclassical economics. The Massachusetts side did not abandon equilibrium theory, did not accept Sraffa's alternative as a replacement economics, and did not grant that distribution must be explained by class and history rather than by preferences and endowments in some more carefully specified model. Disaggregated general equilibrium theory, in which capital goods are heterogeneous and each carries its own price, survived the controversy formally intact, at the price of surrendering the aggregate parable and with it any simple story about why the rate of profit is what it is.

Nor did the concession settle the positive standing of Sraffa's own system. A demonstration that the rival's capital concept is incoherent does not by itself validate the classical alternative, and Sraffa's construction had and has its own open questions: it takes the quantities produced as given rather than explaining them, it says nothing about how the economy moves from one position to another, and its treatment of the distributive variable as settled outside the system is, depending on the reader, either its deepest insight or an admission that the theory stops where the interesting questions start. The neo-Ricardians who took up Sraffa's work, Garegnani above all, spent decades developing answers: a theory of output and employment to stand alongside the price core, a reading of Keynes that made effective demand the determinant of quantities as the surplus approach made class the determinant of distribution. That programme belongs to the section on the school below. The controversy's verdict, stated without inflation, is this: on the specific question of whether capital can be measured independently of distribution and its marginal product used to explain the rate of profit, the answer is no, and the other side's champion said so. Keep the claim at that size and it is unanswerable; inflate it and it becomes false.

## The redundancy argument at full strength

Turn the construction toward Marx and a difficulty appears at once. The classical problem Marx inherited, and the problem the third volume of Capital addresses, is the transformation of values into prices of production: commodities exchange, in competitive conditions, not at ratios proportional to the labour embodied in them, but at prices that yield a uniform rate of profit on the capital advanced, and Marx's solution attempted to derive the second set of magnitudes from the first and to show that the derivation conserved the totals, total profit equalling total surplus value and total price equalling total value. The Sraffian construction seems, at a glance, to cut through the entire problem by declining to start where Marx started. If prices, and with them the rate of profit, can be obtained from the physical table of production conditions together with the real wage, then the labour values are computable, if at all, only as a redundant intermediate step, a detour that begins from the same physical data, wanders through a set of magnitudes that nothing observable corresponds to, and returns to prices that were already in hand. That is the redundancy argument, and it deserves to be met at its strongest rather than at its weakest.

### Why do Sraffians say Marx's values are redundant?

Because prices and the profit rate can be derived directly from the physical conditions of production and the division of the product between wages and profits. If that derivation is complete, calculating labour values first adds a step without changing the result.

The argument's strength is its austerity. It does not deny that labour values can be defined; given the physical table and the labour inputs, embodied labour coefficients follow by the same bookkeeping that yields prices. It denies that the definition earns its keep. A scientific theory, on the austere view, is answerable for every magnitude it carries: each must do explanatory work that no cheaper magnitude does, and a magnitude that can be deleted from every derivation without loss is not a discovery but a decoration. Values, the argument continues, fail the audit twice over. They are not needed to determine prices, since prices come from the physical data and distribution directly; and they are not observable independently of prices, since the only route to an embodied labour coefficient runs through the same input table that determines prices without them. Nor, say the argument's proponents, can values retreat into explaining profit, because the rate of profit too emerges from the physical system once the wage is fixed: the size of the surplus is a physical fact before it is a value fact, visible in the excess of outputs over inputs, and the theory that starts from the physical surplus sees everything the value theory sees, with less machinery. The challenge, notice, is not that Marx made an arithmetical mistake. It is that his entire first volume's apparatus, on this showing, answers a question that a leaner theory answers on the way to somewhere else.

## Steedman and the charge of incoherence

The challenge was pressed to its sharpest form by Ian Steedman, whose Marx after Sraffa of 1977 remains the book every subsequent participant must answer. Steedman worked strictly inside the Sraffian framework and drew two conclusions. The first was redundancy, stated without cushioning: the value magnitudes are a redundant detour between the physical data and the price results, and Marx's transformation procedure, which begins from values, therefore starts from quantities that the theory does not need and cannot justify. The second conclusion was stronger and more wounding, because it alleged not superfluity but incoherence. In systems with joint production, where one process yields two outputs together, as wool and mutton come from the same sheep, the bookkeeping that defines labour values can return a negative value for a commodity: a negative quantity of embodied labour, a result without physical sense. Worse, Steedman exhibited cases in which aggregate profit is positive while aggregate surplus value, computed by the same definitions, is negative, so that the theory's central claim, that profit is unpaid labour, appeared to be contradicted by its own arithmetic in admissible cases. Values were not merely unnecessary; under conditions no theory may exclude, they misbehaved.

### How can joint production yield a negative labour value?

When one process produces two commodities together, the labour of the process must be divided between its products by solving the value relations simultaneously, and nothing in that arithmetic guarantees a positive answer for each product. In constructed cases one commodity receives a negative embodied labour, a magnitude without physical meaning, even while prices and profits behave normally.

The joint production result deserves its reputation as the hardest form of the challenge, and the replies to it shaped the subsequent literature. One family of replies redefined value for the joint case: if values are defined not by the accounting identity Steedman used but as the minimum labour required to produce a commodity given the available techniques, an optimising definition associated with Michio Morishima, the pathologies vanish and a version of the fundamental theorem, positive profit if and only if positive surplus value, is restored. The critics of that rescue reply that it saves the theorem by changing the subject, since Marx's values were accounting magnitudes, not solutions to an optimisation problem. A second family questioned the constructed examples themselves, arguing that the techniques in them would not be operated, or that the negative value attaches to a product mix no economy would choose. A third family, the temporal one, changed the frame altogether and is treated in its own section below. What no serious reply attempted was to deny the arithmetic. Within the simultaneous framework, on the definitions Steedman used, his results stand, and the candour with which the better Marxist respondents admitted that fact is part of what makes this debate, unlike most in the field, a credit to its participants.

## The first reply: values answer a different question

The most durable Marxist response begins by granting the redundancy argument its entire formal claim and then disputing what the claim is about. Prices and the rate of profit, the reply concedes, can be derived from the physical data and the wage without passing through values; Steedman's arithmetic is not in dispute. But Marx's value theory, on this response, was never principally a device for deriving prices. Its question comes earlier and is of a different kind: not how the surplus is divided among capitals, nor at what prices commodities exchange, but why there is a surplus for labour to be the source of, why the product of a working day exceeds the cost of reproducing the worker, and what social form that excess takes in a society of commodity producers. The physical table records that outputs exceed inputs. It does not and cannot record what that excess is socially: that the worker sells not labour but labour power, that the working day divides into a part replacing the wage and a part appropriated without equivalent, that profit, interest, and rent are forms of one unpaid residue. Those are propositions about social relations, and the value framework is the machinery Marx built to state them. A thermometer is redundant for a theory of heat flow that already has a calorimeter; it does not follow that temperature is a fiction.

### What question do values answer that prices do not?

Values address the origin and social form of the surplus: why the working day yields more than the reproduction of the worker costs, and how that unpaid excess appears as profit, interest, and rent. Prices address the division of the surplus among competing capitals. The first question survives every demonstration that the second can be answered without values.

The reply gains force from an asymmetry in the Sraffian construction itself. The physical system determines prices once the wage is given, but the wage enters the system as a bundle of goods or as a share, and nothing in the system explains the level at which it settles, nor why the working day is longer than the labour required to produce that bundle. The surplus appears in the Sraffian picture as a physical leftover, and its specifically capitalist character, that it arises from the purchase of labour power at its value and the consumption of that peculiar commodity in production, is simply not represented. Defenders of the framework answer that this is scope, not failure: the theory of prices is one theory, the theory of the wage bargain another, and a division of labour among theories is no scandal. The Marxist rejoinder accepts the division and draws the conclusion: if the Sraffian system is a theory of price determination that deliberately leaves the origin of the surplus and the length of the working day outside itself, then it has not made value theory redundant; it has declined to compete with it on its home ground. Redundancy requires two theories answering one question. Here, the reply maintains, there are two questions, and only the appearance of rivalry arises from the fact that both theories traffic in prices.

Candour requires the concession that completes the reply, and this article's brief demands it be stated plainly. If the question is the determination of relative prices and of the profit rate, the redundancy argument succeeds completely: the derivation from physical data and distribution does the work, and values contribute nothing to it. Marxists who continue to present the transformation as a price-deriving machine that outperforms the Sraffian derivation are defending a position the arithmetic has vacated. What survives the concession is the claim that price determination was the least of what the value theory was for, and that claim must then be argued on the theory's explanatory merits, its account of exploitation, of the wage form, of fetishism, rather than assumed. The debate after Steedman, at its best, is exactly that argument.

## The second reply: the data are social before they are physical

A second family of replies attacks the innocence of the starting point. The Sraffian derivation begins from given physical conditions of production, a table of inputs and outputs and labour requirements treated as data in the strong sense: facts available before theory begins, independent of the social relations the theory studies. The reply disputes the independence. The table that exists to be measured is the sediment of past social decisions: which techniques were developed, which were scrapped, how the working day is divided between intensity and duration, what counts as a skill, how machinery embodies the knowledge of one class and the discipline of another. Even the apparently brute magnitudes carry social content. The quantity of labour required to make a coat depends on the customary pace of work, and the customary pace is an outcome of struggle; the input coefficients of an industry reflect the outcome of investment decisions taken under the pressure of the very distribution the theory then pretends to receive as external. To treat the table as prior to social relations, the reply concludes, is to photograph the end of a social process and call the photograph nature.

The Sraffian rejoinder is not weak, and stating it fairly is part of this reply's duty. Every theory must start somewhere, and a theory of prices is entitled to take technique as given for the purpose in hand, exactly as Marx himself takes the productivity of labour as given in long stretches of Capital when his purpose is the analysis of the working day. The question is not whether abstraction is legitimate but what the abstraction hides, and the rejoinder insists it hides nothing relevant to price determination: whatever history produced the table, the prices consistent with it and with the wage are what they are. The Marxist counter-rejoinder narrows the claim accordingly, and in its narrowed form it is hard to refuse: the physicalist presentation encourages a reading of distribution as technically bounded bargaining over a natural surplus, in which the surplus itself, and the length and intensity of the labour that produces it, drop out of the theory of distribution altogether. A framework in which the working day is a datum cannot see the struggle over the working day, and a theory of capitalism that cannot see that struggle has abstracted from something its subject matter contains. The exchange ends, like several in this debate, not in refutation but in a clearer map of what each framework is built to see and built to miss.

## The third reply: time against simultaneity

The third reply changes the mathematical frame rather than arguing within it, and its full development belongs to the companion article on [the new interpretation and the temporal single-system reading](/2016/05/15/new-interpretation-and-tssi/), to which the reader is referred for the detail. Its core objection can be stated briefly. In the Sraffian system, inputs and outputs are valued simultaneously: the price at which an industry buys its means of production is the price at which those commodities sell as outputs, in one timeless solution. The temporal critics argue that this simultaneity is not a harmless simplification but the source of the challenge's results. In an actual economy, inputs are bought at prices formed yesterday and outputs sold at prices formed tomorrow; value is transferred from inputs to product at the prices actually paid, and profit is computed on the sums actually advanced. Under sequential rather than simultaneous determination, these critics maintain, the alleged pathologies disappear: the totals Marx claimed are conserved, surplus value and profit correspond, and the negative-value constructions cannot be built, because they depend on valuing the same commodity at the same moment in two roles. Whether that rescue succeeds, and at what cost in the meaning of the magnitudes involved, is precisely the dispute the companion article adjudicates; here it is enough to register that the strongest technical answer to Steedman denies the premise his framework shares with Sraffa's, the simultaneity of valuation, and that the debate therefore turns on a methodological choice that no amount of arithmetic within either frame can settle.

### Why do temporal critics reject simultaneous valuation?

Because they hold that production takes time and prices form in sequence: inputs are purchased at prices already established before the outputs they help to make come to market. Valuing inputs and outputs at a single simultaneous price set, they argue, builds the anomalies of the challenge into the definitions, and dissolves once determination is allowed to run through time.

## The fourth reply: distribution is not an origin

The last of the four replies returns to the construction's most candid feature, the degree of freedom it leaves open. The Sraffian system, as its author presented it, determines how a surplus is divided and what prices correspond to each division. It does not determine, and does not pretend to determine, the division itself; still less does it explain why a surplus exists to be divided, why the social product exceeds the cost of reproducing the producers. Profit, in the system, is a share: a magnitude whose size is fixed once the wage is fixed, by the geometry of the frontier. Of its origin the system says nothing, because origin is not a question a table of coefficients can pose. The Marxist reply seizes on the silence. A theory of capitalism, it argues, cannot treat the existence of profit as a premise written into the input table, for the existence of a class that lives by appropriating the surplus, and of a class compelled to produce it, is the phenomenon the theory of value exists to explain. The labour theory states that the surplus is unpaid labour time, that the wage buys labour power and not the labour performed, and that the difference between the two is the single source from which profit, interest, and rent are all carved. That statement may be right or wrong, but it is a statement about the world that the price system neither makes nor replaces, and its truth is untouched by the demonstration that prices can be computed without it. The redundancy argument proves that one can do the accounts of capitalism without asking where profit comes from. The reply holds that a science of capitalism should ask.

### Does the Sraffian system explain where profit comes from?

No, and it does not claim to. Given the conditions and the wage, the system determines the rate of profit as a share of a surplus that the input table already contains. The origin of that surplus, why the product exceeds the cost of reproducing the producers, is a question left to other theories, the opening the fourth reply occupies.

Taken together, the four replies do not restore the position Marx's text occupied before 1960, and this article does not pretend otherwise. The transformation problem, treated in the companion analysis devoted to it in this cluster, remains a genuine problem with its own literature; the wider objections are catalogued in [the survey of criticisms of the labour theory of value](/2010/09/01/labour-theory-of-value-criticisms/). What the replies establish is that the challenge bifurcates. As a claim about price determination it is sound, and conceded. As a claim that value theory is therefore pointless, it depends on premises, about what the data are, about how time enters valuation, and about which questions a theory of capitalism must answer, that are themselves contested positions rather than results. The table that follows fixes the state of play in a form a reader can carry away.

That is the honest summary of half a century of literature, and it sets up the biographical question with which this article concludes its argument: what did the author of the construction think he was doing?

## The papers in the Wren Library

Sraffa died in 1983, leaving behind him one slim book, one monumental edition, a shelf of other men's acknowledgements, and a large body of unpublished papers: drafts, notebooks, reading notes, and correspondence accumulated over six decades and deposited at Trinity College, Cambridge, where they are kept in the Wren Library. During his lifetime the papers were effectively closed; Sraffa admitted a handful of collaborators to parts of them and discouraged curiosity about the rest. After his death his literary executor, Pierangelo Garegnani, himself the leading figure of the neo-Ricardian school, supervised their gradual opening, and in the nineteen nineties researchers gained systematic access. What they found rearranged the biography of the ideas. The manuscripts showed the price construction emerging in the late nineteen twenties, in direct reaction against Marshall, years before the Ricardo commission; they showed the standard system and the standard commodity taking shape across the nineteen forties; and they showed something more consequential for this article's subject, a sustained, detailed, and respectful engagement with the texts of Marx, in notebooks and marginalia, extending across the middle decades of the century.

### What did the opening of the archive change?

It changed the biography behind the book. The papers showed the price system taking shape decades before publication, and showed their author reading Marx closely and continuously while he built it. Readings of the project as a reconstruction of the classical surplus approach, Marx included, rest on that base, though the reading remains contested among scholars of the papers.

The reading the archive has supported is associated with several scholars who have worked through the manuscripts, and its outlines can be stated fairly. On this reading, Sraffa understood his enterprise, from the nineteen twenties onward, as the recovery and rigorous restatement of the approach to value and distribution that the classical economists from Petty to Ricardo had possessed and that Marx had inherited and developed: the approach that begins from the physical surplus and treats distribution as the division of that surplus under social rules. His quarrel, on this showing, was never with that tradition but with the marginal theory that had buried it, and his notebooks on Marx, critical at points as any serious reader's are, are the notebooks of a man working inside a shared inheritance, testing and rebuilding its foundations. Supporting details accumulate: his early manuscripts frame the project in terms of the classical economists and Marx in a single sequence; his annotations engage Marx's reproduction schemes and his treatment of the falling rate of profit as live problems rather than as errors; and his famous reluctance to publish reads, on this account, as the perfectionism of a man who believed he was restoring a tradition's foundations and would not issue the restoration in fragments.

The reading is contested, and this article's checkpoint requires the contest to be stated with the same care. Other scholars of the papers reply that engagement is not allegiance, that Sraffa's notes contain sharp criticisms of Marx's value theory alongside the sympathetic reconstructions, and that a man who wished to declare himself Marx's continuator had sixty years and every opportunity to say so in print and never did. They note, further, that the interpretation of a deliberately reticent man's unpublished drafts is an enterprise peculiarly exposed to the interpreter's wishes, since the drafts can be made to emphasise whatever the reader came looking for, and that the published book, the only text Sraffa authorised, is as available to Steedman's use as to anyone's. Between those poles the scholarly argument continues, conducted in the journals of the history of economic thought and in editions of the manuscripts themselves. What can be said without taking sides is narrower but not nothing: the archive destroyed the simple picture of Sraffa as an external critic of the classical and Marxian tradition, and replaced it with a figure whose relation to Marx was internal, informed, and unresolved in ways his published silence was perhaps designed to preserve.

## The two Sraffas

The namable claim of this article can now be stated in its full form. There are two Sraffas in the literature, and most confusion in the debate comes from trading on one while citing the other. The first is Sraffa the constructor, the author of a formal system: a set of relations among physical quantities, prices, and the distributive variables, published in 1960, public property from the day of publication, as usable by Steedman against Marx as by Garegnani against the marginalists. Formal systems do not carry their authors' intentions as licences; a theorem is true for whoever proves its premises, wherever they wish to point it, and the redundancy argument loses nothing of its force if its ultimate source turns out to have loved the theory it inconveniences. The second is Sraffa the historical actor: Ricardo's editor, Gramsci's friend and lifeline, the silent centre of Cambridge economics for half a century, the reader of Marx whose notebooks complicate every simple story told about him. This Sraffa cannot overturn a single derivation. What he overturns is a framing: the framing in which a technician from outside the Marxist tradition arrived in 1960 to inform its inhabitants that their foundations were unsound.

Replace that framing with the documented one and the neo-Ricardian challenge changes its genre. It becomes an argument among heirs of the classical surplus tradition about which of the inheritance's instruments are load-bearing: whether the labour value apparatus is the tradition's foundation or one of its scaffolding poles, necessary while the building rose and removable after. Arguments among heirs are not gentler than arguments with strangers; they are often bitterer, as the literature of this one attests. But they are judged differently, because the parties share the estate whose division they dispute. The neo-Ricardians who pressed the challenge were, in the main, economists of the left who took themselves to be purifying Marxism of a defective component in order to strengthen its critique of capitalism, and Steedman's book is best read in that spirit, as an internal audit conducted with unusual rigour. Marxists who replied in kind, conceding the arithmetic and contesting the conclusions drawn from it, were participants in the same audit. The two Sraffas, held together, explain how both things are true at once: that the challenge was technically formidable, and that it was never the external execution it is sometimes advertised as, by the challenge's enemies quite as much as by its friends.

## Gramsci's books and the prison account

The friendship that began in the Turin of the factory councils acquired, after 1926, the character of a rescue operation conducted at a distance. Gramsci was arrested by the fascist regime in that year and sentenced by a special tribunal, the prosecutor demanding that the authorities stop his brain from working for twenty years; he spent the rest of his life in prisons and prison clinics, his health collapsing, writing the notebooks that made him the most original Marxist thinker of his generation under conditions of censorship, scarcity, and physical misery. Books were the material of that writing, and books were what the regime could most easily deny him. Sraffa, safe in Cambridge, opened an account in Gramsci's name at a Milan bookshop, so that Gramsci could order what he needed and the bills would be settled from England; through that account, and through the mediation of Gramsci's sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht, who carried messages and manuscripts, flowed a lifeline of print: histories, journals, the works of Croce and of the economists, the materials from which the Prison Notebooks were assembled. The full record of the connection is set out in [the intellectual biography of Antonio Gramsci](/2015/01/01/antonio-gramsci-intellectual-biography/) in this series.

The episode belongs in an article on price theory for a reason beyond sentiment. It locates Sraffa inside the living history of Marxism at its most embattled point, not as a commentator but as a participant whom the leading Italian Marxist trusted with the logistics of his intellectual survival. Gramsci's letters show him discussing, through the permitted channels, questions of economic theory that connect directly to the concerns of this cluster: the notebooks contain an extended engagement with Ricardo and with the classical economists, mediated in part by what Sraffa supplied and by what the two men had discussed in Turin. When Gramsci died in 1937, days after his release, Sraffa was among those who had sustained the conditions of his work to the end. A theorist does not have to be a Marxist to do such a thing; friendship explains it. But the challenge his framework later posed to Marx is ill described as the raid of an outsider when its author's documented life runs through the tradition's own catastrophes, and the archive's portrait of his reading gives that biographical fact a theoretical counterpart.

## The gesture that corrected Wittgenstein

The most famous sentence ever written about Sraffa occurs in someone else's preface. Ludwig Wittgenstein, acknowledging the debts behind the Philosophical Investigations, wrote that he was indebted to Sraffa's criticism for the most consequential ideas of that book, after years of conversations in which Sraffa subjected Wittgenstein's early philosophy to a persistent, anthropological cross-examination. Norman Malcolm's memoir preserves the emblematic scene: Wittgenstein expounding the doctrine that a proposition and what it describes must share a logical form, and Sraffa responding with a Neapolitan gesture of contempt, brushing the fingertips of one hand outward from under his chin, and asking what the logical form of that was. Wittgenstein recalled the moment as the one that broke the spell of his earlier system, the picture theory of the Tractatus, and set him toward the philosophy of language as use, of meaning embedded in practices, that defines his later work.

### What did Wittgenstein owe to Sraffa's criticism?

By Wittgenstein's own account in his preface, the most consequential ideas of his later philosophy. Years of Sraffa's anthropological questioning, crystallised in the famous gesture that demanded the logical form of a Neapolitan sign of contempt, broke the picture theory of meaning and pushed Wittgenstein toward meaning as use within shared practices.

The anecdote earns its place here because it exhibits the same intellectual gesture that Sraffa's economics performs. In both cases he confronts a self-contained formal system, a theory of meaning in one case and a theory of value in the other, with a demand that it account for something recalcitrantly concrete that the system had defined out of existence: a gesture whose meaning every Neapolitan understands and no logical form captures, a quantity of capital that every accountant records and no theory can measure without circularity. The style is consistent across the two careers, the published one and the conversational one: find the point where the elegant construction touches the ground, and press there. It also explains a biographical puzzle, how a man who published so little could be credited by Keynes, Wittgenstein, and Gramsci alike as a decisive influence. His medium was the objection, delivered in person, at the moment a system was being built, and the twentieth century's intellectual history is dotted with systems that changed direction after an afternoon with him.

## The Marxist in the next room

Cambridge Marxism in Sraffa's decades had a public face, and it was not Sraffa's. Maurice Dobb, lecturer at Trinity, author of Political Economy and Capitalism and of the widely read Studies in the Development of Capitalism, was the leading Marxist economist in Britain, a member of the Communist Party, and the man who had first welcomed the young Sraffa into Cambridge economics in the nineteen twenties. It was Dobb who collaborated with Sraffa on the Ricardo edition, Dobb who mediated between the silent Italian and the institutions, and Dobb whose lecture room supplied the generation of British Marxists, historians as much as economists, with their formation. The two men's temperaments were complementary opposites: Dobb prolific, engaged, and doctrinally committed; Sraffa silent, uncommitted in public, and destructive of systems from within. British Marxist economics grew up in the corridor between them.

The corridor matters to the challenge's later history because it defeats the geography of the popular version, in which Sraffa figures as a mandarin of the establishment lobbing a theorem into the Marxist camp from outside. The camp's own British headquarters was down the corridor, its leading economist was Sraffa's collaborator of twenty years, and the Ricardo edition that restored the classical tradition was a joint product of the mandarin and the party member. When the neo-Ricardian challenge emerged in the nineteen seventies, it emerged in a Cambridge where the surplus tradition and Marxist economics had been institutionally entangled for two generations, through personnel as much as through doctrine. None of this softens the challenge's content. It does make its usual description, as an external assault, unsustainable as history, which is precisely the correction this article exists to make.

## The school that followed

A construction as austere as Sraffa's was bound to attract builders. The economists who took up the framework in the decades after 1960, Garegnani in Rome, Luigi Pasinetti in Cambridge and Milan, John Eatwell and Murray Milgate among others, became known as the neo-Ricardians, a name that itself encodes the article's thesis: what they took themselves to be reviving was Ricardo's standpoint, the classical surplus approach, with Sraffa's rigour substituted for Ricardo's unfinished value theory. The school's programme had two storeys. The ground floor was Sraffa's own: the determination of prices and the profit rate from the physical conditions and one distributive variable. The upper storey, which Sraffa left unbuilt, was a theory of quantities: of what determines how much is produced and how many are employed, and here the school reached for Keynes, whose theory of effective demand Garegnani argued could be detached from the marginalist scaffolding Keynes himself had left standing and re-founded on classical ground. The synthesis proposed a complete alternative architecture: distribution settled by social and institutional forces, output and employment by effective demand, prices by the conditions of production, and the marginal theory retired from every floor of the building.

### Why do the school's conclusions travel separately from its method?

Because the method is only a construction, while the conclusions are further claims that particular economists built upon it. Garegnani never drew Steedman's redundancy verdict from the identical machinery, and nothing in the construction compels it. Accepting the machinery while refusing a conclusion drawn from it is a coherent position, not an evasion.

The distinction in that answer matters for reading the whole literature, because the school's name is often used as if method and conclusions were one package. They are not. Steedman's redundancy verdict is a conclusion drawn from the method by a further premise, that a theory of capitalism needs no account of the surplus's origin beyond its physical existence; Garegnani, working with the identical method, never drew that conclusion and spent his career on the other storey of the building. The method itself compels remarkably little beyond its theorems: the determination result, the frontier, the standard construction, and the impossibility results about aggregate capital. Everything else, including the challenge to Marx, is argument erected on the method by identifiable people for identifiable reasons, and it can be dismantled storey by storey without touching the foundations. Readers who keep the separation in view will find the debate's literature suddenly legible: half the polemics consist of one side defending the method against attacks aimed at a conclusion, and the other side attacking a conclusion as if it were the method.

The school's institutional history also explains the controversy's strange afterlife. The neo-Ricardians won the argument of the nineteen sixties in the journals and lost the profession in the seminar rooms: the centres of the approach, Cambridge in England and a scatter of Italian universities, contracted as the mainstream consolidated around forms of theory, first the disaggregated equilibrium models and then the representative-agent constructions, in which the questions Sraffa had made unanswerable were not so much answered as ruled out of the syllabus. Pasinetti's work on structural dynamics and Garegnani's on the reconstruction of classical theory continued to appear, the journal literature of the surplus approach persists, and the framework remains the standard of rigour against which theories of distribution are quietly checked. But a student can now complete an elite education in economics without meeting reswitching, a state of affairs the teaching section below examines.

## A miniature economy, worked in words

Abstractions in this territory repay one concrete exercise, and the reader who works a miniature system by hand will own the rest of the debate. Imagine an economy with two industries and one kind of labour. The corn industry uses seed corn and labour to produce corn; the iron industry uses corn, to feed its workers' horses and its furnaces' keepers in an older idiom, and iron tools, and labour, to produce iron. Suppose the year's accounts are these. The corn farms sow a quantity of corn and harvest a larger quantity; the iron works consume corn and wear out iron and produce a larger quantity of iron. After every used-up input has been replaced, a surplus remains: corn and iron left over, available to be consumed by the non-producing classes, accumulated, or divided as the social settlement dictates. That leftover is the surplus in its physical nakedness, visible before any price is named, and the classical economists' starting point was the observation that this leftover, and not any contract or market, is what distribution divides.

Now introduce the wage. The workers must be fed and housed during the production period, and their consumption is a definite bundle: so much corn, so much iron embodied in tools and shelter and fuel. Pay that bundle, and what remains of the surplus belongs to the owners of the means of production as profit. The rate of profit is the ratio between that remainder, valued consistently, and the value of the capital advanced, and here the miniature shows its teeth: to value the remainder and the advance, prices are needed, and the prices themselves depend on the rate of profit they are being used to compute. The circle is not vicious but simultaneous, and Sraffa's construction is precisely the demonstration that the circle closes: there exist prices at which every industry recovers its costs, pays the given wage, and earns the uniform rate on its advances, and those prices and that rate are fixed together by the physical table and the wage. Change the wage bundle, and the whole solution shifts: the rate of profit falls, the relative price of the more capital-intensive good moves, and nothing in the physical table has altered. Change the technique, give the iron works a better furnace, and the surplus grows and the frontier moves outward. The miniature also shows what the system does not contain. Nothing in it says why the wage bundle is what it is, why the working day lasts as long as it does, or why the surplus takes the social form of profit rather than of a lord's due or a temple's tithe. Those silences are not defects the construction hides; Sraffa marked them himself by leaving the distributive variable open. They are the spaces into which the Marxist replies insert themselves, and the spaces in which, on this article's assessment, the value theory continues to work.

## Fixed capital, joint production, and the second half of the book

The first half of Sraffa's book wins its results on simplified ground: one product per process, no durable instruments, no land. The second half takes up the complications, and its solutions shaped the later debate as much as the core results did, because two of the three great complications became battlegrounds. Fixed capital is handled by an elegant transformation: a machine that lasts ten years is treated, in each year of its life, as a joint product of the process that uses it. The process that begins the year with a new machine ends it with a one-year-old machine alongside its main product; the ageing machine is priced as an output, year by year, its value declining according to a schedule that the price system itself determines, so that durability, depreciation, and obsolescence all become internal to the construction rather than assumptions pasted onto it. Joint production receives the same treatment in general form: processes yielding several outputs together, as the sheep yields wool and mutton, are handled by requiring enough processes to disentangle the outputs' prices, and it was exactly here, in the disentangling arithmetic, that Steedman later located the negative values of his challenge. Land, finally, is treated through the classical theory of rent: lands of differing fertility are worked side by side, the price of the product is set by the cost on the marginal land that pays no rent, and rent appears on the better lands as a share of the surplus, a distribution result rather than a price-determining one, in strict fidelity to Ricardo.

The treatment of these complications matters for a fair hearing of the challenge, because a recurrent move in the debate is to present the Sraffian framework as a toy, a model of circulating capital and single products whose results evaporate on contact with the real economy's durable instruments and joint processes. The book's second half forecloses that dismissal: its author faced the complications himself, in print, in 1960, and the challenge's strongest results, including Steedman's, were derived inside the general construction rather than against its simplifications. What the second half did not and could not settle is the framework's standing silences: the quantities produced are still given, accumulation and technical change are still outside, and money is absent from first page to last. The neo-Ricardian programme treated those silences as a research agenda. The sterner Marxist critics treated them as evidence that the framework, whatever its rigour, describes the surface of a capitalist economy, its prices at a moment, rather than its motion. Both judgments can be true of the same book, and in this article's assessment both are.

## The challenge's most constructive heir

One response to the redundancy argument deserves separate notice because it accepted the argument's terms and rebuilt on the cleared ground. John Roemer, an economist working in the current that became analytical Marxism, used models of exactly the linear, physical-data kind that Steedman had used, and asked what remained of exploitation once labour values were set aside. His answer, developed in the early nineteen eighties, was that almost everything remained: exploitation could be defined and demonstrated through property relations rather than through embodied labour, by asking whether a group would be better off withdrawing from the economy with its per capita share of society's assets. On that definition, capitalist exploitation appears as a consequence of the unequal distribution of productive property, the labour market becomes one mechanism among several by which the propertyless are disadvantaged, and the normative charge at the heart of Marx's theory survives translation into a framework with no labour values in it. The full development of that current belongs to the article on [analytical marxism and its reconstruction of Marx's claims](/2020/01/01/analytical-marxism-complete-guide/).

Roemer's construction cuts both ways in the present debate, and both cuts are instructive. For the challengers, it is evidence that the redundancy argument was fruitful rather than merely destructive: surrendering the value detour did not end Marxist economics but forced it to state its central normative claim in terms that any economist could audit, and a generation of rigorous work followed. For the defenders of value theory, the same construction is evidence of what the surrender costs: an exploitation defined by counterfactual asset shares is a theorem about distribution, silent about production, about the working day, about the peculiar commodity labour power and the hidden abode where it is consumed. The argument replays, inside analytical Marxism, the same bifurcation that the challenge and replies table records at the level of the whole debate: what can be derived without values is considerable and grows more considerable the cleverer the derivation, and the question that values were built to answer keeps reappearing at the edge of the cleared ground, waiting for whoever cleared it to notice that it was never touched.

## What fills the open variable

A system that determines everything except the division of its own surplus invites an obvious question, and the history of the surplus approach is the history of answers to it. The classical economists filled the gap with subsistence: the wage gravitated toward the worker's subsistence, conventionally understood and historically variable, and profit took the remainder. Marx filled it with the value of labour power, itself a historically and morally conditioned magnitude, together with the reserve army of the unemployed, whose size disciplined the wage bargain from outside the market's visible exchanges. The neo-Ricardians, Garegnani most systematically, proposed that the distributive variable be treated as settled in what he called the core of the theory: by the balance of class forces, by institutions, by the state's posture, by custom and by conflict, all investigated historically rather than deduced from preferences. On every one of these fillings, distribution is a social and political fact before it is an economic one, and the price system's job is to work out the consequences of the settlement, not to pretend the settlement away.

The contrast with the marginal filling is the substantive heart of the whole dispute, deeper than any theorem. In the marginal account, wages and the profit rate figure as prices like others, formed in markets for factors, and the theory of distribution is a branch of the theory of price. In the surplus account, the prices cannot be computed until the settlement is known, and the settlement is formed in history: in enclosures and poor laws, in trade union law and its repeal, in the franchise, in wars and inflations and the memories they leave. Each account accuses the other of leaving the decisive thing unexplained. The marginalist says the surplus approach has a hole where its theory of distribution should be, a hole stuffed with sociology. The surplus theorist replies that the marginal hole is worse, being stuffed with a magnitude, aggregate capital, that cannot even be measured, and that a theory which names its open variable honestly is methodologically ahead of one that closes the system with a fiction. Readers of this series will recognise the shape of the quarrel from other clusters: it is the standing dispute between a theory that prices everything and explains the social order as an outcome, and a theory that treats the social order as the premise and prices as its arithmetic. Sraffa's book is the most rigorous brief ever written for the second party, which is why its author, whatever his own affiliations, could not be a stranger to the tradition this series chronicles.

The open variable also explains the framework's otherwise puzzling fertility outside its home debates. Development economists working in the surplus tradition, Krishna Bharadwaj most prominently in India, found in Sraffa's standpoint a way to analyse economies in which markets for labour and land were visibly constituted by caste, custom, and coercion rather than by the anonymous exchanges of the textbook: if distribution is settled socially in Cambridge's model too, then the Indian village and the industrial economy differ in their settlements, not in their logic. Structuralist economists in Latin America, and the theorists of unequal exchange treated elsewhere in this cluster, worked adjacent ground. A framework that begins by admitting that its central magnitudes are socially set travels better across social formations than one that must find factor markets everywhere it looks.

## The formation of the method

The archive's most consequential revelation concerned dates. The standard story, drawn from the publication record, made Sraffa's construction a work of the nineteen fifties, a late fruit of the Ricardo years. The manuscripts redrew the timeline: the turn to the classical standpoint belongs to the late nineteen twenties, within a year or two of his arrival in Cambridge, when the young lecturer, fresh from his assault on Marshall's supply apparatus, began filling notebooks with price relations built from physical quantities and with the question of what an invariable measure would require. The Marshall articles and the Sraffian system are thus not two careers but one: the critique of 1925 and 1926 had shown that the orthodox theory of the firm and the industry could not be stated consistently, and the notebooks record the search for the standpoint from which a consistent statement was possible. He found it where Ricardo had stood, before the marginal revolution redrew the map: output given, technique given, the surplus visible, distribution open.

Between the first notebooks and the book lie the famous episodes of Cambridge's golden age, and the papers show Sraffa at the centre of more of them than the published record suggests. He was of the circus that interrogated Keynes's Treatise on Money in 1930 and 1931, the discussions out of which the General Theory's machinery of saving and investment emerged, and Keynes's acknowledgement of him in that book's chapter on own rates of interest marks a debt the manuscripts particularise: Sraffa's insistence that every commodity has its own rate of interest in a monetary economy, deployed first against Hayek, became a load-bearing element of Keynes's restatement. The 1932 review of Hayek, the one public demolition of those years, can now be read in its draft stages, and it shows the method of the later book already formed: grant the opponent his premises, follow his construction with more care than he did, and locate the exact joint at which it requires the world to be other than it is. Hayek, to his credit, conceded ground in reply, and the exchange helped clear the field for Keynes. Sraffa then withdrew from print for a generation, and the withdrawal itself became part of the legend: the Ricardo commission absorbed the nineteen thirties and forties, the price manuscripts matured in drawers, and colleagues learned to treat a conversation with him as a publication event, since ideas he gave away in talk reappeared, acknowledged or not, in other men's books.

Understanding the formation matters for the challenge because it fixes what the book's silences mean. A system built in the nineteen twenties, by a man formed in the Turin of the factory councils and the Cambridge of the Keynesian revolution, was not built as an anti-Marxist instrument and cannot be read as one without anachronism. Its target, from the first notebook to the final preface, was the marginal theory of distribution and value, the orthodoxy of Sraffa's teachers. Marx enters the manuscripts as a member of the classical family whose foundations were being rebuilt, sometimes as the family's most rigorous son, sometimes as the source of specific constructions to be tested and occasionally corrected. That is the intellectual biography the archive restored, and any account of the neo-Ricardian challenge that omits it mistakes a family dispute, conducted two generations later by other hands, for the intention of the man who built the house.

## How to read a book that took thirty years

New readers regularly report the same experience: the book is short, the sentences are plain, and yet the whole resists summary, because Sraffa writes results and omits the reasoning that would make them intuitive, trusting the construction to teach its own standpoint. The commentaries exist because the trust was misplaced for most readers. Heinz Kurz and Nergis Salvadori's long study of the theory of production in the long-period tradition works through the construction chapter by chapter and remains the standard technical companion; Garegnani's essays supply the interpretation from inside the school; and the surveys of the capital controversy by Harcourt give the polemical context the book itself refuses to provide. The order in which the parts of this article were arranged is itself a reading plan: the physical construction first, the distributive frontier and the standard second, the switching results third, and only then the controversies and the challenge, since every famous misunderstanding of the book comes from meeting its polemical uses before its construction.

Two reading disciplines repay insistence. The first is to keep the given and the determined apart at every step: nearly every error in the secondary literature, friendly and hostile, comes from quietly treating as determined something the system takes as given, the quantities, the technique, or one distributive variable, and then marveling at a conclusion the smuggled premise produced. The second is to resist translating Sraffa's results back into the vocabulary they displaced. The rate of profit in his system is not the marginal product of anything, the wage is not the price of a factor in the marginal sense, and the standard commodity is not money; readers who keep reaching for the old dictionary will finish the book believing it says the opposite of what it says. The disciplines sound austere, and they are, but the reward is proportionate: a reader who finishes the ninety pages with the construction intact possesses a standpoint from which both the mainstream theory of distribution and the Marxian theory of value look different, and permanently so. Few books of any length can promise as much, and the promise is the real explanation of the influence that the next section finds so strangely distributed.

## Why the result is so little taught

A question recurs wherever this material is discussed outside the profession, and it deserves a direct answer: if the concession of the nineteen sixties was real, why is the episode absent from the education of economists, so that most graduates have never heard of reswitching and many have never heard Sraffa's name? Several causes compound. The parable the controversy damaged was never the whole of the mainstream theory, and the theory's custodians could honestly judge that its research frontier, built on disaggregated and dynamic models, had no need of the discredited aggregate; a profession is not obliged to teach its discarded scaffolding. The victorious critics, for their part, failed to supply the one thing that displaces a textbook: a complete alternative textbook, with a theory of output, employment, money, and growth rendered teachable at the undergraduate level, and the neo-Ricardian programme's upper storeys remained research literature rather than curriculum. Fashion did the rest. The decades after the controversy rewarded formalised microeconomics and then formalised macroeconomics of representative agents, idioms in which the classical questions about distribution and class were not refuted but simply unasked, and a result that answers an unasked question has no institutional home.

Marxist economics education absorbed the episode differently, and the difference explains the temperature of the literature this article has surveyed. Within the Marxist tradition the challenge could not be treated as a curiosity, because it aimed at the tradition's own foundations and came armed with the tradition's own classical ancestry. It was therefore taught, fought, and absorbed: the transformation literature reorganised itself around the simultaneity question, the temporal and new interpretations built their programmes in direct response, and a generation of Marxist economists learned Sraffa's construction as an obligatory passage, the way theologians learn the strongest form of the argument against their own position. The two Cambridges' quarrel is forgotten by the side that conceded it and remembered by the side it wounded, which is perhaps the general law of such episodes. For the purposes of the wider comparison between the two great research programmes, the reader should proceed to [the head-to-head survey of Marxist and mainstream economics](/2016/07/15/marxist-vs-mainstream-economics/), where the capital controversies figure as the mainstream programme's most instructive internal wound.

## The editor's Ricardo and its own controversy

The introduction to the first volume of the Ricardo edition made a claim about its author that was itself a piece of theory, and it attracted the same kind of fight as the book of 1960. Sraffa's Ricardo of 1815, the Ricardo of the Essay on Profits, determined the rate of profit in agriculture by a ratio of corn to corn: the corn harvested against the corn advanced for seed and for the subsistence of the labourers, a determination in physical terms that required no theory of value at all, since numerator and denominator were the same commodity. Only later, on Sraffa's telling, did Ricardo burden himself with the search for an invariable measure, as he tried to generalise the corn ratio to an economy in which capital and product are different bundles. The reading electrified classical scholarship and founded the modern understanding of Ricardo as a surplus theorist. It also drew dissent that has never entirely closed: Samuel Hollander and others argued that the corn-ratio Ricardo was an editor's construction, that Ricardo's early reasoning already ran in value terms, and that Sraffa had read his own system backwards into his author.

The parallel with the archive debate over Sraffa and Marx is exact, and instructive in both directions. In both cases a reticent editor-theorist leaves a body of work from which later scholars reconstruct a lineage; in both cases the reconstruction is powerful, documented, and contested; and in both cases the contest is partly about evidence and partly about what the lineage is for. Sraffa's Ricardo served the revival of the surplus approach by giving it a pedigree at the very fountainhead of classical economics. The counter-readings served the older picture of Ricardo as a forerunner of marginalism, from whom the surplus approach is a deviation. Nothing in this article's argument depends on the 1815 question being settled in Sraffa's favour, and the checkpoint discipline applied to the Marx archive applies here too: the readings are serious, the documents are real, and the verdicts of specialists remain divided. What the episode establishes beyond dispute is that Sraffa's editorial work was theory conducted by other means, and that his contemporaries in the profession understood this at the time: the introduction was reviewed as an event in economic theory, not in bibliography.

## Input and output: the Leontief kinship

Readers who meet Sraffa's table of production conditions often sense a kinship with Wassily Leontief's input-output analysis, the interindustry tables that national statistical offices now compile as a matter of routine, and the kinship is real but easily misdrawn. Both constructions begin from the same thought: an economy is a circular flow in which industries supply one another, and the whole can be represented as a table of deliveries. The difference lies in what each construction is for. Leontief's is a theory of quantities: given the final demands to be met, it computes the gross outputs each industry must produce so that every delivery, intermediate and final, is covered. Sraffa's is a theory of prices and distribution: given the quantities produced and one distributive variable, it computes the prices at which the system reproduces itself and the remaining distributive variable that corresponds. The two tables are mirror images, each holding constant what the other solves for, and their complementarity is one of the quiet beauties of twentieth-century economics: a complete account of an economy's reproduction needs both, and neither author pretended his half was the whole.

The kinship matters to the challenge for a defensive reason. A recurring rhetorical move against the Sraffian framework treats its physical table as a scholastic fantasy, a blackboard economy of corn and iron with no relation to empirical economics. The input-output comparison answers the move: tables of exactly the required kind are compiled annually by the statistical services of every advanced economy, Leontief received the Nobel memorial prize for the construction, and the magnitudes Sraffa's theory consumes are among the best-measured in all of economics. Dispute the theory's questions if one will; its data are the national accounts. The genuinely unmeasurable magnitude in the controversies was never Sraffa's. It was the aggregate capital stock of his opponents, which no statistical office has ever succeeded in valuing without assuming the rate of profit it was meant to explain.

## The British Marxist reception

The challenge arrived in British Marxism through a door its target had built, and the first responses set the pattern of concession and counterattack that the literature has followed since. Dobb himself, in his last book of 1973, treated Sraffa's system as a vindication of the classical and Marxian approach to distribution against the marginal theory, emphasised that the determination of the wage from outside the price system was Marx's own procedure, and declined to see the framework as any threat to value theory, whose questions he took to lie elsewhere. The younger generation was less serene. The Conference of Socialist Economists and its journal became the arena in which Steedman's argument, published in book form in 1977, met its most systematic opposition, and Ben Fine and Laurence Harris, in Rereading Capital of 1979, delivered the fullest statement of the hostile reading: the neo-Ricardian framework, they argued, was not Marx purified but Marx abandoned, a return to Ricardo's standpoint that discarded precisely what distinguished Marx from Ricardo, the analysis of value as a social form specific to capitalism, of the capital relation as a relation between classes constituted in production, and of fetishism as the necessary appearance of that relation. On their account the physicalist framework could describe an economy of any epoch that produced a surplus, slave, feudal, or capitalist, and a theory that fits every surplus economy equally explains the capitalist one hardly at all.

That criticism, the charge of epoch-blindness, is the deepest of the Marxist objections and the one least dependent on disputed arithmetic, and it completes the map of replies that the challenge and replies table summarised. The Sraffian answer is available and was given: generality is a virtue in a price theory, the Roman economy had prices too, and a theory of the specifically capitalist forms must be added to the price core rather than confused with it, exactly as the neo-Ricardian programme proposed to add Keynes to Sraffa. The Fine and Harris rejoinder denies the separability: the forms are not an addition to the economics but its content, and a price theory built in abstraction from them has abstracted from capitalism itself. The dispute at this depth is no longer about Sraffa at all; it is about what Marx's critique of political economy is, whether it is a theory of prices with a sociology attached or a critique of the categories of price, wage, and profit as forms of a specific social order. Readers will meet the same fork in the cluster's other debates, and it is the honest terminus of several of them: beyond a certain depth, the parties are not disagreeing about results but about the identity of the science they practise.

## The objection from the value form

A further Marxist objection, developed by the current known as value-form theory, aims at both parties to the redundancy dispute and is worth recording because it rearranges the whole field. On this reading, descending from the new reading of Marx associated with Hans-Georg Backhaus and developed by scholars such as Geert Reuten and Michael Williams, the labour theory of value in Marx is not a theory of embodied labour coefficients at all. Value, for Marx, is a monetary social form: labour counts as value only when its products are validated in exchange, and any reading of Capital that treats values as physical quantities of congealed labour, computable from an input table before prices are formed, has already mistaken a critique of political economy for a Ricardian accounting system. From that standpoint, Steedman's refutation refutes the Ricardianised Marx of the textbooks, and the Sraffian framework and its Marxist target share the same physicalist misunderstanding: both suppose that the economy's secret is written in its material flows, when Marx's claim was that the secret is written in the social form those flows are compelled to wear. The redundancy argument then dissolves from the other side, since the values it finds redundant were never the magnitudes Marx's theory was about.

This objection also has its price, and stating it preserves the article's symmetry. A purely monetary and form-analytic reading of value must still explain, somewhere, the connection between the working day and the surplus that every party to the debate agrees exists; and critics of the value-form current charge it with making Marx's theory immune to the kind of test Steedman applied, at the cost of withdrawing it from economics into philosophy. Where the matter rests is instructive for the whole cluster: the Sraffian challenge did not merely damage one interpretation of Marx. It forced every interpretation to declare itself, to say whether value is an accounting magnitude, an origin story, a social form, or a norm of distribution, and the contemporary landscape of Marxist economics, with its temporal, monetary, and form-analytic schools, is the map of those declarations. A ninety-page book that can reorganise the self-understanding of the tradition it challenged has earned the attention this article has given it, whatever verdict the reader finally returns on its challenge.

## Neighbours, not twins: the transformation problem

Because the Sraffian challenge and the transformation problem are so often mentioned in one breath, their exact relation deserves a section of its own, if only to keep the two apart. The transformation problem is internal to Marx's text: granted Marx's value magnitudes, can his procedure for deriving prices of production from them be made consistent, and do the two aggregate equalities he claimed, of total price with total value and total profit with total surplus value, survive the derivation? That problem, its history from Bortkiewicz onward, and its proposed solutions are the subject of [the dedicated treatment of the transformation problem and its solutions](/2016/04/15/transformation-problem-explained/) in this cluster, and they are not re-argued here. The Sraffian challenge stands one level higher. It does not ask whether the transformation can be repaired; it asks why one would start from values at all, when the destination magnitudes are reachable without them. A perfect solution to the transformation problem would therefore leave the challenge untouched, and a complete vindication of the challenge would make the transformation problem moot rather than solved: the two disputes share a cast of magnitudes and a century of literature, but they are different disputes, and half the confusion in the secondary writing comes from scoring points in one as if they counted in the other.

The distinction also orders the replies. The temporal and new interpretations answer both disputes at once, by redefining the determination so that Marx's equalities hold and the Sraffian derivation's simultaneity is refused; that is their ambition and their vulnerability, since a frame rejected at the root must be rebuilt entire. The different-question reply answers only the challenge, and leaves the transformation problem to its own literature, which is why its proponents can afford their notable calm about Steedman: nothing in the reply depends on the transformation succeeding. The reader who keeps the map in view can place every contribution to this literature in its proper dispute within a paragraph of opening it, a skill worth more than any single verdict the literature contains.

## The long period and the method of the classicals

A methodological commitment runs beneath the whole construction and explains several of its features that puzzle readers trained in modern idioms. Sraffa's prices are long-period prices: they are the prices that correspond to a uniform rate of profit on the supply prices of the means of production, the centres of gravitation around which market prices oscillate as capital moves between industries in pursuit of higher returns. The standpoint is the classical economists' own, and Adam Smith's distinction between market price and natural price is its ancestor. It is not an equilibrium in the modern textbook sense, a state in which every agent's plans are simultaneously fulfilled given preferences and endowments, because preferences and endowments play no part in it; it is a description of the systematic forces at work in a competitive economy once the noise of accidental circumstance is stripped away, and its data, technique and one distributive variable, are exactly the magnitudes that can plausibly be treated as persistent while market prices fluctuate.

The commitment drew a characteristic criticism: that a method which holds quantities and technique fixed while solving for prices describes a photograph, not a film, and that capitalism is essentially a film, a process of accumulation and technical revolution in which each position arrived at is obsolete before the ink dries. The neo-Ricardian defence, Garegnani's above all, distinguishes the method's levels: long-period analysis isolates the persistent forces, distribution and the prices corresponding to it, precisely so that the changing magnitudes, accumulation, innovation, the cycle, can be studied as movements of and around those centres rather than as an undifferentiated flux. The same defence is available, and was used, for Marx, whose prices of production are long-period magnitudes in exactly this sense and whose analysis of accumulation proceeds by comparing positions rather than by surrendering the positions. Whether the defence succeeds is again a methodological judgment on which reasonable practitioners differ. What it establishes is that the stillness of Sraffa's system is a chosen analytical device with a classical pedigree and a stated purpose, not a failure to notice that economies move.

## Technical change, the given that moves

The sharpest version of the photograph objection concerns technique itself. The construction takes the methods of production as data, yet the most conspicuous fact about capitalist production is that its methods are revolutionised continuously, and a theory that must freeze its central datum to begin seems to exclude the phenomenon that most needs theorising. The school's considered answer came from Pasinetti, whose work on structural dynamics built an economics of technical change on Sraffian foundations by treating change as a succession of positions to be compared: as productivity grows at different rates in different sectors, the structure of prices, employment, and demand must shift continuously to keep the system reproducible, and the theory's task is to trace those required shifts and the institutional failures, unemployment above all, that occur when actual economies fail to make them. On this development the frozen datum is thawed sector by sector, and the framework yields a theory of structural unemployment as a failure of composition rather than of wage flexibility, a result with obvious resonance for the Marxist side of this series.

The exchange over technical change is a fair specimen of how the framework has grown: not by its author's hand, for Sraffa added nothing to the book in the twenty three years he survived it, but by the labour of a school that treated the construction as foundations rather than as scripture. That pattern, a master's silence and a school's industry, has precedents in the tradition this series chronicles, and it complicates any attempt to fix the meaning of Sraffian economics by appeal to its founder's intentions. The intentions matter to the biographical question, and the archive speaks to them. The theory's meaning is what its theorems and its developed applications make of it, and those now exceed anything the ninety pages contain, in directions their author neither blessed nor forbade in print.

## The Italian reception

In Italy the book landed in a culture prepared for it, and the Italian debate gave the challenge a form distinct from the British one. Italian Marxism possessed, through Gramsci, a native classical lineage; Italian economics possessed, through the Turin school in which Sraffa himself had been formed, a living memory of the classical tradition; and the Italian Communist Party sustained an intellectual culture in which questions of economic theory carried political weight. Claudio Napoleoni, the most philosophically minded of the Italian Marxist economists, engaged Sraffa's construction within the decade, in a study that moved from Smith through Ricardo to Marx and treated the Sraffian restatement as the event that forced Italian Marxism to re-examine its own theoretical foundations. The Italian discussion that followed was correspondingly double: the framework was received simultaneously as a resource, the rigorous form of the classical standpoint that Gramsci's tradition claimed as its own, and as a provocation, since its austere physicalism sat uneasily beside the Hegelian and historicist idioms in which Italian Marxism preferred to speak.

The double reception is worth dwelling on because it generalises. Every national Marxism that met the Sraffian challenge met it in its own accent: the British fought over the heritage of Dobb and the future of socialist economics, the Italians over the classical lineage and the party's culture, the Americans, when the debate reached them, largely through the analytical reconstruction described above. The theorems travelled unchanged; the meanings were local. That is what one should expect when a formal system enters traditions that are also political cultures, and it is a further reason to keep the two Sraffas distinct: the constructor's results are international and identical everywhere, while the historical Sraffa, Gramsci's friend and Ricardo's editor, meant different things in Rome, in London, and in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and the challenge's career in each place cannot be understood from the theorems alone.

## The post-Keynesian adoption

The framework's other great adoption was by the post-Keynesian school, the current descending from Keynes's more radical followers, Joan Robinson and Nicholas Kaldor chief among them, which had always insisted that distribution is settled by forces the marginal theory misdescribes: by the investment decisions of firms, the markups of oligopolistic industry, and the monetary and financial institutions within which accumulation proceeds. Kaldor had proposed, in the middle nineteen fifties, an alternative theory of distribution in which the shares of wages and profits adjust to keep saving equal to investment, a Keynesian mechanism that needed no marginal products; Robinson's accumulation models worked with a profit rate determined by investment and the saving habits of the classes. Sraffa's construction gave that current something it had lacked, a rigorous price theory compatible with its distribution theory, and the Cambridge Journal of Economics, founded in 1977 as the current's house journal, made the surplus approach one of its foundations. The alliance was natural and consequential: the post-Keynesians supplied the theory of quantities and money that the Sraffian core lacked, the Sraffians supplied the theory of prices and the critique of capital that post-Keynesianism needed, and the joint programme remains the most fully articulated alternative to the mainstream in modern economics. Its bearing on the Marxist debates of this cluster is indirect but real, for it demonstrates that the Sraffian construction's silences, on money, on demand, on accumulation, were filled by living research programmes rather than left as fatal gaps, and that the framework's fertility, like the fertility of Marx's own unfinished system, is a property of the questions it leaves open as much as of the results it closes.

## Why the name neo-Ricardian is exact

The school's label is a thesis about the history of economics, and unpacking it clarifies what the whole dispute is over. Between Ricardo's death and the nineteen sixties, the discipline's centre of gravity moved. The classical economists, from Smith to John Stuart Mill, had organised their science around the production and distribution of a surplus among three great classes, landlords, capitalists, and workers, and their central question was how the division of the product among those classes was determined and how it changed as accumulation proceeded. The marginal revolution of the eighteen seventies replaced that question with another: how scarce resources are allocated among competing ends by rational agents, a question in which classes disappear, distribution becomes the pricing of factor services owned by individuals, and the surplus, as a category, dissolves. For a century the older question survived mainly in the Marxist tradition, which had inherited it from Ricardo through Marx and was routinely dismissed by the mainstream as a museum piece for exactly that reason.

Sraffa's edition of Ricardo, and then his book, demonstrated that the older question had never been answered by the newer science, only abandoned by it, and that the classical approach could be restated with a rigour the marginal theory could not match on its own ground. The name neo-Ricardian announces the restoration: not a new theory but an old one rebuilt, Ricardo's standpoint with Ricardo's confusions removed. The label is also a provocation aimed at both camps of this article. To the mainstream it says that the discipline's founding question was mislaid, not solved. To the Marxists it says that the classical inheritance is larger than Marx, that Ricardo's chair stands empty at the head of the table, and that any Marxist economics which cannot say what it owes to Ricardo and what it adds to him does not understand its own descent. Marx himself, who called Ricardo the economist of production par excellence and built the Theories of Surplus Value as a critical history of exactly this lineage, would have recognised the provocation's terms instantly. The neo-Ricardian challenge is unintelligible except as a move within that lineage, which is the deepest sense in which it is an argument among heirs.

## The wage, twice treated

A technical subtlety of the book rewards attention because it conceals a theoretical decision with Marxist resonances. In the classical treatment that Sraffa inherited, the wage belongs among the physical data: the workers' subsistence is a definite bundle of commodities, advanced at the start of the production period like the seed and the fodder, and consumed in keeping the labour force at its work. Wages, on that treatment, are part of the capital advanced, and the surplus is what remains after every advance, subsistence included, has been replaced. Sraffa begins there, in his opening chapters, and then performs a quiet relocation: the wage is taken out of the data and restated as a share of the net product, a proportion of the surplus itself, to be determined by the same social settlement that fixes the rate of profit at the frontier's other end. The relocation is what makes the straight-line frontier possible, and Sraffa was explicit about its significance: the wage had ceased, in the economics of developed capitalism, to be a mere subsistence advance and had become a participation in the surplus, so that workers appear in the accounts not only as costs but as claimants.

The resonance for Marxist readers is direct. Marx's variable capital is the classical subsistence wage in a developed form, the value of labour power, and the whole of his theory of exploitation turns on the distinction between that magnitude and the value the worker's labour creates. Sraffa's two treatments map, loosely but recognisably, onto the two poles of Marx's own account: the wage as the cost of reproducing labour power, and the wage as the outcome of a class settlement over the working day's product. That the Sraffian framework can state both treatments, and can show exactly what each implies for the price system, is a further instance of the pattern this article has traced throughout: the construction is not Marx's theory, and does not do Marx's work, but it is built on the same classical ground, and its results keep casting light, from an unexpected angle, on the structures Marx built there. The challenge and the kinship are the same fact viewed from two sides, which is why the debate has lasted sixty years without either exhausting itself or being absorbed into a settlement.

## The parable's empirical afterlife

One province of the mainstream never received the concession's memo, and its experience completes the controversy's record. Applied economists continued, through the decades after the nineteen sixties, to estimate aggregate production functions on national data, recovering elasticities that were read as the shares of capital and labour and used in growth accounting across the profession. A line of critics, among whom Jesus Felipe and John McCombie have been the most persistent, argued that the exercise is vitiated at a level deeper than reswitching: the estimated relations, they showed, largely recover the accounting identity that the value of output is exhausted by wages and profits, an identity that holds whatever the technology, so that a good statistical fit certifies bookkeeping rather than behaviour, and the recovered parameters cannot bear the technological interpretation placed on them. The parallel with the original controversy is exact: a magnitude that appears to measure the physical world turns out, on inspection, to be measuring the distribution it was summoned to explain. The mainstream's considered response has been divided between defence of the practice as approximation and a quiet migration of growth theory toward models in which the aggregate function is a deliberate parable rather than a claim. For the purposes of this article the episode matters as evidence about the concession's scope. The logical point Samuelson surrendered in 1966 has never been re-won; what continued was not the parable's defence but its use, defended as convenience. A result can be conceded by a science's theorists and outlived by its habits, and the capital controversy is the type specimen of that uncomfortable possibility.

## The hint about money rates

Buried in the book's austere middle is a sentence that its post-Keynesian readers have treated as a door left deliberately ajar. Having established that the rate of profit must be fixed from outside the system of production if the wage is not fixed instead, Sraffa remarks that the rate of profit is accordingly susceptible of being determined by the level of the money rates of interest. One sentence, unelaborated, in a book without money: no banks, no liquidity preference, no finance. Yet the hint reorganises the causal imagination of everyone who takes it up. If the money rate of interest, set by central banks and financial convention, anchors the rate of profit that capitals must earn, and the wage then takes the residual share of the surplus, distribution is governed from the financial heights of the economy downward, and the classical open variable acquires a monetary determinant of a peculiarly modern kind. The post-Keynesian theory of distribution built extensively on the hint, joining it to Keynes's and Kalecki's accounts of investment and markup pricing, and the junction produced some of the surplus approach's most distinctive later work.

The hint matters here for a biographical reason as well. It shows the constructor thinking, inside his most abstract construction, about the actual monetary economy he had helped Keynes to theorise in the nineteen thirties, and it undermines in advance the portrait of the system as a barter model indifferent to capitalism's institutions. Sraffa's silence about money elsewhere in the book is a delimitation, not a denial: the price core was to be established on the ground where the marginal theory claimed its strongest results, and money, demand, and accumulation were left for the storeys others would build. The school's subsequent history, in which those storeys were in fact attempted, is the biography of that sentence. A reader who wants to know what Sraffa's system is for should weigh its author's single venture outside the construction as heavily as any theorem inside it.

## The critique that never appeared

The subtitle promised a critique, and the critique, as a book, never came. Sraffa lived twenty three years after the prelude and published almost nothing, and the question of what became of the promised demolition has exercised his scholars as much as any theorem. The archive supplies a partial answer: drafts and fragments of critical writing exist among the papers, including materials directed at the marginal theory's foundations, worked on and set aside across the decades, along with evidence that the Ricardo edition's demands, and the standard of finish he required of himself, consumed the years in which the critique was to have been completed. There is also a subtler possibility that the papers suggest and that Garegnani entertained: that the construction itself came to seem to its author a sufficient critique, since a rigorous alternative standpoint, fully stated, exposes the rival's incoherences more completely than any polemic, and the capital controversies conducted the demolition in public, with the profession's own champions conceding the decisive points, more thoroughly than a single author's book could have done.

Whatever the explanation, the absence shaped the challenge's career. A prelude without its sequel is a text that others must complete, and the completions diverged exactly as the two Sraffas would predict: the technicians completed it as a theory of prices and distribution, the controversialists as a verdict on capital theory, Steedman as a verdict on Marx, the school as a programme for rebuilding economics entire. The book's extraordinary influence is inseparable from its incompleteness. Finished systems are admired and shelved; foundations invite building, and three generations have built on these, in directions that agree about nothing except the solidity of the ground floor. Marxists assessing the challenge should take the point seriously in both directions: the framework they must answer is not a closed doctrine with an author's authority behind every use of it, and their own tradition's masterwork, three volumes of which its author left as drafts for another's hand, is in no position to sneer at unfinished cathedrals.

## The opponent's tribute

The controversy's most authoritative verdict was delivered, fittingly, by its most distinguished loser. Samuelson returned to Sraffa repeatedly in his later writings, in essays that combined undiminished resistance to the neo-Ricardian programme with an admiration for its founder that younger combatants found disorienting. He ranked the construction among the century's achievements in economic theory, treated its author as a classic in the line of Ricardo himself, and continued to insist, in the same breath, that the parables of the textbooks, though indefensible as general theory, remained useful approximations for many purposes. The combination is worth pausing over, because it is the mature form of the concession this article has been careful to size exactly. The strongest neoclassical economist of the age granted the logical result, honoured the man, and declined the programme: granted that the aggregate parable cannot be grounded, honoured the construction that had shown it, and declined to follow the surplus approach into its reconstruction of the discipline. Critics who cite Samuelson as having surrendered economics to Sraffa misread him, and critics who cite the survival of the textbooks as proof that nothing was conceded misread him in the opposite direction. What the tribute certifies is the precise shape of the event: a theorem won, a programme refused, and a profession that absorbed the theorem by ceasing to examine the ground on which it had been standing.

## Joan Robinson and the opening of the fight

The controversy did not begin with the book, and its first soldier deserves her paragraph. Joan Robinson, Keynes's most formidable Cambridge disciple and the author of the century's most ambitious attempt to extend Keynes into a theory of accumulation, opened the attack on the aggregate production function in 1953 with an article whose apparent simplicity was its weapon. She asked what the quantity of capital in the function denoted: if it denoted a collection of physical machines, it could not be added into a single number without prices; if it denoted a value, then the function explained the rate of profit by a magnitude that presupposed the rate of profit, and the argument had eaten its own tail. The article was received by the mainstream, in the main, as an impertinence, a philosopher's trick played on working economists; Robinson herself later remarked that she had not at first grasped the full depth of the difficulty she had uncovered. Sraffa's book changed the status of her question by answering it in a complete construction: once prices could be built from physical data, the contrast between the measurable magnitudes of the classical system and the unmeasurable aggregate of the parable stood in full view, and Robinson spent the rest of her career as the controversy's most relentless publicist, translating its results into polemics that the profession could not politely ignore.

Her later judgment on the episode was characteristically unsparing toward her own side. The critics, she concluded, had won the logical battle and failed the peace: they had not built the alternative economics, teachable and complete, that alone could have displaced the orthodoxy in the universities, and she numbered her own accumulation theory among the incomplete constructions. The verdict antedates and matches the teaching problem examined above, and it has a further edge for Marxist readers. Robinson stood outside Marxism, respectful of Marx, whom she had studied seriously and written a book about, and unsparing toward Marxists she thought had mistaken slogans for analysis. That the most effective champion of the Sraffian critique in public was neither a Marxist nor a member of the school, but a Keynesian with her own programme, is one more reason the controversy's history refuses the simple shape of an assault on Marxism from without or a defence of it from within. The lines of the fight ran through every camp, which is why its map, drawn carefully, teaches more than any of its battles.

## A fair bill of limitations

Symmetry requires the article to state, with the same care given to the challenge, the standing objections to the Sraffian framework itself, since a construction immune to criticism exists only in pamphlets. The bill has four items. First, the given quantities: the system determines prices for an output it does not explain, and its defenders' answer, that quantities belong to a complementary theory of demand and accumulation, concedes the framework's partiality even while justifying it. Second, gravitation: the long-period method asserts that market prices and the actual profit rate are drawn toward the system's positions, but the stability of that drawing, under realistic processes of capital movement and price adjustment, is a further claim requiring its own argument, and the literature on it is neither closed nor unanimous. Third, the anomalies of the general case: in systems with joint production, the construction can generate its own embarrassments, including negative quantities in the standard system and prices that behave strangely at the boundaries, which its defenders treat as boundary curiosities and its critics as symptoms. Fourth, and most often pressed by economists of several camps, empirical rarity: Bertram Schefold and others, examining the input-output tables of actual economies, found the wage-profit frontiers of real techniques to be nearly straight and actual reswitching to be rare in the recorded data, a finding the Sraffian side answers, correctly within its own terms, by repeating that the result is a possibility theorem about the rival's logic and not a frequency claim about the world, while conceding, as it must, that the theorem's practical bite varies with the phenomenon's prevalence.

None of these items restores the parable, and the bill's purpose is not rehabilitation by the back door. Its purpose is the discipline this article has applied throughout: every framework in this debate is a partial instrument with named silences, and the scholarly task is to keep the silences and the results in view at once. The Sraffian construction survives its bill of limitations with its central results intact, which is more than the aggregate production function can say. But a reader who finishes this article believing the framework to be a complete economics has been failed by it, exactly as a reader who finishes believing Marx's value theory to be a superseded price machine has been failed by the challenge's pamphleteers.

## The measuring rod in everyday dress

The problem the standard commodity solves has an everyday relative that every newspaper reader meets without recognising it: the construction of a cost of living index. A statistical office that reports inflation must compare the cost of a basket of goods across years in which relative prices have moved, and every choice of basket, the old one, the new one, or some compromise between them, builds a different answer into the measurement before the measurement begins. There is no basket whose cost measures the change in the cost of living free of the changes it is used to detect, because the measuring rod is made of the same commodities whose movements are under study. Ricardo's torment and the statistician's are one torment: in an economy of interdependent prices, invariance is not found but constructed, and the construction always embodies a decision about what is to be held fixed. Sraffa's standard commodity is the rigorous limit of that thought, the basket built so that its own composition makes it indifferent to the distributional changes whose effects are being traced. The index number problem shows that the difficulty is not a scholastic refinement of classical economics but a permanent feature of measuring any economy, which every finance ministry manages daily by convention. Sraffa's distinction is that he refused the convention and built the invariant instead, and the construction's strangeness to newcomers is the strangeness of seeing a familiar fudge replaced by an honest machine.

## The theorem that cut the other way

Inside the challenger's own mathematics sits a result that cut in Marx's favour, and no honest map of the debate omits it. Working with linear models of exactly the Sraffian kind, Michio Morishima proved in the early nineteen seventies what he called the fundamental Marxian theorem: in a viable economy with single-product industries, the rate of profit is positive if and only if the rate of surplus value is positive, if and only if, that is, the labour embodied in the workers' consumption falls short of the labour they perform. Exploitation, stated in value terms, is the necessary and sufficient condition for profit, stated in price terms, and the correspondence holds for any distribution and any technique satisfying the model's conditions. The theorem mattered because it separated two questions the polemics had fused. Whatever one thinks of values as a route to prices, the central Marxian claim about profit's source is not an arithmetical error: translated into the linear framework, it is a theorem of that framework, and Steedman's own later work acknowledged its force within its domain.

The domain's boundary is where the fighting resumed. The theorem's proof requires the conditions of the single-product case, and Steedman's joint production counterexamples were constructed precisely outside them, in the territory where values misbehave. Morishima's response, to redefine value for the joint case as the minimum labour cost of production over the available techniques, restored the theorem at the price the earlier section recorded: the magnitudes saved are optimising constructs rather than Marx's accounting values, and whether the rescue preserves the theory or replaces it remains genuinely disputed among specialists. The episode's lesson generalises across the whole debate. Again and again, a result proclaimed as the end of the argument proved to be the end of one formulation of it, and the argument migrated to the definitions. Readers who understand that migration understand the literature; readers who collect verdicts without it will be perpetually surprised by the debate's refusal to die.

## The probabilistic and empirical replies

Two further families of reply changed the question's terms rather than contesting its ground, and a complete map includes them. The probabilistic reply, stated by Emmanuel Farjoun and Moshé Machover in Laws of Chaos in 1983, holds that both parties to the redundancy dispute share a false idealisation: the supposition that a capitalist economy has exact prices and values at all, of the kind a simultaneous system computes. Real economies, on their argument, are statistical systems with vast numbers of exchanges, in which the right theoretical objects are distributions and averages rather than point magnitudes; and at that level, they argued, the labour content of commodities reasserts itself as the statistical attractor of market prices, not because any derivation compels each price but because exchange ratios fluctuate around centres set by production conditions in which labour is the universal input. The redundancy argument, on this showing, compares two idealisations and finds one of them dispensable, while the economy itself obeys the statistical version of the theory both idealisations approximate. The proposal has its own difficulties, of measurement and of what would falsify it, and it remains a minority programme; but it identified, earlier than most, that the debate's shared determinism was a choice and not a fact.

The empirical reply, associated above all with Anwar Shaikh, meets the challenge on the ground of measurement. If values were a redundant detour, the reply observes, one would expect prices of production, computed from input-output tables, to wander far from values, the detour's magnitude being unconstrained by anything in the rival theory. The studies report the opposite: across the input-output tables of several economies, prices of production track labour values closely, market prices track prices of production closely, and the deviations, though real, are modest beside the variation in the magnitudes themselves, a pattern Shaikh interpreted as the signature of values regulating prices through the turbulence of competition, in the manner of a centre of gravity that is never occupied and always operative. Critics reply that high correlations among large aggregates are easier to obtain than the interpretation requires, and that closeness is not determination; the exchange continues in the journals with improving data. For this article's map the point is simpler. The redundancy argument is a claim about derivations, and it stands; the empirical literature is a claim about the world the derivations describe, and it reports that the redundant magnitude shadows the indispensable ones closely enough to demand explanation. Both claims can be carried together, and the tension between them is one of the live seams along which the next generation of this debate will run.

## The classical revival in the history of thought

The construction's quietest conquest was over the past itself. Before Sraffa, the standard history of economics told a story of progress: the classical economists had groped toward truths that the marginal revolution finally stated correctly, and their confusions, Ricardo's above all, were the growing pains of a science. After the edition and the book, that story became untenable in its simple form, because the classical system had been shown to possess its own coherent logic, different from the marginal logic rather than deficient in it, and organised around a different question. The history of economic thought reorganised itself around the distinction: a surplus approach, running from Petty through the Physiocrats, Smith, Ricardo, and Marx, and a scarcity approach, descending from the marginalists, with the two traditions distinguished not by their dates but by their treatment of distribution, as a social datum in the one and as a market outcome in the other. Textbooks of the discipline's history now routinely present the fork, and the fork is Sraffa's construction wearing historical dress.

The revival reached Keynes as well. Garegnani argued, in essays that reshaped Keynes scholarship, that the General Theory's core, its theory of effective demand and its treatment of the rate of interest as a monetary and conventional magnitude, belonged in structure to the classical standpoint rather than to the marginal theory Keynes had been educated in and never fully escaped: Keynes, on this reading, had recovered for the theory of output what Sraffa recovered for the theory of distribution, and his book's confusions arose where the two recoveries were left unjoined. Whether or not the reading commands assent, its existence demonstrates the framework's reach: by the end of the century the surplus approach had become a standing interpretive option across the whole classical and Keynesian corpus, the lens through which a significant school reads three centuries of theory. Marx's place in that corpus is the point on which this article's argument turns one final time. In the revival's family tree, Marx is not a deviation from the classical line but its most ambitious continuator, the heir who tried to ground the surplus in a theory of value and to derive the system's laws of motion from it. The neo-Ricardian challenge, on the showing of the revival itself, is the family's internal audit of that ambition, conducted by cousins who kept the estate's other rooms in repair while questioning the foundations of his.

## The supermultiplier and the framework's working descendants

A research programme's vitality is measured by the work it generates in hands its founder never guided, and by that measure the framework remains alive in a quarter its chroniclers underreport. The Sraffian supermultiplier, developed from the nineteen nineties by Franklin Serrano and elaborated with Fabio Freitas, Sergio Cesaratto, and others, joins the surplus approach's theory of distribution to a demand-led theory of growth: autonomous expenditures that do not create capacity, government spending, exports, residential investment, grow at their own pace, the multiplier and induced investment adjust the economy's productive capacity to that pace, and the trend of output is thereby governed from the demand side while distribution is governed, as the classicals held, by social and monetary forces rather than by factor scarcities. The programme matters here for two reasons. It is a standing refutation of the charge that the Sraffian core is a museum piece, since it functions as the foundation of an active and publishing research front in growth theory. And it completes, in a form its originators did not foresee, the two-storey architecture the school section described: Garegnani's projected synthesis of Sraffa's prices with Keynes's quantities, carried out by a later generation under a new name. Marxist readers will note the family resemblance to their own tradition's concerns, growth governed by demand and distribution governed by class, stated in the idiom of the surplus approach; the resemblance is not accidental, and the conversations between the supermultiplier literature and Marxist theories of accumulation are among the more productive border traffics in modern heterodox economics.

## Five misreadings, corrected

The debate's longevity has deposited a sediment of standard errors, and clearing them is a service this article owes readers who will meet them everywhere. The first misreading makes Sraffa an anti-Marxist, a hired demolition expert of the bourgeoisie. The biography refutes the caricature in every particular: the formation in Gramsci's Turin, the prison lifeline maintained at personal effort across a decade, the collaborator who was British Marxism's leading economist, the decades of close reading of Marx recorded in the papers. None of that makes his framework Marxist, and the article has not claimed it does; it makes the framework's author a figure internal to the tradition's history, whose work must be answered as argument rather than dismissed as allegiance.

### Was Sraffa an enemy of Marx?

The record shows the opposite posture: a scholar formed in Gramsci's circle, who sustained Gramsci's work in prison, collaborated for twenty years with Britain's leading Marxist economist, and read Marx closely across his career. His framework inconvenienced Marx's value theory, but inconvenience delivered from inside a tradition differs from enmity arriving from outside it.

The second misreading concerns the capital controversies: that they were won by the critics and then ignored, a formula that manages to understate the victory and overstate the sequel in a single breath. What was conceded, by the strongest available opponent in print, was the logical core of the parable of distribution, an event without real parallel in the discipline's modern history; that is more than the formula's first clause suggests. What followed was not ignoring but absorption without adoption: the theory migrated to forms in which the damaged magnitude played no part, the textbooks taught on, and the critics, lacking a complete alternative curriculum, could not convert a theorem into a succession. The third misreading is the philosopher's stone of the whole subject: that redundant means false. A magnitude can be perfectly well defined, and even true of the world, while being dispensable in a given derivation; the redundancy argument, at its most rigorous, claims dispensability for the determination of prices and profits, and every step beyond that claim, to incoherence except through the joint production results, and to falsity nowhere, must be argued separately. Readers who keep the three verdicts, dispensable, incoherent in a special case, and false, in their separate boxes will be immune to most of the debate's journalism.

### Does redundant mean false in this debate?

No. Redundancy is a claim about derivations: that prices and the profit rate can be obtained without passing through values. It asserts nothing about whether labour values are well defined or whether the theory built on them explains what it claims to explain, which are separate questions, answered differently by the different replies this article has mapped.

The fourth misreading inverts the framework's allegiance: that Sraffa's construction is a species of neoclassical economics, presumably because it is formal, simultaneous, and concerned with prices. The construction was aimed at the neoclassical theory's foundations, restores the classical arrangement that theory had abolished, determines distribution outside the price system exactly where neoclassicism insists on determining it inside, and produced the results that the neoclassical side conceded it could not answer. Formality is not allegiance; mathematics is the common property of all the camps, and the same equations that refute the parable cannot be enrolled in the parable's army. The fifth misreading is the subtlest: that the debate is about arithmetic, a quarrel over whether one set of magnitudes can be derived from another. The arithmetic was settled early and is conceded on all sides by everyone who has done it. The debate is about what a theory of value is for: whether it exists to compute prices, to explain the surplus's origin, to expose the social forms of capitalism, or to ground a critique of distribution as a political fact. That is a philosophical and methodological dispute wearing mathematical dress, and it will continue for exactly as long as its underlying question does, which is to say indefinitely.

### Is the debate about arithmetic or about the purpose of value theory?

The arithmetic is the one closed part of the dispute: the derivations succeed, the pathologies in the special case are real, and competent parties concede both. What remains open is the purpose of a value theory, whether it exists to determine prices, to explain the surplus, or to criticise social forms, and that question is methodological rather than mathematical.

## The strongest objection, in full, and its answer

The brief for this article requires the redundancy argument to be stated at its strongest before it is answered, and the requirement has been honoured in pieces; here it stands assembled. Given the technical conditions of production and the real wage, prices and the uniform rate of profit follow by a derivation every step of which is forced, and at no step does the derivation consult a labour value. The values are therefore not a foundation on which the price structure rests but a commentary written alongside it, computable from the same data and contributing nothing to the result. A theory is its derivations; magnitudes that appear in none of them are ornaments, and an ornament that has consumed a century of exegetical labour is worse than an ornament, it is a tax on the science. Add the joint production results, in which the ornament turns incoherent, and the case is complete: Marx's economics should be rebuilt, as Roemer and the analytical school began rebuilding it, on the physical and institutional facts directly, keeping the critique of capitalism and discarding the machinery that a leaner theory has shown to be scaffolding. That is the objection. It is a strong one, and the answer that follows does not pretend it can be waved away.

The answer begins with the concession the brief also requires, stated without reserve: if the task of a theory of value is the determination of prices and of the profit rate, the objection succeeds completely, and nothing in this article should be read as qualifying that success. The derivation from physical data and distribution is complete, and values contribute nothing to it. The answer then relocates the dispute to the premise the objection smuggles: that the determination of prices is the task, the whole task, for which Marx's value theory exists. The textual and structural evidence of Capital assigns the theory further work that the Sraffian derivation does not attempt: to explain why labour power is a commodity and what follows from its peculiar character, to derive the surplus from the working day rather than from the input table's courtesy, to exhibit profit, interest, and rent as forms of a single unpaid residue, and to explain why those relations appear to their participants as relations among things. An objection that proves a theory unnecessary for a task it shares with a leaner rival, and silent about the tasks it does not share, has proved less than its rhetoric announces. Where the objection's rebuilding programme has been carried out, in the analytical reconstruction, the result confirms the division: the rebuilt theory recovers exploitation as a distributional theorem and does not recover, because it does not seek, the theory of the working day and the forms. The reader's verdict will depend on which tasks a theory of capitalism is for. That dependence is not an evasion; it is the debate's actual finding, purchased with sixty years of unusually honest argument, and an article that concealed it behind a declared winner would be worth less than the debate it summarises.

## The symposium on paradoxes, in the record

The controversy's decisive engagement deserves its documentary detail, because the published record is the strongest evidence for the concession's reality and the best corrective to both camps' folklore. The sequence ran through the Quarterly Journal of Economics, then as later the house journal of the Harvard and MIT mainstream. In 1965 David Levhari published a proof, or what purported to be one, that while an individual industry might exhibit reswitching, an economy composed of such industries could not: the technique adopted for the whole could not return as the profit rate rose, and the parable, though dented locally, survived in aggregate. Pasinetti, then a young fellow at King's College, Cambridge, constructed a counterexample, an economy, satisfying every assumption in play, in which the economy-wide technique did exactly what Levhari's proof forbade. The journal's editors took the unusual step of organising a symposium for 1966, Paradoxes in Capital Theory, in which the principals stated their positions side by side: Pasinetti's counterexample, Garegnani's analysis of capital and distribution, a joint examination by Michael Bruno, Edwin Burmeister, and Eytan Sheshinski, Morishima's contribution, and Samuelson's summing up, in which the concession was made in the words this article has already recorded. A symposium in which one side's champion withdraws the contested proposition, in the journal that had published the proposition's defence the year before, is as close as economics comes to a formal surrender, and the documents sit in the bound volumes for any reader who doubts the retellings.

The aftermath in the record is equally instructive and less often quoted. The Massachusetts side did not dispute the counterexample; it disputed its importance, arguing variously that the cases were pathological, that approximate parables remained serviceable, and that the theory's future lay in constructions where the question did not arise. Garegnani's rejoinders through the nineteen seventies pressed the point that a defence resting on the rarity of counterexamples had abandoned the theory's claim to be a theory, since a law of distribution that holds except when it does not is a description of some economies and not an explanation of any. The exchange never produced a second concession, because there was nothing left at that level to concede: the parties had divided over significance, and significance is not surrendered in journals. Readers who work through the symposium itself, rather than its summaries, will find the temperature lower and the quality higher than the folklore of either camp suggests, a reminder that the episode's participants, at the moment of decision, behaved as scientists are supposed to behave and so rarely do in public.

## The standard system as a shadow economy

One refinement of the standard construction repays a section of its own, because students regularly mistake the standard system for a description and then cannot see what it is for. Every actual economy produces its basics in proportions fixed by its own structure: so much iron, so much corn, in ratios that reflect the techniques in use and the composition of final demand. The standard system is a second, imaginary economy built from the same techniques but with every industry rescaled, expanded or contracted on paper, until the proportions come right: until, that is, the bundle of basics used up as inputs and the bundle left over as net product have the same composition. The rescaling changes no technique and no physical fact; it changes only the weights with which the industries enter the aggregate, the way a chemist might re-express a mixture in proportions that make its properties transparent. In that shadow economy the surplus appears as a pure expansion of the input bundle, more of the same, and the maximum rate of profit is simply the proportion by which the bundle grows: a physical growth rate of the standard system, visible without prices.

The shadow's uses are analytical, and they are three. It supplies the invariable measure, since the standard commodity is the shadow's net product and inherits its immunity to distributional change. It supplies the straight-line frontier, since wages measured as a share of that net product trade against the profit rate in exact proportion. And it supplies a standpoint from which an actual economy's prices can be interpreted, as the prices that would rule if the actual economy's proportions were the standard ones, corrected for the difference. Nothing in the construction claims that any real economy is in its standard proportions, any more than the surveyor's benchmark claims to be the landscape. Misreadings of Sraffa as a utopian of balanced growth, or as a theorist of some ideal proportioned economy, rest on confusing the benchmark with the terrain, and they dissolve the moment the standard system is understood as an instrument built to make distribution visible, the analytical equivalent of draining a pond to inspect its bed.

The shadow also yields a summary statistic of an economy's technique that comparativists have found hard to do without. The standard ratio, the proportion by which the standard system's net product exceeds its means of production, is fixed entirely by the physical data: change the technique and the ratio changes, change only the distribution and it stands fast. Two economies, or one economy at two dates, can therefore be compared in a dimension that prices corrupt in every other reckoning, since each carries, hidden in its table, a maximum rate of profit that no bargain and no inflation can alter. Historical work on the classical economists' own tables, and modern reconstructions of the input structures of past economies, have used the device to ask how much of an era's distributive conflict was a fight over a large surplus and how much over a small one, a question that the wage and profit shares alone cannot answer, since identical shares of different surpluses are different worlds. The instrument's modesty is again the point: it does not explain the conflict, it measures the size of the prize, and a theory of distribution that begins by measuring its prize is already ahead of one that assumes the prize away.

## Services, software, and the basics of a changed economy

The framework was built in the idiom of corn and iron, and readers reasonably ask whether its distinctions survive translation into an economy of services, software, and finance. The translation is more straightforward than the idiom suggests. The basic and non-basic distinction turns not on materiality but on position: a commodity is basic if it enters, directly or indirectly, into the production of everything, and in a modern economy the basics are electricity, transport, telecommunications, computing services, and the produced means by which labour is trained and maintained, while a haircut, a concert, and a consultancy report consumed for their own sake stand as non-basics whatever their price. Software occupies both shelves at once, as a non-basic consumption good on a telephone and as a basic input in every industry that runs on it, and the framework handles the duality exactly as it handles a sheep that is both dinner and breeding stock, by distinguishing the uses and pricing each in its place. The surplus, likewise, is no less physical for being partly intangible: an economy whose net product consists substantially of services still produces more than its reproduction consumes, and the division of that net product between the wage share and the profit share obeys the frontier's logic whether the product can be warehoused or not.

The genuine difficulties of translation lie elsewhere, in the magnitudes the framework was always silent about and which a service economy makes harder to ignore: the measurement of output where quality transforms faster than quantity, the treatment of finance, whose product is a claim on the surplus rather than a part of it, and the role of the state, whose services enter every production process while obeying no frontier of their own. The school's literatures have addressed each, with varying success, and the honest summary is that the construction's core, prices and distribution from physical conditions and a social settlement, survives the change of industrial costume intact, while its silences grow more conspicuous as the economy's centre of gravity moves into the sectors the silences concern. That is the condition of every classical framework in a world its authors did not live to see, and Marx's own categories face the same examination in other articles of this series. The test of a framework is not whether its silences vanish but whether its results continue to organise inquiry, and by that test the surplus approach, in the age of the platform and the data centre, remains a working instrument rather than a period piece.

## The parallel construction of von Neumann

The framework has a famous sibling, and the comparison sharpens both. In the nineteen thirties, in Vienna and then in print in 1937, John von Neumann built a model of an expanding economy in the same mathematical family: processes that transform inputs into expanded outputs, a uniform rate of return across processes, and the whole system growing at a single maximal rate determined by the technology alone. The kinship with Sraffa's construction is real, down to the family resemblance between von Neumann's maximal growth rate and the standard system's maximum rate of profit, and historians of the period have traced the two constructions' common roots in the classical and in the Austrian-adjacent debates of the interwar years. The differences are as instructive as the kinship. Von Neumann's model is a growth model, in which the surplus is reinvested by assumption and workers are fed from the input side as a matter of course; it determines a growth rate and an interest rate and is largely silent about distribution as a social settlement, the wage being one input among others. Sraffa's is a distribution model, in which growth is absent and the wage is the contested variable. Each construction took from the classical inheritance the half its author needed: von Neumann the reproduction and expansion, Sraffa the division of the product.

The parallel matters to this article for what it shows about the century's economics. The linear, physical, surplus-based way of seeing an economy was not a sectarian possession of any camp: it occurred independently to the founder of game theory and modern computing, working in the idiom of mathematical optimisation, and to the editor of Ricardo, working in the idiom of the classical texts. When a standpoint is reached from such distant starting points, the presumption shifts against the view that it is a mere ideological artefact. The marginal tradition that both constructions bypassed was itself the historically local formation, a particular late nineteenth century answer to the classical questions, and the rediscovery of the older standpoint by the century's most rigorous minds, in Cambridge and in Princeton alike, is a fact about the questions' permanence. Marxists and Sraffians disputing the inheritance are disputing over an estate whose title deeds are older than either, which is this article's thesis arriving by a second road.

## The library and the evidence of the margins

Sraffa's books survived with his papers, and the margins of those books have become a scholarly source in their own right, the closest thing to a record of his conversation that posterity will get. He read with a pencil, annotating densely, cross-referencing editions, and quarrelling in the margins with authors living and dead; his copies of Marx, of Ricardo's predecessors, of Keynes and of the marginalists preserve a running commentary that the archive's cataloguers have made progressively accessible. Scholars of the papers have learned to grade this evidence with care. A marginalium is a note to oneself, not a published position; it can record a passing irritation as readily as a settled conviction; and the man who declined for sixty years to state his conclusions in print is ill served by interpreters who treat his pencil as print by other means. The checkpoint this article applied to the archive applies here with doubled force: the readings the margins support are real, documented, and contested, and the difference between a contested reading and an established one is the difference between scholarship and advocacy.

Even graded with that care, the margins corroborate the outline the documents establish. The reading was widest where the legend supposed it narrowest: the classical economists in their originals and in their critics, Marx in sustained engagement across the decades, the marginalists mastered to the last footnote, since Sraffa's demolition work required knowing the enemy's texts better than the enemy did. A man does not annotate three generations of economic literature in four languages to construct a debating point. The library is the material record of the formation the archive describes, and the two together close the biographical question as far as evidence can close it: whatever the final verdict on his intentions toward Marx, the constructor of the challenge was formed inside the tradition it challenged, at a depth few of the tradition's card-carrying members have matched.

## Gravitation in practice

The long-period method lives or dies by a dynamical claim its equations do not contain: that competition actually drives market prices toward the system's prices and the realised profit rate toward uniformity, as capital quits low-return employments and crowds into high-return ones. The classical economists asserted the gravitation as a tendency visible in the record; the modern literature has tried, with mixed success, to prove it in explicit models of adjustment, and the results divide by the adjustment process assumed, some formulations converging handsomely and others cycling or wandering. The empirical side of the question is better behaved than the theoretical side. Studies of price formation in actual industries report cost-based pricing as the norm, with normal costs and a target margin doing the work that the theory's uniform rate idealises; and the input-output literature surveyed earlier, comparing computed centres of gravitation with observed prices, finds the long-period positions, whether computed from values or from prices of production, to be powerful predictors of the prices actually charged, which is what a centre of gravitation is supposed to be. The fairest summary is that gravitation is a well-supported empirical tendency and an incompletely demonstrated theorem, a combination that the classical tradition, Marx emphatically included, has lived with since its beginnings: the tendency is the theory's claim, and the counteracting influences are part of the theory too.

For the challenge's assessment the point cuts in a direction its critics rarely notice. The Sraffian derivation and the Marxian derivation are rival accounts of the same centre of gravitation, and the empirical closeness of both to observed prices, reported in the studies above, is evidence that the centre exists and that both accounts approximate it, the Sraffian exactly by construction and the Marxian nearly by the regulation of competition. A world in which prices of production bore no relation to market prices would embarrass both frameworks equally; the world the statisticians report embarrasses neither, and leaves the dispute where this article has located it throughout, in the interpretation of a shared and successful approximation.

## Administered prices and the degree of monopoly

At the level of the individual firm, the framework's prices have a working counterpart in the pricing practice that Michal Kalecki theorised and that every cost accountant recognises: the firm computes its normal unit costs, materials and labour at standard utilisation, and adds a margin, a markup, whose size reflects what Kalecki called the degree of monopoly, the firm's shelter from competition afforded by concentration, product differentiation, and the overhead structure of its industry. The realised margin, aggregated across firms and disciplined by the requirement that total profits equal total profit income, yields the economy's distribution between wages and profits from the side of price-setting, complementing the frontier's view of the same division from the side of technique. The two accounts are not rivals. The frontier states the constraint, the divisions that a given technique permits; the markup states a mechanism, the process by which firms' pricing decisions grope toward one of the permitted divisions; and the post-Keynesian synthesis that joined Kalecki to Sraffa and Keynes treated the pair as the micro and macro faces of a single classical theory of distribution, in which the shares are set by power and convention within limits that technique enforces.

The junction matters for Marxist readers because it reconnects the austere price theory to the phenomena their tradition has always placed at the centre: monopoly, concentration, and the political economy of the large firm. A framework sometimes accused of describing a frictionless world of perfect competition turns out to accommodate, in its developed forms, the administered pricing of oligopolistic industry without strain, since nothing in the construction requires that the uniform rate of profit be achieved by atomistic competition rather than by the conventions of price leadership and target returns. The rate that competition equalises in the classical picture is a norm toward which capitals press by whatever institutional means the epoch provides, and the epochs differ in their means more than in the pressure. Sraffa's silences, once again, prove on inspection to be the school's agenda rather than its refutations, and the agenda's items read like a table of contents for the heterodox economics of the following half century.

## The shelf: a map of the literature in prose

The debate's literature is large, but its load-bearing documents are few enough to map, and a reader equipped with the map can find every position this article has described in its primary form. The foundation is the book itself, ninety pages that must eventually be read unmediated, since every summary, this article included, flattens distinctions its author built in silence. Beside it stands the Ricardo edition's first volume, whose introduction is the construction's seed and the surplus tradition's modern charter. For the construction's exposition, Kurz and Salvadori's Theory of Production remains the comprehensive companion, patient where its subject is terse. Garegnani's essays, from his 1960 book on capital in the theory of distribution to his later papers on the long-period method, are the school's canonical development, and his edition of Sraffa's papers for the archive represents the tradition's documentary conscience.

The controversy's documents come next. Robinson's 1953 article opens the file; the 1966 symposium in the Quarterly Journal of Economics closes its decisive phase, and should be read entire rather than in extracts, since the concessions and the reservations live in adjacent paragraphs. Harcourt's book on the Cambridge controversies and his survey articles supply the narrative and the scorecard, written by a participant with the rare equipment of fairness to both camps. Steedman's Marx after Sraffa is the challenge's canonical statement, and it should be read with its two strongest respondents open beside it: Fine and Harris's Rereading Capital for the form-theoretic and historical objection, and the temporal literature reached through this series' companion article for the methodological one. Roemer's general theory of exploitation represents the constructive acceptance, Farjoun and Machover's Laws of Chaos the probabilistic refusal of the shared premises, and Morishima's Marx's Economics the theorem that complicates every simple verdict. The archive literature, finally, is best approached through the editions and studies produced by those who have catalogued the papers at Trinity, including the volumes of essays on the manuscripts' evidence and Alessandro Roncaglia's biographical study, which remains the most accessible life. A reader who works through that shelf owns the debate; a reader who works through half of it owns more of it than most of its journalistic summarists, on whichever side they write.

## Unequal exchange in a Sraffian mirror

The framework found one of its most charged applications at the border of this cluster's territory, in the theory of unequal exchange that the dependency article of this series treats in its own right. Arghiri Emmanuel had argued, from within a broadly Marxian frame, that trade between high-wage and low-wage economies at prices of production transfers value systematically from the poor economy to the rich, wages being the independent variable and the equalisation of the profit rate the transmission belt. The Sraffian construction proved to be the natural instrument for examining the claim, since it handles exactly the configuration Emmanuel described: two economies, or two regions of one system, sharing a rate of profit while their wages differ by social settlement, with prices following from the physical data and the two wages. Worked in that instrument, the transfer Emmanuel asserted appears as a determinate feature of the price structure rather than as a metaphor, and the debate over his thesis could proceed on the terrain of what the transfers depend on and how large the mechanisms make them. The episode is a further instance of the article's recurring pattern. A framework built without reference to Marx's value magnitudes turned out to be the sharpest available tool for formalising a claim made by a Marxist about the world economy, and the tool's neutrality between the traditions is precisely what made it useful to both. The heirs keep borrowing one another's instruments, whatever their pamphlets say, because the estate's problems are shared even where its doctrines are not.

The formal literature that followed Emmanuel's intervention refined the instrument for the trading case in ways worth a sentence of record. In multi-country Sraffian systems, the equalisation of the profit rate across borders, combined with nationally settled wages, generates price structures in which the low-wage economy's exports systematically embody more labour and more surplus than the imports they command, and the size of the transfer can be computed rather than merely asserted, which moved the unequal exchange debate from the rhetoric of dependency to the arithmetic of price formation. Defenders of the trade orthodoxy replied within the same instrument, disputing the wage's exogeneity and the direction of the specialisation effects, and the exchange that resulted is one of the cleaner examples in modern economics of a heterodox claim being sharpened, tested, and bounded by the most rigorous tool available rather than dismissed for its provenance. The dependency theory article of this cluster carries that debate's substance; its presence here is to register that the tool which performed the sharpening was built by the subject of this article, for purposes that had nothing to do with development, in a ninety-page book whose applications its author declined to enumerate.

## The surplus approach travels: Bharadwaj and the study of development

The framework's most consequential journey outside the Cambridge debates was to the economics of development, and its guide was Krishna Bharadwaj, the Indian economist whose work joined the classical surplus tradition to the study of Indian agriculture. Bharadwaj came to Cambridge, absorbed the Sraffian standpoint at its source, and carried it home to questions the Cambridge controversialists had never faced: how production and exchange actually work in an economy of peasant households, interlocked markets, and customary obligations, where the landlord is also the moneylender and the employer, and the labourer's wage is entangled with debts, tenancies, and shares of the crop. Her studies of Indian agriculture showed markets functioning through these interlocked relations rather than despite them, and her methodological writings argued that the classical approach, which begins from the social structure of production and treats distribution as institutionally settled, fits such economies as the marginal approach never can, since the marginal approach must first invent the anonymous markets its theory requires. Her Themes in Value and Distribution, published at the end of the nineteen eighties, stands as the surplus tradition's most considered statement from outside its European home.

The journey matters to this article's argument in two ways. It demonstrates, in the field rather than in the seminar, the claim made for the open variable in an earlier section: a framework that expects distribution and market forms to be socially constituted can describe economies whose social constitutions differ radically from industrial capitalism's, while a framework that deduces market forms from universal rationality must treat most of the world's economic history as a deviation awaiting correction. And it completes the biography of the surplus approach's revival. The tradition Sraffa restored in the Wren Library's manuscripts and in ninety printed pages became, within a generation, a working instrument on three continents: in Rome and Cambridge for the theory of distribution, in the post-Keynesian centres for the theory of accumulation, and in Delhi for the study of agrarian change. Traditions are vindicated less by the controversies they win than by the work they make possible, and by that standard the classical revival needs no further defence. The Marxist tradition, which shares the surplus approach's founding insight that production and its social relations come before exchange and its prices, should recognise in this diaspora a kinship and a resource, and in the redundancy debate that occupies this article's centre, a family quarrel whose heat has sometimes concealed how much of the house the quarrelling parties hold in common.

## How the book argues

The book's rhetoric is as distinctive as its results, and learning to hear it is part of learning the framework. Sraffa almost never argues against an opponent in the text. There are no refutations addressed to named contemporaries, no reviews of the literature, no marshalled quotations; the marginal theory, whose demolition the subtitle promises, is not so much attacked as left standing in a landscape from which its foundations have been quietly removed. The method is construction as criticism: build the alternative standpoint completely, with full rigour, in ninety pages, and let the reader discover that the questions the old standpoint existed to answer have either been answered differently or have dissolved. The preface contains the book's one paragraph of open polemic, and even it confines itself to observing that the standpoint may be unfamiliar to readers formed by the marginal theory and that the author makes no apology for the omission of references. Everything else is showing rather than saying, and the parallel with the later Wittgenstein, whose method Sraffa's conversation helped to form, is not a coincidence of milieu but a genuine kinship of intellectual style: the conviction that a confusion is dispelled not by denouncing it but by constructing the description in which it cannot arise.

The rhetoric has costs that the debate's history itemises. A book that will not state its applications invites applications its author might have disowned, and the two Sraffas of this article are partly the creature of that reticence: had the constructor annotated his construction with its intended targets and its forbidden uses, Steedman's reading would have been one interpretation among others rather than the field's default, and the archive's counter-evidence would have been unnecessary. There is also a plainer cost in pedagogy: generations of students have met the results through controversialists because the text itself refuses the office of guide, and the flattening that every summary performs was, in this case, licensed by the original's silence. None of this diminishes the construction. It explains why a book of ninety pages generated a library, and why the library's quarrels so often take the form of rival completions of a deliberately unfinished utterance. The reader who finishes the book should therefore resist the temptation to ask what its author would have said about its uses. The evidence of the papers answers part of that question; the rest he arranged, with intent or by temperament, to leave permanently open, and the openness is now a fact of the theory's life that no scholarship can close.

The comparison that Cambridge itself drew was with the Tractatus, the other slim classic produced in that milieu a generation earlier, whose author Sraffa had corrected in conversation: two books of fewer than a hundred pages, each the residue of decades of thought, each organised as a sequence of numbered propositions or their equivalent, each followed by a long silence from its author on the subject it had transformed. The parallel should not be pressed into a theory, but it records something true about the intellectual style the two men shared and transmitted: a distrust of commentary, a belief that the construction rightly made renders most commentary unnecessary, and a willingness to let the work stand unguarded before its readers. Economics has no other classic in that style, and the singularity explains both the reverence the book commands among those who have mastered it and the exasperation it provokes among those conscripted to teach it. A discipline accustomed to textbooks met a treatise written like a proof, and it has been deciding what to do with the encounter ever since.

## The dictionary and the textbook that almost was

The school's institutional high-water mark came in 1987 with The New Palgrave, the great dictionary of economics whose editors, John Eatwell, Murray Milgate, and Peter Newman, opened its pages to the whole discipline's traditions and gave the classical surplus approach a representation no reference work had offered it: substantial entries on Sraffa, on the capital controversies, on the classical theory of value and distribution, written by the school's own scholars and placed on equal terms with the mainstream's self-descriptions. For a generation of students the dictionary performed the office the controversy had failed to perform in the curriculum: it certified that the questions Sraffa had raised were part of the discipline's permanent record, to be consulted by anyone mapping the field, whatever their allegiance. Eatwell and Milgate went on to attempt the harder task, a systematic restatement of the Keynesian and classical synthesis in treatise form, and the attempt's partial character confirms Robinson's verdict from the other side: the alternative economics exists as a literature, a dictionary, a set of journals and research programmes, but not as the single teachable system that alone displaces an orthodoxy from the lecture hall. The observation is not a counsel of despair. The surplus approach's mode of survival, dispersed through reference works, specialist journals, and the working toolkits of adjacent traditions, may simply be the form that fundamental criticism takes in a discipline whose centre is held by other means than argument. Marx's own work survived its first half century in a comparable dispersion, as this series has had repeated occasion to note.

## The subsistence starting point

The book's first chapter performs a thought experiment whose modesty conceals its function. Imagine an economy that produces exactly what it consumes in producing: the corn grown replaces the seed and the growers' food, the iron forged replaces the tools worn out and the furnaces' keep, and nothing is left over. In such an economy there is no surplus, no profit, and no problem of distribution, and prices have a single humble office: they are the exchange ratios at which each producer can recover the means of production and subsistence advanced, so that the round can begin again. Sraffa begins there because the device isolates the exact moment at which the theory's real subject enters. Let the same economy, by better methods or harder work, produce more than replacement, and everything changes at once: there is now a residue to be divided, the division is not dictated by the replacement requirements, prices must do a second office of distributing the residue, and the rate of profit appears as the form the capitalists' share assumes when the division is made through the price system. Every subsequent construction in the book, the frontier, the standard, the switching results, is an elaboration of that single transition, from replacement ratios to distributive prices, and the reader who holds the transition in view holds the book's argument in miniature. The classical economists had begun from the same place, with the surplus as the pivot of their science, and Marx's analysis of the working day is, from this angle, the deepest account ever given of how the residue the device displays is produced: not found in nature, but extracted, hour by unpaid hour, in the hidden abode of production. The Sraffian device shows the residue's existence and its arithmetic. What produces it, the device does not say, and the space between those two sentences is the space in which this article's whole debate has been conducted.

## For students and exam candidates

Your route into this topic runs through the physical picture, and you should refuse to proceed until the picture is solid. Begin with the miniature economy of corn and iron in this article and work it until the surplus, the wage settlement, and the simultaneous determination of prices feel like bookkeeping rather than doctrine; every later subtlety is a commentary on that miniature. The standard examination traps are three. Do not present reswitching as an empirical frequency; it is a possibility result, and examiners reward the candidate who says so. Do not report the nineteen sixties concession as the death of neoclassical economics; state its exact scope, the aggregate parable and the measurement of capital, and you will outscore every inflationist in the room. And do not let the redundancy argument and the transformation problem merge in your answers: the first asks why values are needed, the second whether Marx's derivation from them is consistent, and confusing them forfeits the marks both questions carry separately. When you revise the challenge and replies, keep the table from this article as a card in your [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), with the concession column filled in your own words, and route onward to [the transformation problem in its dedicated treatment](/2016/04/15/transformation-problem-explained/) for the neighbouring dispute in full.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This topic teaches best as a controversy with documents, and the documents are unusually classroom-ready. Set the 1966 symposium as the core reading: students who watch a proof fail, a counterexample land, and a champion concede, in twenty pages of primary text, learn more about how economics actually proceeds than a semester of methodology lectures conveys. The capital controversies are the clearest available example of a logical concession in the discipline's history, and they repay being taught as one: begin with Robinson's question about the units of capital, let the class attempt the measurement themselves on a two-good example, and the impossibility will teach itself before Sraffa's name is mentioned. For the Marxist half of the syllabus, the challenge and replies table organises a seminar series: one session per row, with the concession column as the discussion prompt, since students argue most productively about what each side gives up. Guard the two checkpoint disciplines in your own presentation. Present the Sraffian argument as a technical result rather than as a raid, and present the archival reading of Sraffa's intentions as contested rather than settled, and your students will inherit the debate in its honest form, which is the only form in which it remains instructive.

Your primary sources are in Cambridge and in the journals, and both reward direct work over secondary summary. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Piero Sraffa?

An Italian economist, born in Turin in 1898, who spent his working life at Cambridge and altered two disciplines while publishing almost nothing. Trained under Luigi Einaudi, he belonged in his youth to the circle of Antonio Gramsci, and he left Italy in 1927 after an article on a failed Italian bank drew Mussolini's anger and a demand for retraction he would not make. Keynes brought him to Cambridge, where he became a fellow of Trinity College, edited the works and correspondence of David Ricardo across two decades, influenced Keynes's monetary theory and, by his own account in a famous preface, Wittgenstein's later philosophy. His single theoretical book appeared in 1960. He died in 1983, leaving a large body of manuscripts at Trinity that scholars have mined ever since.

### Q: What is Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities about?

It is a short treatise of 1960 that rebuilds the theory of prices and distribution on classical foundations. Starting from the quantities of commodities that each industry uses up and produces, Sraffa shows that if one share of the product, the wage or the rate of profit, is settled from outside, then every price and the other share are fixed together by the conditions of production. Along the way it separates basic commodities, which enter into everything, from non-basics, constructs an invariable standard of value, and demonstrates that the choice among techniques as the profit rate varies can behave in ways the orthodox theory declared impossible. Its subtitle announces it as the groundwork for a critique of marginal value and distribution theory, whose separate statement was never published.

### Q: What is the standard commodity?

It is a manufactured measuring rod. Take an economy's basic industries and rescale them, on paper, until the collection of goods they use up and the collection they leave as net product are made of the same things in the same proportions. The leftover bundle in that rescaled system is the standard commodity. Because its composition matches the composition of the inputs, its size relative to those inputs is a pure physical proportion that no shift in wages or profits can disturb, which makes it the fixed point Ricardo hunted for when he searched for a measure of value that distribution could not bend. It exists only inside the construction: a benchmark for analysis, not a bundle any economy actually produces.

### Q: What were the Cambridge capital controversies?

A dispute of the nineteen fifties and sixties between economists in Cambridge, England, and their namesakes in Cambridge, Massachusetts, over whether capital can be treated as a single measurable factor whose marginal product explains the rate of profit. Joan Robinson opened it by asking in what units the capital of the aggregate production function was supposed to be counted; Sraffa's book gave the question a rigorous frame; and the argument converged on reswitching, the possibility that a technique discarded as the profit rate rises can become the cheapest again higher up. In a 1966 symposium in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, Paul Samuelson, the leading defender of the orthodox construction, accepted that the possibility was real and that the simple parable behind the aggregate function could not be sustained. Textbooks changed far less than the logic required.

### Q: What is reswitching?

The return of a beaten technique. Suppose two methods can make the same commodity, differing in how much labour and equipment they use and in when the labour is applied. At a low rate of profit the first method may be the cheaper; at a middle rate the second takes over; and at a high rate the first can undercut the second again, although neither method has changed in any physical respect. The rankings flip twice because profit is charged on wages and materials advanced over time, and the compounding weighs differently on methods whose inputs fall at different dates. Reswitching is a claim about what the logic of prices permits, not a claim about how often factories change their minds, and a single admissible case is enough to break any law that requires rankings to move in one direction only.

### Q: Why do Sraffians say labour values are redundant?

Their claim is about the order of calculation. Hand an economist the economy's input structure, what each process consumes and yields, together with the workers' share of the product, and the full set of prices and the uniform profit rate can be worked out directly, with no preliminary step. Embodied labour totals can also be computed from the same input structure, but inserting them changes nothing downstream: every price and the profit rate come out exactly as they did without them. A step that can be skipped without altering any result is, for Ian Steedman and those who followed his 1977 statement of the argument, a step the theory should drop, and on that basis the value apparatus is pronounced an unnecessary detour between the data of production and the prices it was meant to explain.

### Q: How do Marxists reply to the redundancy argument?

The replies form four broad families, and most respondents combine several. One holds that the calculation was never the point: value theory exists to explain where the surplus comes from and what social shape it wears, questions about the length of the working day and the purchase of labour power that a price derivation does not raise. A second questions the innocence of the starting data, arguing that techniques and labour requirements are themselves deposits of past class relations. A third refuses the shared frame and insists that inputs bought earlier and outputs sold later cannot be valued in one simultaneous instant, a line developed by the temporal interpretations. A fourth points out that the rival system takes the surplus as already present in its tables and allocates it, while saying nothing about its origin. Nearly all respondents grant the narrow point: as a machine for deriving prices, the detour through values is dispensable.

### Q: Did Sraffa reject Marx?

Not in print, and not in any form the record supports simply. His published book barely mentions Marx, and his life's published labour was the restoration of the classical tradition to which Marx also belonged. What evidence exists about his private view comes from the manuscripts: decades of close reading, notes that treat Marx's constructions as live problems within a shared inheritance, criticism at points and reconstruction at others. Scholars who have worked through those papers divide over how far the engagement went, some reading a deliberate recovery of the surplus approach with Marx inside it, others warning that engagement is not allegiance and that a man who stayed silent for sixty years cannot be conscripted posthumously. The challenge to Marx associated with his name was built by later hands, Steedman's above all, from a framework its author left unannotated.

### Q: What did Sraffa's unpublished papers reveal?

They revealed a workshop. Deposited at Trinity College, Cambridge, and opened to systematic research in the nineteen nineties under the care of his literary executor Pierangelo Garegnani, the manuscripts showed the price construction taking shape in the late nineteen twenties, within a few years of his arrival in England, rather than in the decade of its publication. They showed the standard system maturing across the nineteen forties. Most consequentially for the debates in this article, they showed their author reading Marx closely and continuously while the construction developed, annotating the texts and testing the arguments, which gave documentary footing to the interpretation of the whole project as a rebuilding of the classical surplus tradition. Other specialists caution that notebooks record a mind at work, not a set of conclusions, and the interpretation remains a scholarly position rather than a settled verdict.

### Q: Is the Sraffian critique fatal to Marxism?

It is fatal to one version of one component, and the precision matters. As a device for deriving prices of production, Marx's value apparatus cannot compete with the direct derivation, and that verdict is arithmetic rather than opinion. The wider theory is a different matter. Within the same linear mathematics, Morishima's fundamental theorem shows profit arising exactly when surplus labour is performed, so the exploitation claim survives translation into the challenger's own idiom. The remaining Marxian claims, about the origin of the surplus in the working day, about the social forms of wage and profit, and about fetishism, were never addressed by the derivation at all, and they stand or fall on arguments the critique does not contain. A challenge that retires a calculating machine and leaves the theory of exploitation to be re-argued on new ground is a serious wound and a strange kind of fatality.

### Q: How do the two Sraffas change the way the book is cited?

The distinction disciplines two opposite abuses. Cited as a constructor, Sraffa is the author of theorems: the determination of prices from physical conditions and one distributive variable, the frontier, the standard construction, the switching results. Those stand on their proofs and are available to any user, friendly or hostile, exactly as Steedman used them. Cited as a historical figure, he is Ricardo's editor, Gramsci's lifeline, and the reader of Marx whose papers complicate every simple story, and that figure is evidence about intentions and lineage, not about the truth of any theorem. Confusing the citations corrupts arguments in both directions: dismissing a result because its author's biography is uncongenial, or borrowing the biography's authority to bless a conclusion the proofs do not contain. Careful writers say which Sraffa they are quoting, and careful readers check.

### Q: What should a student separate between the standard commodity and a numeraire?

Everything except the superficial resemblance. A numeraire is the unit in which prices happen to be expressed: pick gold, or wheat, or pounds sterling, and every relative price and every real magnitude in the system is untouched, because a numeraire is only a label for the measuring scale. The standard commodity is not a label but a built object, a composite of basic goods in proportions fixed by the technique itself, chosen because its value cannot be twisted by the distributional changes under study. Swap one numeraire for another and nothing in the theory moves. Remove the standard commodity and the theory loses its invariable measure, its straight-line frontier, and its direct route to the maximum rate of profit. Students who keep the two apart understand the construction; students who merge them mistake a theorem for a bookkeeping convention and miss the point of the book's most famous device.

### Q: Why did a slim book carry such a large challenge?

Because it struck at load-bearing points with a closed construction. Its targets were not decorative: the measurement of capital, on which the mainstream's account of distribution rested, and the necessity of labour values, on which the Marxian account of prices rested. A pamphlet could have been ignored; a complete alternative system, every step forced, compact enough to be checked entire, could not. The timing helped, since Joan Robinson's unanswered question about the units of capital had been hanging in the air for seven years, and the profession's most gifted controversialists on both sides took the book up at once. Brevity itself became a weapon: ninety pages that opponents had to answer whole, because the argument's force lay in the construction's completeness, and a gap anywhere would have been a refuge. There was no gap, and the refuge had to be built elsewhere, in arguments about significance.

### Q: How does reswitching trouble the idea of a capital intensity ranking?

A ranking by capital intensity promises an ordering of techniques that holds independently of distribution: this method uses more capital per worker than that one, whatever the wage and the profit rate happen to be. Reswitching shows the promise cannot always be kept. If the same method is the cheapest at a low and at a high rate of profit with a rival between, then which method counts as the more capital intensive cannot be read off the techniques themselves, since the crown passes back and forth as distribution changes, and any measure of the capital embodied in a method shifts with the prices that the rate of profit helps to set. The ranking the parable needs, a staircase of methods ordered once for all, with lower profit rates reliably selecting the more capital-intensive step, is exactly what the phenomenon dissolves. What survives is a local comparison at a given distribution, not the universal ordering the theory of distribution by marginal productivity required.

### Q: What separates the neo-Ricardian conclusion from the Sraffian method?

The method is a piece of machinery: a way of obtaining prices and the profit rate from the physical conditions of production once one distributive share is given, together with the results that machinery delivers, the frontier, the standard measure, and the switching possibilities. The conclusions are claims built on the machinery by particular economists, and they do not all stand or fall together. That the aggregate production function cannot ground a theory of distribution follows closely from the machinery itself. That labour values are redundant adds a premise about what theories are for, and was drawn by Steedman, not by the machinery's maker. That Keynes should be re-founded on classical ground is Garegnani's further construction. A reader may accept the machinery entire and refuse any one of the conclusions without contradiction, and much of the debate's confusion comes from treating the package as indivisible, by defenders who want the conclusions to inherit the machinery's certainty and by critics who hope a flaw in a conclusion will loosen the machinery's bolts.
