The single most useful thing to know about The German Ideology is that the chapter everyone quotes is not a chapter. What circulates under the heading "I. Feuerbach" is an editorial assembly, built in the twentieth century out of loose, separately paginated manuscript sheets that Marx and Engels never arranged into a finished text and never saw through a press. Seven distinct pieces of surviving material are involved. No two editors have ordered them the same way. The book you can buy with that chapter printed first, running continuously, with numbered sections and a confident argumentative sequence, is a scholarly construction presented as an authorial one.

That fact changes how the text should be read and how it must be cited, and almost nothing written about the work online tells you about it. The guides summarise the argument. They rarely say that the argument's most famous statement survives as fragments, that roughly two thirds of the manuscript is a line-by-line demolition of a philosopher almost nobody now reads, or that a reader who wants the materialist conception of history in its early form needs perhaps thirty pages out of a volume that runs to hundreds.

![The German Ideology reader's guide, manuscript status and which parts to read - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-72.webp)

This guide is written to make you able to open the book, find what matters, understand what you are looking at, and cite it in a way that would survive a supervisor or an examiner. It covers what the work was for, why it was abandoned, how it became a book, which parts repay close reading and which can be skimmed without loss, what the four or five load-bearing passages actually say, how to handle the two passages that are most often misread, which edition to buy, and what to read before and after. The theoretical content itself belongs to the [complete guide to historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/), which sets out the mature theory the manuscripts are groping towards; this article is about the text as an object you have to handle.

## What The German Ideology is, and what it is not

The manuscripts were written between the autumn of 1845 and the middle of 1846, mainly in Brussels, by Marx and Engels working together, with contributions from others in their circle. Moses Hess is generally credited with a section of the second volume, and the newest scholarly edition adds Joseph Weydemeyer, who made fair copies, to the list of hands involved. The project began as a set of contributions to a quarterly journal that never appeared, and turned into a plan for a two-volume book after the journal's backers withdrew. The title itself was devised late, and Marx referred to the intended book by that name in print in the spring of 1847, in a short declaration attacking Karl Grün.

What the work was for is easy to state and routinely misdescribed. It is a polemic. Its purpose was to settle accounts with the German philosophical scene from which both authors had just emerged: the Young Hegelian left, which had turned Hegel's system into a series of increasingly abstract criticisms of religion and consciousness, and the school of writers around Hess and Grün who had welded that criticism onto French socialism and produced what Marx and Engels contemptuously called true socialism. The German ideology of the title does not mean ideology in the general sense the word later acquired inside Marxism. It names a specific national intellectual formation and a specific vice: the belief that changing how people think about their circumstances amounts to changing their circumstances.

What the work is not is a treatise on the materialist conception of history. That conception is developed in it, and developed for the first time at length, but it is developed in passing, as the ground on which the polemic stands. The pages that state the theory are the pages where the authors stop attacking and explain why the attack is warranted. They are the best pages in the manuscripts and they are a minority of them.

Nor is the work a book its authors published, approved, or corrected. They failed to find a publisher, partly because of censorship and partly because the publishers they approached had sympathies with the writers under attack. One chapter did appear: the critique of Grün, which became the fourth chapter of the second volume, was published under Marx's name in the monthly Das Westphälische Dampfboot in August and September of 1847. Everything else lay in manuscript. In the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, written in 1859, Marx described what happened to it in a phrase that has done more to shape the text's reputation than any other sentence about it: they abandoned it, he wrote, to "the gnawing criticism of the mice". He added that they had achieved their main purpose, which was self-clarification, and that is the correct description of the document's status. It is a working-out, not a statement.

### Is The German Ideology worth reading?

Parts of it, yes, and the parts are identifiable. Roughly thirty to forty pages of the Feuerbach material contain the first extended statement of the materialist conception of history and repay close reading. The long attacks on Bruno Bauer, Max Stirner, and the true socialists are of specialist interest and can be skimmed or skipped by most readers without loss.

## How a bundle of manuscripts became a book

The publication history is not antiquarian detail. It is the reason the text you hold has the shape it has, and it explains why two editions can present the same material in a different order without either being wrong.

After Engels died in 1895 the manuscripts passed to the leaders of the German Social Democratic Party, who published them slowly and in pieces over the following decades. Most of the attack on Stirner appeared in a documentary series edited by Eduard Bernstein in 1903 and 1904, with a further fragment following in 1913. The preface and the chapter on Bruno Bauer appeared in a scholarly journal in the early 1920s, edited by Gustav Mayer. The Feuerbach material, which is what almost everyone means when they refer to the work, appeared in Russian in 1924 and in German in 1926, in the Marx-Engels Archive published under the direction of David Ryazanov at the Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow. The first complete publication in a single volume came in 1932, in the fifth volume of the first section of the original Marx-Engels-Gesamtausgabe.

That sequence should be read carefully, because it establishes the single most consequential fact about the work's reception. The materialist conception of history was elaborated by Marx and Engels in 1845 and 1846, was not available to anybody in its early form until the 1920s, and was not available in full until the 1930s. Every account of historical materialism produced in the classical period of Marxism, by Engels in his later popular writings, by Kautsky, by Plekhanov, by Lenin, by Luxemburg, was produced without access to this text. The work that is now routinely presented as the foundational statement of the theory played no part in the formation of the tradition that treats it that way. It arrived as a rediscovery in the decade when the Paris manuscripts also surfaced, and it was received into a Marxism that had already hardened into orthodoxy and was looking for canonical support.

### Why was The German Ideology not published in Marx's lifetime?

The publishers approached in 1846 and 1847 either feared censorship or sympathised with the writers being attacked, and the authors ran out of both money and interest. Marx said later that the main purpose, clarifying their own position, had been served, so the manuscript was left unpublished and largely uncorrected.

### The construction of the Feuerbach chapter

Here is the problem in detail, because the detail is what makes the point usable rather than merely interesting.

There is no manuscript in Marx's or Engels's hand headed "I. Feuerbach" that runs continuously from a beginning to an end. What survives is a set of separate pieces: a fragment of a draft preface, a large body of material that was originally written as part of the polemic against Bauer and Stirner and was then extracted by the authors and set aside for a projected opening chapter, sheets with two different systems of pagination, marginal additions in both hands, deletions, and passages that break off mid-argument. The newest critical edition treats the Feuerbach material as seven separate texts rather than as one chapter, and the largest of those seven is itself a composite of extracted passages.

The consequence is that the order in which you read the argument is an editorial decision. At least seven distinct orderings have been proposed by serious editors. The familiar English arrangement, with the celebrated opening about premises verifiable in a purely empirical way, followed by the account of the division of labour, followed by the account of ruling ideas, is one solution among several. It is defensible. It is not authorial. When a commentator writes that Marx moves from the premises to the division of labour to the state, what is being described is a movement created by an editor.

The editions that made the running for most of the twentieth century did not say this. They printed a chapter, numbered its sections, and gave it a heading, and readers reasonably assumed they were reading a text in the order its authors intended. The critical scholarship that established otherwise has been building since the 1960s and reached its fullest form in the newest Gesamtausgabe volume, published in two parts with a separate apparatus, which presents the manuscripts and printings of the period without pretending to reconstruct a finished chapter. Terrell Carver and Daniel Blank have published an English presentation of the Feuerbach manuscripts page by page as discontinuous fragments, together with a study of the political history through which the fragments were turned into a chapter, and Carver has argued in print that even the newest edition concedes too much to the book form. The scholarly dispute now is not about whether the chapter was constructed. It is about how far the constructing should be undone.

### What this means for citing the text

The practical rule follows directly. A citation to this work should identify the manuscript and the edition, not merely a chapter and a page. Writing "Marx and Engels, The German Ideology, ch. 1" tells a reader nothing about which arrangement you were using and may point at a passage that a different edition places forty pages away or omits from the chapter altogether. Writing "The German Ideology, in Collected Works vol. 5, p. ..." at least fixes the text you used. For research work at postgraduate level and above, naming the manuscript grouping and the critical edition is now expected, and the [guide to the MEGA2 project and the Marx manuscripts](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/) sets out how the editorial apparatus is organised and what each volume contains.

This is the article's namable claim, and it is worth stating in a form you can carry into an essay or a seminar. Call it the constructed-chapter warning: any argument that leans on the order, the completeness, or the internal structure of the Feuerbach chapter is leaning on an editor rather than on Marx and Engels. Arguments that lean on what the passages say, taken as fragments of a working draft, are unaffected. The distinction is between using the text as evidence, which is legitimate and rich, and using it as scripture, which the manuscript status does not support.

## The structure of the work, part by part

The two-volume plan is the frame everything else sits inside, and knowing it is what allows a reader to navigate a text whose table of contents is unhelpful and whose headings are jokes.

The first volume was to be a critique of the German philosophical scene, organised around three figures. The second was to be a critique of German socialism as practised by writers who had absorbed that scene. The headings of the two volumes do not survive in the manuscript; they are known because Marx mentioned them in the declaration against Grün. The internal headings that do survive are elaborate mock-ecclesiastical jokes: the section on Bauer and Stirner is framed as a church council, the Leipzig Council, and the two men are canonised as Saint Bruno and Saint Max. Readers who find this baffling should know that it is baffling on purpose and that the joke was funny in Brussels in 1846 to about forty people.

### Volume one: the philosophical demolition

The draft preface is short and is worth reading, because it states the target in a compressed form. It presents German philosophical criticism as a struggle against phantoms of the brain, and it announces the intention to demystify a scene that mistakes its own vocabulary for a description of the world. It is a fragment, and the fair copy that survives is not complete.

The Feuerbach material, however arranged, is the part that matters and the part the rest of this guide concentrates on. Feuerbach is treated with more respect than the other targets, because his transformation of theology into anthropology was the move that made Marx's own break possible, and because the authors' quarrel with him is precise rather than contemptuous: they charge him with a materialism that observes rather than acts, that treats the sensible world as given rather than as produced, and that therefore cannot explain historical change. The eleven brief notes now known as the Theses on Feuerbach were jotted in Marx's notebook in the spring of 1845, immediately before this material was drafted, and they contain the same charge in aphoristic form.

The section on Bruno Bauer is short and is almost entirely of interest to specialists in the Young Hegelian movement. Bauer had responded to earlier criticism from Marx and Engels, and this is the reply. It contains a few sharp formulations about the relation between criticism and material interests, and nothing that cannot be found better stated elsewhere.

The section on Max Stirner, headed Saint Max, is by far the longest part of the work. It is a page-by-page, sometimes line-by-line assault on Stirner's book The Ego and Its Own, which had appeared at the end of 1844 and which had unsettled the Young Hegelian left by pushing its logic to an egoist conclusion: if religion, the state, humanity, and morality are all abstractions imposed on the individual, then socialism and the species-being that Feuerbach and the early Marx invoked are abstractions too. Stirner's argument was aimed at Feuerbach and it landed. The length of the reply is a measure of how seriously it was taken.

Saying plainly that most readers should skip this section is a service, and this guide performs it. It is repetitive by design, since its method is to quote Stirner and mock each quotation in turn, and the passages of independent theoretical value inside it are the ones the authors themselves later extracted and set aside for the Feuerbach chapter. A reader with a specialist interest in anarchism, in individualism, or in the history of the Young Hegelians will find it valuable. A reader who wants the materialist conception of history will find perhaps a dozen useful pages spread across several hundred.

### Volume two: true socialism

The second volume attacks the German socialist writers of the middle 1840s. Its surviving chapters are uneven and some are fragmentary. The most important is the fourth, the critique of Karl Grün's book on the social movement in France and Belgium, which Marx published separately in 1847 and which is therefore the only part of the whole work that appeared in print in either author's lifetime. It is a demolition of Grün's scholarship, and its interest now is largely historical, though it shows Marx's method of reading a text against its sources with some force.

A further chapter attacks Georg Kuhlmann, a self-styled prophet who had attached religious rhetoric to communist ideas, and the manuscript for this section is generally attributed to Moses Hess rather than to Marx or Engels. This is a useful thing to know before quoting the work as though every page were by its two named authors.

### Why is The German Ideology so long?

Because most of it is a line-by-line refutation of Max Stirner's book, which runs for hundreds of pages and quotes its target constantly. The theoretical content that made the work famous occupies a small fraction of the whole and was extracted by the authors themselves from that polemic.

## The passages that carry the argument

Five passages do the theoretical work. If you read nothing else, read these, and read them knowing that they are drafts.

### The premises

The materialist conception is introduced by way of its starting points. The premises, the manuscripts say, are not arbitrary or dogmatic; they are real individuals, their activity, and the material conditions under which they live, and they can be verified in a purely empirical way. This is a methodological claim before it is a historical one, and it is aimed directly at the philosophical habit of beginning from a concept of man, of consciousness, or of the human essence. The first premise of all human history, the text says, is the existence of living human individuals, and the first fact to be established is their physical organisation and their consequent relation to the rest of nature.

What makes this more than a truism is the next step. Human beings distinguish themselves from animals, the argument runs, as soon as they begin to produce their means of subsistence, and what they are coincides with what they produce and with how they produce it. The nature of individuals therefore depends on the material conditions determining their production. Production is not one activity among others; it is the activity through which a form of life is constituted. Everything else in the theory descends from this move.

### The production of life and the division of labour

The second load-bearing passage traces the development of the division of labour and connects it to forms of ownership. The argument is that each stage in the division of labour determines the relations of individuals to one another with respect to the material, instrument, and product of labour, and that a sequence of forms of ownership can be read off from that development: tribal ownership, ancient communal and state ownership, feudal or estate ownership, and the modern form. The vocabulary here is ownership rather than mode of production, and the later, more precise apparatus is not yet in place. The [guide to the mode of production](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/) traces how the terminology tightened between these manuscripts and the 1859 preface, and a reader writing about the development of Marx's categories should not project the later vocabulary backwards onto this text.

The passage also introduces the separation of town and country as the first great division of labour, and the separation of material from mental labour as the point at which consciousness can begin to flatter itself that it is independent of the world. That last step is what licenses the treatment of philosophy as an occupation with material conditions rather than as a free-standing enquiry, and it is the hinge on which the whole polemic turns.

### Consciousness and being

The formulation that circulates most widely is the claim that consciousness does not determine life but life determines consciousness, and that morality, religion, metaphysics, and the rest of ideology have no history and no development of their own. The second half of that claim is more radical than the first and is much less often quoted, because it is much harder to defend. It does not say that ideas are unimportant. It says that they cannot be explained by a story that stays inside their own domain, that a history of philosophy written as philosophers succeeding philosophers is an illusion produced by the division of labour that separated thinking from doing.

Whether this survives as stated is a real question, and the honest answer is that it does not survive in its strongest form. Ideas do have transmission mechanisms of their own, and a body of doctrine constrains what can be thought next in ways that are not reducible to the material conditions of the thinkers. The later Marxist tradition spent a century qualifying this passage, and the qualifications are the substance of the [base and superstructure model](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/) and of the arguments about relative autonomy that grew out of it.

### The ruling ideas

The fourth passage is the most quoted sentence in the work, and it is short enough to quote exactly: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas." The sentences around it are what give it content. The class which has the means of material production at its disposal has control at the same time over the means of mental production, so that the ideas of those who lack the means of mental production are on the whole subject to it. The ruling class, having to present its interest as the common interest of all members of society, gives its ideas the form of universality and represents them as the only rational, universally valid ones.

Three things are worth noticing, because all three are routinely lost. The passage specifies a mechanism: control over the means of mental production, which in the 1840s meant presses, universities, and clerical appointments, and which in any period means whatever the equivalent institutions are. It does not assert that the ruled believe everything the rulers say; it asserts a distribution of institutional capacity. And it identifies the characteristic form of ruling ideas as universality, which is the claim that the interest of a part is the interest of the whole. That third element is what later became the analysis of hegemony, and the [complete guide to hegemony](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/) traces the route from this passage to Gramsci's far more developed account of consent.

### Communism as a real movement

The fifth passage defines communism not as a state of affairs to be established, nor as an ideal to which reality must adjust, but as the real movement which abolishes the present state of things, with the conditions of that movement resulting from premises now in existence. This is the sentence that separates Marx's communism from the utopian projects he spent the decade attacking, and it is the one to reach for when a reader asks why Marx never described the future society in detail. The refusal was principled and it is stated here.

## The hard passages decoded

Four passages cause more trouble than the rest of the manuscript combined. Each is difficult for a different reason, and each can be handled once the reason is named.

### What does the hunting and fishing passage mean?

It sits inside an attack on the division of labour and ends with a jab at the authors' philosophical rivals, so the tone is polemical rather than programmatic. It illustrates what the abolition of fixed occupational roles would mean, not a timetable for life under communism, and reading it as an institutional proposal misses both the satire and the argument.

The passage describes a society in which nobody has one exclusive sphere of activity, in which a person may hunt in the morning, fish in the afternoon, rear cattle in the evening, and criticise after dinner, without ever becoming hunter, fisherman, shepherd, or critic. It is the most quoted image in the manuscripts and the most abused. Three things about it are worth having in hand.

The first is the final term. The list ends with the critic, and that is not a neutral choice of occupation. Critic was the self-description of Bruno Bauer's circle, whose "critical criticism" Marx and Engels had already ridiculed at length in an earlier book. The joke is at the expense of philosophers who thought that criticising was a form of world-changing labour. A reader who does not see the joke reads the sentence as a lifestyle description; a reader who sees it reads it as a dig with an argument attached.

The second is the argumentative context. The passage occurs in a discussion of how the division of labour fixes a person's activity into a role that confronts them as an alien power, so that their own social act becomes something that rules them. The image is meant to specify what its opposite would look like, in the most concrete and deliberately homely terms available. The point is the absence of fixation, not the presence of hunting.

The third is the manuscript status, which cuts in both directions. This passage is in the extracted material that became the Feuerbach chapter, and the ordering questions that affect the rest of it affect this too. That is a reason for caution about how much weight it can bear, and it is also a reason not to dismiss it: the passage exists in the manuscript, was written by one of the two authors, and does express a view about the division of labour that both held and that Marx restated in more sober language decades later, most notably in the discussion of the fully developed individual in the first volume of Capital.

The safest way to use it in written work is to quote it briefly, name the polemical context, and say what it illustrates rather than what it proposes. The unsafe way, which appears in a great deal of undergraduate writing and in most hostile commentary, is to present it as Marx's plan for the organisation of labour and then to observe that it would not work. The passage is not a plan and the observation is not a criticism of anything Marx argued.

### Why does the text say ideology has no history?

Because ideology is being defined here as inverted consciousness, a picture in which real relations appear upside down, and an inversion cannot have an independent development. The claim is about explanatory autonomy, not about whether ideas change. Ideas change; the changes cannot be explained from inside the ideas.

The image the manuscripts use for this is the camera obscura, in which people and their circumstances appear inverted as they do on a retina, and the text says this inversion arises from their historical life process as much as the retinal inversion arises from a physical one. The analogy is doing more work than it can carry, since the optical inversion has a mechanism and the ideological one is asserted rather than specified, and this is one of the places where the early formulation is weaker than the later theory. But the sense of the passage is clear enough: what needs explaining is not the content of the ideas taken on their own terms but the practical situation that generates them and makes them plausible to the people who hold them.

This is also the passage that most often gets confused with false consciousness. That phrase does not appear in these manuscripts and is not Marx's. It first appears in a letter of Engels written in 1893, decades later, and it means something narrower than the camera obscura passage does. Attributing it to Marx is one of the most common errors in student writing on ideology, and the [complete guide to the Marxist theory of ideology](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/) sets out the difference between the several things the tradition has meant by the word.

### What Verkehr means and why the translation matters

The German word Verkehr runs through the manuscripts and is a genuine difficulty. It covers commerce, traffic, communication, intercourse, and social interaction generally, and the standard English translations render it as intercourse, which in modern English carries a connotation the German does not. Where the text speaks of forms of intercourse, it means the whole set of relations through which people deal with one another in production and exchange, and this is the concept that will later be sharpened into relations of production. A reader who translates the term mentally as "relations of production" will understand the argument, but a writer who quotes the English word without explanation will confuse a modern audience, and a researcher who treats Verkehr and Produktionsverhältnisse as interchangeable will flatten a development in Marx's vocabulary that took a decade.

### The premises passage and the word empirical

The claim that the premises can be verified in a purely empirical way has been read as a positivist commitment and as an anti-philosophical gesture, and neither reading survives contact with the argument. What follows the claim is not a programme of observation but a conceptual reconstruction of what production involves. The word is doing polemical work against philosophers who begin from concepts, and it should not be pressed into service as a statement of scientific method. The later methodological statements, particularly the introduction drafted in 1857 and left unpublished, are far more careful about the relation between abstraction and observation, and anyone writing on Marx's method should work from those rather than from this phrase.

## Which edition and translation to use

The choice of edition matters more for this work than for almost anything else in the corpus, because editions differ not only in translation but in what they contain and in what order.

Four kinds of edition circulate. The first is the abridged student edition, which prints the Feuerbach material alone, usually with a short introduction, and omits the attacks on Bauer, Stirner, and the true socialists. The best known of these was edited by C. J. Arthur and has been the standard classroom text in the English-speaking world for decades. It is cheap, it is short, it contains the pages that matter, and it is honest about being an abridgement. Its weakness is precisely that it presents the constructed chapter as the work, which reinforces the impression this guide exists to correct.

The second is the complete translation in the Collected Works of Marx and Engels, where the whole of both volumes occupies the fifth volume of the edition. This is the text to cite for any serious purpose in English. It contains everything, it has an editorial apparatus that explains the manuscript situation in its notes, and its pagination is stable and widely available in libraries. Its ordering follows the twentieth-century convention, which is why the apparatus matters.

The third is the German critical edition, where the manuscripts and printings of the period are presented with a full apparatus in a separate volume, without a reconstructed opening chapter. This is the reference text for research and the one a doctoral examiner will expect a candidate to have consulted if the argument turns on the manuscript.

The fourth is the specialist English presentation of the Feuerbach manuscripts as discontinuous fragments, page by page, edited by Carver and Blank, which is the text to work from if the argument concerns the composition of the chapter itself rather than its content.

### Which edition of The German Ideology is best?

For coursework, the abridged Feuerbach edition is sufficient and inexpensive. For citation in serious writing, use the complete text in the Collected Works. For any argument that turns on the order or completeness of the Feuerbach material, work from the German critical edition or from the fragment-by-fragment English presentation.

The translation question is less severe here than in Capital, because the manuscripts are not stylistically dense in the same way, but two recurring choices are worth watching. Verkehr as intercourse has been discussed. The rendering of Ideologie and its cognates is generally uncontroversial. The word Aufhebung, which appears in the discussion of the abolition of the division of labour and of private property, is translated variously as abolition, supersession, and sublation, and the three carry different implications about whether what is abolished is preserved in a higher form. Where an argument turns on that word, name the German and the rendering you are using. The [research guide to Marx's works and the order to read them in](/2013/06/15/marx-reading-order-guide/) sets out the same discipline for the corpus as a whole.

## What to read before and after

The manuscripts assume a reader who knows the German philosophical scene of the 1840s, and a reader who does not will find the polemic incomprehensible and the theory disconnected from anything.

### What should you read before The German Ideology?

Read the eleven Theses on Feuerbach first; they take ten minutes and contain the same argument in compressed form. A short account of Feuerbach's transformation of theology into anthropology helps. Knowing that Stirner attacked Feuerbach from an egoist position explains why so much of the manuscript is devoted to him.

The Theses are the natural entry point and they are also a trap, because of how they reach the reader. Marx wrote them in a notebook in the spring of 1845 and never prepared them for publication. Engels published them in 1888 as an appendix to his book on Feuerbach and the end of classical German philosophy, and in doing so he edited them, smoothing the German and altering wording in places. Marx's original notebook text was published later and differs. The eleventh thesis, the one about philosophers having only interpreted the world, is the most famous sentence Marx ever wrote that he did not publish, and the version everybody quotes is Engels's version. This is a small thing and it is exactly the kind of small thing that separates a citation that survives scrutiny from one that does not. A researcher should cite the notebook text and note the difference; the [profile of Engels as an independent thinker and editor](/2013/05/15/friedrich-engels-intellectual-profile/) works through the wider question of what his editorial hand did to the corpus.

Feuerbach himself repays a short visit rather than a long one. What matters is the method: he read theology as a disguised anthropology, arguing that the attributes people ascribe to God are alienated human attributes, projected outwards and then worshipped. Marx took the structure of that argument and applied it to politics, to law, and eventually to economics. Once you see that the critique of religion is the template, the shape of everything Marx did afterwards becomes visible. Feuerbach's failure, on the account given in these manuscripts, is that his materialism stops at contemplation: he sees the sensible world as given rather than as the product of accumulated human activity, and he therefore has no account of how anything changes.

Stirner requires only a paragraph of background, but the paragraph is necessary. His book argued that every abstraction, God, humanity, the state, morality, and society itself, is a spook imposed on the concrete individual, and that the Young Hegelian critics who had liberated people from God had simply substituted new abstractions. Since Feuerbach's species-being was one of the abstractions attacked, and since the early Marx had used species-being freely, the attack landed near home. The scale of the response in these manuscripts is a measure of how uncomfortable it was, and readers of the Paris manuscripts should notice that species-being largely disappears from Marx's vocabulary after this encounter. Whether that disappearance is a break or a refinement is the substance of the [debate between the young Marx and the mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/), which this guide does not adjudicate.

### What to read after

The natural next text is the polemic against Proudhon written in 1847, which applies the same method to a target with more economic content and which was published, and after that the pamphlet of 1848, where the historical argument appears in its most compressed and most rhetorically effective form. The [reader's guide to the Communist Manifesto](/2011/10/15/communist-manifesto-readers-guide/) covers that text and its own considerable editorial complications.

For the theory rather than the sequence of texts, the preface of 1859 is the destination. It is three pages long, it states the same conception in mature vocabulary, it was published by Marx himself, and it is therefore the citation of choice whenever an argument needs an authorial statement rather than a draft. A reader who has worked through these manuscripts and then reads that preface will notice both how much is already present in 1846 and how much sharper the later formulation is.

## The standard misreadings

Five errors dominate the secondary literature and the classroom, and each has a one-sentence correction.

The first is that the Feuerbach chapter is a chapter. It is not, for the reasons set out above, and any argument resting on its structure rests on an editor.

The second is that the work was influential before the twentieth century. It was not. It was unpublished apart from one chapter, unknown apart from a handful of fragments, and unavailable in full until 1932. Statements that Marx "set out his theory of history in The German Ideology" are true about composition and misleading about reception, since the theory reached the movement through other channels entirely: through the Manifesto, through the 1859 preface, and above all through Engels's later popularisations.

The third is that the hunting and fishing passage is a proposal. It is an illustration inside a polemic, with a joke at the end.

The fourth is that the whole book is about the materialist conception of history. Most of it is a demolition of Max Stirner. This matters practically, because a student who buys the complete text expecting a treatise will read several hundred pages of ridicule directed at a book they have not read.

The fifth is that German ideology means ideology in general. It names a specific national intellectual scene and a specific vice within it. The general concept of ideology in Marxism is built partly out of material in these manuscripts, but the title is not announcing that concept.

A sixth error deserves separate mention because it is subtler. The manuscripts are frequently used to prove that Marx held a technological-determinist view of history, on the strength of the passages linking forms of ownership to stages in the division of labour. What those passages actually offer is a developmental sequence for western Europe, sketched in a draft, with no claim to universality and no explicit statement of what causes what. The strong causal reading was constructed later, largely by readers who came to these manuscripts already holding it. The [debate over economic determinism](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/) works through the textual evidence on both sides, and this text is one of the exhibits, not the verdict.

## The strongest objection to using this text at all

A serious objection deserves a serious statement before any reply, and this one is often made by scholars sympathetic to Marx rather than hostile to him.

The objection runs as follows. A text that its authors abandoned, that they never corrected, that survives in fragments whose order is unknown, that was assembled into a book by editors with a political interest in producing a canonical statement of the theory of history, and that was published for the first time in Moscow in 1932 under an institute that was in the process of being purged, cannot bear the doctrinal weight placed on it. Reading the materialist conception of history out of an unfinished polemic is a methodological error of the same kind as reading a philosopher's discarded drafts as their considered position. Worse, the very features that make the manuscripts attractive, the vividness, the confidence, the sweeping formulations, are the features one would expect in a draft written at speed for the authors' own clarification and never revised for an audience. If Marx had wanted the world to have this text he had thirty-seven years in which to publish it, and he did not.

The reply has to concede a great deal. The twentieth-century reception did treat an abandoned draft as scripture, and the editorial history was not innocent. The Feuerbach chapter was constructed, and constructed by people who wanted it to exist. Nothing in this article disputes that.

What the reply establishes is a distinction between two uses of the text. Evidential use asks what Marx and Engels thought in 1845 and 1846, how their vocabulary was developing, what problems they were working on, and where their later formulations came from. For that purpose the manuscripts are among the richest documents in the corpus precisely because they are unrevised: a draft shows the working. Canonical use asks what the theory is, and cites the text as authority for a formulation. For that purpose the manuscripts are the wrong instrument, and the 1859 preface, the Manifesto, and the published books are the right ones. The rule that follows is simple enough to apply while writing: cite this work for what its authors were thinking, cite the published works for what the theory claims.

There is a further point that is easy to miss. The passages of independent theoretical value inside the Stirner polemic were extracted by Marx and Engels themselves, physically, and set aside for a projected opening chapter. That act of extraction is authorial. The authors identified this material as the material that stood on its own. What they did not do is arrange it, finish it, or publish it. The editors supplied the arrangement; the authors supplied the selection. Knowing which is which is most of what a careful reader of this text needs.

### How long does The German Ideology take to read?

The Feuerbach material takes an evening, perhaps two hours with notes. The complete text, including the Stirner polemic, takes most readers between twenty and thirty hours and is worth that investment only for specialists in the Young Hegelian movement or in the composition history itself.

That estimate is worth breaking down, because the difference between the two figures is where most of the frustration with this text comes from. A reader who buys the abridged edition, reads it over a weekend, and comes away with the premises passage, the division of labour, the camera obscura, the ruling ideas, and the definition of communism has got everything the work is cited for. A reader who buys the complete text and begins at page one will spend the first several hours on a preface fragment, a short attack on a forgotten theologian, and then the opening of a polemic that quotes its target relentlessly and answers in puns. Many readers stop there and conclude that the work is impenetrable. It is not impenetrable; it is misarranged for the purpose most readers bring to it, and the arrangement is a nineteenth-century publishing plan rather than a reading order.

## What these manuscripts contain that nothing else in the corpus does

A guide that only tells you what to skip has done half its job. Four arguments appear here in a fuller or earlier form than anywhere else, and each is a reason for the text's standing.

### The state as an illusory community

The manuscripts develop an account of the state that Marx never restated at comparable length. The argument is that because the division of labour produces a conflict between the interest of each individual and the common interest of all who deal with one another, the common interest takes on an independent form, separated from the real interests of individuals, and does so as the state. That separated form is described as an illusory community, and the illusion is specific: the state presents itself as the general interest while resting on a determinate class basis, and the struggles within it are the illusory forms in which the real struggles of classes are fought out.

Two things follow that are worth carrying. The state is not, on this account, merely an instrument wielded by a ruling class; it is a form that the common interest necessarily takes when interests conflict, which is a structural claim rather than a conspiratorial one. And the illusion is not a lie told by anyone; it is a real appearance generated by the separation. Later Marxist theories of the state divide largely on how much weight to put on each half of that, and readers coming to those debates will find the seed here.

### Big industry and the world market

The manuscripts contain a description of the development of large-scale industry and its creation of a world market that anticipates the Manifesto's most famous passages and does so in analytical rather than rhetorical form. The argument is that big industry universalises competition, destroys local particularity, produces for the first time a world history rather than a set of local histories, and creates a class that bears no relation to a particular nationality because its conditions of life are the same everywhere. The claim that history becomes world history is a philosophical claim about the object of historical study, not merely an observation about trade routes, and it is stated more clearly here than in the published works.

It also carries a prediction that the tradition has argued about ever since: that the proletariat's conditions of existence become international before its consciousness does, and that the international character of the class is a fact about its situation rather than an achievement of its politics. The record of the twentieth century is not kind to any simple version of that claim, and the [case against historical materialism](/2010/04/15/case-against-historical-materialism/) treats the failure of class identity to override national identity as one of the serious empirical objections.

### The critique of philosophy as an occupation

The passage on the separation of material and mental labour supports an argument that is unusual in the corpus and highly usable. Philosophy, on this account, is not simply mistaken; it is an activity whose social position generates a characteristic illusion. Thinkers who do not participate in material production, whose livelihood comes from producing ideas, and whose training consists in relating ideas to other ideas, will naturally come to believe that ideas move history, because within the horizon of their own practice they do. The illusion is not stupidity; it is an accurate report of a partial situation, mistaken for a report of the whole.

This is a sharper and more interesting claim than the flat assertion that ideas reflect material interests, and it is one a student can deploy against a modern target without any anachronism. It is also, and this is worth saying, an argument that applies to Marxist intellectuals with exactly the same force, a point that Gramsci recognised and made central.

### The formation of a revolutionary class

The manuscripts contain the clearest early statement of why the working class is assigned the role it has. The argument is not moral and it is not that the class suffers most. It is structural: the class must be one that bears a universal grievance rather than a particular one, that is formed by the development of production rather than left over from an earlier form, that cannot emancipate itself without abolishing the conditions of its own existence, and that is therefore unable to establish itself as a new ruling class in the way earlier revolutionary classes did. Each clause of that is doing work, and the argument is a piece of reasoning that can be assessed rather than a slogan that can only be accepted or rejected. The [complete guide to the Marxist theory of class](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/) sets out how the account developed afterwards and where the strain shows.

## Who wrote what, and why the question is not pedantic

Most of the surviving Feuerbach material is in Engels's handwriting. Engels insisted for the rest of his life that the materialist conception of history was Marx's discovery and not his own. Those two facts sit awkwardly together and have generated a century of argument.

Several explanations are on offer and none has won. One is that Engels was the better and faster writer, so he held the pen while the two worked together, which would make the handwriting evidence about scribal labour rather than authorship. A refinement of this proposes that Marx dictated and Engels wrote, which would explain both the handwriting and Engels's later attribution; the proposal has been argued at length in the specialist literature and has not been accepted by the editors of the newest critical edition. Another explanation is that Engels's contribution was larger than he later admitted and that his modesty about it was political, a way of consolidating Marx's authority after his death. A third is that the question is badly posed, because the manuscripts were produced in a genuinely collaborative process in which the distinction between having an idea and writing it down does not track anything meaningful.

What a reader should take from this is a rule about attribution. Do not write "Marx argues in The German Ideology that ..." unless you are prepared to defend it. Write "Marx and Engels argue" for the jointly composed material, name Hess where the attribution to him is standard, and where an argument depends on which author held a position, say what the evidence for the attribution is. The temptation to attribute everything valuable to Marx and everything crude to Engels is strong in the secondary literature and it is not supported by the manuscript evidence for this text.

The question also bears on the larger dispute about whether Engels systematised Marx into something Marx would not have recognised. That dispute concerns the later Engels, the Engels of the popular expositions and of dialectics in nature, and importing it into a discussion of 1846 is anachronistic. In 1846 the two men were collaborators of five years' standing, both in their twenties, working out a position together in a rented room, and treating their partnership as a hierarchy misdescribes the document.

## How the text was received and what the reception did to it

The reception history is short, because the text has only had one, and it is instructive because it shows a document acquiring a function it was not written for.

When the Feuerbach material appeared in the 1920s, Marxism already possessed a theory of history. That theory had been assembled from Engels's later expositions, from the 1859 preface, from the Manifesto, and from the systematising work of Kautsky and Plekhanov, and by the 1920s it had been codified further in the Soviet Union. It was a theory with stages, with a determining base, and with a claim to scientific status. What the new manuscripts provided was not a challenge to that theory but an origin story for it: here, in the authors' own hands, was the conception being stated for the first time, with a vividness the later formulations lacked.

The material was therefore read through the theory rather than against it, and the editorial decisions reflected that. Presenting the extracted sheets as a first chapter, headed with Feuerbach's name and placed before the polemics, produced a document that looked like a systematic exposition interrupted by topical attacks. The alternative presentation, in which the theoretical passages appear as material extracted from a polemic against Stirner, produces a document that looks like what it is: two young men working out what they think while demolishing a rival, and finding that the demolition requires a general account of how societies change.

The consequences run in two directions. Western Marxism, which discovered these manuscripts alongside the Paris manuscripts and the fuller Hegel, used them to argue for a Marx who was philosophically serious, continuous with the early humanism, and irreducible to the Soviet textbook. Soviet scholarship used the same text to demonstrate the early maturity of the scientific conception. Both readings were available because the text is a draft, and drafts contain more than finished works do: the abandoned formulations, the hesitations, and the passages that point in several directions at once are all there because nobody edited them out.

The Althusserian intervention of the 1960s complicated matters further by drawing its epistemological break between the Paris manuscripts and these ones, making this text the first work of the mature Marx. That reading depends on treating the Theses on Feuerbach and these manuscripts as a decisive rupture with the humanist vocabulary, and it is contestable, not least because species-being and alienation do not vanish as cleanly as the thesis requires. The [debate over the young and the mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/) sets out the evidence on both sides, and the point for a reader of this text is only that a great deal of interpretative weight has been placed on a document that cannot easily bear it.

## Using the text in written work without getting caught

Three habits separate writing that reads as informed from writing that reads as second-hand.

The first is naming the manuscript status when it is relevant and not when it is not. If you are describing what Marx and Engels thought in 1846, the status is context and one clause covers it. If your argument depends on the sequence of the exposition, the status is load-bearing and must be discussed. If you are quoting the ruling ideas sentence to illustrate a point about ideology, the status is irrelevant and mentioning it is throat-clearing.

The second is quoting precisely and briefly. The passages people quote from this text are short, memorable, and constantly misquoted, usually by expansion: a clause acquires a qualifier it did not have, or two sentences from different pages are run together. Check the wording against the edition you are citing rather than against memory or against another commentator, and quote the shortest fragment that carries the point.

The third is attributing the polemical passages to their targets. A sentence in this text that begins by describing an absurd position is very often describing Stirner's position or Grün's, not the authors'. Reading a page of the Stirner polemic without knowing this produces confident misattributions, and the misattributions circulate: several claims about what Marx believed concerning morality and egoism originate in passages where the manuscript is ventriloquising a position in order to ridicule it.

## Who Max Stirner was and why he takes up so much of the book

Johann Kaspar Schmidt, who wrote under the name Max Stirner, published Der Einzige und sein Eigentum at the end of 1844, and the book is usually rendered in English as The Ego and Its Own. He was a member of the Berlin circle of Young Hegelians, a schoolteacher by occupation, and by any measure the most destructive thinker the group produced.

His argument was aimed at his own comrades. The Young Hegelian project had been to dissolve religion by showing that its objects were human creations. Feuerbach had performed this on God, replacing the divine with the human essence. Stirner's move was to observe that the human essence is itself an abstraction, no less imposed on the individual than God had been, and that in swapping one for the other his colleagues had changed the object of worship without abolishing worship. Humanity, morality, the state, society, the good, and the cause of the proletariat were all, on his account, spooks: fixed ideas that individuals were expected to serve. What remains after the demolition is the unique individual and their property, meaning whatever they can actually take and hold.

The attack was effective because it used the group's own method against them, and it was particularly awkward for anyone who, like Marx in the Paris manuscripts, had built an account of alienation on the concept of species-being. If species-being is a spook, the account of alienation as estrangement from species-being loses its ground.

The length of the reply in these manuscripts has been read in two ways. The unflattering reading is that Marx and Engels overreacted to a book whose reputation collapsed within a few years, and buried a valuable theoretical statement inside an enormous and now unreadable polemic. The more generous reading is that answering Stirner forced them to abandon the essentialist vocabulary of the Paris manuscripts and to replace it with an account of individuals as historically produced by their relations, which is precisely the theoretical advance the manuscripts contain. On the second reading the polemic is not a detour from the theory but the process by which the theory was arrived at, and the extracted material owes its existence to the argument it was extracted from.

Both readings can be held at once, and most careful commentators do hold both. The reply is far longer than it needed to be, and it produced something the shorter version would not have.

## What the manuscripts say about nature, and what they do not

A short section is warranted here because this passage is now cited far more often than it once was, in arguments about whether Marx had an ecological dimension.

The manuscripts contain a sharp criticism of Feuerbach for treating nature as a given. The nature that surrounds Feuerbach, the text argues, is not nature preceding human history but nature transformed by industry and commerce, altered by generations of human activity, so that even the cherry tree in his garden arrived through trade. The point is directed against a contemplative materialism that looks at the world and forgets that the world it is looking at has been made.

Two cautions apply. The passage establishes that Marx and Engels understood the human relation to nature as historical and mediated by production, which is a real and important commitment. It does not by itself establish an ecological position in any modern sense, and reading a theory of metabolic exchange back into 1846 requires much more than this passage supplies; the fuller material for that argument lies in the Paris manuscripts and in the treatment of soil exhaustion in the third volume of Capital. The second caution is that the passage has a polemical target and is written to embarrass Feuerbach rather than to state a considered position on the human relation to the natural world.

## Where the Feuerbach criticism actually lands

The criticism of Feuerbach in these manuscripts is more careful than its reputation, and reconstructing it properly is useful for anyone writing about the transition from Hegel to Marx.

Feuerbach is credited with the decisive move: reading theology as anthropology, showing that the predicates of God are alienated human predicates. He is criticised on three grounds. He conceives the human essence as an abstraction inherent in each isolated individual, rather than as the ensemble of social relations, which is the sixth thesis of the Feuerbach notes and the most quoted of them after the eleventh. He treats the sensuous world as an object of contemplation rather than as practical activity, which means he can register that people are alienated but not that they might do anything about it. And when he turns to history he falls back on precisely the idealism he has criticised elsewhere, so that his materialism and his history are two separate enterprises that do not meet.

That third criticism is the one that matters most for the theory being developed. Feuerbach's failure is not that he is insufficiently materialist about nature; it is that his materialism has no purchase on society, because he has no account of production. The passage where the manuscripts say that Feuerbach's conception of the sensuous world is confined to mere contemplation on one side and mere feeling on the other is the hinge of the whole critique, and a reader who grasps it will understand what the addition of production to materialism was supposed to accomplish. The full argument about the two conceptions of history is set out in the [comparison of historical materialism with Hegelian idealism](/2010/04/01/historical-materialism-vs-idealism/), which handles the Hegel side of the inheritance that this text is working through.

## What people look for in this text and do not find

Several phrases and doctrines are routinely attributed to these manuscripts and are not in them, and knowing the list saves time and prevents errors.

False consciousness is not here, and it is not Marx's phrase at all. It appears in a letter written by Engels in 1893.

The base and superstructure metaphor is not here in that form. The architectural image belongs to the 1859 preface. The manuscripts contain the substance of the claim about determination and about the derivative character of legal and political forms, but a student who quotes this text for the metaphor is quoting the wrong text, and the [treatment of base and superstructure](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/) works from the preface where the image actually occurs.

The five-stage sequence of modes of production is not here. What is here is a sequence of forms of ownership for western Europe, offered descriptively. The canonical five-stage scheme with primitive communism, slavery, feudalism, capitalism, and socialism was assembled much later, principally in Soviet expositions, and projecting it onto this text is one of the commonest anachronisms in student writing.

The religion-as-opium passage is not here either. It belongs to the introduction to the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, written two years earlier, and its meaning is very substantially altered by the sentences around it that almost every quotation omits.

Dialectical materialism as a named doctrine is not here. The term was coined after Marx's death, and the [complete guide to dialectical materialism](/2012/07/01/dialectical-materialism-complete-guide/) sets out its actual provenance.

## How the argument here differs from the mature statement

Setting these manuscripts beside the preface of 1859 shows what thirteen years of economic study did to the conception, and the differences are the most useful thing a student of Marx's development can carry out of this text.

The vocabulary changes. Forms of intercourse becomes relations of production. Forms of ownership becomes mode of production. The division of labour, which does almost all the explanatory work in 1846, is demoted in the mature theory to one aspect of the organisation of production, and property relations take its place at the centre. That demotion matters, because a theory built on the division of labour explains change through the growing complexity of tasks, while a theory built on property relations explains change through conflict over the appropriation of surplus, and those are different theories with different implications.

The mechanism of change is stated far more precisely in the later text. The manuscripts say that a contradiction arises between the productive forces and the existing form of intercourse, and that this contradiction issues in revolution. The preface specifies that the material productive forces come into conflict with the existing relations of production, which turn from forms of development into fetters, and that an era of social revolution then begins. The later formulation names the relation that changes and the point at which it changes; the earlier one gestures at it.

The historical scope narrows and sharpens. The manuscripts sweep from tribal ownership to modern industry in a few pages. The preface names four epochs in a single sentence and makes no claim that every society passes through them, and Marx spent his last decade explicitly refusing to convert his account of western European development into a universal law, most pointedly in a letter drafted in 1877 to a Russian journal.

The status of consciousness is handled with more care. The manuscripts say flatly that ideology has no history. The preface distinguishes between the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be determined with the precision of natural science, and the ideological forms in which people become conscious of the conflict and fight it out, which cannot. That distinction is a substantial retreat from the flat claim, and it is also a considerable improvement, since it locates ideology as the medium of struggle rather than as mere reflex.

A reader who notices all four differences has a genuine argument available about Marx's development, grounded in two texts rather than in a commentator's summary. That is the strongest reason to read the manuscripts at all.

## Studying this text for an examination

Examiners rarely set a question on The German Ideology as such. They set questions on the materialist conception of history, on ideology, or on the young Marx, and this text supplies evidence for all three. The mark-earning use is therefore evidential and precise rather than expository.

The single distinction that earns marks is between the manuscript and the doctrine. A candidate who writes that Marx set out his theory of history in The German Ideology has written what every other candidate has written. A candidate who writes that the conception was first developed at length in manuscripts of 1845 and 1846 which the authors abandoned and which were not published in full until the 1930s, so that the theory reached the movement through the Manifesto and the 1859 preface instead, has said something the marker has probably not read that morning and has demonstrated knowledge of the corpus rather than of a textbook.

The standard trap is the hunting and fishing passage, which candidates deploy either as evidence that Marx had a naive picture of communist society or as a charming illustration of human flourishing. Both uses invite the same response from a marker who knows the text. Handle it as an illustration of the abolition of fixed roles, note the polemical context, and move on quickly.

Two quotations are worth having verbatim, and both are short: the ruling ideas sentence, and the definition of communism as the real movement which abolishes the present state of things. Everything else should be paraphrased, because paraphrase of a draft is safer than quotation of a passage whose wording varies between editions. The [exam answer guide for historical materialism](/2010/05/01/historical-materialism-exam-answers/) sets out the answer structures in which this evidence is deployed, and this article deliberately leaves the structures to it.

## Teaching this text without losing the class

The misconception that dominates a seminar on these manuscripts is that the students are reading a book. They have bought an object with covers, a title page, and a first chapter, and everything about its physical form tells them it is a finished work. The correction has to be made before the reading, not after, or the discussion will proceed on the wrong footing and the more careful students will be confused by the discontinuities they notice.

The question that surfaces the misconception productively is to ask the class why the first chapter is about Feuerbach when Feuerbach is barely mentioned in it. Students who have read attentively will have noticed this and assumed it was their fault. Telling them that the heading was supplied by editors and that the material was extracted from a different chapter converts a source of confusion into the seminar's central problem, which is what the difference is between a draft and a work.

The extract that resolves it best is the ruling ideas passage, set beside the same claim in the 1859 preface and in a later text. Students can then see a formulation developing across a decade and can be asked which version they would cite in an essay and why. This is also the moment to introduce the distinction between evidential and canonical use of a source, which transfers to every other text they will study.

For a politically mixed class, the manuscripts are unusually safe ground, because the questions they raise are textual and methodological rather than programmatic. A seminar can spend two hours on how to read an unfinished manuscript without anyone having to declare a political position, and the discipline learned transfers directly to reading a religious text, a constitutional draft, or a set of cabinet papers.

## What a researcher must verify before citing this text

Three things need checking every time, and a fourth is worth checking once.

Check which arrangement your edition uses for the Feuerbach material, and say so in your citation. Two editions can print the same passage in positions that imply different arguments, and a reference that names only a chapter and a page number is not reproducible. Where the argument turns on sequence, cite the critical edition and identify the manuscript.

Check the authorship attribution for the passage you are using. Most of the surviving Feuerbach material is in Engels's hand, the Kuhlmann material is generally attributed to Hess, and the newest edition includes Weydemeyer among the contributors. Blanket attribution to Marx is common in the secondary literature and is not supportable.

Check the wording against the edition you are citing rather than against a commentator. The most quoted sentences in this text are also the most frequently misquoted, usually by conflation of nearby passages.

The fourth check, worth doing once and remembering, concerns the Theses on Feuerbach. The familiar text is Engels's edited version of 1888, and Marx's notebook original differs. If your argument rests on the wording of the eleventh thesis or the sixth, say which text you are using.

The unsafe claim in general circulation, and the one most likely to be repeated by a journalist working quickly, is that Marx wrote a book called The German Ideology setting out his theory of history. Each element of that sentence needs qualification and the sentence as a whole is misleading. The reliable material sits in the editorial apparatus of the Collected Works, in the critical edition's separate apparatus volume, and in the specialist literature on the composition history. A writer who needs one sentence that is safe can use this: the materialist conception of history was first worked out at length in manuscripts of 1845 and 1846 that the authors left unpublished. Keeping citation cards with the edition, manuscript, and wording attached to each quotation is exactly the discipline this text demands, and you can [save your notes and build a citation-linked reading list free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) so that a quotation never travels without its provenance.

## What to take away

The German Ideology is a working document of extraordinary value and a book of doubtful existence. The material that made it famous is a small, extracted, unarranged portion of a large polemic, and it contains the first sustained statement of a conception of history that its authors would spend the next thirty years refining and that reached the world through other texts entirely.

What is settled is the manuscript situation. No serious editor now defends the constructed chapter as an authorial artefact, and the newest critical edition presents the Feuerbach material as separate texts. What remains disputed is how far the deconstruction should go: whether the manuscripts should be published as a book at all, whether the ordering proposed by the newest edition is better than its predecessors, and how much of the theoretical unity readers have found in the Feuerbach material is in the manuscripts and how much was supplied by the arrangement.

For the reader, the practical position is stable regardless of how that dispute resolves. Read the Feuerbach material, read it as a draft, take the five load-bearing passages seriously, treat the polemics as a specialist resource, cite the edition and the manuscript, and go to the 1859 preface when you need a statement its author stood behind.

## The passage on revolution that most guides omit

One further passage deserves attention because it is rarely quoted and because it answers a question readers bring to Marx constantly: why revolution rather than reform, and what exactly is supposed to change.

The manuscripts argue that a revolution is necessary not only because the ruling class cannot be overthrown in any other way, but because the class overthrowing it can only in a revolution succeed in ridding itself of the accumulated deposit of the old society and become fit to found a new one. The claim is about the revolutionary class, not about its opponents. Its premise is the account of consciousness that the manuscripts have already established: if what people think is generated by the conditions under which they live and work, then a class formed under those conditions carries their imprint, and no amount of instruction can remove it, because instruction is itself one of the activities the conditions shape. What removes it is the practical transformation of the conditions, in which the people acting are altered by the acting.

Two consequences follow that are worth having available in an argument. The first is that this is a real answer to the standing objection that Marx expected an exploited and demoralised class to behave like a rational political agent. The answer is that he did not expect it in advance; the transformation of the class and the transformation of the circumstances are the same process, and neither is a precondition of the other. Whether that answer succeeds is a separate question, and it is one of the places where the theory is hardest to test, since a proposition that says the agent will be adequate only in the course of acting cannot be checked before the acting.

The second is that this passage rules out a reading of Marx that is otherwise tempting. If communist consciousness had to be produced by education before the transformation, the theory would need an educator standing outside the process, which is exactly the position the third thesis on Feuerbach attacks with the observation that the educator must himself be educated. The manuscripts and the Theses are consistent on this point, and the consistency is one of the better pieces of evidence that they belong to a single moment of Marx's thinking.

The related claim, that communist consciousness must be produced on a mass scale and that this requires a practical movement rather than a doctrine, is what separates the position from the utopian socialism of the period, which had a doctrine and a plan and expected adoption to follow from persuasion. Marx and Engels were attacking that expectation throughout these manuscripts, and the definition of communism as a real movement rather than an ideal is the positive statement of the same point.

A last practical word about buying the book. Because the abridged and the complete editions carry the same title, readers regularly order one expecting the other, and the disappointment runs in both directions: a student who wanted the theory receives eight hundred pages of Stirner, and a researcher who needed the Grün chapter receives a hundred pages that stop before it. Check the table of contents before purchase, and check specifically whether the second volume is present, since that is the fastest way to tell the two apart.

## A note on what the manuscripts do not settle

Three questions readers arrive with cannot be answered from this text, and knowing that in advance saves a fruitless search.

The manuscripts do not settle what determines what. The formulations range from strong statements that consciousness is nothing but conscious existence to careful accounts of the state as a form generated by conflicting interests, and a reader can find support for several positions. The determination question is worked out, so far as it ever was, in the correspondence of Engels in the 1890s and in a century of argument since, and the [debate over economic determinism](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/) lays out the spectrum of defensible positions rather than pretending the drafts resolve it.

They do not settle whether the sequence of forms of ownership is meant universally. The passage is descriptive and European, the later Marx explicitly refused to universalise his account of western development, and reading a universal law out of a draft written before he had studied any non-European society in depth is not defensible.

They do not settle the status of the individual. The manuscripts insist on real individuals as the premise and simultaneously insist that individuals are what their conditions of production make them, and the relation between those two claims is precisely what the tradition has argued about ever since, under headings from humanism to structuralism.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is The German Ideology about?

It is a polemic against the German philosophical scene of the 1840s, written by Marx and Engels between 1845 and 1846 and left unpublished. Its named targets are Ludwig Feuerbach, Bruno Bauer, Max Stirner, and the German writers who had grafted French socialist ideas onto Young Hegelian philosophy. The German ideology of the title is not ideology in general but that specific scene and its characteristic error, the belief that changing how people understand their conditions is the same as changing those conditions. Along the way, and mainly in material extracted from the attack on Stirner, the authors develop the first extended statement of the materialist conception of history: that human beings are what they produce and how they produce it, that forms of ownership track the development of the division of labour, and that the ruling ideas of an epoch are the ideas of its ruling class. That theoretical material is a minority of the text.

### Q: Why was The German Ideology not published in Marx's lifetime?

The authors could not find a publisher. The manuscript was long, censorship was a live obstacle in the German states, and the publishers they approached were sympathetic to the very writers being attacked, which made them reluctant to print several hundred pages of ridicule aimed at their own circle. Money ran out and the political situation moved on. One chapter did appear: the critique of Karl Grün was published under Marx's name in a monthly journal in August and September of 1847, after Marx had announced his intention to write it in a short declaration earlier that year. Everything else stayed in manuscript. In the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, Marx said they had left the manuscript to the gnawing criticism of the mice, and added that they had achieved their main purpose, which was clarifying their own ideas. That is the accurate description of the work's status: a self-clarification, not a suppressed masterpiece.

### Q: Which parts of The German Ideology should you actually read?

Read the Feuerbach material and almost nothing else, unless you have a specialist interest. Within it, five passages carry the argument: the statement of the premises, in which history begins from real individuals producing their means of life; the account of the division of labour and the succession of forms of ownership; the passage on consciousness and ideology, including the camera obscura image; the passage on ruling ideas; and the definition of communism as the real movement which abolishes the present state of things. Together these run to a few dozen pages and take an evening. The draft preface is short and worth a skim. The attacks on Bruno Bauer, on Max Stirner, and on the true socialists are of interest to specialists in the Young Hegelian movement and to researchers working on the composition history, and can be skipped by everyone else without losing anything an examiner or a seminar will ask about.

### Q: Is the Feuerbach chapter of The German Ideology a real chapter?

No. There is no continuous manuscript in either author's hand headed "I. Feuerbach" running from a beginning to an end. What survives is a set of separate pieces, including material the authors themselves extracted from the polemic against Stirner and set aside for a projected opening chapter, with two different pagination systems, marginal additions, deletions, and passages that break off. The newest critical edition treats this material as seven distinct texts, the largest of which is itself a composite. At least seven different orderings have been proposed by serious editors. The chapter you can read in an ordinary edition is therefore an editorial construction, defensible as a solution to a real problem but not authorial. The practical consequence is that any argument leaning on the order or completeness of the chapter is leaning on an editor, while arguments about what the individual passages say are unaffected.

### Q: How long does The German Ideology take to read?

The Feuerbach material, which is what almost everybody means by the work, takes about two hours with notes, or an evening at a comfortable pace. The abridged student editions that print only this material can be read in a single sitting. The complete text is a different proposition: including the attacks on Bauer, Stirner, and the true socialists, it takes most readers somewhere between twenty and thirty hours, and the great bulk of that time is spent on the Stirner polemic, which quotes its target constantly and answers in puns. That investment is worthwhile for researchers working on the Young Hegelians, on the history of anarchism and individualism, or on the composition of the manuscripts themselves. For a student preparing an essay on historical materialism or on ideology, the two-hour route delivers everything the text is cited for.

### Q: What does the hunting and fishing passage mean?

It describes a society in which nobody has one exclusive sphere of activity, so that a person might hunt in the morning, fish in the afternoon, rear cattle in the evening, and criticise after dinner, without becoming a hunter, a fisherman, a shepherd, or a critic. Three points make it usable. The final term is a joke at the expense of Bruno Bauer's circle, who called themselves critics and thought criticism was world-changing labour. The passage sits inside an argument about how the division of labour fixes a person's activity into a role that then confronts them as an alien power, so it is illustrating the absence of fixation rather than proposing a schedule. And it belongs to the extracted material whose arrangement is editorial. Quote it briefly, name the polemical context, and say what it illustrates. Presenting it as Marx's plan for organising labour and then pointing out that it would not work attacks a position nobody held.

### Q: Which edition of The German Ideology is best?

That depends on the use. For coursework and general reading, an abridged edition printing only the Feuerbach material is sufficient, short, and inexpensive, and the widely used classroom edition of that kind is honest about being an abridgement. For citation in serious writing, use the complete English text in the fifth volume of the Collected Works of Marx and Engels, which contains both volumes of the work, has a substantial editorial apparatus, and has stable pagination available in most libraries. For any argument that turns on the order or completeness of the Feuerbach material, work from the German critical edition, which presents the manuscripts and printings with a separate apparatus volume and does not reconstruct an opening chapter, or from the English presentation of the Feuerbach manuscripts as discontinuous fragments. Whichever you use, name it, because editions of this text differ in content and arrangement rather than only in translation.

### Q: What does The German Ideology say about ruling ideas?

It contains the sentence "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas," and the surrounding argument gives that claim its content. The class that controls the means of material production controls the means of mental production at the same time, so those without access to the means of mental production are on the whole subject to its ideas. And because a ruling class has to present its particular interest as the common interest of society, it gives its ideas the form of universality and represents them as the only rational and universally valid ones. Three elements are usually lost in quotation: the passage names a mechanism, namely institutional control over the means by which ideas are produced and circulated; it claims a distribution of capacity rather than universal belief; and it identifies universality as the characteristic form ruling ideas take. That third element is the seed of the later theory of hegemony.

### Q: Who was Max Stirner and why does The German Ideology attack him?

Max Stirner was the pen name of Johann Kaspar Schmidt, a Berlin schoolteacher and member of the Young Hegelian circle, whose book The Ego and Its Own appeared at the end of 1844. His argument turned the group's own method against itself: if religion is a human creation projected outwards and worshipped, then so are humanity, morality, the state, society, and the human essence that Feuerbach had substituted for God. All of these he called spooks, fixed ideas imposed on the concrete individual. The attack was awkward for Marx because his earlier account of alienation relied on species-being, which Stirner had classified as one more abstraction. The reply in these manuscripts is by far the longest section of the work and proceeds line by line through Stirner's book. Its scale is a measure of how seriously the challenge was taken, and answering it pushed Marx and Engels towards an account of individuals as historically produced by their relations.

### Q: What should you read before The German Ideology?

Read the eleven Theses on Feuerbach first. They take ten minutes and state the same criticism in compressed form, particularly the charge that earlier materialism treated the world as an object of contemplation rather than as practical activity. A short account of Feuerbach helps: what matters is his method of reading theology as disguised anthropology, since Marx took that structure and applied it to politics and economics. A paragraph on Stirner is enough to explain why so much of the manuscript is devoted to him. If you have time for one more text, read the introduction to the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, which shows the method being applied to religion and politics two years earlier. What you do not need is prior knowledge of Hegel's system in detail; the manuscripts are attacking Hegel's followers rather than expounding Hegel, and the necessary background is the scene, not the philosophy.

### Q: Did Marx and Engels write The German Ideology together?

They composed it together, but the surviving evidence complicates simple attribution. Most of the Feuerbach material is in Engels's handwriting, while Engels insisted for the rest of his life that the materialist conception of history was Marx's discovery. Explanations on offer include that Engels was the faster writer and held the pen, that Marx dictated while Engels wrote, and that Engels's later modesty was political rather than accurate. None has been settled, and the editors of the newest critical edition did not adopt the dictation hypothesis. Beyond the two named authors, Moses Hess is generally credited with the section on Georg Kuhlmann in the second volume, and the newest edition adds Joseph Weydemeyer, who made fair copies, among the contributors. The practical rule is to write "Marx and Engels argue" for the jointly composed material and to state your evidence whenever an argument depends on which of them held a view.

### Q: Is The German Ideology the same as the Theses on Feuerbach?

No, though the two are constantly run together. The Theses are eleven short notes Marx jotted in a notebook in the spring of 1845, immediately before the manuscripts were drafted. They were never prepared for publication. Engels found them after Marx's death and published them in 1888 as an appendix to his own book on Feuerbach, editing the German as he went, so the familiar text is Engels's version and Marx's notebook original differs in wording. The manuscripts are a separate and far longer project, begun months later, with different targets and a developed argument. The Theses contain the criticism of contemplative materialism in aphoristic form; the manuscripts contain the positive theory that criticism was clearing space for. Cite them separately, and if your argument rests on the wording of a particular thesis, say which of the two texts you are quoting.

### Q: How do you reference The German Ideology when editions differ?

Name the edition, and where the argument depends on sequence, name the manuscript as well. A reference of the form "The German Ideology, ch. 1" is not reproducible, because the chapter is an editorial construction and different editions place the same passage in different positions or exclude it from the chapter entirely. For most purposes, citing the complete text in the fifth volume of the Collected Works with a page number is sufficient and stable. For research where the composition history is at issue, cite the German critical edition and identify which of the Feuerbach texts the passage belongs to. If you are quoting the Theses on Feuerbach, state whether you are using Engels's published version or Marx's notebook text. Keeping edition, manuscript, and exact wording attached to every quotation card is the habit that prevents these errors from surfacing at viva or peer review.

### Q: Which passages from The German Ideology are safe to quote in an essay?

Two are short, stable across editions, and immediately recognisable to a marker: the sentence that the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, and the definition of communism as the real movement which abolishes the present state of things. Both can be quoted in under fifteen words and both do real argumentative work. The premises passage, the camera obscura image, and the account of the division of labour are better paraphrased than quoted, because their wording varies between translations and the surrounding sentences matter. Avoid quoting the hunting and fishing passage unless you are going to explain its polemical context, and avoid quoting anything from the Stirner polemic unless you are certain the sentence expresses the authors' view rather than ventriloquising their target. Paraphrase of a draft is almost always safer than quotation of it.

### Q: What does The German Ideology say about the state?

It offers an account of the state that Marx never restated at comparable length. Because the division of labour produces a conflict between the interest of each individual and the common interest of all who deal with one another, the common interest takes on an independent form separated from real individuals, and does so as the state. The manuscripts call this an illusory community. Two features of the argument are worth carrying. It is structural rather than conspiratorial: the state is not merely an instrument that a ruling class picks up, but the form the common interest necessarily takes when interests conflict, which is why it can appear genuinely universal to the people living under it. And the illusion is nobody's lie; it is a real appearance produced by the separation. Later Marxist theories of the state divide largely on how much weight to put on the instrumental and the structural halves of this account.

### Q: Is The German Ideology hard to read?

The theoretical passages are not; they are among the most vivid and direct pages Marx and Engels ever wrote, and a reader with no philosophical training can follow them. The polemical bulk of the work is genuinely difficult, for reasons that have nothing to do with conceptual depth. It quotes at length from books nobody now reads, it answers in elaborate puns and mock-ecclesiastical jokes that were funny to a small circle in 1846, its section headings canonise its targets as saints, and it assumes familiarity with a set of quarrels that ended before the revolutions of 1848. The difficulty is contextual rather than philosophical. A reader who buys the complete text and starts at page one will meet the hard material first and the valuable material never, which is the most common reason people report bouncing off this work.

### Q: Where does the title The German Ideology come from?

The title was devised late, after the original plan for a journal collapsed and the material was being reorganised as a two-volume book, and Marx referred to the projected book by that name in print in the spring of 1847. Neither the title of the whole nor the headings of the two volumes survive in the manuscript itself, which is why they are known from Marx's separate declaration rather than from a title page. The phrase names its target rather than its subject: German ideology means the philosophical scene in the German states and its characteristic habit of treating ideas as the motor of history. Readers who assume the title announces a general theory of ideology are reading a later meaning of the word back into it, and the general concept, though partly built from material in these manuscripts, is not what the phrase is naming.

### Q: Does The German Ideology contain the base and superstructure model?

It contains the substance of the claim without the image. The manuscripts argue that legal and political forms arise from the way production is organised, that consciousness is determined by life rather than the reverse, and that the ruling ideas of an epoch belong to its ruling class. What they do not contain is the architectural metaphor of a real foundation with a legal and political superstructure rising on it, which belongs to the preface of 1859. A student who cites this work for the metaphor is citing the wrong text. The difference is not merely bibliographic: the preface's formulation is tighter, distinguishes the economic transformation from the ideological forms in which conflict is fought out, and was published by Marx himself, which makes it the citation of choice whenever an argument needs an authorial statement rather than a working draft.
