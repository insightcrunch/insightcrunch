The Modi era presents a puzzle that party labels cannot solve. Between 1998 and 2004, a government led by the Bharatiya Janata Party under Atal Bihari Vajpayee administered India for six years, and between May 2014 and the verdict of May 2019 a second BJP-led government under Narendra Modi administered the same republic. Both governments drew on the same party organisation, the same Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh cadre base, and the same ideological inheritance. Yet the two periods differ in kind rather than in degree, and the difference is the subject of this article. The claim defended here is precise: the Modi era is the first deliberate, named attempt to renegotiate all three permanent tensions of the founding settlement at once, the tension between religion and state, the tension between the federal and the unitary in the constitutional design, and the tension between rights and development, where the Vajpayee government engaged the same inheritance one case at a time and set its own party's three core commitments aside in order to govern at all.

## Two Governments of One Party: The Puzzle of Substantive Distinctness

The shared inheritance is real, and any serious account begins by conceding it. The BJP of 2014 was the BJP of 1998 in organisational descent: the party founded in April 1980, led to power in the states during the early 1990s, and carried into national office by the National Democratic Alliance in March 1998. The RSS, founded at Nagpur in 1925, supplied both governments with cadres, campaigners and ideological formation. Vajpayee and Modi belonged to the same political family, and Modi had been a pracharak of the RSS before his secondment to the BJP in the late 1980s and his appointment as Chief Minister of Gujarat in October 2001. A reader who stopped at the level of affiliation would predict continuity of governing method, and most newspaper commentary in May 2014 did predict exactly that, expecting a second Vajpayee period conducted with greater numerical strength.

The numbers alone break the prediction. Vajpayee never commanded the Lok Sabha. The BJP won 182 seats in the election of February and March 1998 and 182 again in September and October 1999, and on both occasions the party governed because a ring of regional allies, from the Telugu Desam Party of N. Chandrababu Naidu to the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam of J. Jayalalithaa in the first formation and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in the second, chose to sustain it. The dependence was not decorative. When Jayalalithaa withdrew support in April 1999, the Vajpayee government lost a confidence motion on 17 April 1999 by a single vote, 269 to 270, and fell. Modi's BJP, by contrast, won 282 seats in May 2014 on 31 percent of the national vote, the first single-party majority in the Lok Sabha since the Congress victory of 1984, and improved the tally to 303 seats in May 2019 on 37.4 percent of the vote. The arithmetic was a structural fact, not a stylistic one: a prime minister with 303 seats behind him faces a different constitution in practice from a prime minister who must renegotiate his survival with alliance partners every quarter.

### How large were the BJP majorities of 2014 and 2019, and why do the two numbers carry different weight?

The BJP won 282 Lok Sabha seats in May 2014 and 303 in May 2019, the first party since the Congress of 1984 to hold a single-party majority, and the first to renew that majority with a larger tally. The 2014 figure broke the coalition arithmetic of the post-1989 era; the 2019 figure converted a breakthrough into a ratified settlement.

The deeper difference lies in what each government did with the party's own programme. The BJP's inherited commitments were neither secret nor recent. The party and its predecessor, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh founded in 1951, had carried three signature pledges across decades: the abrogation of Article 370 and the full constitutional integration of Jammu and Kashmir, a uniform civil code for all citizens in place of religion-specific personal law, and the construction of a Ram temple at Ayodhya on the site of the Babri Masjid, demolished by a mobilised crowd on 6 December 1992. These pledges appeared in manifesto after manifesto. In March 1998, before taking office, the NDA issued its National Agenda for Governance, a negotiated common document in which the three commitments were expressly set aside. The agenda recorded that the allies would not pursue the abrogation of Article 370, would not enact a uniform civil code, and would seek a negotiated or judicial resolution at Ayodhya. The deferral was the price of the coalition, paid openly and in writing. The Vajpayee governments kept the bargain for six years: the nuclear tests at Pokhran on 11 and 13 May 1998, the Lahore bus journey of February 1999, the Kargil war of May to July 1999, disinvestment, highway construction through the Golden Quadrilateral announced in 1998, and a foreign policy of engagement with the United States, all proceeded, and the three pledges waited in the manifesto of a party that had suspended them.

The Modi government reversed the sequence. It treated the three inherited commitments not as embarrassments to be managed but as the spine of a programme, and it executed the two that executive and legislative action could reach: Article 370 was effectively abrogated in August 2019 through a presidential order and a parliamentary statute, and the Ayodhya question was closed by a Supreme Court verdict in November 2019 followed by the construction and consecration of the temple in January 2024. The uniform civil code advanced at the state level, with Uttarakhand enacting its code in February 2024. Around these headline acts, the government performed a parallel reconstruction in fiscal federalism and in the welfare architecture that no reading of the era as ordinary strong government can absorb. The argument of this article is that the pattern across all three domains, religion and state, centre and states, rights and development, is one integrated renegotiation, and that the simultaneity is the era's defining institutional fact.

The pattern also distinguishes the period from the last Indian government that held comparable numerical strength. The Congress government of Rajiv Gandhi after December 1984 held 404 seats, a majority far larger than Modi's, and used its strength episodically, reversing a Supreme Court judgment by statute in the Shah Bano affair of 1986 and unlocking the Ayodhya site the same year, without presenting the moves as parts of a named design. The Modi government, by contrast, named its design repeatedly: in the BJP manifesto of April 2014, in the address of the President to Parliament, and in the legislative sequence itself, which moved from the economic architecture of 2016 and 2017 to the constitutional architecture of 2019. The hub article of the coalition era in this series records how [the coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) treated each settlement as provisional and negotiated; the Modi era inherited that terrain and attempted to make its own settlements final and simultaneous.

## The Settlement Being Renegotiated: Three Tensions from the Founding

The three-tensions framework used across this series originates at the founding. When the Constituent Assembly completed its work in November 1949, it left three questions answered provisionally rather than permanently, because the Assembly's own members could not agree on final answers and chose workable settlements instead. The first concerned religion and the state: whether the Indian republic would practise equidistance among religions, a distinctive Indian secularism in which the state could intervene in religious practice for reform, or whether it would drift toward recognising one tradition as the cultural centre of the nation. The Assembly adopted equidistance with interventionist licence, protecting religious freedom in Articles 25 to 28 and minority educational rights in Article 30, and then immediately used the interventionist licence for the Hindu code reforms of 1955 and 1956. The second concerned federalism: whether India was a federation of sovereign-leaning states or a union with a dominant centre. The Constitution answered with a union whose centre held residuary powers, the power to redraw state boundaries under Article 3, and the power to dismiss state governments under Article 356, a design its defenders called federal with a unitary bias. The third concerned rights and development: whether the enforceable fundamental rights of Part III or the non-enforceable developmental directives of Part IV would govern the state's use of property and resources, a contest Parliament and the courts fought through the property amendments of 1951 onward and the basic structure doctrine of 1973.

The founding hub of this series traces [the three founding tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) through the Assembly's debates, and the Nehruvian hub records how the settlement hardened into practice between 1947 and 1964. The settlement that emerged from those decades had a recognisable shape by 2014. On religion and state, equidistance in rhetoric combined with asymmetric intervention in practice, deep reform of Hindu personal law in the 1950s and a politically costly protection of Muslim personal law in 1986. On federalism, a strong centre bargained with increasingly assertive states, and after 1989 coalition arithmetic at the centre gave regional parties a veto inside national government itself. On rights and development, the post-1991 liberalisation shifted the developmental state from production toward regulation and facilitation, and the United Progressive Alliance governments of 2004 to 2014 added a rights-based welfare layer, employment guarantee in 2005, education in 2009, food security in 2013, built on statute and entitlement.

### What settlement did the Modi government inherit on each of the three tensions in May 2014?

On religion and state, it inherited Nehruvian equidistance, with Hindu personal law codified in the 1950s and Muslim personal law protected by the 1986 statute. On federalism, it inherited coalition-era bargaining, in which regional parties held ministries and vetoes. On rights and development, it inherited the UPA's entitlements, the employment, food security and education acts over a liberalised economy.

Each element of the inherited settlement was the product of a named historical process rather than an accident. The religion-state settlement carried the signature of Jawaharlal Nehru's government and the 1950s: the Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 and its companion codes reformed the majority community's family law by legislation, an intervention no government attempted against minority personal law with equal force, and the asymmetry became a standing grievance in BJP politics for three decades. The federal settlement carried the signature of the coalition decades, when the fall of governments in 1989, 1990, 1996, 1997 and 1999 taught every national party that state-level partners could not be treated as subordinates. The rights-development settlement carried the signature of the Sonia Gandhi-led National Advisory Council period within the UPA, in which welfare was reframed as legal entitlement enforceable against the state.

The three-tensions reading holds that the Modi government's distinctness lies in refusing to treat any of these inheritances as settled, and in moving against all three within a single parliamentary term and its successor. The claim is empirical and checkable. It does not require the reader to approve or deplore the renegotiation; it requires the reader to test whether the decisions cluster in the three domains, whether they were announced as connected parts of a programme, and whether their institutional consequences point in a consistent direction on each tension. The sections that follow conduct that test on the legislative and executive record: the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order of 5 August 2019 and the Reorganisation Act that followed it, the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of December 2019, the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act of 2016 that built the goods and services tax, the demonetisation order of 8 November 2016, and the reconstruction of welfare delivery between 2014 and 2020. Each act is dated, named and traceable to a text, and the cumulative direction on each tension is the analytical substance that treatments organised by ministry or by controversy tend to fragment.

## Religion and State: The First Tension Renegotiated

The renegotiation of the religion-state settlement opened late and moved fast, and its sequence was the reverse of what the government's critics and supporters both expected in 2014. For five years the first Modi government left the inherited religious settlement largely untouched in law, concentrating on the fiscal and welfare architecture. The movement came in a compressed sequence after the second verdict of May 2019: the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act received assent on 31 July 2019 and criminalised instant triple talaq; on 5 August 2019 the President issued the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 2019, which applied the entire Indian Constitution to the state and neutralised Article 370; on 9 November 2019 a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court delivered the Ayodhya title verdict; and on 11 December 2019 Parliament passed the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, which received presidential assent on 12 December 2019. Within five months of the renewed mandate, the three most consequential elements of the inherited religion-state settlement had each been addressed by statute, order or judgment.

The Article 370 sequence requires precision, because its legal engineering is the strongest single exhibit for the claim that the renegotiation was deliberate rather than opportunistic. Article 370, placed in Part XXI of the Constitution as a temporary provision, limited Parliament's power over Jammu and Kashmir and allowed the state its own constitution. The order of 5 August 2019, Constitution Order 272, used the interpretation clause of Article 367 to read references to the state's defunct Constituent Assembly as references to its Legislative Assembly, and references to the state government as references to the Governor, who at that moment administered the state under President's Rule. On that substituted reading, the Governor's concurrence sufficed to apply all provisions of the Indian Constitution to the state, which rendered Article 370 inert. Constitution Order 273 of 6 August 2019 then declared that Article 370 would cease to operate. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, assented to on 9 August 2019 and effective from 31 October 2019, divided the state into two Union Territories, Jammu and Kashmir with a legislature and Ladakh without one. A state disappeared from the federal map by parliamentary statute for the first time since the reorganisation era, and the Supreme Court upheld the abrogation in its judgment of 11 December 2023, accepting the Union's undertaking that statehood would be restored and elections held.

Defenders of the August 2019 action, in the government's own statements and in sympathetic commentary, framed the sequence as integration completed: Home Minister Amit Shah, moving the resolutions in the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2019, argued that Article 370 had obstructed the extension of Indian law, investment and reservation benefits to the state's own residents, and that its removal fulfilled a commitment running from Shyama Prasad Mukherjee's campaign of 1952 and 1953 through every BJP manifesto. Critics of the action, among them historians and Kashmiri political parties across the spectrum, framed the same sequence at its strongest as constitutional hardball: a provision whose alteration the text tied to the concurrence of the state's own elected institutions was altered at a moment when those institutions had been suspended, and a state was demoted to a Union Territory without the consent of any sitting legislature of that state. Both characterisations are stated here at their strongest because the hub's task is analytical: whether the reader accepts the integration reading or the hardball reading, the act belongs to the religion-state tension, because Article 370 was the constitutional marker of a Muslim-majority state's negotiated terms of belonging within the Union, and its removal redefined those terms unilaterally. A fuller anatomy of the August 2019 sequence and its consequences is reserved for this pillar's dedicated treatment of the abrogation; this hub records the act as the first column of the renegotiation.

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2019 performed the renegotiation in the register of membership. The Citizenship Act of 1955 had defined citizenship by birth, descent and naturalisation on formally religion-neutral terms. The 2019 amendment inserted a proviso into section 2 of the principal Act under which persons belonging to the Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014 would not be treated as illegal migrants, and it shortened their residence requirement for naturalisation from eleven years to five. The statement of objects and reasons named religious persecution in the three named states as the ground. The amendment's passage coincided with the completion of the National Register of Citizens in Assam, whose final list of 31 August 2019 excluded approximately 1.9 million applicants, and with statements by senior BJP leaders, including Amit Shah's description in April 2019 of a nationwide register to follow, which connected the two instruments in public argument.

The strongest form of the government's case, presented by Shah in the Lok Sabha on 9 December 2019, held that the amendment was a bounded humanitarian correction: the three named states were constitutionally Islamic or had documented records of persecution of the named minorities, the amendment excluded no Indian citizen of any faith from any right, and the classification followed the logic of the Nehru-Liaquat Pact of April 1950, which had committed both dominions to protect minorities stranded by Partition. The strongest form of the critics' case, presented in Parliament by opposition members and in petitions filed before the Supreme Court, held that a citizenship statute sorting claimants by religion for the first time in the republic's history breached the secular character the founding settlement had protected, that the exclusion of Muslim claimants from the fast track could not be reconciled with the persecution rationale because persecuted Muslim sects existed in the named states, and that a religion-sorted citizenship law paired with a population register shifted the burden of proving belonging onto Indian Muslims. The hub's analytic claim survives both framings: prior governments had adjusted citizenship law on grounds of territory and descent, and the government of 2019 adjusted it on the named ground of religion, which is a renegotiation of the first tension by legislative text whatever verdict the courts eventually deliver on its validity.

Ayodhya completed the triad through the courts rather than the legislature. The five-judge bench headed by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi delivered its unanimous verdict on 9 November 2019, awarding the disputed site at Ayodhya to the deity Ram Lalla through a trust to be created by the government, and directing that five acres be allotted at a prominent place in Ayodhya for the construction of a mosque. The verdict closed a title dispute whose modern phase began with the placement of idols inside the Babri Masjid in December 1949 and ran through the mosque's demolition on 6 December 1992 and the Liberhan Commission's report of 2009. The government accepted the verdict as closure, created the Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra trust in February 2020, and presided over the temple's consecration on 22 January 2024, an event the Prime Minister attended as chief participant in the ritual. Defenders read the sequence as the lawful resolution of the movement's central demand, achieved through the judiciary rather than the street; critics read the consecration's fusion of state and ritual office, a prime minister performing the central ceremonial role in a religious rite, as a public redrawing of the boundary between state and religion that equidistance had policed. Both readings agree on the fact the framework needs: a question the founding generation left to litigation, and the Vajpayee generation left to the courts without state participation in the outcome, was closed with the state as celebrant.

The pattern extends past the three celebrated cases. The Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code, enacted in February 2024, carried the third inherited pledge into law at the state level, establishing a common code for marriage, divorce, succession and live-in relationships for all residents of the state regardless of religion, with exemptions for Scheduled Tribes. The Waqf (Amendment) Bill, introduced in the Lok Sabha in August 2024 and passed by Parliament in April 2025, restructured the administration of Muslim endowment property, altering the composition of waqf boards and the evidentiary treatment of waqf claims. Marriage-age and personal-law questions in the Hindu code tradition were adjusted through the period without a comparably public contest. Read one file at a time, each measure had a local justification; read together, the files describe a settlement moving from equidistance with asymmetric intervention toward a framework in which the majority tradition supplies the public grammar of the state and minority institutions are administered as objects of regulation. Critics of the government name that destination majoritarian; defenders name it the correction of a pseudo-secular asymmetry, in the vocabulary the BJP has used since the 1980s; the framework names it the renegotiation of the first tension, and the pillar's subsequent articles test the description domain by domain.

## Centre and States: The Federal-Unitary Tension Rebuilt

The second renegotiation began earlier and worked through architecture rather than symbolism, and for that reason it is the column of the pattern that treatments organised by controversy most often miss. The goods and services tax restructured the fiscal constitution. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, assented to by President Pranab Mukherjee on 8 September 2016 after ratification by more than half the state legislatures, inserted Article 246A, empowering Parliament and state legislatures to tax goods and services concurrently, and Article 279A, creating the GST Council as a permanent constitutional body in which the Union Finance Minister chairs and state finance ministers sit. The tax itself commenced on 1 July 2017, subsuming central excise, service tax, state value added tax, octroi and entry tax into a dual levy administered through a common portal. The Council's voting weights encode the settlement's precise balance: the Union holds one third of the vote, the states together two thirds, and decisions require a three fourths majority, which gives the Union a veto and denies the states the power to act over the Union's objection. The GST (Compensation to States) Act of 2017 guaranteed each state 14 percent annual growth in subsumed revenue for five years from a compensation fund, the consideration for state consent to surrendering independent taxation.

Defenders of the design, in the tradition of the Thirteenth Finance Commission chaired by Vijay Kelkar whose 2009 report first drafted the grand bargain, describe the GST as cooperative federalism's largest achievement: states traded fiscal autonomy for a seat in a body where national tax rates cannot change without them, and the Union traded unilateral control of indirect taxation for a common national market. Critics, among them finance ministers of opposition-ruled states during the compensation dispute of 2020, describe the same body as a ratchet: rates and rules are set in a forum the Union chairs and can block, the compensation guarantee expired in June 2022 despite the disruption of the pandemic years, and states emerged with narrower independent tax handles than any Indian state had held since 1950, dependent on transfers whose timing the Union influences. The dispute of 2020, in which the Union initially proposed that states borrow to cover the compensation gap before conceding a special borrowing window, is the episode each side cites. For the framework, the verdict to record is narrower than either: fiscal centralisation under the Modi government proceeded not by the Indira-era method of dismissal and imposition but by building a constitutional body whose design locks the centre's primacy inside a form the states consented to ratify. That is a renegotiation of the federal bargain, and its method, architecture rather than coercion, recurs.

### Which taxes lost their separate existence when the goods and services tax began in July 2017?

The launch of the goods and services tax in July 2017 subsumed central excise duty, service tax, customs duties, state value added tax, octroi, entry tax, purchase tax, luxury tax and entertainment tax into a single dual levy on goods and services. Petroleum products, alcohol for human consumption and electricity stayed outside the new tax, inside the older state powers.

Demonetisation displayed the other face of the federal renegotiation: the centre's capacity for unilateral action over the heads of every state government at once. On the evening of 8 November 2016, the Prime Minister announced that currency notes of 500 and 1000 rupees would cease to be legal tender at midnight, withdrawing approximately 86 percent of the currency in circulation by value. The decision had been taken by the Union Cabinet that same evening, on the recommendation of the central board of the Reserve Bank of India delivered the same day, and state governments learned of it from the broadcast. Its fiscal and monetary incidence fell on every state economy, its administration fell on bank branches and district administrations that states staffed, and its political defence fell to the Union alone. The Reserve Bank's annual report for 2017 and 2018, published in August 2018, recorded that 99.3 percent of the specified notes, by value, had returned to the banking system, a figure that structured the subsequent argument: critics, including former Reserve Bank Governor Urjit Patel's successors in public debate and the Congress leadership, treated the return rate as proof that the stated aim of extinguishing unaccounted cash had failed; the government's defenders treated the same figures as evidence of formalisation, pointing to growth in direct tax filings and digital payments in the following years. The hub's framework interest in demonetisation is neither verdict but the form of the act: a decision with economy-wide consequences, taken at the centre without consultation of the states, executed simultaneously in every district, and defended as a national moral project. Indira Gandhi's bank nationalisation of July 1969 is the nearest precedent for the form, and the pillar's treatment of demonetisation examines the comparison in full.

Institutional redesign completed the federal column. The Planning Commission, created by a cabinet resolution of March 1950 and chaired by every prime minister from Nehru onward, had allocated plan assistance to states through a formula and a negotiating ritual that state governments experienced as supplication. The Modi government abolished it and created NITI Aayog by a Union Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015, stripping the new body of allocation power and recasting it as a forum for what the resolution called cooperative federalism, with a Governing Council of all chief ministers and lieutenant governors that first met on 8 February 2015. Fiscal transfer itself moved to the Finance Commission channel, and the Fourteenth Finance Commission under Y. V. Reddy raised the states' share of the divisible tax pool from 32 to 42 percent for the period from 2015, a devolution the government accepted and cited as federalist intent. The combination is the federal renegotiation in miniature: the centre surrendered a discretionary lever of the planning era and enlarged formula transfers, and at the same time built tax architecture and executive habits that concentrated rate-setting and crisis decision at the centre. State governments of opposition parties experienced the period as centralisation; the Union government described the same record as the replacement of discretionary hierarchy with rule-bound partnership. The three-tensions reading records the direction of the institutional net movement, which runs toward a centre that designs the systems inside which states operate, and reserves the pillar's later articles for the contests the design provoked.

## Rights and Development: The Third Tension Rebuilt on Platforms

The third renegotiation replaced the developmental state's delivery machinery, and its founding document was a bank account. The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, launched on 28 August 2014, opened zero-balance accounts at a pace the Guinness records certified, and by the end of the government's first term the programme counted its accounts in the hundreds of millions. Jan Dhan by itself was financial inclusion of a familiar kind. Its significance in the framework is architectural: joined to the Aadhaar unique identity number, whose statutory basis the Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies) Act supplied in March 2016, and to the mobile telephone, the account formed the three-part stack the government called JAM, Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile, through which subsidies and benefits could travel directly from the Union treasury to a named citizen, bypassing the district and block bureaucracies and the state-level intermediaries who had administered the older developmental state. The Unified Payments Interface, launched by the National Payments Corporation of India in 2016, extended the stack into daily transactions, and the architecture later carried the emergency cash transfers of 2020 and the free grain distribution of the pandemic years.

The renegotiation's direction on this tension is easiest to see against the settlement it displaced. The UPA's rights-based model had made the citizen a claimant against the state: the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act of 2005 gave a rural household a justiciable right to one hundred days of wage employment, the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act of 2009 made schooling an enforceable entitlement, and the National Food Security Act of 2013 converted subsidised grain into a legal right for two thirds of the population. The Modi government's model made the citizen a beneficiary inside a platform: the entitlements largely continued, the employment guarantee was retained and funded, the food security framework was carried into the pandemic expansion of free grain, but the government's own programme-building energy went into delivery systems and asset schemes administered as missions, the Ujjwala programme launched at Ballia on 1 May 2016 that distributed deposit-free liquefied petroleum gas connections to women in poor households, the housing programme consolidated under the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana for rural areas from 2016, the Swachh Bharat Mission's toilet construction drive from October 2014, and piped water under the Jal Jeevan Mission from 2019. The vocabulary difference is the institutional difference: rights vest in citizens and survive governments; missions belong to governments and report to missions' directors, and a delivery architecture built on a biometric identity and a bank account recentralises the citizen-state relationship at the level of the Union even as it disintermediates the local state.

### What did the Reserve Bank of India eventually report about the currency notes withdrawn in November 2016?

The Reserve Bank of India's annual report for 2017 and 2018, published in August 2018, recorded that 99.3 percent of the demonetised notes, by value, had returned to the banking system. The figure became the central exhibit in the argument over the decision's stated purpose: proof for critics that unaccounted cash had survived, and for defenders a measure of formalisation.

The productive economy received a parallel reconstruction whose pieces are individually familiar and jointly directional. Make in India, launched on 25 September 2014, organised industrial policy around twenty-five sectors and an invitation to foreign manufacturers. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 created a creditor-led resolution process that displaced the older sick-companies regime. Start-up India, launched on 16 January 2016, built a recognition and tax framework for new firms. The four labour codes, the Code on Wages of 2019 and the Industrial Relations Code, the Code on Social Security and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code of 2020, consolidated twenty-nine central labour statutes into four texts, a legislative consolidation the rights-era parliaments had discussed for decades without completing. Atmanirbhar Bharat, announced by the Prime Minister on 12 May 2020 during the pandemic, reframed self-reliance as the organising slogan of industrial and trade policy, and production-linked incentive schemes from 2020 paid firms for output in named sectors. Defenders of the record describe a state learning to build platforms and frameworks rather than factories; critics describe the same record, citing the manufacturing share of output that did not rise toward the announced 25 percent target, as rhetoric outrunning outcomes. The framework records the institutional substitution, which is not in dispute between the two readings: the instruments of the third tension moved from public production and statutory entitlement toward platform delivery and incentivised private production, and the substitution was announced as a philosophy rather than accumulated as improvisation.

## The Renegotiation Map: One Pattern Across Three Tensions

The hub's findable artifact assembles the preceding sections into a single citable object. The Three-Tensions Renegotiation Map for the post-2014 period sets each founding tension in a row, names the specific decisions that renegotiated it, and records the institutional consequence each decision produced, so that a reader can test the integration claim row by row rather than accept it as an impression. The map also serves the working needs of the pillar: teachers and candidates organising the period for study can lay the documentary record out in a structured presentation, and a plain presentation viewer such as the one at [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) opens such assembled decks without proprietary software, which keeps the map portable across the pillar's articles.

Read down the middle column, the map shows the simultaneity the thesis claims: each row contains decisions from the same half-decade, several rows share dates, and the two constitutional amendments of the period, the 101st for taxation and the effective use of Article 370's own machinery against itself, sit in different rows of the same design. Read across a row, the map shows direction rather than drift: no row contains a major decision that restored the inherited settlement on that tension. The strongest version of the contrary evidence, which the coherence objection examined below takes seriously, is that the rows were filled by different ministries at different speeds for unrelated reasons, and that the map's neatness is the analyst's imposition. The adjudication section answers that the announcement record, manifesto, presidential addresses and legislative sequencing, ties the rows together in the government's own presentation, so the integration is documented in the primary record and not only in the reading of it.

## Three Readings of the Era: Ethnic Democracy, Institutional Decline, Competitive Clientelism

The scholarly argument over the Modi era is unusually well defined, because three of its participants wrote their frameworks as books and essays with names, dates and testable claims. The hub's task is to state each reading at its strongest, test it against the record assembled in the map, and adjudicate. The first reading is Christophe Jaffrelot's. In Modi's India: Hindu Nationalism and the Rise of Ethnic Democracy, published in English in 2021 after a French edition of 2019, the French political scientist argued that India under Modi was moving from a liberal democratic settlement toward an ethnic democracy, a regime in which elections remain competitive and universal in form while the state progressively identifies the nation with the majority ethnic and religious community, and minorities are relegated to a second tier of belonging. Jaffrelot's evidence includes the composition of the BJP's parliamentary party, in which Muslim representation fell to zero among its Lok Sabha members in 2014 and remained absent in 2019, the citizenship legislation of 2019, episodes of vigilante violence over cow slaughter and their uneven prosecution, and the majoritarian content of campaign rhetoric, documented constituency by constituency. The reading's strength is its account of the first tension: it names a destination for the religion-state renegotiation and supplies a comparative category, drawn from the study of other ethnic democracies, that the renegotiation can be measured against.

The second reading is Pratap Bhanu Mehta's. Across essays in the Economic and Political Weekly and in newspaper columns through the decade from 2014, Mehta, a political theorist and former vice chancellor of Ashoka University, argued that the era's central process was institutional decline: the systematic weakening of the autonomy of bodies designed to check the executive, including the higher judiciary's control over its own appointments and calendar, the Election Commission's perceived latitude, the Reserve Bank's governorship, which saw Raghuram Rajan denied a second term in 2016 and Urjit Patel resign in December 2018 amid a public dispute over reserves and regulatory power, the investigative agencies' deployment against opposition figures, and the universities' governance. Mehta's evidence is institutional rather than electoral: he reads the period as one in which the forms of constitutional democracy persist while the conventions and independent centres that made the forms binding are hollowed. The reading's strength is its account of method: it explains how a government with large majorities can transform a polity without amending the Constitution's text, by capturing the appointments, budgets and timetables through which institutions act.

The third reading is Milan Vaishnav's. In When Crime Pays: Money and Muscle in Indian Politics, published in 2017, and in subsequent essays written with colleagues at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, Vaishnav argued that much of what appears as Modi-era rupture is the continuation of a competitive clientelism that long predates 2014: parties across the spectrum compete by distributing state resources through networks, candidates with criminal records win because they credibly deliver protection and services where the state is weak, and centralisation of campaign finance and party organisation under a dominant leader extends a logic visible in the Congress system Rajni Kothari described for the 1960s. On this reading, the era's distinctness is a matter of degree and talent rather than kind, and the analytical error to avoid is treating a skilled practitioner's dominance as a new regime.

### Which commission first drafted the fiscal bargain that the goods and services tax later enacted?

The Thirteenth Finance Commission, chaired by Vijay Kelkar and reporting in 2009, drafted the first design for a dual goods and services tax with a revenue-sharing bargain between the Union and the states. The 101st Amendment Act of 2016 and the compensation legislation of 2017 enacted a version of that design, converting a commission's blueprint into constitutional architecture.

The adjudication this hub defends begins by assigning each reading its verified territory. Jaffrelot's reading captures the first tension with the greatest precision: the citizenship amendment's religion-sorted text, the Article 370 sequence and the Ayodhya consecration are decisions about the terms of belonging, and a framework centred on belonging predicts their clustering better than a framework centred on administration. Mehta's reading captures the operating method across all three tensions: the renegotiation proceeded through appointments, orders, boards and councils at least as much as through constitutional amendment, and the Reserve Bank dispute of 2018 and the governorship succession at the Election Commission are evidence that the method reached institutions with no religious or federal content at all. Vaishnav's reading captures the substrate: the era's politics of distribution, candidate selection and campaign finance run on rails laid down across the coalition decades, and the 2019 verdict itself rewarded delivery of visible benefits, gas connections, toilets, houses and cash transfers, in a recognisably clientelist currency.

The synthesis the hub defends is that the three readings describe one process at three depths, and that the three-tensions framework is the level at which they combine. Ethnic democracy describes the destination claimed for the first tension; institutional decline describes the technique by which all three tensions were moved without a constituent assembly; competitive clientelism describes the electoral economy that financed the project and set its limits. What the synthesis rejects is Vaishnav's continuity conclusion at the level of design. A government extending inherited clientelism does not need to abrogate Article 370 in its tenth week after re-election, does not need a religion-sorted citizenship statute, and does not need to consolidate twenty-nine labour laws into four codes in a single parliamentary period. The decisions cluster, they were announced as connected, and their direction on each tension is consistent, which is the signature of a programme rather than a practice. The synthesis equally rejects any reading in which the programme required no electoral skill: the renegotiation was ratified at the ballot box in May 2019, after demonetisation and during an economic slowdown, by an electorate that enlarged the government's majority, and an account of the era that cannot explain that ratification has missed the phenomenon it claims to describe.

## Personality and Institution: The Difference from the Indira Precedent

Every Indian debate about a dominant prime minister runs through the comparison with Indira Gandhi, and the comparison disciplines this hub's claim, because the Indira years of 1966 to 1977 and 1980 to 1984 supply the strongest precedent for centralised, personalised rule and the clearest contrast in method. The Indira-era hub of this series develops [the personality-as-institution framework](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) for that period: a leader who, distrusting the Congress organisation she inherited, converted the party into an extension of her household and the state into an instrument of her decisions, splitting the Congress in 1969, nationalising banks by ordinance in July 1969, abolishing privy purses, winning the war and the election of 1971, and finally suspending constitutional government itself through the Emergency proclaimed on 25 June 1975. The institutional signature of that era was substitution: where an institution resisted, it was replaced, packed or bypassed, and the supersession of judges in 1973 and 1977 made the judiciary the emblematic case.

The Modi era's signature, on the record assembled here, is occupation rather than substitution, and the difference matters for prediction. The government of 2014 onward rarely abolished a checking institution; it staffed, scheduled and steered existing ones, and where it built anew, the new architecture, the GST Council, NITI Aayog, the digital payment rails, was designed to be operated rather than merely obeyed. The judiciary delivered the Ayodhya verdict the movement had sought for decades and upheld the Article 370 abrogation in December 2023, outcomes reached through the court's own processes with benches constituted in the ordinary course, which defenders cite as evidence that the institutions functioned, and critics cite as evidence that occupation had already succeeded by the time the cases arrived. Both descriptions can be tested against the appointment record, and the pillar's institutional articles conduct that test. The framework point for the hub is comparative: Indira Gandhi's centralisation was personalist and fragile, collapsing in the verdict of March 1977 the first time the electorate was asked freely, whereas the Modi-era renegotiation was programmatic and organisational, carried by a party and a movement with a cadre structure that predated the leader, and ratified in 2019 with an increased majority. A renegotiation resting on an organisation survives its occasions; a centralisation resting on a person does not, and the distinction between the two is precisely what [the 1998 NDA's strategic-paradox precedent](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) throws into relief, because Vajpayee led the same movement and chose, under coalition discipline, not to attempt the renegotiation at all.

The antecedent sharpens further against the coalition era's own renegotiations. The governments of 1989 to 2014 moved each tension in turn, liberalising the economy under minority government in 1991, opening the religion-state question through the Ayodhya movement's pressure from outside government, deepening federal bargaining by necessity, and the hub of that era in this series describes [the coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) as substantive but sequential and negotiated. The verdict that opened the Modi era carries the same point in electoral form: [the 2014 verdict's structural analogue to 1971](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/) rests on the conversion of a plurality into a parliamentary instrument large enough to legislate a programme, a conversion Indian elections had not produced since 1984. Sequence is the variable that separates the eras: coalition governments renegotiated one tension per crisis, Indira Gandhi renegotiated by substitution under one tension's dominance, and the government of 2014 onward is the first to have legislated across all three within two connected terms, announcing the simultaneity in advance.

## The Coherence Objection: Contingency, Demonetisation and the Limits of the Framework

The strongest counter to the integrated reading deserves its own section, because if it succeeds the hub's claim collapses into a pattern imposed on noise. The counter runs as follows. Several of the era's largest decisions display the marks of improvisation rather than design. Demonetisation was announced with four hours of effective notice to the banking system, its operational details, the exchange limits, the exemptions, the design and supply of replacement notes, were revised through more than fifty notifications in the weeks that followed, and the Prime Minister's own speech of 8 November 2016 framed the aims in terms, unaccounted cash, counterfeit currency and terror finance, that shifted in later official statements toward digital payments and formalisation. A decision taken inside a strategic framework, the objection holds, does not need its rationale rewritten in transit. The same objection points to the national lockdown of 24 March 2020, announced with four hours to the movement of a billion people, and to the farm laws of September 2020, enacted without consultation of the states whose constitutional domain agriculture is, defended for a year, and then repealed in November 2021 after sustained protest, the era's clearest case of a renegotiation attempted and abandoned. A framework that licenses both the GST's six-year gestation and demonetisation's four-hour fuse, the objection concludes, explains everything and therefore very little.

The hub's answer distinguishes the two levels at which a government acts, and the distinction is stated in the brief and defended here as the correct reading of the evidence. Tactical execution in the Modi era is frequently contingent, compressed and theatrical, and demonetisation is the type case; the objection proves that proposition, and this article concedes it without reservation. Strategic integration is a claim about a different level: which questions a government treats as open, in which direction it moves every question it touches, and which inherited settlements it refuses to treat as constraints. On that level the record is consistent in a way contingency cannot explain. No major decision of the period restored the equidistance settlement on the first tension, enlarged state fiscal autonomy on the second, or rebuilt the entitlement architecture on the third. Governments improvising from occasion to occasion distribute their decisions on both sides of an inherited settlement over a decade; the distribution here is one-sided across three independent domains, and one-sidedness at that scale is the empirical signature of a framework, whether the framework is written in a single document or held as the common sense of a movement that had waited, in opposition and in coalition, for the arithmetic that would let it act. The repeal of the farm laws in 2021 marks the framework's limit rather than its absence: the renegotiation of the third tension stalled where it met organised federal resistance in the states and at the capital's borders, which is evidence about the balance of forces, and the pillar's later articles on the period's contests take up that evidence in full.

A second, quieter objection concerns the hub's own vantage. A periodisation named after a person imports the person's prominence into the analysis, and the Modi era label, used in this series as the pillar's title, risks crediting to one office the work of a movement, a party organisation, a sympathetic media economy and a generation of voters. The framework guards against the risk in the comparison it insists on: the same person governed Gujarat from 2001 to 2014 without producing a national renegotiation, and the same movement governed India from 1998 to 2004 without attempting one. The era, on the evidence, began when a movement's programme met a parliamentary majority capable of enacting it, on 16 May 2014, and the analytical name attaches to the conjunction rather than the biography.

The hub's conclusion returns to the puzzle of the two governments of one party. Between 1998 and 2004 the BJP in office treated its inheritance as a liability to be deferred; between 2014 and the years that followed, the BJP in office treated the same inheritance as a programme to be executed, and the instrument that separated the two periods was not a change of ideology but a change of structure, a majority that freed the programme from coalition arithmetic and a framework that organised the programme across the founding settlement's three open questions. Christophe Jaffrelot's ethnic-democracy reading identifies the destination on the first tension, Pratap Bhanu Mehta's institutional-decline reading identifies the method across all three, and Milan Vaishnav's competitive clientelism identifies the electoral economy that paid for the attempt. The three-tensions renegotiation is the category under which the three accounts stop competing and start composing a single explanation, and it is the framework the remaining articles of this pillar apply, test and, where the evidence demands, correct.

## The Machinery: How Simultaneity Was Organised

A simultaneous renegotiation across three constitutional domains is an organisational achievement before it is an intellectual one, and the hub must account for the machinery, because the machinery is the strongest material evidence that the integration was designed rather than retrospectively imagined. The programme's textual spine is a sequence of public documents. The BJP's manifesto for the 2014 election, released on 7 April 2014, committed the party in writing to the abrogation of Article 370, to exploring a uniform civil code, and to the construction of the Ram temple at Ayodhya through constitutional means, alongside the economic programme of minimum government and maximum governance. The address of President Pranab Mukherjee to the joint sitting of Parliament on 9 June 2014 carried the same combination into the government's own inaugural statement, pairing the constitutional commitments with the delivery agenda of bank accounts, sanitation and housing. The manifesto of April 2019 repeated the constitutional commitments after five years in which they had not been executed, and added the citizenship amendment in terms, a sequence that matters to the framework: the commitments were announced before the first majority, retained in office unexecuted during the first term, restated before the second election, and executed in the second term's first five months. Governments do not usually publish their designs in advance and then follow the publication order; the documentary sequence is the primary evidence on which the integration claim rests, and it is available to any reader in the manifesto texts and the Gazette.

The administrative machinery matched the textual spine. The Prime Minister's Office under Modi operated through a small senior secretariat, with Nripendra Misra as Principal Secretary from 2014 to 2019 and P. K. Mishra succeeding him in September 2019, and through the Cabinet Secretariat's coordination of groups of ministers and committees that processed the legislative programme. The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs and the Cabinet Committee on Security cleared the period's largest decisions, demonetisation through the Cabinet on the evening of 8 November 2016, the Article 370 resolutions through the Cabinet on the morning of 5 August 2019. The under-cited stratum of this record, the coordination memoranda through which the PMO sequenced decisions across ministries between 2014 and 2019, has surfaced only partially through Right to Information disclosures and official memoirs, and its incomplete availability is the main documentary gap in the study of the period: the public record establishes the order and the content of the decisions beyond dispute, and the internal record that would establish the degree of advance integration in the government's own papers remains largely closed. The hub flags the gap because the framework's confidence should rest on the public sequence, which is sufficient, and not on claims about closed files.

Party machinery supplied the second half of the capacity. Under Amit Shah's presidency of the BJP from July 2014 to January 2020, the party conducted membership drives that it reported in the tens of millions, built booth-level committees as its standard unit, and contested state elections as referendums on the national leadership. The electoral consequence was a party that governed, by the end of 2017, in a majority of India's states, which changed the federal politics of the renegotiation: the GST Council's state votes were held substantially by finance ministers of the ruling alliance during the design's formative years, and the Rajya Sabha arithmetic that had blocked the citizenship amendment's predecessor bill in the first term turned favourable by 2019 as state victories accumulated. The upper house numbers are the unglamorous key to the simultaneity: the Constitution Order and the citizenship statute of 2019 required parliamentary passage that the BJP of 2014, with 46 Rajya Sabha seats, could not have delivered, and the BJP of 2019, its bloc enlarged by six years of state results, could. Simultaneity was therefore not only chosen; it was scheduled by the federal accumulation of the party's own power, a fact that binds the second tension to the first in the era's mechanics.

The opposition's organisational condition completes the mechanical account. The Congress, reduced to 44 Lok Sabha seats in 2014 and 52 in 2019, spent the decade in a leadership transition it never completed, and the regional parties that had disciplined national governments throughout the coalition era held their states without a common national programme. A renegotiation requires not only a government capable of moving three tensions but a legislature in which no veto coalition can protect the inherited settlements one at a time, and between 2014 and 2019 that condition held in India for the first time since 1984. The framework does not treat the opposition's weakness as part of the government's design; it treats it as part of the era's enabling structure, the negative space inside which the programme's simultaneity became practicable.

## The States as Testing Ground: Where Each Tension Met Resistance

The renegotiation was national in design and federal in experience, and the states supply the evidence of where each column of the map met its limit. On the first tension, BJP-ruled states legislated in the same direction as the centre: Uttar Pradesh's government enacted its Prohibition of Unlawful Conversion of Religion law in 2021, following an ordinance of November 2020, and Madhya Pradesh enacted a comparable statute the same year, regulating religious conversion with prior-notice and burden provisions that petitioners challenged as burdens on interfaith marriage and that the state governments defended as protection against coerced conversion. Karnataka's dispute of 2022, in which the state government enforced a uniform rule that excluded the hijab from classrooms of state institutions and the Karnataka High Court upheld the restriction in March 2022, placed a BJP state government and a Muslim minority practice in direct legal contest, with the Supreme Court's divided bench of October 2022 referring the question onward. Each case is litigated on its own record; the framework notes their common property, that the religion-state renegotiation proceeded at state level through ordinary legislation and executive rule-making in the years when the centre's own sequence had paused after 2019, which is the behaviour of a programme distributed across a party's governments rather than a single capital's improvisation.

On the second tension, resistance organised itself through the federal institutions the renegotiation had built. The GST compensation dispute of 2020, in which finance ministers of Kerala, Punjab, West Bengal and other opposition-ruled states rejected the Union's initial borrowing proposal in the Council's meetings before a settlement was reached, demonstrated that the Council could function as a bargaining chamber as well as a ratification chamber, and the dispute's resolution through a Union-arranged borrowing window demonstrated the asymmetry inside the chamber. The government of the National Capital Territory of Delhi contested the Union over control of the services through the period, a dispute that ran from the High Court through the Constitution Bench judgment of May 2023, which assigned control of services to the elected Delhi government, to Parliament's Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act of August 2023, which reassigned the substance to a Union-chaired authority. Supporters of the 2023 Act described it as the ordinary law of a Union Territory whose capital functions justify central control; the Delhi government and its supporters described the sequence as the nullification of a constitutional judgment by statute within three months. The hub records the sequence as the federal tension's sharpest single episode of the period, because it displays in miniature the era's method and its contest: institutional form preserved, decisional centre moved.

On the third tension, the states appeared as co-producers rather than subjects. The platform architecture of Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and direct transfer was built by the Union and operated through state administrations, and the pandemic years stress-tested the division of labour: the lockdown of March 2020 was ordered by the Union under the Disaster Management Act of 2005, its relief delivered substantially through state machinery and the grain stocks of the Food Corporation, and the free-grain programme that followed made the Union the named provider in households whose daily administration remained the states'. State governments of every party used the same rails, which is the strongest evidence that the third tension's renegotiation produced infrastructure rather than merely advantage: a delivery system adopted by one's opponents has crossed from programme into settlement, and the pillar's later articles examine whether the first two tensions' renegotiations achieved the same crossing or remained contested property.

The farm law repeal, already noticed in the coherence objection, belongs to this section's evidence as much as to that one. The three farm statutes of September 2020 attempted to renegotiate agricultural marketing, a domain the Constitution assigns substantially to the states, by Union legislation defended under the trade and commerce entries. The protest that occupied Delhi's borders from November 2020 drew its strength from Punjab and Haryana's state-level political organisation, and the repeal of November 2021 was the era's one unambiguous federal defeat. Its lesson for the framework is that the renegotiation's simultaneity had a frontier: where a tension's terrain was constitutionally state-held and politically organised, the centre's architecture of councils and platforms did not substitute for consent, a qualification the hub carries into the pillar as a standing test for each domain article.

## The Ratification: What the Verdicts of 2014 and 2019 Each Established

The two general elections that bound the era are not interchangeable evidence, and the framework assigns each a distinct evidentiary role. The verdict of May 2014 established possibility. The BJP's 282 seats on 31 percent of the vote, the lowest vote share ever converted into a single-party majority, reflected the first-past-the-post system's amplification of a fragmented opposition as much as a national wave, and post-poll surveys by the Lokniti programme at the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies recorded an electorate voting against the incumbent UPA's record on prices and corruption at least as much as for a programme few voters described in ideological terms. A hub that treated 2014 as a plebiscite for the renegotiation would overread it. The verdict's institutional meaning is narrower and stronger: it transferred the parliamentary capacity for the renegotiation, in the Lok Sabha immediately and in the Rajya Sabha by accumulation, to the single party that had published the programme.

The verdict of May 2019 established ratification. By 2019 the electorate had experienced the first term's signature acts, demonetisation above all, and the delivery architecture in its own households, and the BJP's tally rose to 303 seats on 37.4 percent of the vote, the first time since 1984 that a governing majority renewed and enlarged itself. Lokniti's post-poll analysis recorded the Prime Minister's personal rating as the single strongest predictor of the BJP vote, with a substantial fraction of the party's voters reporting that they would have voted differently under another leader, and recorded beneficiaries of the government's named schemes, housing, toilets, gas connections, bank accounts, distributed across the coalition that returned the government. The sequencing between the verdict and the programme then inverted the 2014 relationship: the second term's constitutional sequence of August to December 2019 was executed on the strength of a mandate that the government's defenders described as permission sought and received, because the 2019 manifesto had restated the commitments the 2014 manifesto had announced. Critics answered that a manifesto's clauses cannot function as itemised consent for measures of constitutional magnitude, and that the 2019 campaign's public emphasis fell on national security after the Pulwama attack of 14 February 2019 and the Balakot air strike of 26 February 2019 rather than on Article 370 or citizenship. Both descriptions of the mandate are live in the scholarship; the framework requires only the institutional fact, that the capacity was renewed with knowledge of the first term's methods, and that the constitutional sequence followed the renewal by weeks rather than preceding it.

Between the two verdicts stands the period's most instructive negative result. In the state elections of December 2018, months before the national renewal, the BJP lost Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh to the Congress, and in the state elections that followed the second national term its performance remained uneven, including defeat in West Bengal in 2021 after a campaign of maximum national investment. The electorate that ratified the renegotiation at the centre repeatedly declined to ratify the same party's state governments, a divergence the framework must carry honestly: the simultaneity was a property of the national arena, powered by a national leadership and a national programme, and it coexisted with a federal electorate that continued to bargain state by state. The three-tensions reading of the era is a reading of the Union government's project, and its claims extend exactly as far as that project's writ, which the farm law repeal and the state results map from opposite directions.

## Tests for the Pillar: What Would Confirm or Break the Framework

A hub article that cannot say what evidence would damage it has offered a vocabulary rather than an argument, so this section records the tests against which the pillar's domain articles should be read. The first test is documentary: if the closed coordination record of the period, as it opens through archives, memoirs and information requests, shows the three domains proceeding in mutual ignorance, with no sequencing intent at the centre, the integration claim weakens to a claim about effects rather than design, and the hub's wording about deliberateness would require revision. The second test is directional: a major decision restoring the inherited settlement on any tension, a re-enacted compensation principle in fiscal federalism, a citizenship amendment restoring religion-neutral fast tracks, a rebuilt statutory entitlement replacing platform delivery, would count against the pattern in proportion to its scale, and the farm law repeal stands in the record as a partial instance whose limits the pillar must weigh rather than explain away. The third test is comparative: the framework predicts that the era's distinction from the Vajpayee period will survive the correction of any single factual dispute, because the distinction rests on the distribution of decisions across three domains and two terms, and a reading that collapses when one case is reclassified was never about the distribution.

The fourth test is successor behaviour, the same test this series applied to earlier settlements. A settlement is real when successors administer it rather than reverse it. The GST architecture passed that test inside the period itself, since opposition-ruled states operated the Council and collected the tax throughout their disputes with the Union; the platform architecture passed it as state governments of every party built on the same identity and payment rails; the first tension's renegotiation remains, at the close of the period this hub surveys, the property of one political formation, defended by its authors and contested by its opponents in court and in election campaigns, and its settlement status is therefore undecided in a way the other two columns are not. A hub that claimed more would be writing advocacy. The claim defended here is the one the record carries: the Modi era is the first period in which a single government, armed with a published programme and an earned majority, attempted the renegotiation of all three founding tensions simultaneously, built durable architecture on two of them, and left the third as the open contest of the republic's politics. The articles that follow in this pillar take one domain each, the premiership's architecture, demonetisation, the goods and services tax, Article 370, citizenship, the party system and the verdicts, and test the hub's pattern against the full record of each domain.

## The Instruments in One View: Amendment, Order, Statute, Verdict, Notification

A reader testing the integration claim can approach it through the instruments, because each column of the renegotiation map was built with the tool that column's constitutional terrain allowed, and the selection of tools displays a legal literacy that improvisation does not explain. On the federal-fiscal column, where the Constitution distributes tax power between the Union and the states in the Seventh Schedule, the government used the heaviest instrument in the system: a constitutional amendment under Article 368, ratified by the state legislatures, followed by a council designed inside the amended text. The 101st Amendment's passage in August 2016, after a decade in which the measure had stalled under two governments, required negotiations with state governments of every party and a compensation guarantee to close them; the instrument's weight is the measure of the column's intended permanence. On the religion-state column's largest item, where Article 370 contained its own amendment machinery, the government used the lightest sufficient instrument: presidential orders under the article itself, issued on the substituted concurrence the orders constructed, requiring no special majority, no state ratification and no session of any constituent body. On the same column's membership item, citizenship being a Union subject under the Union List, an ordinary statute by simple majority sufficed, and the Citizenship (Amendment) Act passed the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha in three days of December 2019. On Ayodhya, where the title was sub judice, the instrument was patience plus litigation: the government waited for the Constitution Bench, accepted a verdict it had not authored, and converted the verdict into closure through a trust created by executive notification in February 2020. On the third column, the instruments were statutes of consolidation, the four labour codes and the insolvency code, a money-bill-routed identity law in the Aadhaar Act of 2016, whose passage as a money bill the Supreme Court upheld in September 2018 over a dissent that called the routing a fraud on the Constitution, and pure executive construction, the payment rails and account drives, which required no legislation at all.

The taxonomy rewards a second look. In the founding and Nehruvian periods, the era-defining moves travelled by amendment and statute because the Congress majorities made those instruments cheap; in the Indira period the signature instruments were the ordinance and the proclamation; in the coalition period the characteristic instrument was the negotiated statute trimmed to the weakest partner's tolerance. The Modi period's instrument profile is distinctive in its completeness: every instrument in the constitutional repertoire was used at least once for a programme item, amendment for taxation, order for Kashmir, statute for citizenship and labour, verdict for Ayodhya, money bill for identity, notification for currency, resolution for the planning body's replacement. A government feeling its way case by case tends to reuse the instrument that last worked; a government executing a survey of the whole settlement reaches for the shelf. The hub does not claim the shelf was stocked in advance in a single plan document, and the documentary gap noted earlier forbids the claim; it claims that the pattern of selection, heaviest where permanence was needed and lightest where the text permitted, is the behaviour of lawyers working a map of the Constitution's pressure points, and that the three-tensions framework is simply that map described from the receiving end.

One further instrument deserves its own paragraph because it structured the era's tempo: the ordinance and its cousin, the decision announced before its paperwork. The farm laws began as ordinances in June 2020; the conversion law in Uttar Pradesh began as an ordinance in November 2020; demonetisation was executed through a notification under section 26 of the Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 issued the same night as the announcement. Critics of the method, including the opposition's parliamentary leadership across the period, treated the pattern as contempt for legislative deliberation; the government's defenders answered that every ordinance faced Parliament within the constitutional window and that the statutes stand or fall by their passage, which the farm laws' repeal in November 2021, carried through both Houses without debate at the government's own request, demonstrated in reverse. The framework registers the tempo as part of the renegotiation's method on all three tensions: settle the fact, then legislate its permanence, a sequence the GST inverts and the Article 370 orders exemplify, and against which the inherited settlements, most of them built by legislation first and administration after, stood at a procedural disadvantage throughout the period.

## The Wider Field: Hegemony, Development and Resilience Readings

Beyond the canonical three readings, the scholarship of the period supplies three further frames that the hub must place, because the pillar's domain articles draw on all of them. The first is the new-dominant-party reading. Suhas Palshikar, writing in the Economic and Political Weekly from 2014 onward, argued that the period's deepest change was the construction of a second dominant-party system around the BJP, comparable in structure to the Congress system Rajni Kothari anatomised for the 1960s: a party of consensus at the centre of the system, opposition parties defined by their distance from it, and a hegemonic common sense that sets the questions elections are fought on. Rahul Verma and colleagues developed the companion claim that the BJP's dominance differs from the Congress precedent in resting on an ideological core, a cadre organisation and a leadership cult in combination, where Congress dominance rested on accommodation of factions. The reading matters to this hub because it explains the renegotiation's supply side: simultaneous constitutional movement requires a party whose internal opposition has been retired, and the BJP of 2014 to 2019, its senior generation moved to a mentors' body in August 2014 and its organisation rebuilt under Shah, met that condition as the Congress of the 1950s had met it and the BJP of 1998 had not.

The second frame is the development reading held by the government's own economists and sympathetic analysts, stated in its strongest form rather than the caricature. On this reading, the era's centre of gravity is the third tension, and the constitutional episodes are the continuation of a majoritarian politics that Indian governments of every colour have practised when armed with a mandate; the historically significant record is the construction of state capacity for delivery, the tax base's widening, the insolvency regime, the infrastructure programme of highways, railways and airports, and the macroeconomic management that kept India the fastest-growing large economy through much of the period. Bibek Debroy, chairman of the Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister from 2017 until his death in 2024, gave this reading its most literate official form across his columns and council papers. The hub's adjudication does not dismiss the frame; it denies its ordering. The development record explains why the government was re-elected, and the framework adopted here holds that re-election was the enabling condition rather than the era's content, because governments that deliver competently without touching the settlement are common in the Indian record, and the government of 2014 onward touched every part of the settlement it inherited.

The third frame is the resilience reading, associated with scholars of Indian federalism and democracy who caution against declension narratives. Louise Tillin's work on federalism and the Constitution argues that the Indian Union's federal resilience runs through its states' political vitality, and the period supplies her evidence as readily as it supplies the centralisation evidence: state elections remained genuinely competitive, regional parties governed large states throughout, courts decided against the Union in prominent cases, including the privacy judgment of August 2017 in which nine judges of the Supreme Court held privacy a fundamental right against the Union's argued position, and the electoral bond scheme the government had built for campaign finance was struck down as unconstitutional by a Constitution Bench in February 2024. On the resilience reading, the renegotiation is real but bounded by a system whose checking mechanisms bend and recover, and the era will be classified with earlier centralising phases that the system's federal and judicial depth absorbed. The hub's synthesis accepts the resilience evidence as the correct statement of the renegotiation's limits and incorporates it as the fourth test recorded above: the columns of the map differ in how far successor and rival institutions have absorbed them, and the framework's confidence is graded column by column accordingly.

Placing the six frames together clarifies what the three-tensions claim adds. Jaffrelot explains the first column's destination, Mehta the method, Vaishnav the substrate, Palshikar the party condition, the development reading the electoral fuel, and the resilience reading the boundary. None of the six, taken alone, predicts the simultaneity, because each is organised around one variable; the simultaneity is a fact about the distribution of the government's decisions across the founding settlement's open questions, and it becomes visible only when the settlement itself is the unit of analysis. That is the hub's contribution to the pillar and the reason the series' founding framework, built for 1949, turns out to be the instrument the post-2014 period is most legibly read with.

## Periodisation: Why the Modi Era Begins in 2014 and Not in 2019

Every hub in this series must defend its dates, and this hub's dates have been contested from two directions. One school of commentary treats 2019, not 2014, as the era's true beginning: the first term, on this view, was a strong but conventional government of infrastructure and delivery, and the regime-defining sequence arrived only with the second mandate, when Article 370, the citizenship amendment and the Ayodhya closure landed within five months. The opposing school treats the entire post-2014 period as continuous with the BJP's longer rise since 1989, dissolving the era into a movement history. The framework's answer to both turns on the distinction between a programme's existence, its capacity and its execution. The programme existed in published form in April 2014. The capacity arrived in instalments, Lok Sabha majority in 2014, Rajya Sabha viability by accumulation across 2016 to 2019, state governments across the map by 2017. The execution, on the first tension, waited for the capacity, and the waiting is itself the evidence that execution was scheduled rather than improvised: a government that intended improvisation had no reason to leave its oldest commitment untouched for five years and then execute it in the first session of its renewed term, in the sequence the manifesto had listed. The era therefore begins when the programme met its first instalment of capacity, on 16 May 2014, and 2019 marks its second phase rather than its birth. The Nehruvian parallel is instructive and is developed in this series' account of [the Nehruvian-era settlement and its three tensions](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/): Nehru's settlement too was announced at the founding, built across the 1950s as capacity allowed, and only named as a settlement by its historians afterwards. The Modi era differs in that its builders named the design while building it, which is why the periodisation argument matters less here than the documentary one.

The opposing dissolution, the era as movement history, fails on the same evidence that defeated the Vajpayee-continuity assumption at this article's opening. A movement that existed from 1925, a party that existed from 1980, and a programme printed from the 1990s produced, in the six years when they held office without a majority, the deferral documented in the National Agenda for Governance. The variable that changed in 2014 was institutional, and a periodisation that cannot register the change mistakes the biography of an idea for the history of a state.

## The Public Argument: Media, Universities and the Contest over Description

A renegotiation of a founding settlement is fought twice, once in the institutions and once in the description of the institutions, and the era's public argument forms part of the hub's record because the contestants themselves treated description as a stake. The government's supporters built a vocabulary for the period, New India, drawn from the Prime Minister's address of 15 August 2017, in which the destination was dated to 2022, the seventy-fifth year of independence, and later extended through the Amrit Kaal formulation of the 2021 and 2022 budgets, a twenty-five year horizon to the centenary of 2047. The vocabulary performed the framework's claim in the government's own register: the period was presented as a founding-scale interval in which inherited arrangements were legitimately open. The critics' counter-vocabulary, undeclared emergency, institutional capture, majoritarian state, performed the opposite classification, assigning the period to the republic's pathology chapters. Between the two vocabularies, the older scholarly one of this hub, renegotiation of tensions, has the advantage of being confirmable in the Gazette, which is where the pillar's articles return for their evidence whenever the public argument heats.

The argument's institutional sites were specific. The universities became a contested site from 2016, when the arrest of students at Jawaharlal Nehru University on sedition charges in February of that year made the campus a national controversy, through the appointment disputes and curricular revisions that followed at several central universities; defenders of the government's approach described the correction of a professoriate's monopoly on the national narrative, and critics described the disciplining of the institutions that produce independent description. The news media's economy shifted through the period, with television news consolidating around ownership sympathetic to the government, a process documented by the Reuters Institute's India reports and contested by the channels' own claims of market choice, and the independent digital press expanding in the space the consolidation left. The Election Commission's scheduling and model-code decisions faced public challenge from opposition parties in both national elections, and the Commission's defenders pointed to the elections' scale and the acceptance of their results by all parties as the operative verdict. None of these contests needs the hub to settle it; all of them belong in the record of an era whose definition is disputed precisely because the settlement it renegotiates includes the institutions that describe settlements.

The sedition law's career condenses the public argument into a single statute. The provision under which the JNU students were charged in 2016, section 124A of the Indian Penal Code, was a colonial inheritance that governments of every party had used; the Modi government defended its retention through the period and announced its review in 2022, when the Supreme Court suspended its operation in May of that year pending the Union's reconsideration, and Parliament then re-enacted the substance within the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita of 2023 under a new name and a wider definition, in the code that replaced the Penal Code from July 2024. Defenders of the sequence described the modernisation of a colonial code; critics described the retention of its most colonial instrument under a national name. The hub cites the episode because it resists the framework's own columns: it belongs to no single tension, it shows continuity with every predecessor government, and it reminds the pillar that a renegotiation framework explains the period's direction without explaining every act the period contains, a discipline the domain articles inherit.

The conclusion the hub rests on is the comparative one stated at the outset and tested through the map, the instruments, the verdicts and the states. Two governments of one party faced the same three open questions of the founding settlement. The first, governing by permission of its allies, set the questions aside in writing and administered the settlement it found. The second, governing on a majority its programme had been published to win, reopened all three questions in sequence and rebuilt the settlement's operating arrangements on two of them, leaving the third, the terms of religious belonging, as the contested centre of the republic's politics at the period's close. The scholarship of the period divides because the phenomenon has several true descriptions at different depths; the three-tensions framework is the depth at which the descriptions compose. What the era will finally be called depends on how long its architecture outlives its authors, the test the founding settlement passed and the Indira settlement failed, and that test, by its nature, the pillar can only prepare and not perform.

## The Economic Record: Growth Claims and the Evidence Both Sides Cite

The third tension's renegotiation was conducted under an economic argument that the hub must summarise honestly, because the scholarly readings divide over numbers as well as over constitutions. The government's record, as its finance ministers presented it in the annual Economic Surveys and budget speeches, rested on a sequence: growth averaging above 7 percent in the first term on the 2011 and 2012 base series, inflation brought within the Reserve Bank's target band after the monetary policy framework agreement of February 2015 gave the central bank a statutory inflation mandate, a widening direct tax base documented in filing statistics, the insolvency regime's recovery rates, and a capital expenditure programme in highways, railways and defence production that the budgets expanded year by year. The pandemic contraction of 2020 and 2021, in which output fell by more than 7 percent in the first pandemic year on the estimates then current, interrupted the sequence, and the recovery that followed, among the fastest of the large economies, was claimed by the government as vindication of its fiscal choices, including the free grain programme that carried food support to approximately 800 million persons under the food security framework.

The critics' economic record, as stated by Raghuram Rajan in his public interventions after leaving the Reserve Bank in 2016 and by opposition economists across the period, rested on a different sequence from the same statistics: a growth deceleration already visible before the pandemic, from above 8 percent in 2016 and 2017 to approximately 4 percent in 2019 and 2020 on the official series; the suppressed release and subsequent publication of the Periodic Labour Force Survey for 2017 and 2018, whose unemployment rate of 6.1 percent was the highest in the series' history and whose delayed release cost two members of the National Statistical Commission their offices in January 2019; the demonetisation and GST-transition costs borne by the informal economy, measured by the Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy's household surveys; and a private investment rate that remained flat across the decade despite the corporate tax reduction of September 2019, which cut the headline rate to 22 percent in the government's largest single supply-side act. Arvind Subramanian, the government's own former Chief Economic Adviser, added the period's most unsettling internal critique in a 2019 paper arguing that growth in the years 2011 to 2016 had been overestimated by approximately 2.5 percentage points under the revised methodology, a claim the government's statistical establishment disputed in print. The hub does not adjudicate the national accounts; it records that the economic argument of the era is fought over the reliability of the state's own measurements, which is itself a datum for the institutional reading, and that both sides' sequences are built from named, checkable publications rather than impressions.

For the framework, the economic record matters at one precise point. The third tension's renegotiation, the substitution of platform delivery and incentivised production for entitlement and public production, was a wager that state capacity could be rebuilt around identity, accounts and payments faster than the older bureaucracy could be reformed. The wager's returns are the numbers both sides cite: the transfer system's leakage savings claimed by the government's Direct Benefit Transfer reporting, the payment volumes the Unified Payments Interface reached, crossing ten billion transactions in a single month in August 2023, against the employment figures the labour surveys report and the manufacturing share that stayed near its starting level. A development policy is judged by its outcomes over a generation, and the hub's period closes before the wager matures; what the pillar can test, and its domain articles do test, is whether the instruments were built as designed and whether the design was of a piece with the constitutional programme on the other two tensions. On the evidence of the announcement record, the manifesto's economic chapters and its constitutional chapters were drafted as one document, and the government of 2014 campaigned on the conjunction, development as the warrant and the settlement as the work. The voters who returned the government in 2019 had experienced both halves, which returns the hub to its ratification argument with the economic caveat honestly attached: consent to a programme is real consent even when the programme's outcomes remain disputed, and the dispute belongs to the pillar's economic articles rather than to the framework's foundations.

## Five Months of 2019: The Sequence as Primary Evidence

Because the integration claim rests on sequence, the hub sets out the decisive sequence in one place, dated act by dated act, as the primary exhibit a reader can verify against the Gazette and the parliamentary record. The second term opened with the verdict of 23 May 2019. Parliament's first session carried the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Bill, which the previous Lok Sabha had passed and the Rajya Sabha had held; it cleared both Houses and received assent on 31 July 2019. On the morning of 5 August 2019 the Union Cabinet approved the Kashmir resolutions and orders; the Rajya Sabha adopted the statutory resolution recommending the presidential order the same day, the Lok Sabha on 6 August, and the Reorganisation Act followed on 9 August. The session closed with the government's constitutional arrears of thirty years discharged in eleven weeks. On 9 November 2019 the Supreme Court's Ayodhya verdict landed in the government's favour on the movement's oldest demand. On 9 December 2019 the Citizenship (Amendment) Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha and passed the same night; the Rajya Sabha passed it on 11 December; assent followed on 12 December. Protests against the citizenship sequence began within days, centred from mid-December at Shaheen Bagh in Delhi, and the government's public position through the winter, stated by the Prime Minister at the Ramlila Maidan rally of 22 December 2019, was that no nationwide register of citizens had been discussed by the government, a statement whose relationship to earlier parliamentary answers became a documented controversy of the following months.

The compression is the datum. Five months carried the resolution of the temple dispute by verdict, the integration of Kashmir by order, the criminalisation of instant divorce by statute, and the introduction of religion into citizenship law by statute, each item an arrears of a different decade: the temple question from 1949 and 1992, Kashmir from 1947 and 1953, the divorce question from the Shah Bano judgment of 1985, citizenship's religious neutrality from 1955. No previous Indian government, including the governments of 1971 and 1984 with larger majorities, had discharged four settlement-level arrears of different vintages in a single parliamentary season. The hub's framework explains the compression as the programme meeting its scheduled capacity; the contingency reading must treat the compression as coincidence of calendars, and the reader is invited to weigh which explanation the dates support. The opposition's failure to block any element of the sequence, despite the Rajya Sabha arithmetic that had held the divorce bill for a year, is part of the same exhibit: by August 2019 the accumulation of state victories had moved the upper house, and abstentions and absences among opposition parties widened the margin further, a mechanical fact that the era's institutional readings sometimes pass over in favour of arguments about will.

The sequence also disciplined the era's later years. After December 2019 the government's constitutional tempo slowed; the pandemic consumed 2020 and 2021, the farm law defeat closed 2021, and the remaining programme items, the uniform civil code at the centre, a nationwide register, population legislation discussed in party fora, remained unexecuted at the national level through the period this hub surveys, advancing instead through state governments and committee reports, including the Law Commission's consultations and the Uttarakhand code of February 2024. The pattern the pillar inherits is therefore a pulse, not a plateau: the renegotiation moved at maximum speed when a fresh mandate met accumulated parliamentary capacity, and it consolidated thereafter. Future movement, if it comes, will on this pattern follow the same trigger, and the framework's periodisation of the era into an announcing term, an executing season and a consolidating remainder is offered to the domain articles as the chronology to test.

## Parliament as the Stage: The Legislative Method in Aggregate

The renegotiation's legislative method can be measured in aggregate, and the aggregate is part of the hub's evidence because it distinguishes the period's parliament from the coalition period's. PRS Legislative Research, the non-partisan research service whose session reviews supply the standard statistics, recorded a long decline in the share of bills referred to parliamentary committees for scrutiny: roughly seven in ten bills in the Fifteenth Lok Sabha, roughly a quarter in the Sixteenth, and a smaller share still in the Seventeenth. The same reviews recorded high legislative output, sessions that passed the government's programme at a pace coalition-era parliaments rarely sustained, and sittings in the later years disrupted repeatedly by protest over the agenda itself. The Seventeenth Lok Sabha completed its term without electing a Deputy Speaker, the first such vacancy in the office's history, a fact the opposition cited as constitutional discourtesy and the government's defenders treated as an unremarkable scheduling matter.

The two readings of the aggregate mirror the era's larger argument. For the institutional-decline school, the committee figures are the method's confession: a government executing a programme at speed treated scrutiny as friction, carried money bills past the upper house's amendment power, and normalised passage amid din, so that the renegotiation's statutes arrived with less deliberative testing than their constitutional weight warranted. For the government's defenders, the same figures record the correction of a veto culture: committees had become instruments of delay in the coalition decades, the electorate had returned a programme with a majority twice, and a legislature that enacts its government's published manifesto is performing the function parliamentary systems assign it. The Waqf (Amendment) Bill's route illustrates the contest inside a single case: introduced in August 2024, it was referred to a joint parliamentary committee after opposition protest, heard evidence through the winter, and passed in April 2025 with amendments, a sequence each side describes in its own vocabulary, scrutiny honoured or scrutiny staged. The hub's framework needs the aggregate for one purpose only: the renegotiation was legislated, in the open, by majorities that the electoral system had produced, and its method combined the forms of parliamentary government with a tempo those forms were not designed to impose on their own scrutiny conventions. That combination, neither coup nor business as usual, is the institutional signature the pillar's articles keep meeting in domain after domain.

The period's last constitutional amendment of scale illustrates the method at its most consensual. The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam reserving one third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies for women, passed a special session of Parliament in September 2023 with near-unanimous support, thirty years after the proposal first entered Parliament. Its implementation was tied to the next census and a subsequent delimitation, which deferred its operation and gave the opposition its line of criticism, reservation announced and postponed in the same text. The government presented the Act as the settlement of the representation question deferred since 1996. For the hub's map, the episode belongs to the rights column's political edge: the era's final constitutional act enlarged the legislature's descriptive promise by amendment, executed through the heaviest instrument, at the moment of the government's choosing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Modi era in Indian politics?

The Modi era is the period of Bharatiya Janata Party government under Prime Minister Narendra Modi that began with the general election verdict of 16 May 2014, when the BJP won 282 Lok Sabha seats and the first single-party majority since 1984. The period is defined analytically by the argument of this article: it is the first occasion on which an Indian government attempted to renegotiate all three permanent tensions of the founding settlement, religion and state, the federal and the unitary, and rights and development, at the same time and as a published programme. The era's second verdict, in May 2019, enlarged the BJP tally to 303 seats and was followed within five months by the Article 370 sequence, the Citizenship (Amendment) Act and the closure of the Ayodhya dispute, the sequence on which most periodisations of the era rest.

### Q: How is the Modi government different from the Vajpayee government?

The two governments shared a party, an ideological inheritance and an RSS cadre base, and differed in structure. Atal Bihari Vajpayee governed from 1998 to 2004 without a Lok Sabha majority, dependent on regional allies whose price, recorded in the National Agenda for Governance of March 1998, was the deferral of the BJP's three core commitments: Article 370, a uniform civil code and the Ayodhya temple. His government fell in April 1999 when an ally withdrew. Narendra Modi governed on single-party majorities of 282 seats in 2014 and 303 in 2019, owed no partner a deferral, and executed the deferred commitments in the second term's first five months. The difference, on the argument of this article, is one of structure and programme rather than ideology: the same inheritance, in one case set aside to govern at all and in the other case enacted as a simultaneous renegotiation.

### Q: What are the major Modi-era policies?

The policies cluster on the three tensions this article uses as its map. On religion and state: the effective abrogation of Article 370 in August 2019, the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of December 2019, the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act of 2019, and acceptance of the Supreme Court's Ayodhya verdict of November 2019. On the federal tension: the goods and services tax from July 2017 under the 101st Amendment, demonetisation in November 2016, the replacement of the Planning Commission by NITI Aayog in January 2015, and the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act of 2019. On rights and development: the Jan Dhan account programme from August 2014, the Aadhaar Act of 2016, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016, Ujjwala from 2016, the four labour codes of 2019 and 2020, and Atmanirbhar Bharat from May 2020.

### Q: Is the Modi era ethnic democracy?

Ethnic democracy is the classification proposed by Christophe Jaffrelot in Modi's India: Hindu Nationalism and the Rise of Ethnic Democracy, published in English in 2021. Jaffrelot argues that elections in the period remained competitive in form while the state increasingly identified the nation with the majority community, relegating minorities to a second tier of belonging, and he cites the citizenship legislation of 2019, the absence of Muslim members in the BJP's parliamentary party, and episodes of vigilante violence in support. The classification is contested. The government's defenders reject it, pointing to universal welfare delivery and formal equality of citizenship law for existing citizens; other scholars, including Pratap Bhanu Mehta, prefer an institutional-decline account, and Milan Vaishnav reads the period as competitive clientelism. This article treats Jaffrelot's reading as the strongest account of the religion-state tension while declining to adopt any single label for the whole period.

### Q: How did the Modi era change Indian institutions?

The changes ran through staffing and architecture more than through abolition. The Planning Commission, a fixture since 1950, was replaced by NITI Aayog in January 2015 without allocation powers. The goods and services tax created the GST Council in 2016, a constitutional body that moved indirect tax rates into a Union-chaired forum. The Reserve Bank saw a governor denied a second term in 2016 and a governor resign in December 2018 during a public dispute over reserves. Investigative agencies, the Election Commission and the universities all faced documented controversies over autonomy, which Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays gathered into an institutional-decline argument. Defenders of the record answer that the same institutions delivered the period's largest outcomes, from the Ayodhya and Article 370 judgments to elections whose results every party accepted, and that staffing by an elected government is accountability rather than capture.

### Q: What is the three-tensions framework for the Modi era?

The framework reads Indian politics through the three questions the Constituent Assembly settled provisionally: the relation of religion and state, the balance between the federal and the unitary in the constitutional design, and the balance between rights and development. Applied to the period after 2014, the framework claims that the Modi government is the first to have renegotiated all three at once as a published programme: the religion-state settlement through the Kashmir, citizenship and Ayodhya decisions of 2019; the federal settlement through the GST architecture, demonetisation and the replacement of the Planning Commission; and the rights-development settlement through the substitution of platform delivery, Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and direct transfers, for the entitlement architecture of the preceding decade. The framework's value is comparative: it makes the era's simultaneity, and its distinction from the Vajpayee period, measurable against a dated record.

### Q: Did the Modi era reverse the Vajpayee era?

On the three inherited commitments, the reversal is textual and complete: the National Agenda for Governance of 1998 set aside Article 370's abrogation, a uniform civil code and the Ayodhya temple, and the Modi government executed the first in August 2019, closed the third through the verdict of November 2019 and the consecration of January 2024, and advanced the second through the Uttarakhand code of February 2024. On governing method, the relationship is more mixed. The Modi government continued and extended several Vajpayee-era directions, including highway construction, disinvestment's successor policies and engagement with the United States, and its economic programme owed debts to the 1998 to 2004 period. The precise claim defended in this article is narrower than total reversal: where Vajpayee engaged the founding settlement case by case under coalition discipline, Modi renegotiated it simultaneously under majority discipline, and the contrast is structural before it is ideological.

### Q: What voting weights do the Centre and the states hold inside the GST Council?

Article 279A of the Constitution, inserted by the 101st Amendment in 2016, gives the Union one third of the total vote in the GST Council and the states, taken together, two thirds. Decisions of the Council require a majority of not less than three fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting. The arithmetic produces a double lock: the Union can block any decision because the states' two thirds cannot reach three fourths alone, and the states can block any decision the Union proposes without broad state support, because the Union's one third needs a large part of the state share. The design was the price of the grand bargain of 2016, in which states surrendered independent indirect taxation for a guaranteed voice, and it explains why Council decisions through the period were recorded as consensus rather than as divided votes.

### Q: What revenue compensation did the GST settlement guarantee the states, and for how long?

The GST (Compensation to States) Act of 2017 guaranteed each state that its revenue from the taxes subsumed into the goods and services tax would grow at 14 percent a year over the base year of 2015 and 2016, with any shortfall paid from a compensation fund financed by a cess on specified goods. The guarantee ran for five years from the tax's commencement, expiring in June 2022. The guarantee was the consideration for state consent to the constitutional amendment, and its expiry became the federal dispute of the early 2020s: states that had surrendered independent tax handles argued for extension, pointing to the pandemic's disruption of the guarantee period, and the Union held to the enacted term while extending the cess levy to service the borrowing raised for the compensation shortfall of 2020 and 2021. The episode is the federal tension's clearest ledger entry in the period.

### Q: How does the Governing Council of NITI Aayog bring chief ministers into national planning?

The Governing Council of NITI Aayog, created by the Union Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015 that established the Aayog, consists of the chief ministers of all states and the lieutenant governors of Union Territories, chaired by the Prime Minister. It first met on 8 February 2015. The design replaced the Planning Commission's bilateral negotiations, in which each state government travelled to Delhi to have its annual plan approved and its central assistance fixed, with a multilateral forum in which chief ministers deliberate together on national development strategies. The change carried a subtraction as well as an addition: NITI Aayog holds no power to allocate funds, the lever through which the Planning Commission had disciplined and rewarded states, so the Governing Council's role is deliberative and agenda-setting, exercised through sub-groups of chief ministers on specific subjects and through the Aayog's indices ranking states on health, education and water outcomes.

### Q: Which Union Territories did the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act 2019 create?

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, assented to on 9 August 2019 and effective from 31 October 2019, divided the state of Jammu and Kashmir into two Union Territories. The Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir comprises the larger part of the former state and was given a legislative assembly on the model of Puducherry, with the lieutenant governor retaining specified powers. The Union Territory of Ladakh comprises the districts of Leh and Kargil and was created without a legislature, administered directly by a lieutenant governor. The division marked the first occasion on which a full state of the Indian Union was converted into Union Territories, reversing the direction of every previous reorganisation, in which territories had graduated to statehood. The Supreme Court, upholding the reorganisation's companion abrogation in December 2023, recorded the Union's undertaking that statehood for Jammu and Kashmir would be restored and directed that elections to its assembly be held.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in the Ayodhya title dispute in November 2019?

On 9 November 2019 a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court, headed by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi, delivered a unanimous verdict in the Ayodhya title suits. The court held that the disputed site, where the Babri Masjid had stood until its demolition on 6 December 1992, would be given to the deity Ram Lalla Virajman through a trust to be created by the Union government, for the construction of a temple. The court directed the government to allot five acres at a prominent place in Ayodhya to the Sunni Central Waqf Board for the construction of a mosque. The judgment rested on the archaeological and documentary record of possession rather than on faith, held the demolition of 1992 to have been a violation of law, and closed a dispute whose modern phase had begun in 1949. The trust was created in February 2020, and the temple's consecration followed on 22 January 2024.

### Q: How does Milan Vaishnav's competitive clientelism differ from Christophe Jaffrelot's ethnic democracy?

The two readings disagree about what is new. Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India of 2021, argues rupture: the period moves India toward an ethnic democracy in which the state identifies the nation with the majority community and minorities occupy a second tier of belonging, a change of regime character measured in citizenship law and representation. Milan Vaishnav, in When Crime Pays of 2017 and later essays, argues continuity: Indian politics has long run on competitive clientelism, in which parties of every ideology distribute state resources through networks and voters reward credible delivery, and the Modi period extends that logic with superior organisation rather than replacing it. The difference is testable against the citizenship amendment of 2019. Jaffrelot's reading predicts and explains a religion-sorted statute; Vaishnav's reading treats the statute as majoritarian positioning inside an unchanged distributive game. This article accepts Jaffrelot's description of the first tension and Vaishnav's of the electoral substrate, and locates the disagreement in the three-tensions adjudication.

### Q: What is the institutional-decline argument that Pratap Bhanu Mehta developed after 2014?

Pratap Bhanu Mehta, a political theorist, argued across essays in the Economic and Political Weekly and in newspaper columns from 2014 onward that the period's central process was the systematic erosion of institutional autonomy: the executive's growing control over the appointments, budgets, tenure and timetables of bodies designed to check it. His exhibits included the judiciary's internal crises, the Reserve Bank's loss of a governor in December 2018, pressure on the Election Commission's perceived independence, the deployment of investigative agencies against opposition figures, and the governance of universities. The argument's distinctive claim is about method: a polity can be transformed without amending its constitutional text if the conventions and independent centres that make the text binding are hollowed out. The government's defenders answer that elected executives staffing institutions is democratic accountability. This article treats Mehta's reading as the strongest account of the renegotiation's technique across all three tensions.

### Q: What role does digital public infrastructure play in the Modi-era welfare architecture?

Digital public infrastructure is the delivery spine of the period's welfare model. Its components are the Aadhaar identity number, placed on a statutory basis by the Aadhaar Act of 2016; the Jan Dhan bank accounts opened from August 2014; the mobile telephone; and the Unified Payments Interface, launched in 2016, which by August 2023 was processing more than ten billion transactions a month. Joined as the stack the government called JAM, the components let the Union treasury pay subsidies, pensions and emergency transfers directly into a named citizen's account, bypassing the layered bureaucracy through which the older developmental state delivered benefits and through which leakage and delay accumulated. The architectural consequence matters more than the technology: the citizen's welfare relationship is established with the Union's platforms, while state administrations operate systems the centre designs, a shift that belongs to the federal tension as much as to the developmental one.

### Q: Which four labour codes did the consolidation of 2019 and 2020 produce?

Parliament consolidated twenty-nine central labour laws into four codes. The Code on Wages, enacted in 2019, unified the payment and bonus statutes and extended a statutory floor wage. The Industrial Relations Code of 2020 combined the trade union, standing orders and industrial disputes legislation. The Code on Social Security of 2020 gathered nine statutes covering provident fund, insurance and gratuity and extended its frame toward unorganised and platform workers. The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code of 2020 consolidated the factory, mines and contract labour regimes. The consolidation had been recommended by the Second National Commission on Labour in 2002 and attempted by no intervening government, which is why this article counts it among the third tension's renegotiations: the rights architecture of the industrial era was rewritten as a consolidated code framework under a single parliamentary majority, with implementation dependent on state rules.

### Q: How did the Jan Dhan programme change the relationship between the state and account holders?

The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, launched on 28 August 2014, opened bank accounts at a scale the programme's own mission documents measured in the hundreds of millions within its first years, with zero-balance facilities and an accident insurance cover attached to the RuPay card. The change in the state-citizen relationship was directional. Earlier welfare reached a household through intermediaries, the panchayat, the block office, the fair-price shop, each able to tax the flow. A Jan Dhan account joined to Aadhaar gave the Union a direct channel to a named individual, and the channel carried the period's signature transfers, including the income support of the PM-KISAN programme from 2019 and the emergency payments to women's accounts in the pandemic spring of 2020. Critics note that balances remained small and dormancy significant in the programme's early years, and defenders answer that the account's value was the rail it laid. The framework records the rail: the developmental state's last mile was rebuilt as a payment.

### Q: What did the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana change for households that cooked with solid fuel?

The Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana, launched at Ballia in Uttar Pradesh on 1 May 2016, gave deposit-free liquefied petroleum gas connections to women in poor households, with the programme reporting connections in the tens of millions across its phases and an extension, Ujjwala 2.0, from 2021. The change it targeted was domestic and epidemiological: households cooking on firewood, dung and crop residue, whose smoke burden falls on women, were offered a clean fuel connection without the security deposit that had priced them out of the market. The programme's contested figure is the refill: critics citing consumption data argued that beneficiary households refilled cylinders at rates well below the national average because the per-fill price remained heavy against their incomes, while the government's reporting emphasised connections delivered and the health logic of the first cylinder. The dispute is characteristic of the era's welfare argument, in which the state counts architecture built and its critics count usage sustained.

### Q: What distinguishes strategic integration from tactical contingency in the three-tensions reading?

The distinction separates the two levels at which a government acts. Tactical contingency describes execution: the four hours of notice before demonetisation took effect on 8 November 2016, the dozens of notifications that revised its rules, the compressed announcement of the March 2020 lockdown. Strategic integration describes selection: which inherited settlements a government treats as open questions, and in which direction it moves every question it touches. The three-tensions reading concedes the first level to the contingency critics and rests its claim on the second. Across a decade, no major decision of the period restored the equidistance settlement on religion and state, enlarged state fiscal autonomy against the centre, or rebuilt the statutory entitlement architecture the platform model displaced. Improvising governments distribute their decisions on both sides of an inherited settlement over time. A one-sided distribution across three independent domains is the empirical signature the framework reads as design, compatible with improvised execution of individual decisions.

### Q: Why does the three-tensions reading treat coalition arithmetic as a structural constraint on the Vajpayee government?

Because the Vajpayee government's dependence on allies was continuous, priced and enforceable. The BJP held 182 Lok Sabha seats after the elections of both 1998 and 1999, and governed because regional parties, the Telugu Desam Party, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and others, chose to sustain it. Their price was recorded before the government formed: the National Agenda for Governance of March 1998 set aside the abrogation of Article 370, the uniform civil code and the Ayodhya construction, the BJP's three core commitments. The constraint was enforced in fact when J. Jayalalithaa withdrew her party's support in April 1999 and the government lost its confidence motion by one vote, 269 to 270. A constraint that can end the government is structural rather than a matter of the prime minister's preference, which is why this article attributes the Vajpayee era's case-by-case engagement with the founding settlement to its arithmetic, and the Modi era's simultaneity to the majorities of 2014 and 2019 that removed the price.
