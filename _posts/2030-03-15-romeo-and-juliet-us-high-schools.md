## The Verdict First: A Deposit, Not a Destiny

Romeo and Juliet holds a position in US high schools that no law created and no national authority maintains. The play is the first Shakespeare most American students read in depth, and it arrives in the ninth grade, the opening year of high school, in district after district whose curriculum committees have never compared notes. The position looks designed. A tragedy about teenagers, assigned to teenagers, at the threshold of the high-school years: the fit seems so natural that the assignment is usually explained by the fit. This article argues the opposite order of events. The ninth-grade slot is a historical deposit, laid down by college entrance examinations that never named the play, by textbook economics that rewarded a single national list, by teacher inheritance, and by two films that made the text teachable at scale. The developmental justification, that fourteen-year-olds are the play's natural readers, was composed afterward to explain a placement already made, and the record supports it only in part. The play deserves its American eminence. It has not earned the specific chair it sits in, and the difference matters for how the chair should be used.

![Romeo and Juliet in US high schools, the ninth-grade Shakespeare classroom - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-58.webp)

The play's own Chorus announces the pattern in advance. "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, lines 5 to 6, Folger Shakespeare Library edition). The Prologue tells the audience the ending before the action begins, and American schooling does something similar to its students: the curriculum announces, years ahead, that these lovers will be waiting in the ninth-grade classroom, and the announcement is fulfilled with a regularity that no other book-length work in the system matches. What the surveys measure, what the entrance lists explain, and what the critics dispute is how a local decision, repeated fifty thousand times, hardened into something that behaves like a national one.

The argument that follows rests on three kinds of evidence, and the distinctions between them are kept visible throughout. The first is the survey record, a century of counting what American schools actually teach, from the school readers' contents through Applebee's department-chair studies to the NCTE's teacher survey of 2025; surveys say what happens, and this article treats their findings as the ground of everything else. The second is the documentary record of the systems that produce the pattern: entrance examination lists, publishers' keys, standards frameworks, course catalogues, and the published curricula districts purchase, each saying not what teachers feel but what the machinery prescribes, sells, and defends. The third is the critical record, the scholars and practitioners who have tried to explain the pattern and who disagree about whether it should survive. Where the three records agree, the article says so; where they part, the disagreement is the finding. The questions the evidence is asked to answer are the three the verdict already posed: how the ninth-grade standing is measured, how it was made, and whether the making justifies the keeping.

The scale of the pattern should be held in mind before the history explains it. The NCTE's researchers drew their respondents from the national schools database and emailed more than 107,000 public secondary English teachers to obtain their 4,096; the American system they sampled comprises tens of thousands of high schools and millions of ninth graders in a single cohort year. A practice that repeats across that scale, in systems answerable to fifty state governments and some thirteen thousand local districts, is not a fashion. Fashions are what the long tail shows, thousands of titles each chosen by a handful of teachers; the head of the distribution is a different phenomenon, and the difference is what this article exists to explain.

The governing metaphor should itself be graded before it hardens. A deposit implies a depositor, and the American case had thousands, none acting in concert: examiners who never named the play, publishers who printed for lists they did not write, reformers whose teenager needed a text, teachers who kept the book room's stock in service. A deposit also implies withdrawal, and the analogy holds there too, since the capital is spent down only by the slow decision of each generation of account holders to keep it. Where the metaphor fails is intention: nothing in the vault was placed there for safekeeping by anyone who foresaw the vault. The article keeps the word because the alternatives, tradition, canon, custom, each smuggle in a reverence the record does not support; a deposit is wealth without worship, accumulated, interest-bearing, and entirely available for spending by owners willing to pay the transaction costs.

## Romeo and Juliet in US High Schools: What the Surveys Actually Count

The strongest evidence for the play's American standing is a survey tradition with an unusually clean pedigree. In 1989 the education scholar Arthur Applebee, working from the Center for the Learning and Teaching of Literature, surveyed department chairs in public, Catholic, and independent schools serving grades 7 to 12, asking which book-length works all students in each grade studied. The design deliberately replicated a study first run in the spring of 1963 and published by Scarvia Anderson in 1964, so that change could be measured across a quarter century. Applebee's headline finding was stability. The ten most frequently taught titles in the public and Catholic samples were identical, and nearly so in the independent schools. Romeo and Juliet stood in the top ten in every sample, and in the comparison the National Council of Teachers of English later published, the play stood first on Applebee's list outright.

Thirty-six years after Applebee, the National Council of Teachers of English funded the successor study, The State of Literature Use in US Secondary English Classrooms, by Kyungae Chae and Ricki Ginsberg, released in July 2025. The researchers emailed every secondary English teacher in the United States with a publicly listed address, more than 107,000 teachers, between January 2023 and June 2024, and received 4,096 responses spanning all fifty states. Asked to name the ten texts used most frequently in their classrooms, teachers collectively produced 5,108 distinct titles. The most frequently used text in American secondary English was Romeo and Juliet, ahead of The Great Gatsby and The Crucible, with Macbeth fourth and Hamlet eighth. Almost one teacher in four reported using Romeo and Juliet or The Great Gatsby. Tonya B. Perry, the Council's president, summarized the result in Education Week: "We are reading the same books that our parents and grandparents read."

The report's context makes the finding harder to wave away. The report's own comparisons set its title lists beside those gathered in the 1980s and 1990s and found the core intact, and Education Week headlined the survey as showing that American teachers assign the same books as in the 1980s. Stability on this scale is not a sampling artifact: with 4,096 respondents naming 5,108 unique titles, the long tail of American reading is genuinely long, and the head of the distribution is genuinely fixed. Both facts belong to the same system, a profession of individual choosers whose aggregate choices reproduce a canon none of them voted for.

A second instrument gives the grade-level precision the national lists blur. Jonathan Burton, a Shakespeare scholar at Whittier College who had himself taught high school English, surveyed 400 high school English teachers across the country about which of the plays entered their curricula. His report is the source of the figure most often quoted about the play's school career: Romeo and Juliet appears in roughly 93 percent of all ninth-grade classes. The concentration extends across the canon. The four tragedies of Macbeth, Hamlet, Romeo and Juliet, and Julius Caesar account for 85 percent of all Shakespearean plays included in high school instruction, and adding Othello brings the share to roughly 90 percent: five plays of thirty-eight do nearly all the work. The transnational question of why this play, of all plays, became the standard first text is treated in the companion study of [why Romeo and Juliet is the standard school text](/2030/01/18/why-it-is-the-standard-school-text/); the American numbers are its confirming case, and they are consistent with the grading that study applied.

The grade-level tables behind the headline findings sharpen the picture. Applebee's surveys asked department chairs to list the works required of all students in each grade, and the pattern those tables show is a division of labor among the tragedies: the lovers in the ninth grade, the Roman and Scottish plays in the middle years, Hamlet reserved for the twelfth. His follow-up study of 1993, Literature in the Secondary School, found the same architecture standing after another round of reform talk, and Stotsky's century review of required book lists treats the division as one of the most stable structures in the American curriculum. The division matters because it makes the ninth-grade claim stronger than a mere plurality. A title can lead a national list the way a party leads a fragmented parliament, with a fifth of the vote; the play's ninth-grade concentration, near nine-tenths of classes on Burton's count, is closer to a constitutional settlement, reproduced annually by thousands of independent actors who have never conferred.

The counterweight belongs in the same section, because the surveys do not all count the same thing. In the spring of 2010, Sandra Stotsky, with Joan Traffas and James Woodworth, published Literary Study in Grades 9, 10, and 11: A National Survey for the Association of Literary Scholars, Critics, and Writers. Her team surveyed 406 public-school teachers and catalogued 773 standard and honors courses. By that denominator the canon thins dramatically: only three titles, Romeo and Juliet, To Kill a Mockingbird, and The Crucible, appeared in as much as 20 percent of courses, and the most assigned title of all appeared in 22.38 percent. Stotsky's conclusion was blunt: "There is no canon anymore. It may look like a canon, but each school is doing its own thing." The two pictures reconcile once the denominators are kept apart. Stotsky spread her count across three grade levels and every course a teacher described; Burton concentrated on the ninth grade, where the play's American life is lived. A title can stand in a fifth of all high-school English courses and in nearly every ninth-grade classroom at the same time, because the ninth grade is where the system has agreed, without ever voting, to put it.

One methodological caution governs every figure quoted hereafter, and the surveys' differences make it concrete. The instruments do not count the same animal. Applebee asked department chairs what was required of all students, a measure of formal curriculum filtered through the person responsible for it; Burton asked teachers about ninth-grade classes; Stotsky catalogued courses across three grades; the NCTE asked individual teachers about texts used most frequently, a category that reaches from the required whole-class novel down to the frequently assigned. Each instrument is honest, and each would give a different answer to the crude question of how canonical the play is, because canonicity itself dissolves into denominators. The NCTE's response rate demands the same candor: 4,096 replies from more than 107,000 invitations is fewer than four in every hundred invited teachers, a self-selected slice of the profession, likelier than the median teacher to answer a council's questionnaire about literature. Burton's survey, the source of the single most quoted figure in this field, circulated through the Folger's channels rather than a peer-reviewed journal, at a sample of 400 whose sampling frame its summaries do not fully describe.

The article rests its standing claims on the triangulation, not on any single instrument. Three sponsors, a testing service in 1963, an NCTE-line scholar in 1989, and the Council itself in the 2020s, using three respondent types across six decades, return the same shape: a stable head of familiar titles, the play at or near its summit, and its concentration in the ninth grade wherever grade is recorded. A shape that survives that much methodological variety is entitled to be called a finding; any single number plucked from it is entitled only to its denominator, which is why this article quotes denominators as faithfully as numerators. The same discipline explains the grading vocabulary used in the closing guide, where survey findings, documentary records, and reported practices are kept in separate rows of confidence.

### Which text did American teachers name most often in 2025?

Romeo and Juliet. In the NCTE survey released in July 2025, 4,096 public secondary teachers named the texts used most frequently in their classrooms, and the play finished first, ahead of The Great Gatsby and The Crucible, the same summit it had held in Applebee's survey thirty-six years earlier.

## Shakespeare Before Shakespeare Was a Subject: The Readers' Century

To see why the entrance examinations mattered, the prior century's arrangement needs its own account. For most of the nineteenth century, American schooling had no subject called English literature. It had reading, taught from graded readers; rhetoric, taught as the art of speaking; and the Latin and Greek classics, which carried the prestige. Shakespeare entered that arrangement as raw material for the voice. The school readers excerpted speeches and scenes, and, as Joseph Haughey's research for the Folger Shakespeare Library records, the early readers often printed the words without the author's name, Shakespeare being classed with the theatre, and the theatre with popular amusement rather than with instruction. A child might declaim Antony's funeral oration as an exercise in delivery without being told whose it was, or that it came from a play at all.

The excerpt had a canon of its own. Burton's survey of the early texts counts forty-six Shakespearean passages in William Enfield's The Speaker, the English elocution anthology whose selections American readers copied, and the pieces chosen skew toward oratory and maxim: Brutus and Antony over the body, Hamlet's soliloquies, the merchant's courtroom. When Burton totals the American readers of the nineteenth century's second half, The Merchant of Venice leads all plays in frequency of extraction, with Julius Caesar and Hamlet behind it. The lovers' tragedy, a play of private speech, of dialogue in bedrooms and orchards, offered the elocution master little: its most famous scene is two people talking quietly across a garden at night. A curriculum built on the spoken set piece had no slot for it, and the readers' century passed it by.

The moral frame of the readers' century explains the selection's shape. Burton describes the reigning objectives of the era's Shakespearean education as inculcating piety, patriotism, and eloquence, and the extracts served all three: speeches that modeled public virtue, soliloquies that modeled conscience, and a general elevation of tone credited to the language itself. The McGuffey readers, the century's dominant American series, handled the material in the same spirit, admitting Shakespearean excerpts while the early editions withheld the playwright's name, as Haughey notes, because the source was the stage. A tragedy of clandestine marriage, a compliant friar, a double suicide, and a heroine of thirteen had no extractable surface for such a program: no public oration, no patriotic set piece, nothing a child could recite at an exhibition without the story's whole machinery of desire and disobedience trailing behind it. The play's absence from the readers is thus overdetermined, explained by its form, its matter, and its venue at once.

The readers themselves changed across the century, and Haughey's account marks the stages. After the 1850s the passages begin to carry the author's name. By the 1860s and 1870s the readers were printing whole scenes, with headnotes supplying the story before and after, something recognizably ancestral to literary study rather than elocution drill. Julius Caesar served as the bridge text of the transition, a Roman play in English dress that let schools move from Latin authors toward English ones while keeping the rhetoric: the forum speeches could be studied as literature and still be declaimed. Publishers completed the bridge with the single-play school edition, glossed and introduced for classroom use; the editions of William J. Rolfe and his successors, visible in college and school catalogues of the 1880s, put a whole play, priced for students, into the market that the readers had prepared.

The century's end brought the conditions of change together. The theatre's respectability rose; the playwright's name became an ornament rather than a liability; and the high school itself expanded from a preparatory annex of the colleges toward a mass institution with students who would never sit an entrance examination. Each development loosened the elocution canon. None of them, by itself, installed the lovers. Installation required the new subject of English, a publishing industry organized around set books, and the twentieth century's redefinition of the student, and those arrive in the sections that follow. What the entrance examinations then did, in the 1870s and after, was to choose which of the whole plays a school was obliged to teach, and they chose from the readers' stock, not from the stage's: the Roman play, the Venetian play, the Scottish play. Romeo and Juliet reached the twentieth century as the most famous love story in the language and, simultaneously, as a play the American curriculum had never been organized around. Its later installation is therefore not a survival but an event, and events have causes that can be dated.

## Before the Play Had a Grade: Readers, Elocution, and the Entrance Examinations

The deposit's first layer is nineteenth-century, and the play is not in it. Shakespeare entered American schooling through the reader, the graded anthology of extracts from which children learned to read aloud. Joseph Haughey of Northwest Missouri State University, whose research traces the evolution of the English curriculum, describes the early practice in the Folger Shakespeare Library's Shakespeare Unlimited interviews: passages from the plays were printed without the author's name, because Shakespeare's reputation was that of popular entertainment, "not appropriate to be read in school," and the speeches were set as exercises in elocution rather than objects of study. After the 1850s the readers began to attribute the passages, then to print whole scenes with context, and the bridge play was Julius Caesar, whose Roman rhetoric let a Latin-trained school system cross into English literature while keeping its bearings.

Burton's history of the extract era quantifies the taste. In the second half of the nineteenth century no play was extracted for American readers more than The Merchant of Venice, followed closely by Julius Caesar and Hamlet. The forty-six Shakespearean passages in Enfield's The Speaker had already established a de facto canon for American students earlier in the century, and the lovers' tragedy is absent from the commanding heights of that canon. Romeo and Juliet was a famous play in nineteenth-century America; it was not yet a school play. The distinction is the article's foundation: fame and curricular installation are different events, separated in this case by the better part of a century.

The machinery of installation was the college entrance examination. Applebee's history, Tradition and Reform in the Teaching of English (1974), locates the birth of the school subject in Harvard's entrance examinations of 1873 and 1874, for which candidates wrote compositions on set works that included The Tempest, Julius Caesar, and The Merchant of Venice. In Applebee's account the requirement institutionalized the study of standard authors and forced English to consolidate its position in the schools. The National Education Association's Committee of Ten, reporting in 1893, regularized the framework around those examinations: a four-year secondary course, English studied in every year, and high schools oriented toward the colleges' published requirements. Publishers moved at once to serve the market the lists created. The printed key to the works required for the years 1901 to 1905, sold with Maynard's English Classic Series, shows what careful study then meant: the Shakespeare prescribed for those years was Macbeth, The Merchant of Venice, and Julius Caesar. The play that American ninth graders would inherit is not on the list.

The machinery deserves a closer look, because its details explain its durability. The college requirements of the period divided English into two examinations with different logics. Reading and practice ranged widely: candidates wrote on topics drawn from a considerable list of books read in school and at home, and breadth was the point. Study and practice narrowed to a handful of books prescribed for careful study, examined, as the printed requirements put it, on subject matter, literary form, and logical structure, with questions on grammar and on the leading facts of the literary periods to which the works belonged. A prescribed book under this regime was not a story to have enjoyed; it was an apparatus of examinable features, and publishers built the apparatus into the book itself. The school edition of a set play arrived with the introduction, notes, and questions that the study examination rewarded, and a play without such an edition was, for practical purposes, unteachable at scale. The Maynard key quoted in this section is a publisher's document: it lists the required works for 1901 to 1905 together with the serial numbers of the editions in which the publisher sold them. Requirement and commodity were printed on the same page.

Coordination tightened the screw. The colleges, competing for students yet embarrassed by divergent requirements, moved through the 1890s toward common lists, and in 1900 founded the College Entrance Examination Board to set unified examinations; its first papers were offered in 1901. A unified examination meant a unified shelf: a school anywhere in the country prepared candidates for the same study list, and a publisher could print for a national market with confidence. The Committee of Ten's settlement of 1893 had already pressed in this direction, recommending English in every year of a four-year course and treating the colleges' requirements as the curriculum's north point. By the first decade of the twentieth century the American high school's literary core was, in effect, syndicated, and the syndicate's Shakespeare was Macbeth, The Merchant of Venice, and Julius Caesar, the plays of the 1901 to 1905 key, joined across the years by Hamlet and As You Like It in rotation. The lovers' tragedy held no seat in the syndicate. When reformers later attacked the canon as rigid, this is the rigidity they meant: not a conspiracy of taste but a coordination technology, examinations plus editions, that made deviation expensive for every participant at once.

Applebee's history adds the coda that matters for everything after. The examination system also created the profession that administered it: a teacher of English was, by definition, a person prepared to teach the listed books, and teacher preparation reproduced the list in each new cohort. When the lists loosened, and they did loosen under progressive pressure in the decades after 1910, the preparation remained, because it was embodied in people and in the stock of annotated editions their schools already owned. The canon thereby acquired its characteristic American property, visible in every later survey: it could survive the repeal of its own legal basis.

The states built a parallel track that mirrored the colleges'. New York's Regents system, the oldest state examination regime, published syllabi in English that named the same canon: its English language and literature syllabus in force from September 1902 lists Julius Caesar, Macbeth, and The Merchant of Venice among its set texts. The correspondence is the mechanism in miniature. A state syllabus writer in Albany and a college examiner in Cambridge reached for the same three plays because both were drawing on the readers' stock and the available school editions, and each man's list then justified the other's. The lovers' absence from both lists is therefore a single absence with two addresses, not two independent judgments, and this article counts it once, as the entrance system's own verdict on the corpus it inherited.

### Did the college entrance lists ever prescribe this play?

No. The surviving keys to the college requirements for 1901 to 1905 prescribe Macbeth, The Merchant of Venice, and Julius Caesar for careful study, and Harvard's examinations of 1873 and 1874 set The Tempest beside the Roman and Venetian plays. The lovers entered by a later road, after the entrance system had chosen its Shakespeare.

Dewey-era reform loosened the examination boards' grip, as Applebee documents, without disturbing the underlying economics: a school subject defined by set books needs set books that publishers can sell everywhere and teachers can teach from preparation they already own. Through the first half of the twentieth century the examination inheritance kept Julius Caesar and Macbeth at the front of the school Shakespeare line. The lovers' ascent belongs to the middle of the century, and its causes are datable. Burton names three milestones in the longer evolution: the formation of English as a formal discipline in the 1890s; the invention of the teenager in the 1930s and 1940s, when adolescence was first marketed and studied as a distinct stage of life with its own culture; and rising concern about anti-Semitism after the Second World War, which quietly retired The Merchant of Venice from its position as the most-extracted school play. A canon slot opened at exactly the moment a newly defined audience, the teenager, appeared to fill it. The play of adolescent catastrophe was waiting.

## The Long Middle: Character Study, New Criticism, and the Play's Slow Rise

The half century after the entrance system's peak is the least dramatic part of the record and the most instructive. The lists loosened; the curriculum did not empty. Burton describes the first quarter of the twentieth century as the era of character study, in which the plays were used as a kind of literary moral science: students weighed Hamlet's obligation to avenge his father or Brutus's culpability in the conspiracy, and the exemplary psychology of great figures supplied the lesson. The method dovetailed, in the 1950s and 1960s, with the New Criticism filtering down from the universities, which prized the close reading of the self-sufficient text and asked of a school play exactly what the study-and-practice examination had asked: attention to form, structure, and verbal texture. Regimes changed their slogans while keeping their books.

The student body changed underneath the syllabus. At the century's opening the high school was a preparatory institution serving a minority of adolescents, most of them aimed at the entrance examinations; by 1940 a majority of American teenagers were enrolled, and by the postwar decades the comprehensive high school taught nearly all of them. A curriculum designed for candidates had to become a curriculum for citizens, workers, and consumers of a new youth culture, and the readers gave way across the same decades to the literature anthology, a book organized by genre and period rather than by elocutionary difficulty. Haughey's history marks the readers' long endurance, into the late twentieth century in some lines, but the anthology's logic prevailed: a ninth-grade anthology needed a complete, discussable drama, and needed it to be one a general student, not only a college candidate, could be brought to finish. The full-length play in the anthology is the long middle's characteristic artifact, and the lovers' tragedy, complete in five acts, actable in excerpts, arguable in every scene, fitted the artifact as the extract never had.

Across the long middle the lovers' tragedy climbed without ever being decreed. When Scarvia B. Anderson's survey for the Educational Testing Service, conducted in the spring of 1963 and published in 1964, catalogued the titles required in 222 representative schools, the play stood among the leaders of the list her successors would keep measuring; Applebee's 1989 replication and Stotsky's later century review both treat her top titles as the baseline company from which the modern canon descends. The climb has datable assists. The teenager, invented as a cultural and commercial category in the 1930s and 1940s, gave educators a student whose defining experiences, first love, peer loyalty, quarrels with parental authority, were the play's own materials, and postwar curriculum writing began to say so. The Merchant of Venice's quiet retirement removed the incumbent whose place in the early grades the lovers could take. And the postwar paperback put whole plays, cheaply, into students' own hands, ending the era in which a class met its Shakespeare in extracts chosen by a reader's editor a century dead.

Anderson's 1963 survey deserves its footnote, since the whole tradition leans on it. Sponsored in the Educational Testing Service's orbit and published in 1964, it asked 222 representative schools which books were required of all students, and its forty-two leading titles became the baseline Stotsky's 1991 review, Books in American Education, used to judge a century of lists. The play stood high in that company, and the company's character is the point: Anderson's leaders are the titles the postwar school had settled on, and the lovers' presence among them, achieved without any entrance list's compulsion, is the first hard evidence that the installation the teenager thesis describes had already happened by 1963. Applebee's 1989 study was designed as its replication, down to the earlier survey's categories, which is why the two studies can be laid side by side at all: the tradition's comparability is a deliberate artifact of its makers' respect for their predecessors.

Two cautions grade this part of the record. The first is evidentiary: the mid-century rise is documented at the level of surveys, curriculum guides, and publishing, not at the level of a decision anyone signed, and any single-cause story, the teenager, the film, the paperback, overreads its strand. The second is Burton's own point about the period's objectives: as piety, patriotism, and eloquence faded as the stated aims of Shakespearean education, the plays had to be justified by the uses of the present, and a play that could be taught as a study of adolescence had a present use built in. The reframing the lesson literature of the 1960s and 1970s completed, treating Romeo and Juliet as a contemporary work and teaching it beside its modern descendants, was the long middle's outcome, not its starting point. By the time Zeffirelli's film arrived in 1968 the curricular ground had been prepared for it for thirty years; the film harvested a field it did not plant.

## A Canon Without a Canon-Maker: Decentralization and the Common Core

The United States has no national curriculum, no ministry list, and no examination board that sets texts for the whole country. Text selection sits with states at the level of standards, with districts at the level of adopted materials, and with departments and individual teachers at the level of the classroom shelf. The NCTE survey of 2025 measured the resulting distribution of authority: only one teacher in five reported having no choice over the texts used, the rest reporting varying degrees of flexibility, while most also reported some required reading set by a school or district. The play's ubiquity is therefore an emergent fact. No American institution can claim to have ordered it, and none can rescind it.

The contrast with the system examined in the companion account of [Romeo and Juliet in the UK GCSE](/2030/03/08/romeo-and-juliet-uk-gcse/) is structural. England names its set texts through regulated examination boards and a national curriculum that specifies Shakespeare by author; the American system specifies skills and lets the titles precipitate out of custom. The precipitate, in both countries, happens to be the same small company of plays, which is evidence that the forces at work are not mainly regulatory. Where a board chooses freely and where a market chooses freely, both arrive at Romeo and Juliet, because both are choosing from the stock that teacher training, publishing, and the stage have already made cheapest to choose.

The Common Core State Standards, adopted by most states in the years after 2010, illustrate the American arrangement at its most paradoxical. The standards name no required novel or play in their grade-level requirements; they specify the complexity and range of reading expected. Yet Shakespeare occupies a singular place in the framework. The standards' own supporting guidance treats the playwright as the one author high-school students are directed to read: David Coleman and Susan Pimentel's Publishers' Criteria of 2012 summarized the expectation as a requirement that students read a play by Shakespeare, the only such author-specific obligation in the document. The compulsion attaches to the author, never to the title. And the standards' Appendix B, which offers exemplar texts to illustrate the complexity expected at each band, names Macbeth for grades 9 to 10 and Hamlet for grades 11 to 12. The national framework's one Shakespearean summons points at two other plays. The ninth grade went on teaching the lovers anyway, on the strength of a custom older and stronger than the standards.

The adoption states show the machinery at its most visible. Texas historically chose instructional materials through a state adoption process whose approved list bounded what districts could buy with state funds, and California ran a parallel system for the elementary and middle grades; reforms, including Texas's 2011 instructional-materials legislation, loosened the lists into allotments and local choice, yet the market logic survived the loosening, because publishers still build for the two states' standards and review habits first and sell the resulting book everywhere else as a finished product. The precision that matters is negative as much as positive: no adoption list in either state has ever needed to name this play, since the editions submitted for adoption arrive with the ninth-grade slot already built into their tables of contents. The states ratify; the publishers propose; the districts dispose. It is the federal system's perfect emblem, a national curriculum assembled from three layers of decision, none of which is a decision about Romeo and Juliet. The adoption hearing, in this arrangement, is the closest the United States comes to a national curriculum debate, and its record is instructive: publishers defend reading lists assembled from the surveys' familiar titles, because a list of strangers would be a commercial risk no adoption cycle rewards. The hearing thus records the deposit auditing itself and finding itself sound.

The commercial market states the settlement as plainly as any survey. Apex Learning, the online course provider, sells a ninth-grade English course under the title English 9 with Romeo and Juliet, in standard and honors versions, in which the play occupies the course's drama unit, aligned to state standards that nowhere name it. A vendor does not put a play in a course title out of sentiment; the title is a promise to purchasing districts that the course contains what their outlines already assume. Between the standards that omit the play and the catalogue that headlines it lies the whole American arrangement, and it is an arrangement of documents answering documents, none of which is a law.

One federal hand does touch school Shakespeare, and its shape proves the rule. The National Endowment for the Arts has, since 2003, run Shakespeare in American Communities with Arts Midwest, touring professional productions to communities and schools across all fifty states, with educational materials in support. It is the American state's characteristic gesture toward the school canon: theatrical, voluntary, and silent about curriculum. Washington will pay to bring the play to a town's stage; it will not tell the town's ninth grade to read it. The British system examined elsewhere in this series concentrates the decision in examination boards whose specifications name set texts; the American system disperses it past the reach of any capital, and the dispersion, not any official's preference, is the first fact of the play's American career.

### Is Romeo and Juliet required by law in any American state?

No state statute or standard names the play as required reading. American standards specify skills and text complexity by grade; the Common Core framework directs students to Shakespeare as an author while exemplifying Macbeth and Hamlet. The play's ninth-grade presence rests on district adoption, departmental practice, and custom, not on legal mandate.

## The Machinery That Keeps It There: Textbooks, Adoption, and the Paperback Class Set

Custom becomes durable when it is embodied in objects a school can buy. The American textbook trade supplied the embodiment. Surveys of the trade have long reported that the best-selling national textbooks place Romeo and Juliet in the ninth grade as a matter of course, alongside Hamlet, Macbeth, A Midsummer Night's Dream, and Julius Caesar at other levels, and a ninth-grade anthology that omitted the play would be unsellable to the districts whose course outlines expect it. The anthology is both mirror and mold: it reflects the grade placement and then reproduces it in every adopting school, including schools whose teachers never made the choice themselves.

The geography of American textbook publishing concentrates the effect. Most states leave adoption to districts, but the largest states historically ran statewide adoption processes whose specifications publishers designed entire national lists to satisfy, and what satisfied Texas and California was sold, with light revision, to Ohio and Florida. A title's presence in the big-state ninth-grade volume thus became a national fact wearing local dress. The NCTE report of 2025 adds the modern form of the same machinery: 35 percent of surveyed teachers reported working with a scripted curriculum, in which the sequence of texts arrives preassembled by a publisher or district office. Curriculum committees and adoption panels review those assembled units through shared documents and slide decks, the kind of material a department chair opens through [the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) when a district circulates its proposed course of study, and the play's presence in the deck is rarely the item under discussion. It is the premise the discussion starts from.

Odell is not alone in the market it serves. Amplify Education's published scope and sequence for its ninth-grade ELA program likewise builds a unit on the play, and Fishtank Learning's ninth-grade course does the same, three independent publishers converging on the identical placement without any coordinating authority. Convergence of competitors is the market's way of registering a fact none of them created: the slot exists before the product, and the product is engineered to fit it. A publisher that proposed to fill the ninth-grade drama position with a different play would be proposing, in effect, to sell districts a second book room, and the proposal would die in the first adoption meeting. The curricula therefore enter this article's evidence not as causes but as seismographs, instruments sensitive enough to register the deposit's depth and honest enough, in their convergence, to confirm it.

The training circuit closes the machinery's loop. The Folger's education department, founded in 1981 under Peggy O'Brien, and its Teaching Shakespeare Institute, founded in 1984, train serving teachers in performance-based approaches to the plays, and the library's own account of its mission describes working with teachers and students to make Shakespeare compete in a crowded curriculum. Graduates return to departments carrying methods tuned to the texts the system teaches, the lovers first among them, and alumni networks keep the methods in circulation. Nothing in this is sinister; it is what professional formation looks like. Its structural consequence, though, is the one Applebee's model predicts: the profession's most energetic Shakespeare training presupposes the existing canon and equips teachers to teach it better, so reform energy is continuously converted into conservation skill. The deposit does not merely persist; it is annually re-skilled.

### Who actually chooses the play for a given ninth-grade classroom?

Formally, the choice belongs to districts, departments, and, in four cases out of five by the NCTE's 2025 count, partly to the teacher. Practically, the chooser is usually a document: the anthology's table of contents, the course outline, or a published curriculum's unit sequence, each inherited from its predecessor. Teachers ratify the choice far more often than they originate it.

The clearest single object in this history is a paperback. In February 1958 Washington Square Press issued the first of its Folger Shakespeare Library editions, inexpensive mass-market volumes edited under Louis B. Wright, the Folger's director, with Virginia A. LaMar, carrying the text on the right-hand page and explanatory help drawn from the Library's collections. A research library in Washington, holding the world's largest collection of the playwright's printed works, had put its name on a book priced for a school to buy by the classroom set, and the set could survive a decade of fourteen-year-olds. The series was comprehensively renewed in 1992, when the New Folger editions edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine began appearing, again as mass-market paperbacks, the Romeo and Juliet among the first volumes. Generations of American students have therefore met the play in a physical form that announces institutional custody: the nation's Shakespeare archive, shrunk to pocket size.

Beneath the editions lies a plainer economics. A school does not buy a play the way a reader buys a novel; it buys a class set, thirty or sixty copies, shelved in a book room and issued year after year until the binding fails. A title with a serviceable class set on the shelf has an incumbent's advantage measured in replacement budgets: to change the ninth-grade play is to spend scarce money discarding a working asset and buying an unproven one, and department budgets are rarely voted for experiments. Paperbacks softened the arithmetic without removing it, since even a cheap text is bought by the crate, and the crates accumulate into the stock that anchors the outline. The Folger paperback's long life as the default classroom text is therefore also an inventory fact: libraries and book rooms hold it in depth, teachers plan from its lineation and scene divisions, and the marginal cost of continuing is near zero while the marginal cost of switching is a budget line someone must defend. Deposits are made of such arithmetic as much as of ideas.

Published curricula complete the circuit. Odell Education's High School Literacy Program of 2020, reviewed favorably by EdReports and adopted in districts across the country, builds a ninth-grade development unit around the play under the central question "Why do we still read Shakespeare?", pairing the text with informational readings and contemporary film adaptations; other published ninth-grade programs, Fishtank Learning's among them, do the same. The unit's teacher notes, as Massachusetts reviewers observed, warn that the opening scene's first thirty lines contain lewd jokes, and offer no guidance on contextualizing them, a small documentary trace of the national settlement: the play is installed, its difficulties noted, and their handling left to the adult in the room. A play that entered the curriculum by custom is also distributed by design, and the design is purchased.

## The Shape of the American Year: Where the Play Sits in the Course

Placement is also a matter of neighbors. The American ninth-grade year, as the surveys and the published programs describe it, is built from short fiction, a novel of adolescence, poetry, and one full-length drama, and the drama is the play. Its companions vary by district; its position does not. The tragedies are then dealt upward through the remaining years, the Roman and Scottish plays in the tenth and eleventh grades in the common pattern, Hamlet held for the twelfth, so that the sequence from the lovers to the Dane doubles as the system's unofficial syllabus of Shakespeare, four encounters in four years for the student who stays in the standard sequence. Stotsky's survey design, standard and honors courses in grades 9 to 11, records the play near the head of both tracks' lists, a detail worth pausing on: tracking, the American system's great differentiator, differentiates almost everything except this. The honors student and the standard student in the same building read different novels and the same tragedy.

A second distribution system answers to the same duties. Four years of English stand among the near-universal requirements for an American high-school diploma, a settlement that descends from the Committee of Ten's recommendation of English in every year of the course. The requirement is stated in credits and skills, never in titles, yet its effect is titular: a compulsory four-year sequence must be filled, filled largely with book-length works, and filled in an order teachers can defend. The ninth-grade tragedy is the first brick of a compulsory wall. Arguments for removing it are therefore never arguments about one book; they are arguments about what bears the load at the base of four mandated years, and they fail, when they fail, for structural reasons that have nothing to do with Verona.

The position carries a structural privilege no other title enjoys. For most American students the play is the first complete work of early modern literature attempted at length, and the first text met in a classroom edition whose notes are necessary rather than decorative. Everything later, the Scottish play's witches, Hamlet's soliloquies, even the sonnets when they come, is read with skills the ninth-grade unit installed: how to construe an inverted sentence, how to hear a speech as an action, how to keep a five-act architecture in mind. Teachers of the later plays inherit a trained reader, and know it, which is one more mechanism of the deposit: the senior curriculum presupposes the freshman unit, and a department that dropped the play would have to rebuild the foundation its own upper courses stand on. Custom, in the American case, is load-bearing.

In its published form the unit shows the shape plainly. Odell Education's ninth-grade program gives the play a development unit with section diagnostics and a culminating task, the summative performance toward which the reading builds; Fishtank Learning's ninth-grade course likewise builds its year around the encounter. The unit is long by anthology standards, weeks rather than days, because the text is long: at roughly three thousand lines the play is half as long again as Macbeth, a fact worth holding against the lazy version of its reputation, since the ninth-grade slot cannot be explained by brevity. What the slot is explained by, this section has argued, is sequence: the lovers stand at the gate of the four-year course, and a gate, once built, is used by everyone who passes.

## Why Fourteen? The Developmental Justification on Trial

Every defense of the ninth-grade slot eventually arrives at the same sentence: the students are the lovers' age. The text supplies the warrant. Capulet, refusing Paris, says of Juliet, "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.8 to 9). Juliet is thirteen, on the edge of fourteen, in a play whose other principal is a young man of unstated age, and the American ninth grader is fourteen or fifteen. The match is real. The question is whether the match made the placement or merely ratified it, and the chronology assembled in the preceding sections points one way: the entrance lists that built the canon never named the play; the play rose in the middle of the twentieth century, as the teenager was being invented as a cultural category; and the age argument appears in the curricular literature as the rise's explanation, not as its documented cause. The developmental defense is a rationale fitted to a deposit.

Burton's periodization gives the mechanism its sharpest form. A school system that had used Shakespeare for elocution, then for character study, acquired in the 1930s and 1940s a new object, the adolescent, whose inner life education was newly charged to address. A tragedy whose principals are adolescents, whose adults are catastrophically uncomprehending, and whose plot runs on the speed of young feeling answered the new charge as no Roman play could. Lesson literature of the 1960s and 1970s then reframed the play explicitly as a contemporary work, teaching it beside West Side Story and the new Zeffirelli film, and the ninth grade, the first year in which students are old enough for the material and young enough to be its subjects, became the naturalized home.

The working teacher's version of the defense is concrete rather than theoretical. A ninth-grade English teacher in the Eagle Mountain-Saginaw schools outside Fort Worth, quoted in Education Week's coverage of the NCTE survey, describes students taking to the play immediately: "I teach 9th graders. They're all about falling in love, and it's always immediate, and there's always consequences." Harrison pairs the play with Jason Reynolds's verse novel Long Way Down, and explains the pairing through the feud: "There's this theme of this feud that costs lives, that's so meaningless, that's in both stories." The classroom evidence embedded in that account is worth weighing precisely. What the fourteen-year-olds recognize, on the teacher's own telling, is not Verona's courtship customs but the machinery of loyalty, retaliation, and adult failure, the play's social engine. The recognition defense, tested against classroom practice, supports a narrower claim than its usual form: students are the age of the lovers, and they read the feud, the peer pressure, and the catastrophic adults from inside. The romance is the door; the recognition that carries the unit is sociological.

Scholarship supplied the category before the market did. G. Stanley Hall's Adolescence, published in 1904, had already named the teenage years a distinct and turbulent stage of life, a period of storm and stress requiring its own institutions; the 1930s and 1940s then built those institutions, the high-school social world, the juvenile market, the youth audience, into a culture adolescents inhabited and adults addressed. Burton's milestone is the second of these events, the cultural invention, rather than Hall's book, and the distinction matters for the play: a psychological stage can be ignored by a syllabus, but a market and a social world cannot. Once American society agreed that fourteen-year-olds constituted a kind of person, a curriculum that addressed that kind of person in its own terms acquired an argument no entrance list could supply or repeal.

The play's own adults make the argument's darker half. Capulet's first position in the text is protective refusal: Paris is told that his daughter has not seen the change of fourteen years, and that those married so early are too soon marred (1.2). Within two days of stage time the same father is promising her to Paris on a deadline and threatening disownment at her refusal. The Friar, the play's other senior counselor, responds to a young person's despair by staging a funeral. American teachers did not invent the reading of the play as an indictment of adult failure; the text loads it. What the mid-century reframing added was the identification of the student with the indicted parties' victims, and the ninth grade, where students are newest to institutional authority and most subject to it, is where that identification has its maximum charge.

The age defense also survives inspection from an angle its proponents rarely use: the play's catastrophe is not really about the young. The adults of Verona are not old; Capulet's generation stands one step up, and Tybalt, Mercutio, and Paris are young men whose code kills as efficiently as any elder's command. What destroys the lovers is not a generation gap but a distribution of power, the authority to marry, to forbid, to banish, to pardon, held entirely by people who did not have to live inside the feud's daily costs at the lovers' level. Fourteen-year-olds, the most power-asymmetric audience in the building, are exquisitely placed to read that distribution, and the classroom evidence teachers report, the anger at the Friar and at Capulet that fills the NCTE's accounts of student response, is the sound of the placement working. The defense, restated at this strength, no longer needs the false premise that the students are the lovers; it needs only the true one, that the students are the lovers' subjects, governed by authorities whose quarrels set the terms of their lives.

The limits of the defense are equally concrete. Juliet's situation, marriageable at thirteen under paternal negotiation, has no analogue in a modern ninth grader's life, and the play's speed, catastrophe inside a week, belongs to fable rather than adolescence. Teachers who lean on sameness must spend the unit's second half dismantling it, explaining a world in which a father's consent structured a daughter's future and a friar's pharmacy was a plausible instrument. The play is therefore not a mirror held up to fourteen. It is a near subject handled at a far distance, and the honest form of the developmental claim is that the distance is the right size for a first encounter: close enough to compel, far enough to examine. That claim is defensible. It is also, demonstrably, an argument made after the fact for a placement the examinations, the publishers, and the century had already arranged.

### Why do American students meet the play at fourteen rather than at sixteen?

Because the ninth grade was the slot the machinery left open. The entrance-era canon dealt its Roman and Scottish plays across the later years; when the teenager emerged as education's subject in the 1930s and 1940s, the adolescent tragedy settled into the first year, where students were held old enough for its matter and young enough to be its peers.

## The Films That Teach Beside the Text: The American Pairings

No account of the American unit is complete without the wheeled-in screen. The play's ninth-grade career runs on a documented double feature. Franco Zeffirelli's Romeo and Juliet of 1968 arrived at the exact moment the reframing needed an instrument. Zeffirelli, after a worldwide search for unknown teenagers, cast Leonard Whiting and Olivia Hussey, sixteen and fifteen at their casting by the production's chronology and seventeen and sixteen during filming, the first screen lovers close to the ages the text specifies. The picture was made for about 1.5 million dollars, earned some 50 million, and won Academy Awards for its cinematography and costume design, with nominations for best picture and best director. Schools organized field trips to it; Shakespeare, in a contemporary reviewer's phrase, had been made commercial. The film's period dress and Italian locations gave departments a screening that required no apology for modernization, and its casting gave the developmental argument a face.

The same film carries the era's content settlement in miniature. Released in American cinemas on 8 October 1968, weeks before the new MPAA ratings system took effect, it was classified G when ratings caught up with it and later raised to PG, the brief bedroom scene between the lovers the stated ground; some markets exhibited prints with that scene cut. For classroom purposes the episode matters less as censorship than as calibration: the nation's schools collectively decided that this film, nudity and all, belonged inside the ninth-grade unit, a decision districts have been re-ratifying, print by print and stream by stream, ever since. The handling of the debates that decision continues to provoke belongs to the pedagogy of the play's difficult content; their existence inside the American unit is the point here.

Contemporary documentation preserves the school's-eye view of the film's arrival. Cinema Retro's production history records that American schools organized field trips to see it, because for once Shakespeare had been made commercial; the same history records the patchwork of its exhibition, some markets showing prints with the bedroom scene removed, and one West Texas arrangement in which a censored version rated M played the matinees while the fuller version rated R played the evenings, in the confused first season of the new ratings system. The detail is worth more than its color. From its first American season the film was simultaneously a school text and a ratings problem, and the two identities have never separated: every later debate about screening it, about permission slips, about which print or stream a district licenses, replays the settlement of 1968, in which the educational claim won and the scissors stayed available.

In 1996 the pairing reversed polarity. Baz Luhrmann's William Shakespeare's Romeo + Juliet, released on 1 November 1996, kept the play's language, trimmed, and set it in a modern Verona Beach of gas stations and handguns engraved with the names of swords. It cost about 14.5 million dollars and returned roughly 147 million worldwide in the commonly reported tally, the largest gross of any Shakespeare film. Leonardo DiCaprio, twenty-one, played Romeo; Claire Danes, seventeen, played Juliet, after Natalie Portman, at fourteen, was let go in rehearsals for looking too young beside him, an irony the industry recorded without comment. The rating, PG-13 for scenes of contemporary violence and some sensuality, made the film a permission-slip question in some districts and a default in others. Teachers adopted it quickly and durably; the British Film Institute's 2005 list of fifty films to see by the age of fourteen ratified the instinct. Where Zeffirelli proved the text could be beautiful at classroom scale, Luhrmann proved it could be loud there, and between them the two films bracket the American unit's screen practice: one for the play's world, one for the students'.

The film's manufacture explains part of its classroom durability. Luhrmann, then the director of a single feature, sold the project to Twentieth Century Fox through a workshop in Sydney for which DiCaprio, already Oscar-nominated for another role, flew down at his own expense to rehearse the fight scenes on test footage; the studio bought the evidence. DiCaprio took the Silver Bear for best actor at Berlin in 1997. The casting history also left its trace in the record: Natalie Portman, cast first and rehearsed at fourteen, was replaced, Luhrmann concluding she looked too young beside a Romeo of twenty-one, and Claire Danes, at seventeen, took the part. A film about the exact calibration of youthful appearance was itself calibrated the same way, and teachers who screen it inherit both calibrations, the play's and the production's. Teach with Movies, the longstanding clearinghouse of classroom film guidance, lists both the Zeffirelli and the Luhrmann versions among its recommended supplements for the play at the high-school level, and advises the comparison the profession settled on: one watched in class, the other assigned, the differences themselves becoming the lesson.

A third film completes the pattern and shows its direction of travel. Carlo Carlei's Romeo and Juliet of 2013, with a screenplay by Julian Fellowes, returned to period dress and a softened ending-adjacent sentimentality aimed squarely at the school and family market that the earlier pair had built; [Carlo Carlei's 2013 film and the Fellowes script](/2028/08/11/carlo-carlei-2013-film/) receive their own treatment in this series. The sequence is instructive. By 2013 a producer could count on the ninth-grade unit as a distribution channel: the American classroom had become one of the play's reliable habitats, and the film industry had learned to shoot for it.

The Carlei film's own production record shows how completely the formula had standardized. Its screenplay, by Julian Fellowes, returned the story to period dress and Italian locations, with exteriors filmed in Mantua, Verona, Caprarola, and Subiaco; its Juliet, Hailee Steinfeld, was cast at fourteen, the character's age to the year, opposite a Romeo in his early twenties. Each element repeats a Zeffirelli decision of 1968: age-true casting, the Italian setting photographed as evidence, a text trimmed for pace, a marketing campaign that spoke to schools as much as to cinemas. Where Luhrmann's 1996 film had rebelled against the formula while depending on it, the 2013 production obeyed it outright, and its modest commercial career beside its durable classroom circulation measures the deposit exactly: a Romeo and Juliet film no longer needs the general audience first. The ninth grade is audience enough.

### What did Zeffirelli do in 1968 that no earlier screen Romeo had done?

He cast the lovers at the characters' own ages. After a worldwide search he chose two unknowns, Leonard Whiting and Olivia Hussey, sixteen and fifteen when cast, so that the screen's Romeo and Juliet were actual adolescents rather than adults performing youth. The casting made the play's age arithmetic visible to every classroom that screened it.

The stage completes the curricular triangle. American students meet the play not only on the page and the screen but in school matinees at regional Shakespeare companies and in the Folger's own student programs in Washington, where classes perform scenes on a professional stage. The matinee is the oldest of the three encounters historically, the legitimate theatre's school performances long predating film, and it carries a distinct pedagogy in the profession's literature: the audience of fourteen-year-olds discovers, in a room full of other schools' fourteen-year-olds, that the play is an event rather than an assignment. No survey in this article's record counts matinee attendance, and the encounter is entered here at reported strength, as the practice the companies and the library document about themselves. Its curricular weight is nonetheless real: a district that books the buses has voted for the play with the only ballot that cannot be abstained, its transport budget.

The second film's rating moved the negotiation into the middle schools. Luhrmann's version carries PG-13, a classification whose terms caution parents about material, and published discussion of its classroom use records debate over screening it below the high-school level at all, with some middle schools showing it under permission and others declining. The pattern is the 1968 settlement at a new rating: the educational claim is weighed building by building, and the scales are a consent form. Nothing in the record suggests the debate has ever displaced the film from the high-school unit; its function there, as the modern-dress counterweight in the comparison lesson, is too well established in the profession's materials. The films' ratings history, G to PG to PG-13 across three generations of the same story, is a small exact history of the American settlement itself: the content stays constant, the apparatus of permission grows, and the play remains.

## West Side Story: The Bridge Text and Its Quiet Correction

The musical arrived first, and for a generation of American teachers it was the on-ramp. West Side Story opened on Broadway in 1957: conceived, directed, and choreographed by Jerome Robbins, music by Leonard Bernstein, lyrics by Stephen Sondheim in his Broadway debut, book by Arthur Laurents, 732 performances in its first run. The 1961 film, directed by Robert Wise and Robbins, won ten of the eleven Academy Awards for which it was nominated, including best picture. A retelling of the lovers' story among New York gangs, the Jets and the Sharks, it gave teachers a version of the plot in American dress, in American English, at American scale, and lesson plans of the 1960s and 1970s used it exactly so, setting the musical beside the play so that recognition could precede difficulty.

The bridge deserves its own verdict inside the larger one, because the musical is not a transparent window onto the play. Its ending corrects Shakespeare's. Maria survives. Tony dies in her arms, and the musical closes on her indictment of both gangs and a fragile procession of reconciliation, not on a double suicide in a tomb. The American bridge text thus teaches the feud's cost while withholding the play's terminal logic, in which the catastrophe requires both deaths and the friar's failed letter, and school units that cross the bridge inherit the substitution unless the teacher names it. The correction also runs the other way: the musical's feud is racial and economic, drawn in the colors of postwar New York, and students who meet it first read Verona's causeless grudge through it, finding in the play a sociology its text deliberately leaves blank. The Prince's closing couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3), names the story's owners; the musical redistributes the ownership to the community. As a bridge the work is superb and as a lens it is tinted, and the American record shows the tint was accepted for decades as the price of the crossing. The price was worth paying, provided the toll is itemized in class.

The musical's school career is double. On the English side it is the play's licensed descendant, screened and compared; on the auditorium side it is one of the most performed titles in American school theatre, the rare show whose study in one department and production in another coincide in the same building in the same year. Spielberg's 2021 film, a Best Picture nominee that returned the property to wide attention, gave the pairing a third screen generation: a student can move from the play to a 1961 musical film to a 2021 remake of that film, three strata of American mediation, each correcting the one beneath it. The chain itself has become an argument teachers use, evidence that the story is not a monument but a machine American culture keeps rebuilding, and each rebuilding quietly re-ratifies the ninth-grade text at the chain's head.

## The Pairing Shelf: What the Play Is Taught Beside

The American unit rarely teaches the play alone in its theme. The NCTE's 2025 reporting is full of the pairing instinct: teachers described weaving contemporary pieces through the required classics so that students meet the same matter in two centuries at once, and one Nebraska teacher quoted in the report described weaving poems, stories, and novels that reinforce a classic's themes with more modern backgrounds into a required list that the teacher called a bottled curriculum. The practice predates the report; West Side Story was its first famous instrument. Its modern form pairs the play with young adult fiction on feud, loyalty, and retaliatory violence, of which Harrison's Fort Worth pairing with Jason Reynolds's Long Way Down, a verse novel of a boy deciding whether to avenge his brother, is the documented type specimen in the NCTE coverage. The pairing is not decoration. It is the developmental defense operationalized: if the students are to meet their own world in the play, a contemporary text is stationed at the border to translate in both directions.

The pairing shelf cuts both ways, and the record's honesty requires the other edge. A paired modern text, chosen for thematic rhyme, quietly instructs students in what the play is about before they read it: feud and revenge, in the Long Way Down case, rather than haste, language, or the marriage market. Thompson and Turchi's demand for a stated purpose is, in part, a demand that this pre-interpretation be owned by the teacher rather than smuggled by the shelf. The American evidence supports the practice and the warning together: pairing is how the play is in fact taught in the classrooms the surveys describe, and it is also the point at which the unit's argument is most often decided without being made.

The published curricula formalize the shelf. Odell's ninth-grade unit surrounds the play with an informational text set: a contemporary essay asking why the culture still cares about Shakespeare, contemporary film adaptations, and Ovid's tale of Pyramus and Thisbe, the play's own ancient cousin, read in the same weeks. The arrangement is a small anthology inside the unit, and it shows where the pairing instinct leads when a publisher systematizes it: the play no longer stands alone as the year's drama but as the center of a designed constellation, with the sources behind it and the descendants after it arranged for comparison. The constellation is defensible, indeed elegant. It is also the deposit wearing its newest clothes, a publisher's kit that a district buys precisely because the center of the constellation is the play every neighboring district already teaches.

## Aids, Objections, and the Local Veto

Around the text stands an economy of help. Study guides are a permanent feature of the American unit's landscape, from the summary pamphlets of the examination era to the modern paraphrase industry; the industry's structure belongs to the study-guide article in this series, and the help-or-harm verdict on its best-known product to the debate article, both at passage here. What belongs to the American system is the distribution of the argument: because no national authority chose the play, no national authority adjudicates the aids or the objections. Both are settled at the level of the district, the department, and the individual classroom door.

Objections follow the same geography. The play has been challenged in American schools over its sexual content, its violence, and its deaths, and the documented cases are graded in the companion record of [banned and challenged: the play in schools](/2029/09/28/banned-challenged-in-schools/); the structural fact is the American one. A challenge to Romeo and Juliet is filed with a local board, argued in a local meeting, and decided for one district's shelves, because that is where American curricular sovereignty lives. The NCTE survey of 2025 found 44 percent of teachers reporting some censorship pressure from their school or district, with sexual content among the most commonly cited grounds across all titles. The play's protections are equally local and equally structural: it is defended, district by district, by the same inheritance that installed it, since a text taught by the grandparents of the current board's members carries a presumption few challengers overcome. The lovers' ages, debated in American classrooms as a question of what fourteen-year-olds should read about thirteen-year-olds, receive their dedicated treatment in the consent-and-age article of this series; the bawdy that threads the play's first act receives its own in the bawdy article. The American point is narrower: in a system without a center, every one of these arguments is re-fought at retail, and the play wins almost everywhere because the deposit is deep.

The film-permission file tells the same story from the paperwork side. Guidance clearinghouses for classroom film, Teach with Movies the durable example, supply not only lesson plans but sample permission letters for parents, on the premise that a screening of a rated film needs documented consent; the letter for the Zeffirelli version is among the site's standing materials for the play. A text that requires a consent form is, in the American grammar, a text under negotiation, and the negotiation is renewed annually in every building that screens the film. The deposit survives these renewals because the educational claim behind it is, in the end, the one the parents' own schooling taught them: they met the play in the ninth grade too, and the permission letter asks them to ratify for their children the encounter they remember from their own.

The scripted era has added a new objector, the state's own reviewer. When Massachusetts reviewers examined Odell Education's ninth-grade program through the CURATE process, they praised its texts and tasks while noting a precise gap: the teacher notes flag the bawdy jokes of the opening scene, lines 1 to 30 of Act 1, as lewd, without offering strategies for contextualizing that language or sustaining academic discourse around it. The observation deserves its weight. The same review machinery that certifies the play's place in a purchased curriculum can find nothing to say about the scene American teachers have negotiated since the play entered the classroom, the servants' sexual boasting that opens it. Review rubrics measure alignment, complexity, and usability; the bawdy falls between the categories, and the gap the reviewers recorded is the documentary trace of a negotiation otherwise conducted invisibly, classroom by classroom, edition by edition, in the discretion of teachers whom the script was designed to guide and, at this exact point, does not. The gap is recorded here without adjudicating the classroom handling, which belongs to the pedagogy articles; its structural meaning is that even the newest layer of the deposit, the reviewed script, preserves the oldest feature of the arrangement, an adult in the room deciding in real time what the text may say.

The paperwork of the local veto deserves a paragraph of its own, because it shows the deposit defending itself in writing. American practice equips a challenged title with a rationale, a prepared document setting out the work's value, its place in the curriculum, and the grounds for its selection, filed by the teacher or department before or during a dispute. The NCTE maintains a Book Rationale database supporting some 1,400 titles, a standing arsenal for exactly these proceedings. A play that requires a rationale is, by definition, a play whose presence is argued rather than assumed; that the lovers' tragedy travels with such paperwork, and prevails with it board after board, is the measure of the American settlement: the deposit is deep, but it is a deposit that has learned to keep its receipts.

One American argument runs on a separate track and should be marked rather than made here. The play ends in two suicides, and American schools operate under district policies and state guidance on suicide prevention that no earlier era of the unit had to consult; how a classroom frames the deaths, and what the scholarship on imitation does and does not show, are treated in this series by the articles on the Werther effect and on teaching consent and age. The structural observation belongs to the American system: the argument, like every other argument about the play, is conducted district by district, in guidance offices as well as board rooms, and its outcomes vary with the same localism that governs the challenges to the text itself.

## The Critics' Quarrel, Adjudicated

Four documented positions divide the American evidence, and the verdict of this article is assembled from their adjudication. Arthur Applebee, the education scholar whose 1974 history and 1989 and 1993 surveys frame the whole record, holds the first. In his account the school canon is an inheritance system: entrance examinations created it, publishing sustains it, and reform movements from Dewey onward have rearranged its margins while its center held. The stability his instruments measured across 1963, 1989, and, by extension in the NCTE's hands, 2025 is not a scandal in his telling but a property of institutions that must teach millions of children with finite preparation. Applebee wins on mechanism. Nothing in the American record moves the way reform rhetoric says it should, and everything in it moves the way his inheritance model predicts.

Applebee's history also supplies the quarrel's longer view, and it cuts against easy despair. Tradition and Reform chronicles a profession that has tried, in wave after wave, to dislodge its inherited syllabus: the experience curriculum of the 1930s, the New Criticism's formalism in the postwar decades, the federally funded Project English centers of the 1960s, the Dartmouth Seminar of 1966 with its growth-centered alternative. Each wave changed the profession's language and methods; none changed the ninth-grade shelf. The attempts matter to the adjudication because they rule out the laziest explanation of the deposit, that nobody ever tried to move it. The American pattern survived not a conspiracy in its favor but a century of organized, funded, credentialed attempts to replace it, mounted by the very profession that staffs it. A survival against that opposition is evidence about the structure, not about the play alone: whatever a school system is for, the ninth-grade tragedy kept being useful to it under every successive theory of what it was for.

Jonathan Burton, the Shakespeare scholar whose survey priced the deposit, holds the second position: the inheritance is not ageless but datable, and this play's share of it was created by specific events, the discipline's formation in the 1890s, the invention of the teenager, the Merchant's postwar retirement, and, though he treats it as corroboration rather than cause, the arrival of classroom films. Burton wins on explanation. A generic claim that the best play rises cannot say why the rise waited until the middle of the twentieth century, why it displaced a Venetian comedy rather than a Roman tragedy, or why it concentrated in a single grade. His milestones can.

Ayanna Thompson and Laura Turchi, in Teaching Shakespeare with Purpose (2016), hold the third position, and it is an indictment rather than an explanation. A canon taught by default, without a purpose a teacher can state, renders the plays, in their phrase, fixed, determined, and dead; the remedy is not replacement but intentional framing, in which the teacher chooses a lens and answers for it. Their argument does not dispute Applebee's mechanism or Burton's dates. It denies that either excuses the classroom. Thompson and Turchi win on obligation: a slot held by inheritance is a fact, and a slot taught by inheritance is a professional failure, and the American system produces the second wherever the first goes unexamined.

The teacher's position, the fourth, is held in practice by Peggy O'Brien, who founded the Folger Shakespeare Library's education department in 1981 and its Teaching Shakespeare Institute in 1984, and by working teachers of Harrison's kind, pairing the play with Long Way Down in Fort Worth in the NCTE's own reporting. Its claim is that the variable is method: the play met as a script in students' mouths, performed rather than paraphrased, justifies its chair in the only court that finally sits, the classroom hour. Adjudicated against the other three, the teacher's position is the operational settlement. It concedes Applebee's mechanism and Burton's history, satisfies Thompson and Turchi's demand wherever the performance is framed by a stated purpose, and fails only where performance becomes its own routine, activity without a question. Sandra Stotsky's survey dissent, finally, is upheld as a correction of scale rather than a refutation: the deposit is real, and it is a deposit concentrated in one grade and one subject, inside a national curriculum otherwise too various to be called a canon at all.

The report's reception is itself evidence about the system it measured. Harvard's Gazette covered the survey under a headline asking whether teachers assign the same books as in the 1980s, and coverage in the education press divided between lament for a stagnant canon and defense of a living one. Both readings flatten the data. The survey shows a profession exercising wide individual choice, one teacher in five reporting no choice at all and the rest choosing freely inside a structure, whose aggregate pattern is stable; stability and freedom are both in the numbers, and the adjectives applied to them say more about the appliers than the schools. What the report establishes, and what neither the lament nor the defense can remove, is the shape: a shared core of titles persisting across generations of teachers who were each at liberty to abandon it. A pattern maintained by free choices repeated at scale is not stagnation but equilibrium, and equilibria are changed by altering payoffs, not by deploring outcomes.

## Verdict: Keep the Slot, Change Its Terms

The verdict follows from the adjudication. Romeo and Juliet should keep its ninth-grade place in US high schools, not because fourteen-year-olds are its destined readers, a claim the record supports only in its narrow sociological form, but because the deposit that placed it there has made it the one literary experience the American system reliably shares, and shared reference is a public good no replacement candidate is positioned to supply. What should change is the terms of occupancy. The play should be taught as an inheritance under examination rather than a natural fact: its own curricular history, the entrance lists that excluded it, the teenager's invention that admitted it, the films that carried it, belongs inside the unit, where it converts the students' first Shakespeare into a first lesson in how canons are made. The developmental defense should be retired to its defensible core, the feud and the failed adults, and the romance taught as the play teaches it, inside five days of catastrophic haste: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1) is the line the American classroom keeps, fittingly, because the play's center of gravity was never the balcony. It was the street.

The strongest counterargument deserves its answer in the open. It runs: the slot is an accident, the skills are transferable, and a modern play, or a later Shakespeare, would teach them without the deposit's costs; therefore replace. The record answers in three parts. First, transfer is not free: the skills the ninth-grade unit installs are installed on this text's particular difficulties, and the upper courses presuppose the installation, so replacement reopens four years of sequencing, not one. Second, the commonality is itself an educational good of a kind the system's critics elsewhere prize: Stotsky's tables show the play at the head of standard and honors lists alike, which makes the ninth-grade unit one of the few moments in a tracked system where the whole cohort reads the same difficult book, and a reform that scattered the encounter would stratify it. Third, the costs the counterargument cites, the aids that pre-digest, the pairing that pre-interprets, the script that pre-decides, are costs of the unit's handling, and handling can be reformed in place, as the Folger's pedagogy and Thompson and Turchi's demand both show. Replacement is a cure for a diagnosis the evidence does not support; the diagnosis is not that the wrong play holds the slot but that the slot's holders stopped saying why.

The terms can be stated concretely. A unit that teaches its own history names the entrance lists and finds the play absent from them; it meets Burton's teenager and asks whether the category describes the students in the room or merely their market; it screens the films as arguments rather than illustrations, Zeffirelli's ages, Luhrmann's guns, the musical's surviving Maria; and it lets the students audit the deposit itself, the department's outline, the anthology's table of contents, the district's rationale, as documents in the same evidentiary spirit this series applies to quartos. None of this requires new materials. It requires the one thing the deposit discourages, a teacher's stated answer to the question Thompson and Turchi insist on: why this play, for these students, this year. Where that answer is given, the ninth-grade slot stops being an inheritance and becomes, at last, a decision, which is all the verdict asks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Did American schools always begin Shakespeare with this play?

No. Through the nineteenth century the school Shakespeare of the readers and elocution exercises was dominated by Julius Caesar, Hamlet, and above all The Merchant of Venice, the most-extracted play of the century's second half. The entrance examinations that formalized the canon in the 1870s set The Tempest, Julius Caesar, and The Merchant of Venice. Romeo and Juliet's school ascendancy is a twentieth-century event, concentrated after the 1930s, and its lateness is the central fact of its American career. The canon that governs the classroom was built by other plays for other purposes, and the lovers inherited it entire.

### Q: What did Harvard's entrance examinations of 1873 and 1874 have to do with the American school canon?

They created the mechanism. Harvard required candidates to write compositions on set works, Shakespeare's Tempest, Julius Caesar, and Merchant of Venice among them, and other colleges followed with published requirements of their own. Arthur Applebee's 1974 history credits those examinations with institutionalizing the study of standard authors and forcing English to consolidate as a school subject. High schools taught what the colleges examined, and the set-book canon that resulted is the foundation on which every later American placement, including this play's, was built.

### Q: What did the published college entrance lists of 1901 to 1905 ask candidates to do with a prescribed book?

The requirements divided English into reading and practice, tested by a written paragraph on topics drawn from wide reading, and study and practice, tested on a small number of books prescribed for careful study. For the study portion the candidate answered on subject matter, literary form, and logical structure, and could be examined on the grammar and the literary history of the periods involved. The Shakespeare prescribed for those years was Macbeth, The Merchant of Venice, and Julius Caesar, in the school editions publishers rushed to supply.

### Q: How can a play sit in nearly every ninth-grade classroom when no national authority requires it?

By accumulation across a decentralized system. States set standards that name skills rather than titles; districts adopt anthologies and curricula that place the play in the ninth grade; departments inherit course outlines; teachers, four in five of whom retain some choice by the NCTE's 2025 count, mostly ratify the placement they themselves were taught under. Each layer's decision is small and local. Their sum behaves like a mandate, although no layer could impose one and none, acting alone, could repeal the result.

### Q: What did Jonathan Burton's survey of 400 teachers count, and why does its headline figure not contradict Sandra Stotsky's?

Burton asked high school English teachers which Shakespeare plays their curricula included, and found Romeo and Juliet in roughly 93 percent of ninth-grade classes. Stotsky's 2010 survey counted the courses described by 406 teachers across grades 9 to 11 and found the play in 22.38 percent of all standard and honors courses. The denominators differ: concentration within one grade against diffusion across three. A play can saturate the ninth grade and still appear in barely a fifth of all high-school English courses, and both surveys are needed to see the shape.

### Q: What does the NCTE report of 2025 add that Applebee's 1989 survey could not show?

Scale and self-report at the level of the individual classroom. Applebee surveyed department chairs about the works all students in a grade studied; Chae and Ginsberg for the NCTE surveyed 4,096 teachers directly about the texts used most frequently in their own rooms, collecting 5,108 distinct titles and data on autonomy, scripted curricula, and censorship pressure alongside the rankings. The 2025 report therefore shows both the canon's stability and the conditions around it: a third of teachers working from scripted materials, 44 percent reporting censorship pressure, and four in five retaining some choice.

### Q: Why did The Merchant of Venice fade from American schoolbooks after the Second World War?

Jonathan Burton's history gives the answer its scholarly form: rising concern about anti-Semitism after the war made the play's central material untenable as the default school Shakespeare, and a text that had been the most-extracted of the nineteenth century quietly lost its curricular position. The retirement was never announced or legislated; publishers and departments simply stopped choosing it. Its vacancy at the head of the school canon is one of the openings through which the lovers' tragedy, rising on the teenager's invention, advanced.

### Q: Why does the Common Core name Shakespeare but not this play?

Because the standards were written to specify outcomes rather than reading lists. Their framework obliges students to read complex texts across a range, and its guidance singles out Shakespeare as an author to be read in the high-school years, the only author carrying such an obligation, while its Appendix B illustrates the expected complexity with Macbeth for grades 9 to 10 and Hamlet for grades 11 to 12. The title was left to the states and districts, and the states and districts left it to custom, which had already decided.

### Q: What part do Texas and California play in a decision supposedly made in fifty separate systems?

The part of the largest customers. In the era of statewide textbook adoption, the two states' specifications were so commercially weighty that national publishers designed their ninth-grade anthologies to satisfy them, then sold substantially the same books in states where adoption was local. A play embedded in the big-state volume acquired a national distribution that no single district's preference could have given it. The mechanism has weakened as digital and scripted curricula have spread, but the lists it built are the lists schools still teach from.

### Q: How did a research library in Washington come to publish the standard American classroom text of the play?

The Folger Shakespeare Library, founded in 1932 and holding the world's largest collection of Shakespeare's printed works, lent its name and its holdings to a line of inexpensive editions. Washington Square Press issued the first Folger paperbacks in February 1958, edited under the Library's director Louis B. Wright with Virginia A. LaMar, text and notes in a format priced for classroom sets. The New Folger editions edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine renewed the line from 1992. The classroom text is thus a scholarly institution's publishing arm, not a textbook company's abridgment.

### Q: Why did American schools organize field trips to a film in 1968?

Because Franco Zeffirelli's Romeo and Juliet made the assigned play a popular event. The film cast actual teenagers, Leonard Whiting and Olivia Hussey, photographed the story in Italian locations, and earned some 50 million dollars against a cost of about 1.5 million, with Academy Awards for cinematography and costume design. Contemporary accounts record schools organizing trips to see it, since Shakespeare had been made commercial. A department could take its ninth graders to the cinema and call the outing curriculum, and the film's classroom afterlife as the default screening began there.

### Q: What changed in American classrooms when Luhrmann's film arrived in 1996?

The unit acquired a second screen register. Baz Luhrmann's William Shakespeare's Romeo + Juliet kept the text, trimmed, inside a modern Verona Beach of gangs and engraved handguns, and returned roughly 147 million dollars worldwide, the largest gross of any Shakespeare film. Its PG-13 rating made screening a permission question in some districts; its pace and soundtrack made it, in many teachers' reported experience, the version students asked for. Departments gained a defensible choice between the play's world in Zeffirelli and the students' world in Luhrmann, and comparison of the two became a standard unit assignment.

### Q: How does West Side Story's ending quietly correct the play it adapts?

Maria lives. In Shakespeare both lovers die, and the catastrophe closes the feud; in the musical Tony dies and Maria, surviving, indicts both gangs over his body and leads the fragile reconciliation herself. A bridge text that spares its Juliet teaches the feud's cost while withholding the play's terminal logic, in which reconciliation is purchased with two deaths in a tomb. Teachers who cross the bridge owe their students the difference: the American adaptation forgives where the original does not, and the forgiveness is a change of meaning, not a detail.

### Q: Why do American students meet Hamlet last, in the twelfth grade, after meeting the lovers first?

The four high-school years deal out the tragedies in an order the surveys keep finding: the lovers in the ninth grade, the Roman and Scottish plays in the middle years, Hamlet characteristically in the twelfth. The ladder is justified as rising complexity, and Hamlet's length and inwardness do suit the oldest students, but the order is an inheritance from the same textbook and departmental machinery that placed the lovers first, reinforced by the framing of the twelfth grade as the summit of the course. The sequence feels like a curriculum of difficulty because generations of outlines have taught it as one.

### Q: What did Ayanna Thompson and Laura Turchi ask American teachers to be able to say about this play?

A purpose. In Teaching Shakespeare with Purpose (2016) they argue that a play taught because it has always been taught, under no stated frame, is rendered fixed, determined, and dead, and that the teacher's obligation is to choose an intentional lens for the encounter and answer for the choice. Applied to the ninth grade, the demand is precise: not that the play be dropped, but that its presence be justified to the students actually in the room, in terms those students can test against the text.

### Q: What does a scripted curriculum do to a play that entered classrooms by custom?

It converts custom into inventory. The NCTE's 2025 report found 35 percent of teachers working with a scripted curriculum, in which the sequence of texts arrives preassembled. For Romeo and Juliet the conversion is ratifying rather than disruptive: published programs such as Odell Education's ninth-grade literacy curriculum build a formal unit on the play, complete with central questions and supporting texts, so the placement that custom achieved informally is also a product specification. What script changes is the locus of the decision, moving the last ratification from the teacher to the purchaser.

### Q: Why do American arguments about the play happen at school-board meetings instead of in Congress?

Because the United States assigns curricular sovereignty to the local level. No federal body selects or removes school texts, so a challenge to the play's sexual content, violence, or deaths is filed with a district, heard by its board, and settled for its shelves alone, while the same play stands unchallenged in the district across the county line. The NCTE found 44 percent of teachers reporting censorship pressure from school or district sources. The play's defense is equally local: the inheritance of generations of local graduates, sitting on the board.

### Q: What happens to the play inside a published curriculum such as Odell Education's ninth-grade program?

It becomes a designed unit. Odell Education's High School Literacy Program of 2020 places the play in a ninth-grade development unit organized by the central question "Why do we still read Shakespeare?", surrounds it with informational texts and contemporary film adaptations, and rates its complexity, at 1260L in the publisher's analysis, inside the grade 9 band. Reviewers for Massachusetts noted the unit's teacher notes flagging the opening scene's lewd jokes without guidance for handling them. The design formalizes the placement and, in that gap, leaves the oldest classroom problem exactly where custom left it.
