The simultaneous elections debate in India is usually reported as a contest between a government that wants one national poll day and opponents who resist it, yet the documentary record assembled between 1999 and 2024 shows a different structure underneath the political contest. The Law Commission of India in its 170th Report of May 1999, a parliamentary standing committee in December 2015, NITI Aayog in January 2017, the Law Commission again in a draft report of August 2018, and the High Level Committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind in March 2024 each addressed the same architectural question: whether the terms of the Lok Sabha and the state legislative assemblies should be synchronised by constitutional design, and what such synchronisation would do to federal balance, public expenditure, and legislative accountability. This article defends one namable claim. The simultaneous elections debate operates through three substantive positions; the post-2024 trajectory tests the architectural content of each position.

## A Cycle Broken: How India Moved Away From Simultaneous Elections

Simultaneous elections are not an imported novelty in Indian constitutional practice. They are the original condition of the republic. The first general election of 1951 and 1952, conducted by Chief Election Commissioner Sukumar Sen across a largely illiterate electorate, chose the House of the People and every state legislative assembly in a single exercise, and the elections of 1957, 1962, and 1967 repeated that synchronised pattern. A voter in Madras or Bombay or West Bengal in February 1967 cast ballots for Parliament and for the state assembly within the same polling season, under the same administrative mobilisation, and the terms of the two legislatures ran together from a common starting point. The architecture of the Constitution made this natural. Article 83 fixed five years as the normal duration of the Lok Sabha, Article 172 fixed five years for a legislative assembly, and because every legislature began its life in the same founding moment, the two clocks ticked in step without any synchronising device at all.

The synchrony broke in stages, and each stage has a date. On 31 July 1959 the Union government of Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru dismissed the elected Communist ministry of E. M. S. Namboodiripad in Kerala and imposed President's Rule under Article 356, after the Vimochana Samaram agitation against the state's education and agrarian measures. Kerala therefore voted again in February 1960, on a calendar the rest of the country did not share. The first crack was small, and Kerala realigned at the following general elections, yet the episode demonstrated the mechanism that would shatter the common cycle within a decade: any premature dissolution, whether of an assembly or of the Lok Sabha, starts a new five year clock for that legislature alone, and nothing in the constitutional text pulls the displaced legislature back into step with the others.

The decisive rupture came after the fourth general election of February 1967. The Congress lost its majority in eight states, and coalition ministries of the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal type took office in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Punjab, and elsewhere, assembled from parties with little in common beyond opposition to the Congress. Those ministries proved unstable. Defections crossed floors in both directions, ministries fell, and between 1968 and 1969 a series of assemblies was dissolved before term: Haryana voted again in 1968, and Bihar, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal all faced mid-term elections in 1969. Each mid-term poll reset that state's clock to a solitary rhythm. [The post-1967 asymmetric-cycle context](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/) is the essential background to the whole debate, because the asymmetry that reformers describe as a defect was produced by the ordinary operation of parliamentary government: ministries that lose confidence fall, and elections follow falls.

The second rupture struck the national clock itself. On 27 December 1970 Prime Minister Indira Gandhi advised the dissolution of the fourth Lok Sabha more than a year before its term expired, and the election of March 1971 constituted a fifth Lok Sabha whose five year span matched almost no assembly calendar in the country. From 1971 onward the Indian election cycle was permanently desynchronised, a patchwork in which at least one major election, national or state, has fallen due in almost every calendar year. The Emergency added a further distortion when the fifth Lok Sabha extended its own life beyond five years through constitutional amendment and sat until January 1977, while several opposition ruled assemblies were dissolved in the same period. By the time of [the first general election of 1951 and 1952](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), synchrony had been the unplanned gift of a common founding; after 1971 its restoration would require deliberate constitutional engineering, which is why the debate ever since has been a debate about amendments rather than about administrative convenience alone.

The desynchronised pattern entrenched itself over the following four decades. State elections acquired their own political weather. A state electorate could reward or punish a state ministry in 2013 without any implication for the Lok Sabha contest of 2014, and national waves could break against state governments that survived them comfortably at their own polls. Election administrators treated the rolling calendar as a permanent operating condition: the Election Commission of India plans, in any given span of twelve months, for assembly elections in a rotating set of states, by-elections, and the periodic general election, each with its own deployment of central armed police forces, its own requisition of school buildings and government staff, and its own period under the Model Code of Conduct. Reformers who propose synchronisation are therefore proposing to reverse a half century of institutional habit, and opponents who defend the staggered cycle are defending an arrangement whose political consequences, for federalism above all, they regard as a substantive feature of the constitutional order rather than an accident of its history. That disagreement, between an accident to be repaired and a feature to be preserved, is the oldest layer of the present debate.

## The Documentary Lineage: Five Examinations Before the Kovind Committee

The proposal to restore synchronised elections has been examined on the record five times before the Kovind Committee reported, and the sequence matters because each examination inherited the questions its predecessor left open. The Election Commission of India raised the idea in its annual report for 1983, noting that the Commission's workload and the country's administrative machinery were repeatedly absorbed by election duty, and suggesting that the terms of the Lok Sabha and the assemblies might again be made to run together. The suggestion carried no amendment blueprint. It was an administrator's observation, made by the body that bears the operational burden of the staggered cycle, and it established the efficiency strand of the argument in its earliest form: the claim that the frequency of elections, rather than their conduct, is the burden to be reduced.

The Law Commission of India gave the proposal its first full legal treatment in the 170th Report on Reform of the Electoral Laws, submitted in May 1999 under the chairmanship of Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy. The Commission concluded that the cycle of separate elections was wasteful and disruptive, and that one election to the Lok Sabha and all legislative assemblies in five years was a desirable goal. Its reasoning was constitutional as much as administrative. The report examined how the terms fixed by Articles 83 and 172 could be aligned, and it confronted the problem that defeats every simple version of the scheme: a House that loses confidence before its term ends cannot be kept alive artificially in a parliamentary system, and a House elected after a premature dissolution begins a fresh five year term that immediately desynchronises the cycle again. The 170th Report therefore paired synchronisation with devices for stability, including the idea that a motion of no confidence should also name the alternative government it proposes to install, so that a House could change its ministry without changing its own lifespan.

### What did the parliamentary standing committee propose in December 2015?

The Department Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, chaired by E. M. Sudarsana Natchiappan, reported on 17 December 2015 in its 79th Report and proposed synchronisation in two phases, aligning assemblies whose terms ended near a Lok Sabha election first, and the rest at the cycle midpoint.

In the first phase, assemblies whose terms ended within a defined window around a Lok Sabha election would vote with it; in the second phase, the remaining assemblies would be aligned at the midpoint of the Lok Sabha term, so that elections of consequence would occur twice in five years rather than continuously across all five.

The NITI Aayog discussion paper of January 2017, written by Bibek Debroy and Kishore Desai under the title Analysis of Simultaneous Elections: The What, Why and How, converted the standing committee's phased intuition into a full calendar design. The paper proposed that elections be held in two windows in a five year cycle, modelled loosely on the fixed rhythm of the American midterm, with the Lok Sabha and roughly half the assemblies voting together and the remaining assemblies voting two and a half years later. The authors set out the expenditure arithmetic of the staggered cycle, the governance cost of repeated Model Code periods, and the constitutional amendments a phased calendar would require, and they treated the two phase design as a pragmatic midpoint between full synchronisation and the rolling status quo. The paper's significance in the lineage is that it moved the proposal from a statement of desirability to a dated, costed, and phased plan that a government could adopt as a programme.

The Law Commission returned to the subject under Justice B. S. Chauhan, publishing a draft report on simultaneous elections on 30 August 2018 after circulating a working paper in April of that year. The 2018 draft report is the under cited primary source of the entire debate, because its architectural engagement with the amendment problem was the most granular produced before 2024. The draft examined the wording consequences for Articles 83 and 172 in detail, proposed that elections held after a premature dissolution should constitute a House for the unexpired remainder of the term only, recommended the constructive vote of no confidence in place of the bare negative motion, proposed relaxation of the anti-defection law's rigours to prevent stalemate in a hung House, and suggested that the Prime Minister and each Chief Minister be elected by the whole House in the manner of a Speaker, so that leadership would carry a mandate from the legislature rather than from a party room alone. The draft report held that simultaneous elections were feasible, but only through an amendment package of at least five constitutional changes together with amendments to the Representation of the People Act 1951 and to the rules of procedure of the Lok Sabha and the assemblies. When the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill arrived in December 2024, its central devices, the appointed date and the unexpired term, were recognisable descendants of the architecture the Law Commission had sketched six years earlier.

Two features of this lineage deserve emphasis before the positions are mapped. The first is continuity of personnel and institution: the Election Commission, the Law Commission twice, a parliamentary committee, and the government's own policy institution all reached versions of the same conclusion across four decades and across governments of different parties, which is why proponents describe the proposal as an institutional consensus rather than a party programme. The second is continuity of the unresolved problem. Every document from 1999 onward identified the same two obstacles: the mid-term fall of a government, and the constitutional status of state terms that synchronisation must curtail or extend to achieve alignment. The three positions set out in the following section are, at bottom, three different weightings of the benefits the lineage documents against the obstacles the lineage could never remove.

## The Three Substantive Positions on Architecture

Read as a whole, the documentary lineage and the political contest around it sort into three substantive positions, each answering a different question. The architectural-efficiency position asks what the staggered cycle costs in money, administrative capacity, and governing time, and answers that synchronisation recovers those losses. The federal-balance position asks what the staggered cycle protects, and answers that separate state calendars protect the federal character of the republic against the gravitational pull of a single national contest. The institutional-feasibility position asks what the Constitution would have to say for synchronisation to exist at all, and answers with a schedule of amendments, procedures, and transitional rules. Political commentary tends to collapse these into a single argument between supporters and opponents of the government of the day. The record resists that collapse. A commentator may accept the efficiency arithmetic and still reject synchronisation on federal grounds; a lawyer may draft the amendment package and remain agnostic about whether it should be used; a state party may oppose the present Bill while accepting synchronisation in a two phase design such as the one the NITI Aayog paper proposed. The positions are dimensions of one debate, not teams in it.

The map clarifies what each side must prove. The efficiency position must show that its savings are real and not merely transfers of cost from one budget head to another. The federal position must show that the staggered cycle delivers a distinct democratic good that synchronisation would destroy, rather than a mere preference for familiar arrangements. The feasibility position must solve the remainder problem: the legislature elected after an early fall must fit back into the common cycle, or the entire design dissolves at its first mid-term crisis. The events of 2024 tested all three burdens at once, because the Kovind Committee wrote the feasibility schedule, the Cabinet adopted the efficiency reasoning as the government's public case, and the parliamentary opposition organised its resistance around the federal objection. The sections that follow take each position in turn, in its strongest form, on the evidence its own advocates put on the record.

## The Architectural-Efficiency Position: Cost, Administration and Governing Time

The efficiency position begins from an accounting observation. A general election to the Lok Sabha is among the largest administrative exercises conducted anywhere in the democratic world, and the estimates placed before successive committees put the official cost of a single Lok Sabha cycle at approximately 4,500 crore rupees, borne between the Union and the states under the sharing conventions of election finance, with assembly elections across a full cycle adding an approximately equivalent sum again. The official figure, moreover, is the smaller part of the ledger. The Election Commission told the parliamentary standing committee that a fully synchronised poll conducted at one point in time would require roughly double the electronic voting machines and voter verifiable paper audit trail units held for a single election, an upfront procurement the Commission costed in its evidence; party and candidate expenditure, estimated by the Centre for Media Studies at roughly 60,000 crore rupees for the 2019 general election season alone, multiplies the official outlay many times over. The efficiency argument does not claim that synchronisation abolishes election expenditure. It claims that one consolidated exercise, with one deployment of security forces, one requisition of polling personnel, and one printing and transport operation, costs substantially less than the same elections scattered across five years of separate mobilisations, and committee estimates attached to the proposal have placed the recurring saving in the range of half or more of the duplicated administrative overhead.

The second limb of the position concerns administrative capacity rather than money. Every election draws its polling staff from the same pool: schoolteachers, revenue officials, bank employees, and police personnel are requisitioned for training, polling, and counting, and central armed police forces are moved across state boundaries in phased deployments that the staggered calendar makes almost continuous. The Election Commission's evidence to committees has repeatedly described a machinery that is, in effect, never fully stood down. District administrations in a state facing an assembly poll suspend routine enforcement and development administration for weeks; the same district may repeat the exercise for a Lok Sabha poll within the same year and for municipal and panchayat polls on yet another calendar set by the State Election Commission. Synchronisation, on this argument, converts a permanent low level disruption spread across every district in every year into a single intense mobilisation once in five years, after which the administrative state returns to its ordinary work for an uninterrupted term.

### How long does the Model Code of Conduct restrict governments in a staggered cycle?

In a typical year of the staggered calendar, assembly elections, by-elections, and the periodic general election together keep the Model Code of Conduct in force somewhere in India for a large part of the year, restricting new announcements, transfers, and inaugurations across whichever states are polling at the time.

Committee documents examining the cycle cite an average in the region of two hundred to three hundred days annually in which some significant part of the country is under code restrictions, a figure the efficiency position treats as the measure of the governance time the staggered calendar consumes.

The third limb is the Model Code itself. [The soft-law-architecture-and-limits framework](/2020/07/06/model-code-conduct-soft-law-india/) explains why a code with no statutory force nonetheless freezes administration: governments observe it because the Election Commission's authority under Article 324 makes defiance costly. Under the staggered calendar, the freeze is perpetual in aggregate. A Union government announcing a national scheme must reckon with a code in force in whichever states are polling; a state government spends a measurable share of its five year term either campaigning, under code restrictions, or settling a new ministry. The efficiency position holds that synchronisation concentrates the code into a single season once in five years and thereby returns long, predictable stretches of governing time to both levels of government. The Kovind Committee adopted this reasoning in full, recording that frequent elections and near continuous code application disrupt governance, delivery of services, and the deployment of manpower, language that the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the December 2024 Bill then carried into the parliamentary record nearly verbatim.

The position has a fourth, quieter limb concerning voter behaviour and turnout. A combined poll asks the elector to decide two governments on one day at one booth, reducing the fatigue of repeated journeys to the polling station and, its advocates argue, raising participation in assembly contests that might otherwise draw thinner turnouts than a Lok Sabha election. The evidence for this limb is admittedly thinner than for the cost arithmetic, and the position's critics answer it directly: a single ballot day may also flatten the distinction between the two decisions, inviting the elector to treat the assembly vote as an extension of the parliamentary vote. That answer belongs to the federal position, but its presence in the efficiency debate marks the boundary between the two positions precisely: what the efficiency reading counts as simplification, the federal reading counts as conflation. The efficiency position, properly understood, does not deny that something is lost when two decisions are compressed into one day. It asserts that the loss is small beside the measurable waste of the staggered cycle, and that judgment, about relative magnitudes, is the claim the position must defend when the federal case is put at its strongest in the next section.

## The Federal-Balance Position: Separate Calendars as a Federal Protection

The federal-balance position starts from [the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/), in which the Indian Union was deliberately built with a strong centre and constitutionally entrenched states whose legislatures are not subordinate committees of Parliament. On this reading, the staggered election calendar is not a malfunction. It is the electoral expression of that design. A state assembly elected on its own calendar answers to its own electorate at a moment chosen by its own political rhythm, and the verdict it receives concerns its own record: a state government's management of irrigation, policing, schools, and state finances can be judged in a season when no national question crowds the ballot. Synchronisation, the position argues, homogenises the two judgements. When the assembly vote is cast on the same day, in the same booth, amid a national campaign fought on national leadership and national slogans, state questions are displaced from the state contest. The empirical exhibits most often cited are the elections in which Indian voters demonstrably separated the two judgements when given separate occasions: Delhi, which gave the Bharatiya Janata Party every Lok Sabha seat in 2019 and gave the Aam Aadmi Party a sweeping assembly majority in February 2020; Odisha, which for two decades returned the Biju Janata Dal to the assembly while dividing its parliamentary vote; and the long record, examined in [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/), of state political settlements that matured on their own calendars quite independently of Delhi's cycle.

The position draws strength from the verdict pattern of the coalition decades. [The post-2014 political-architectural context](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/) is usually invoked by synchronisation's advocates, yet the federal reading reaches back further, to 1989 and after, when staggered state polls repeatedly registered regional assertions that a synchronised calendar would have submerged: the rise of state parties in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, and Andhra Pradesh was recorded at assembly elections held on state timetables, in state languages, on state issues, and those verdicts built the federalised party system that governed India for a quarter century. M. R. Sivaraman, the former revenue and expenditure secretary who wrote extensively on the proposal's federal implications, argued in his essays on the subject that synchronisation reverses the logic of that federalisation by subordinating the state's electoral moment to the Union's, and that the curtailment or extension of sitting assemblies to force alignment treats state mandates as adjustable quantities in a national timetable, a treatment the federal scheme of the Constitution does not contemplate for legislatures that are, within their sphere, sovereign.

The federal position also makes an argument from accountability between elections. A staggered calendar gives the national electorate recurring occasions to signal discontent with a Union government between general elections, through state verdicts that Delhi reads as midterm referendums whether it wishes to or not. State polls in 2018, in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Chhattisgarh, were universally read as a warning to the ruling party at the Centre months before the 2019 general election, and the reading disciplined the national campaign that followed. Under full synchronisation, that signalling channel closes for five years at a stretch. The voter's only national verdict is the general election itself, and a government that loses public confidence in its second year faces no electoral reckoning until its fifth. Critics within this position, including the constitutional lawyer Suhrith Parthasarathy in his essays on the amendment trajectory published after 2024, connect the point to the fixed term problem: synchronisation rewards legislative longevity as such, and a parliamentary system, unlike a presidential one, contains no principle that a government deserves to survive for a set span regardless of the confidence of the House or the country.

### What happens to a state government that loses its majority in a synchronised system?

Under the remainder design carried from the Law Commission draft of 2018 into the 2024 Bill, a legislature elected after an early fall serves only the unexpired residue of the common term, so the state faces a truncated mandate or a wait under President's Rule.

Its electors cannot restore a full five year government of their choosing until the national calendar permits, however the fall came about, and any interval of central rule falls on a state whose situation arises from the arithmetic of the cycle rather than from any verdict of its own people. The state therefore faces either a truncated mandate of months or a spell of President's Rule while it waits for the cycle to turn.

The opposition recorded in the Kovind consultation gives the federal position its political form. Of the forty seven political parties that submitted views to the High Level Committee, thirty two supported the proposal and fifteen opposed it, and the opposition was concentrated among regional parties and parties governing states, including the Trinamool Congress, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, and the left parties, whose written submissions pressed the federal and accountability objections in nearly the terms set out above. The Congress, the largest opposition party, recorded its opposition to the committee's premise. State governments that answered the committee's consultation divided on the same line, with several recording that the terms of their assemblies were constitutional entitlements of their electorates, not variables in a Union scheduling exercise. The federal position's advocates also note an asymmetry in the amendment's incidence: alignment is achieved by cutting the terms of assemblies elected after the appointed date to expire with the Lok Sabha, and no corresponding device can cut the Lok Sabha's term to meet an assembly's, so the direction of subordination runs one way, from the states toward the centre, whatever the proposal's neutral vocabulary of synchronisation suggests.

At its strongest, then, the federal position does not defend inefficiency. It concedes the cost arithmetic and denies its relevance: elections are not an administrative overhead to be minimised but the recurring act by which two levels of a federation renew separate mandates, and a design that merges the two renewals into a single national moment purchases administrative calm at the price of the federal distinction itself. The position's burden, fairly stated, is to show that the distinction does real work, that Indian voters use separate calendars to reach separate judgements they could not reach on a single day. The split verdicts of Delhi and Odisha, and the state verdicts that checked national governments between general elections, are its evidence that they do.

## The Institutional-Feasibility Position: The Amendment Architecture

The third position brackets both the savings and the federal objection and asks a prior question: what would the Constitution have to say for simultaneous elections to exist? Its answer, assembled across the Law Commission reports of 1999 and 2018 and completed by the Kovind Committee's amendment schedule of March 2024, identifies a package of textual changes without which synchronisation remains a slogan. Article 83 fixes the duration of the Lok Sabha at five years from its first meeting, unless sooner dissolved. Article 172 does the same for legislative assemblies. Article 85 governs the sessions, prorogation, and dissolution of Parliament, and Article 174 the corresponding matters for state legislatures. Article 356 governs the failure of constitutional machinery in a state, the provision under which assemblies have repeatedly been dissolved ahead of term. A synchronised system must discipline all five provisions, because any one of them, left unamended, supplies a route by which a legislature escapes the common calendar: a dissolution under the existing text starts a fresh five year term, and the cycle dissolves with it.

The feasibility position's central invention is the remainder rule. If every premature dissolution produced a full new term, synchronisation could survive only until the first government fell. The Law Commission's 2018 draft therefore proposed, and the Kovind Committee adopted, the rule that a House constituted after a mid-term election serves only the unexpired term of its predecessor, the period between the dissolution and the date on which the original five years would have ended. The rule converts the fixed term from a property of each legislature into a property of the cycle itself. Governments may fall, Houses may be dissolved and reconstituted, but the calendar endures, and every legislature in the country expires together on the appointed rhythm. Around this invention the package arranges its supporting devices: an appointed date, notified by the President on the first sitting of a Lok Sabha after a general election, from which alignment runs; the curtailment of assemblies first elected after that date so that their terms end with the Lok Sabha's full term; and a power for the Election Commission, where an assembly election cannot practicably be held with the general election, to recommend a deferred poll whose winner nonetheless expires with the common term.

The amendment procedure itself is the position's second subject. Amendments to Articles 83 and 172 proceed under Article 368 by special majority of Parliament, and the Kovind Committee held that the first stage of synchronisation, covering the Lok Sabha and the assemblies, does not require ratification by half the state legislatures, because the duration provisions are not among the entrenched matters listed in the proviso to Article 368. The second stage is different: bringing municipalities and panchayats into the synchronised frame within one hundred days, and creating a single electoral roll and a single elector photo identity card through a new Article 324A and changes to Article 325, touches the state sphere of local government and the State Election Commissions' domain under Articles 243K and 243ZA, and the Committee accepted that this stage requires ratification by the states. Suhrith Parthasarathy's post-2024 essays contest the first stage conclusion, arguing that amendments which curtail the terms of every assembly in the country alter the federal character of the Constitution in substance, whatever the proviso's list says, and that a change of this depth invites a basic structure challenge of the kind the Supreme Court entertained in Kesavananda Bharati in 1973. The feasibility position, at its most careful, does not dismiss that risk; it prices it in, treating litigation over the amendment's validity as a foreseeable stage of the trajectory rather than an aberration.

### Does the synchronisation amendment need ratification by half the state legislatures?

The Kovind Committee held that the first stage, synchronising the Lok Sabha and the assemblies through amendments to Articles 83, 172, and 327 with a new Article 82A, needs no state ratification, since term duration sits outside the proviso list attached to Article 368.

Ratification is required for the second stage, covering local bodies and a single electoral roll, which enters the states' constitutional sphere directly, and the committee's willingness to concede the requirement at that stage is part of what distinguishes its architecture from a bare parliamentary assertion of the synchronising power.

M. R. Madhavan's analyses for PRS Legislative Research, written across the proposal's trajectory from the standing committee report onward, supply the feasibility position with its most disciplined parliamentary reading. Madhavan's work tracks the distinction between what a bare amendment can achieve and what the working of the system will permit: an amendment can fix the calendar, but it cannot fix the confidence of a House, and every device in the package, from the remainder rule to the constructive vote of no confidence, is an attempt to govern the gap between those two facts. The constructive vote deserves its place in the feasibility account. Borrowed from the German Basic Law and recommended by the Law Commission in both 1999 and 2018, it permits a House to dismiss its government only by simultaneously electing a successor, so that a negative majority cannot destroy a ministry without constituting one. The device is the feasibility position's answer to the accountability objection in its sharpest form: the objection that a synchronised House, unable to shorten its own life, might sustain a ministry that has lost the country's confidence, or fall into a hung paralysis with no route to a fresh full mandate. The 2024 Bill, notably, did not carry the constructive vote into its text, and the feasibility debate since December 2024 has turned in part on whether the package as introduced is incomplete without it. That gap between the committee architecture and the introduced Bill is one of the points on which the Joint Parliamentary Committee's examination, taken up below, has concentrated.

## The Accountability Problem: No Confidence, Hung Houses and the Fixed Term

Every version of the synchronisation design must answer a question that the staggered calendar never has to pose: what happens when a synchronised legislature's government falls and no successor can be constituted? Under the working Constitution, the answer is dissolution and a fresh election for a full term, and the possibility of that answer disciplines governments and oppositions alike. A prime minister who loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha resigns or advises dissolution; an opposition that brings down a ministry knows it must face the electorate for a full five year stake. The remainder rule changes both calculations. A House elected after a fall inherits only the residue of the term, which may be months. The Law Commission's documents treat this as the design's stabilising genius: because a mid-term election buys so little time in office, parties will strain to compose their differences inside the House rather than dissolve it, and governments will be changed by negotiation, as in the German model, instead of by repeated recourse to the voter. The federal and accountability critics treat the same feature as the design's deepest vice: a legislature that cannot renew its own mandate in full is a legislature whose members have been partially disenfranchised in advance, and a state placed under President's Rule because no ministry can be formed for a remainder of a few months has lost self government not through any verdict of its people but through the arithmetic of a calendar set in Delhi.

The hung House scenario sharpens the problem. Indian elections have produced hung assemblies frequently and a hung Lok Sabha repeatedly, in 1989, 1990, 1996, and 1998 among other years, and the staggered system's remedy, a fresh poll when coalition building fails, delivered the short lived Houses of 1996 to 1999, three Lok Sabhas in four years. Under synchronisation that remedy survives only in truncated form. The Kovind Committee addressed the scenario expressly, recommending that fresh elections be held to constitute a new House where a no confidence motion or a hung verdict requires it, with the new House serving the unexpired term. The recommendation preserves the form of the remedy while halving its substance whenever the fall comes late in the cycle. Critics answer that a political class asked to fight a statewide or nationwide campaign for a prize of eighteen months in office will instead tolerate almost any arrangement, including arrangements the electorate would reject, and that the design therefore trades one pathology, frequent elections, for another, unaccountable longevity. The defenders answer, in turn, that the trade is the entire point of fixed terms in the presidential systems the critics otherwise admire, and that the Indian voter's recurring midterm signals survive in by-elections, local polls, and the Rajya Sabha's continuing renewal.

The Emergency casts the longest shadow over this argument, and both sides cite it. The fifth Lok Sabha, elected in March 1971, extended its own term twice during the Emergency and sat for nearly six years, demonstrating that a parliamentary majority in possession of a synchronised calendar might postpone its reckoning with the voters by amending the duration provisions; the 44th Amendment of 1978 restored the five year term and stiffened the emergency provisions in response. Opponents of synchronisation invoke the episode to argue that fixed and uniform terms concentrate the temptation to manipulate them, since one amendment moves every legislature in the country at once. Proponents reply that the episode shows the opposite: the staggered calendar of the nineteen seventies did nothing to prevent the extension, which proceeded under emergency powers that any serious amendment design must guard on their own terms. The exchange illustrates the stance this article maintains throughout. The chronology is settled, and it is stated plainly here; what the chronology proves about a future design is disputed, and the dispute is recorded as a dispute.

## The Kovind Committee: Appointment, Consultation and the Report of March 2024

The proposal acquired its decisive institutional form on 2 September 2023, when the Union government constituted a High Level Committee on simultaneous elections under the chairmanship of Ram Nath Kovind, who had demitted office as President of India in July 2022. The committee's membership joined the political executive to constitutional and fiscal expertise: Home Minister Amit Shah; N. K. Singh, chairman of the Fifteenth Finance Commission; Subhash C. Kashyap, former Secretary General of the Lok Sabha and a leading commentator on the Constitution; Harish Salve, senior advocate and former Solicitor General; Sanjay Kothari, former Chief Vigilance Commissioner; and Ghulam Nabi Azad, former Leader of the Opposition in the Rajya Sabha. The Leader of the Congress in the Lok Sabha, Adhir Ranjan Chowdhury, was named to the committee and declined to serve, writing that the exercise's conclusions appeared to him predetermined; his refusal, and the composition that remained, became part of the federal position's evidence that the committee was an instrument of the government's programme rather than an open inquiry. The committee's defenders replied that its consultation was the widest ever conducted on the question, and the record of that consultation is a matter of document rather than characterisation.

### How was the Kovind Committee's consultation conducted?

The committee, constituted on 2 September 2023 and reporting on 14 March 2024, consulted forty seven political parties, of which thirty two supported the proposal and fifteen opposed it, and took evidence from former Chief Justices of India and former Chief Election Commissioners during its sittings.

It invited public responses that ran into tens of thousands, and examined the election cycles of countries including Germany, Japan, South Africa, and Sweden before settling its two stage design, a comparative exercise taken up in its own section below.

The committee submitted its report to President Droupadi Murmu on 14 March 2024, days before the schedule for the 2024 general election was announced. The report recommended a legally tenable mechanism to restore the simultaneous cycle in two steps. In the first step, elections to the Lok Sabha and all state assemblies would be held together. In the second, elections to municipalities and panchayats would be synchronised with the general elections so as to fall within one hundred days of them. To work the first step, the committee proposed an appointed date: the President would notify, on the date of the first sitting of the Lok Sabha after a general election, the date from which the synchronisation provisions take force, and every assembly constituted after that date would end with the expiry of the full term of that Lok Sabha, whether its own five years had run or not. The report proposed a new Article 82A to carry this machinery, consequential amendments to Articles 83, 172, and 327, a further article, 324A, for the local body stage, a single electoral roll and single elector photo identity card prepared by the Election Commission in consultation with the State Election Commissions, an implementation group to oversee the transition, and expanded provisioning of voting machines and paper audit units, with the committee recording the Election Commission's estimate of the additional equipment a synchronised poll demands.

Two features of the report distinguish it from its predecessors and locate it within the three positions. The first is its treatment of the remainder rule for the national House itself: where fresh elections constitute a new Lok Sabha mid-cycle, that House too serves only the unexpired term, so the discipline of the calendar binds the Union exactly as it binds the states, a symmetry the federal position's advocates had demanded and whose inclusion they acknowledged even while rejecting the scheme. The second is its candour about sequence: the report did not pretend that synchronisation could begin at the general election immediately following, since the appointed date must be notified at the first sitting of a Lok Sabha and the curtailment of assemblies then works through one full cycle before full simultaneity is reached. Commentary that later attached the design to the 2029 election therefore ran ahead of the committee's own transitional logic, which points to the cycle after next for complete alignment, a point the Joint Parliamentary Committee's examination would revisit. Placed against [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/), the report is best read as an attempt to relieve one stress on the electoral machinery, the stress of perpetual mobilisation, by accepting another, the stress of a single mobilisation of unprecedented scale, and the Election Commission's own evidence to the committee, on machine requirements and personnel, is the record on which that exchange of stresses will ultimately be judged.

## September 2024: The Cabinet Accepts the Report

On 18 September 2024 the Union Cabinet, chaired by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, accepted the recommendations of the High Level Committee and announced that simultaneous elections would be implemented in two phases, with a common electoral roll and an implementation group to carry the programme forward. The Cabinet's acceptance is the moment the architectural-efficiency position became the stated reasoning of the Union government. The press release issued that day recited the lineage this article has traced, the simultaneous elections of 1951 to 1967, the 170th Report of 1999, the standing committee's 79th Report of 2015, and presented the Kovind Report as the culmination toward which those documents had been tending. Whatever the federal position's objections to that genealogy, the Cabinet's framing was itself an adjudicative act: it declared that the question had been examined enough, that the institutional record supported action, and that the remaining work was implementation rather than inquiry.

The reactions sorted along the lines the three positions predict. Parties of the ruling National Democratic Alliance welcomed the decision in the vocabulary of the efficiency position, speaking of expenditure saved, governance unimpeded, and a development agenda freed from perpetual campaigning. The Congress and the regional opposition answered in the vocabulary of the federal position, describing the acceptance as an attack on the federal structure and on the accountability of governments to legislatures and voters between elections; Mamata Banerjee, the Chief Minister of West Bengal, recorded her objection in the strongest terms used by any state head of government. Constitutional commentators, occupying the feasibility position's ground, asked the narrower questions the Cabinet's announcement had left open: whether the first stage truly escaped the ratification requirement, whether the curtailment of assemblies could survive a basic structure challenge, and whether the amendment would travel alone or with the single electoral roll provisions whose ratification needs the committee had conceded. The Cabinet had accepted a report; converting acceptance into constitutional text was the feasibility position's test, and it arrived three months later.

## December 2024: The Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill

On 17 December 2024 the Minister of Law and Justice, Arjun Ram Meghwal, introduced the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill 2024 in the Lok Sabha, together with the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill 2024, which aligns the terms of the assemblies of Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir through amendments to their governing statutes. The introduction was contested and put to a division, in which 269 members voted for introduction and 198 against, figures that foreshadow the arithmetic any passage of the Bill must command: a constitutional amendment requires a majority of the total membership of each House and two thirds of members present and voting, under Article 368, and the government of the day does not dispose of such numbers in the Rajya Sabha on its own strength. The Bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons speaks the efficiency position's language in the government's own voice: elections have become expensive and time consuming, the imposition of the Model Code of Conduct in poll bound states puts development programmes on hold, disrupts normal public life, and diverts manpower from core duties for prolonged periods.

The Bill's text is the feasibility position made legislative. Its centrepiece is the proposed Article 82A. The President, by notification issued on the date of the first sitting of the Lok Sabha after a general election, brings the synchronisation provisions into force, and that date is the appointed date. All legislative assemblies constituted in elections held after the appointed date end on the expiry of the full term of the House of the People, so alignment is achieved by curtailment at the first transition and maintained thereafter. The Election Commission is placed under a duty to conduct the general elections to the Lok Sabha and all assemblies simultaneously, with simultaneous elections defined as general elections held for constituting the House of the People and all the legislative assemblies together. Where the Commission is of the opinion that an assembly election cannot be held with the general election, it may recommend to the President that an order declare the election to be held at a later date, and the assembly so elected nonetheless expires with the full term of the Lok Sabha. Consequential amendments to Articles 83 and 172 write the remainder rule into the duration provisions: a House dissolved before the expiry of its full term is replaced, on fresh election, by a House that sits only for the unexpired term, the interval between the dissolution and the date five years from the first meeting of the dissolved House. An amendment to Article 327 adds the conduct of simultaneous elections to the subjects on which Parliament may legislate for elections to Parliament and the state legislatures.

Read against the committee architecture, the introduced Bill is a partial transcription, and the omissions define the feasibility debate that followed. The Bill covers the first stage only: there is no Article 324A for local bodies, no single electoral roll, and therefore nothing in the text that, on the committee's own analysis, requires ratification by the states, a drafting choice that the government defended as fidelity to stage one and that critics read as an attempt to carry a federal restructuring on a parliamentary majority alone. The constructive vote of no confidence, present in the Law Commission's thinking since 1999 and in the draft of 2018, does not appear; nor do the 2018 draft's proposals on electing the executive head by the whole House or diluting the anti-defection law for hung Houses. The Bill fixes the calendar and leaves the confidence question to the remainder rule's deterrent logic. For the archival record on which examinations of this kind depend, the preservation habits of legislatures, commissions, and newsrooms matter more than any single speech, and the practices described in accounts of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) belong to the same documentary culture that keeps a Bill's text, its objects and reasons, and its committee record available for adjudication long after the debate of the day has moved on. That documentary trail is what permits the three positions to be tested against texts rather than recollections.

### What does the proposed Article 82A actually do?

Article 82A, as drafted in the Bill introduced on 17 December 2024, supplies the synchronisation machinery the Constitution lacks: an appointed date notified by the President, a common expiry for every later constituted assembly, and a duty on the Election Commission to hold the two general elections together.

The article also permits one deferred assembly poll, on the Commission's recommendation, where an assembly election cannot practicably be held with the general election, without breaking the common expiry that the rest of the machinery protects, and it is this combination of a fixed common end with a single supervised exception that the feasibility position defends as workable drafting.

On 20 December 2024 the two Bills were referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee of thirty nine members drawn from both Houses, chaired by P. P. Chaudhary of the Bharatiya Janata Party, with a membership that included the opposition's principal constitutional voices. Referral to a joint committee is Parliament's standard device for legislation that cuts across party strength and constitutional depth, and in this instance it also served the three positions' debate: the efficiency and feasibility arguments would have to survive clause by clause scrutiny in a forum where the federal objection holds seats and a vote. The committee's mandate was extended more than once as its examination widened to take evidence from constitutional scholars, former election commissioners, state representatives, and the Law Commission's serving members, and the public controversy over incidental episodes of that examination, including a proposed interaction with the higher judiciary that members questioned on separation of powers grounds, illustrates how completely the Bill's progress became a proxy contest among the three positions rather than a technical review of drafting.

## The Post-2024 Trajectory as a Test of the Three Positions

The trajectory since September 2024 tests each position at the point of its greatest vulnerability. The efficiency position, adopted by the Cabinet and written into the Bill's objects and reasons, must survive the costing scrutiny of the joint committee, where the Election Commission's evidence on doubling machine inventories, warehousing a synchronised fleet between five yearly peaks, and staffing a single day poll of more than a billion electors converts the abstract saving into a capital programme whose size the position's advocates had not previously had to state in one figure. If the total system cost of one synchronised mega election, machines, security, personnel, and a common roll, approaches the cost of the staggered exercises it replaces, the efficiency claim shrinks to its governance limb, the recovery of unimpeded administrative time, which is real but harder to quantify in a committee room. The position's honest advocates have begun to make exactly that migration, resting the case less on rupees saved than on the [integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) within which a government elected on a national mandate seeks a full term of execution unpunctuated by code restrictions, which is a claim about governing capacity rather than accounting.

The federal position is tested by the committee process itself. Its advocates predicted that synchronisation would be imposed over state objections by central majority; the requirement of a special majority in both Houses, and the government's dependence on allies whose parties govern states, has instead forced a deliberative pace in which state objections are heard, recorded, and priced. The federal position must then show its evidence in the forum: the split verdict record, the midterm signal function of state polls, the one way direction of curtailment. Its weakest point, exposed in examination, is the status of the 1951 to 1967 period, when synchrony coexisted with a functioning federation and with powerful state ministries; the position answers that the federation of that period operated under one party dominance at both levels, so the calendar was never asked to bear the weight of genuinely divergent verdicts, and that the federalised party system built after 1989 is precisely the structure a common calendar places at risk. The answer is cogent, and it converts the federal claim from a textual one into a historical one, about which kind of party system the Constitution's calendar should presuppose.

The feasibility position is tested most literally, clause by clause. Its strongest ground is the remainder rule, which the Bill carried intact and which no witness has shown to be unworkable as drafting. Its weakest ground is the pair of omissions: the absent constructive vote, which leaves a fallen government and a hung House to the mercy of remainder arithmetic, and the absent ratification, which leaves the whole structure exposed to the basic structure challenge that Parthasarathy and others have drafted in outline in the journals, including the pages of the Economic and Political Weekly. The feasibility position's mature form, as it has emerged in the examination, is therefore a conditional claim rather than a confident one: simultaneous elections are achievable through the amendment package, provided the package is completed by the stabilising devices its own lineage recommended and provided its federal cost is either accepted openly or compensated by ratification sought as a matter of prudence even where the proviso does not compel it. Whether the joint committee's report moves the Bill toward that completed form is the question on which the next stage of the trajectory turns, and it is a question the three positions framework predicts will be fought on architectural grounds, in the vocabulary of articles and clauses, whatever the party positions of the combatants.

## The Administration Question: Machines, Rolls and a Single Electoral Season

Between the architectural argument and the polling station stands an administrative apparatus whose evidence runs through every document in the lineage, and a full account of the debate must give that evidence its own hearing. The Election Commission administers a Lok Sabha election for an electorate that exceeded 960 million names on the rolls in 2024, through more than a million polling stations, and a synchronised election adds an assembly ballot at nearly every one of those stations on the same schedule. The Commission's evidence to successive committees has been consistent on the physical implication: two ballot units and two paper audit units at each station, roughly double the fleet held for a single tier poll, with warehouses to keep the enlarged fleet and a replacement cycle to retire it. The Commission put the additional procurement requirement, in evidence associated with the earlier phased proposals, in the region of several thousand crore rupees, and the Kovind Committee recorded the requirement afresh while recommending that provisioning begin in advance of the appointed date. The efficiency position prices this as a capital cost amortised across cycles; the sceptical reading prices it as a standing fleet bought for one week in five years. Both readings are honestly available on the same evidence, which is why the administration question is best treated as a branch of the feasibility position rather than as a refutation of the whole design.

The electoral roll raises a federal administrative question of its own. India maintains separate rolls for parliamentary and assembly elections, prepared by the Election Commission under Article 325 and the Representation of the People Act 1950, and separate rolls again for municipalities and panchayats, prepared by the State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA from their own machinery. A synchronised system strains at that division. A voter turned away from a synchronised poll because a name appears on one roll and not the other suffers a compounded disenfranchisement, losing two votes to one clerical divergence. The Kovind Committee's answer was the second stage of its design: a single electoral roll and a single elector photo identity card for all three tiers, prepared by the Election Commission in consultation with the State Election Commissions, carried by the proposed Article 324A and amendments to Article 325, and acknowledged by the committee itself to require state ratification because local government sits in the State List. The single roll is thus the point where the feasibility position and the federal position intersect in administrative form: the tidiest machinery solution is also the solution that transfers a state function toward the centre, and the committee's candour in assigning it to a ratified second stage is one of the report's most careful judgements.

Campaign finance forms the third administrative branch, and here the synchronisation proposal touches the territory of [the electoral bonds and political finance record](/2020/06/25/electoral-bonds-political-finance-india/). The efficiency argument claims that one campaign season in five years reduces the total volume of election spending, official and unofficial, because parties and candidates mobilise once rather than in rolling instalments across every year. The counter evidence is the experience of the staggered system's critics themselves: spending concentrates where contests are, and a synchronised season may simply concentrate five years of expenditure into a single quarter, raising the entry cost of politics at exactly the moment when every seat in the country is simultaneously in play. Smaller parties and independent candidates, whose staggered calendar lets them move scarce workers and funds from one state contest to the next, face a synchronised calendar in which every contest demands their whole strength at once. The federal position absorbs this point as an argument about pluralism: the rolling calendar is a subsidy, paid in scheduling, to political diversity, and abolishing it advantages the parties with national treasuries and national cadres. No committee in the lineage fully resolved the finance question, and the joint committee's examination has treated it as an open file, which is the correct disposition for a question on which the documentary record is thinner than the rhetoric on either side.

Finally, the administration question includes the calendar of everything else. A synchronised poll of the Lok Sabha and all assemblies, followed within one hundred days by local body elections, fixes the electoral season against the monsoon, the harvest, school examinations, and the festival calendar of every state at once, a constraint the staggered system distributes. The phasing of polling within a general election, which in 2024 ran across seven phases and six weeks, shows that even a nominally simultaneous election is in practice a season rather than a day, with the Model Code in force nationally for its whole span. The efficiency position's cleanest claim, one code window in five years, survives that observation in comparative form, since the window replaces the perpetual partial application of the code across the states; but the observation disciplines the rhetoric of a single national poll day, and the debate is healthier for the discipline. Synchronisation, as its own documents describe it, is a synchronisation of terms and seasons, not of a single morning's voting, and the positions are most honestly compared at that level of precision.

## The Strongest Counter to the Three-Positions Reading

A framework earns its keep by facing the objection that would dissolve it. The strongest counter to the three-positions reading is that the events it organises are moving faster than the organisation: the joint committee may recommend changes that redraw the Bill, a court may one day pronounce on the federal character of term curtailment, state politics may generate alignments no 2024 document foresaw, and a characterisation of the debate fixed in the committee's vocabulary could be superseded by a trajectory that invents a fourth position or collapses two of the three. The objection is serious, and this article concedes its premise. The framework is a reading of a documentary record, and documentary records grow.

What the objection does not show is that the growth escapes the framework's categories. A joint committee recommendation to add the constructive vote would be a move inside the feasibility position, completing the package the Law Commission sketched in 2018. A recommendation to seek ratification voluntarily would be a move in which the feasibility position prices the federal position's objection and pays it. A judicial pronouncement on curtailment would adjudicate the boundary between the feasibility and federal positions on the very terms, articles, proviso, basic structure, on which both positions have already stated their cases. Even the collapse scenarios run through the framework: if fiscal evidence destroyed the savings claim, the efficiency position would contract to its governance limb rather than vanish, and if a future government abandoned the Bill in committee, the three positions would remain as the standing description of the debate the next committee inherits, as the 2018 draft stood to the 2024 committee. The framework's claim is not that the debate will end in one position's victory. It is that the debate's substance is architectural, that its arguments sort by the question they answer, cost, protection, or possibility, and that the post-2024 trajectory is intelligible as the working out of those three questions under the pressure of a live amendment. On the record assembled from 1983 to the committee rooms of 2024 and after, that claim stands.

## What the Three-Positions Reading Settles

The alternative framings fail on the record in identifiable ways. The political-strategic framing, which treats simultaneous elections as an instrument of one party's advantage, cannot explain why the proposal's documentary lineage runs through the Election Commission of 1983, the Law Commission of 1999, and a standing committee of 2015, across governments of every complexion, nor why the ruling party's own committee built in the remainder rule's symmetry against the Lok Sabha itself and conceded ratification for the local body stage. Strategy exists in the debate, as it exists in every constitutional contest, but the strategic reading mistakes the debate's occasion for its substance. The procedural-feasibility framing fails in the opposite direction: by treating the question as one of operational possibility, it cannot explain why a technically complete package, drafted by 2018 and redrafted by 2024, continues to be contested on grounds that have nothing to do with technique. The contest persists because the architecture is the politics: to fix the calendar is to decide how often, and in what company, the Indian voter judges two levels of government, and that decision is substantive in the fullest sense.

The three positions, held together, state that substance completely. Synchronisation would recover governing time and administrative money spent on a perpetual election season; it would merge the state verdict into the national moment and close the midterm channel through which state electorates have disciplined Union governments since 1989; and it can be built, if it can be built at all, only by a constitutional package whose remainder rule, appointed date, and deferral power are drafted to survive the falls of governments that the calendar cannot prevent. The September 2024 Cabinet acceptance adopted the first of those propositions as policy. The December 2024 Bill wrote the third into legislative text and omitted the stabilising devices its own lineage had recommended. The federal proposition organised the opposition and will organise the litigation if the Bill advances. Each event belongs to its position, and the positions together account for every major move in the trajectory without residue. That is the analytical contribution on offer, and its test is the one this article set at the outset: the simultaneous elections debate operates through three substantive positions, and the post-2024 trajectory, from a Cabinet room in September to a committee room in December and beyond, is the examination in which each position is being marked against its own stated burden of proof.

## Fixed Terms Elsewhere: The Comparative Evidence the Committees Weighed

Committees examining synchronisation have repeatedly looked abroad, and the comparative record, properly read, supports the feasibility position's caution rather than any side's enthusiasm. The Kovind Committee examined the election cycles of Germany, Japan, South Africa, and Sweden among others, and the earlier documents drew on a wider set. Germany supplies the constructive vote of no confidence from Article 67 of the Basic Law of 1949, under which the Bundestag can dismiss the Federal Chancellor only by electing a successor with an absolute majority, a device used successfully once, in 1982, when Helmut Kohl replaced Helmut Schmidt without an election. Germany also elects its state parliaments, the Landtage, on calendars deliberately separate from the federal cycle, and German federalism treats that separation as unremarkable, which is why the German example arms both Indian positions at once: it proves the stabilising device works, and it proves a mature federation can leave its state calendars alone. Sweden holds national, county, and municipal elections on a single day every four years and is the closest working model of the synchronised design, within a unitary state whose local tier carries nothing like the constitutional weight of an Indian state. South Africa votes nationally and provincially together while municipal elections run on their own cycle, a hybrid that resembles the Kovind Committee's staged design more than full simultaneity does.

The British experience carries the sharpest warning in the comparative file. The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011, enacted by the coalition government of David Cameron, fixed five year terms for the House of Commons and restricted early dissolution to defined triggers, including a two thirds vote of the House or a failed confidence procedure. Within a decade the Act had produced a Parliament in 2019 that could neither sustain a government nor dissolve itself, while a minority executive governed through the impasse, and the constitutional argument over its attempted circumvention reached the Supreme Court in the Miller litigation over prorogation. Parliament repealed the Act outright through the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 and restored the prerogative of dissolution. The episode is the standing comparative exhibit for the accountability objection: a fixed term, adopted for stability, collided with a hung House and a confidence crisis, and the collision was resolved by abolishing the fixity. The Indian feasibility literature absorbs the lesson accurately. India's design, unlike the British statute, would be constitutional rather than statutory, harder to repeal in a crisis, and its remainder rule answers the British impasse differently, by letting the House die and be reborn truncated rather than trapping it alive. Whether that answer suffices is a genuine question, and it is the question the joint committee's witnesses from the constitutional bar have pressed hardest.

The United States contributes the midterm, the structural feature the NITI Aayog paper borrowed when it proposed elections in two windows across a five year Indian cycle. American federal and state elections coincide on a fixed two and four year rhythm that no confidence crisis can disturb, because the American executive does not hold office on legislative confidence. The comparison clarifies by contrast what is distinctive in the Indian problem. A presidential calendar can be fixed by the clock because governments there do not fall between elections. A parliamentary calendar can be fixed only by engineering around the fall of governments, which is why every Indian document from 1999 onward is preoccupied with devices, constructive votes, remainder terms, elected executives, that the American debate never needs. The comparative record thus sorts neatly into the three positions: Sweden and South Africa show electors can vote at two tiers on allied calendars, Germany shows how a parliamentary House may change governments without dying, and Britain shows what happens when fixity meets a confidence crisis unprepared. The Indian package of 2024 took the German problem seriously in its committee stage and then, in the introduced Bill, left the German device out, which is why comparative evidence features so prominently in the examination that followed.

## The Timing Question: Appointed Dates and the Road to a First Synchronised Cycle

Synchronisation cannot begin by declaration. Because alignment is achieved by curtailing assemblies elected after an appointed date so that they expire with a particular Lok Sabha, the design contains a built in delay of one full cycle between the notification of the appointed date and the first truly simultaneous general election. The committee room controversy over the year 2029 versus the year 2034, conducted between members of the joint committee in public statements during its examination, is a controversy about this arithmetic. If the amendment is enacted and the appointed date is notified at the first sitting of the Lok Sabha constituted after the general election of 2029, the assemblies elected between 2029 and 2034 serve truncated terms ending in 2034, and the first general election at which the Lok Sabha and every assembly are chosen together for full concurrent terms is the election of 2034. Accounts of the proposal that promised simultaneity at 2029 compressed the transition out of the design, and the correction matters for the debate's honesty: the efficiency savings, the federal costs, and the administrative build out all belong to a timetable whose full state arrives a decade after the appointed date, and the truncated assemblies of the transition cycle are the design's first federal fact, borne by state electorates who voted for five year governments and receive shorter ones by constitutional command.

The transition interacts with two other clocks in the constitutional system. The first is delimitation. The freeze on readjustment of Lok Sabha and assembly constituencies, imposed by the 42nd Amendment of 1976 and extended by the 84th Amendment of 2001, runs until the first census taken after the year 2026, and the resumption of delimitation will redraw constituency boundaries on population figures that shift representation among the states. A synchronised cycle beginning in the same period means that the first fully simultaneous electorate may also be a newly delimited electorate, and the federal position has noted the compounding: states whose share of parliamentary seats falls at delimitation would experience that adjustment inside a calendar that already subordinates their electoral moment to the national one. The second clock is the census itself, postponed from 2021 and rescheduled by the Union government in the middle of the decade, whose publication starts the delimitation clock and therefore the appointed date politics of any synchronisation timetable. None of these interactions invalidates the design, and the feasibility position treats them as scheduling constraints to be sequenced rather than objections of principle. But they establish that the trajectory's next tests are chronological as much as constitutional, and that the three positions will be argued again, on new facts, each time one of the clocks strikes.

State governments have a further timing grievance that the federal position states precisely. Assemblies elected shortly before an appointed date keep their full terms and vote out of step once more; assemblies elected shortly after it are cut. Two neighbouring states with nearly identical electoral histories can therefore receive visibly unequal treatment from the same amendment, one completing its mandate and the other losing a year or more of it, with the difference turning on nothing but the accident of where each state's calendar stood when the notification issued. The committee accepted this as the unavoidable price of any transition from a staggered to a common cycle, and history supports the acceptance in one respect: the original synchrony of 1952 was itself the product of a founding moment in which every legislature began together, a condition no transition can reproduce without some mandates being shortened. The federal answer is that the founding moment was a constituent act in which the states were parties to the creation of the whole order, whereas the appointed date is an act of one Parliament under an amendment power, and that the legitimacy of curtailing mandates differs accordingly. The disagreement is irreducible by evidence, and it is recorded here as the federal position's deepest stratum: not a claim about cost or convenience, but a claim about whose act may shorten a people's mandate.

## The Evidence of the Ballot: What Split Verdicts Show

Both the federal and the efficiency positions claim the Indian voter as their witness, and the ballot record contains exhibits for each. The federal position's prize exhibits come from the staggered calendar. In May 2014 the electorate of Odisha voted for the Lok Sabha and the state assembly on allied dates, and returned the Biju Janata Dal overwhelmingly to the assembly while the national contest ran its separate course; in February 2015, nine months after giving the Bharatiya Janata Party all seven of Delhi's Lok Sabha seats in the general election of May 2014 and again in 2019, Delhi's voters gave the Aam Aadmi Party sixty two of seventy assembly seats in 2015 and sixty two again in February 2020, a separation of judgements so stark that no reading of the evidence can call it accidental. The state elections of December 2018 in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Chhattisgarh removed three incumbent Bharatiya Janata Party state governments months before the same party's national victory of May 2019, demonstrating the midterm signal in its purest form: the voter used the state occasion to warn, and the general election to decide, and the two messages were different because the occasions were different.

The efficiency position's counter exhibits come from the states that already vote simultaneously by the accident of their calendars. Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Arunachal Pradesh, and Sikkim have held assembly elections with the Lok Sabha in recent cycles, and their record complicates the federal claim that a shared polling season forces a shared verdict. In April and May 2019 Odisha voted for both tiers within the same phases, and its electorate raised the Bharatiya Janata Party from one Lok Sabha seat to eight while returning the Biju Janata Dal to the assembly with 112 of 147 seats, a divided judgement delivered on allied dates with the same campaign noise in the air. Andhra Pradesh in 2019 swept the YSR Congress Party to power in the assembly with 151 of 175 seats and gave it twenty two of twenty five Lok Sabha seats, a unified verdict, while Sikkim used the same allied calendar to end the twenty five year rule of the Sikkim Democratic Front in the assembly. Voters in these states, the efficiency reading concludes, can and do separate the two decisions when the parties and records before them differ, using the two ballots in the same booth on the same day for different purposes. The federal reading answers that allied calendars in four states, three of them small and one governed for two decades by a dominant regional party, are a thin base for a national redesign, and that Odisha's divergence was itself the product of a state party system robust enough to hold its own campaign against the national one, a robustness not every state possesses.

The honest adjudication of this evidence is that it underdetermines the design question, and both positions, at their strongest, effectively concede as much. Split verdicts occur under both calendars. Their frequency under a permanently synchronised national calendar, in states with weak regional parties and in the presence of a single dominant national campaign, cannot be read off from four allied states or from the staggered record of the capital. What the ballot evidence does settle is narrower and valuable: the Indian elector is not the passive instrument of either side's rhetoric, neither mechanically nationalising the state vote when the dates coincide nor mechanically separating the verdicts when they do not. Any design adopted on the assumption that voters cannot distinguish two ballots underestimates the electorate that the documentary record, from Sukumar Sen's first rolls onward, shows distinguishing far finer things. A serious debate about the calendar should therefore be conducted, as the three positions framework conducts it, on the architecture itself: on what the terms, the code, the roll, and the amendment permit and forbid, with voter behaviour as a check on exaggeration rather than as a substitute for argument.

The earliest ballots bear on the debate in one further way. The synchronised elections of 1952, 1957, 1962, and 1967 were held when the Congress dominated both tiers, and the opposition's combined share of the vote in those elections was persistently larger than its share of seats, a feature of the electoral system rather than the calendar. The calendar became a live question only when the party system pluralised, because divergent verdicts require parties capable of winning states while losing the country. Synchronisation in a pluralised system is therefore a different intervention from the synchrony the founding generation knew: it acts on a landscape of strong state parties, coalition habits, and regional media markets that did not exist when the common cycle last turned. The Kovind Committee's consultations with the state parties registered exactly this transformation, and the intensity of the federal objection in 2024, compared with the relative calm with which the subject was discussed in 1999, measures how much the party system's federalisation raised the stakes of a scheduling question into a constitutional one. That escalation is itself evidence for the framework defended here. A merely procedural question does not gather constitutional weight as the party system changes; an architectural one does, because architecture distributes power among the system's parts.

One further body of ballot evidence deserves recording, because the committees themselves collected it: the testimony of election administrators about turnout and voter fatigue across repeated polls in a single year. Returning officers and district officials who deposed before the standing committee and the Law Commission described electors in districts polled three or four times within eighteen months, for an assembly by-election, a general election, and a local body round, presenting themselves in falling numbers at each successive exercise, with the local polls suffering most. The Election Commission's own statistical reports record the persistent gap between parliamentary turnout and the thinner participation in many municipal contests held on separate, low salience dates. The efficiency position draws from this record a participation argument that complements its cost arithmetic: a consolidated electoral season, in which the assembly and parliamentary ballots arrive together and stake the country's attention at once, asks the citizen for one great effort of judgement in five years rather than a drip of lesser efforts whose cumulative effect is abstention. The federal position answers that attention is not the same as deliberation, and that a local verdict folded into a national season may gain voters while losing the distinct local scrutiny that a separate date forces upon candidates and electors alike. Both claims are honestly grounded in the administrative record, and their coexistence inside the same body of testimony is a further reason the debate resists reduction to a contest between reform and resistance. The administrators who supplied the fatigue evidence are the same institution whose machine and personnel estimates supply the feasibility debate, and the Election Commission's evidence, taken as a whole, supports neither a dismissal of the staggered cycle nor an untroubled confidence in the synchronised one. It supports the proposition this article has defended throughout: the question is architectural, its costs and protections are both real, and its resolution must be argued at the level of constitutional design rather than administrative preference.

## The Article 356 Question: Dissolved Assemblies and the Memory of Dismissal

No account of the calendar debate is complete without the provision that has done more than any other to desynchronise Indian legislatures in practice: Article 356, under which the President, on the report of a Governor or otherwise, may assume the functions of a state government where the constitutional machinery of the state has failed. The Kerala dismissal of 31 July 1959, the first crack in the founding synchrony, was an Article 356 act. So were the dissolutions that repeatedly reset state clocks across the decades, and two episodes of mass dismissal frame the federal memory within which the synchronisation proposal is heard in the states. In April 1977 the Janata government of Prime Minister Morarji Desai, acting through Home Minister Charan Singh, dissolved nine Congress ruled assemblies on the ground that the Lok Sabha verdict of March 1977 had shown those assemblies no longer represented their people's will, and fresh elections followed in June. In February 1980 the returning government of Indira Gandhi dissolved nine assemblies ruled by the Janata Party and its allies on the mirror image ground, after the Lok Sabha verdict of January 1980. The Supreme Court had upheld the 1977 dissolutions in State of Rajasthan versus Union of India, decided that year, while treating the power as exceptional, and the nine judge bench in S. R. Bommai versus Union of India in March 1994 later subjected Article 356 proclamations to judicial review, required floor tests as the forum for proving majority, and made the provision's casual use constitutionally hazardous for any Union government.

Synchronisation changes the meaning of an Article 356 dissolution in a way neither episode's participants could have imagined. Under the staggered system, a dissolved assembly is replaced at a fresh election by an assembly with a full five year term, so dismissal, however controversial, at least returns the state promptly to self government on a complete mandate. Under the remainder design, the assembly elected after an Article 356 dissolution serves only the unexpired residue of the common term. A dismissal pronounced in the fourth year of a cycle therefore yields a successor assembly of a single year, or, where the Governor's report counsels delay and the Commission's deferral power is engaged, a longer spell of President's Rule while the residue runs down. The federal position draws the conclusion directly: synchronisation raises the destructive power of Article 356 by shrinking the remedy that follows it, and a Union government minded to misuse the provision would face a state electorate unable to answer with a full term government of its own. The Bommai safeguards, the position argues, police the proclamation's grounds; they do nothing for the truncated mandate that a perfectly lawful proclamation would produce under the remainder design.

The feasibility position answers on two levels. At the level of drafting, the remainder rule applies to every route of premature dissolution alike, including a ministry's own decision to seek an early poll, and its deterrent is symmetrical: a state government that might once have dissolved its assembly opportunistically to catch a favourable season gains nothing from the manoeuvre when the successor inherits only the residue. At the level of practice, the position notes that the era of mass dismissal ended with Bommai for reasons independent of the calendar, and that the assemblies dissolved in 1977 and 1980 would, under a synchronised design, have been reconstituted for residues long enough, three years and more in those instances, to govern meaningfully. The exchange has no documentary resolution, because the design has never operated; it is the purest example in the whole debate of a question that turns on institutional behaviour under rules that exist only on paper. The joint committee's examination took evidence on precisely this interaction, and its eventual treatment of the Article 356 interface will be read in the states as the truest signal of whether the federal objection was heard or merely recorded.

There is a historical irony in the interaction that both positions acknowledge. The common cycle broke, in part, because assemblies could be dissolved at will by forces outside their own confidence arithmetic: a Governor's report in 1959, a Home Minister's letter in 1977 and again in 1980. The synchronised design proposes to repair the cycle by making dissolution cheap in calendar terms, since no dissolution can move the common expiry. In doing so it makes the survival of any single assembly less consequential for the calendar, and therefore, its critics say, less protected by the calendar's own stability. A system that cannot be desynchronised also cannot be defended by the argument that disturbing one legislature disturbs the whole. Whether that trade strengthens or weakens the states is the Article 356 form of the debate's central question, and it will remain open until a synchronised cycle meets its first dismissal.

The committees that examined the interaction left one practical suggestion standing between the positions: that any synchronisation statute book should fix, in advance and in the amendment text itself, the maximum interval for which a state may remain under President's Rule awaiting alignment, so that the deferral of a state's self government is bounded by law rather than by the convenience of the cycle. The suggestion has not entered the introduced Bill, and its absence marks another of the points at which the joint committee's report will show whether the examination altered the architecture or only annotated it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is One Nation One Election?

One Nation One Election is the popular name for the proposal to synchronise elections to the Lok Sabha and the state legislative assemblies, so that both are held together on a common cycle, with elections to municipalities and panchayats aligned to follow within one hundred days. The phrase entered wide circulation with the advocacy of the post-2014 Union government and was carried into the formal record by the High Level Committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind, whose report of March 2024 used simultaneous elections as its technical term. The proposal does not create a single election day for every tier at once in its first stage; it aligns terms first, and local bodies in a second stage.

### Q: What is the Kovind Committee?

The Kovind Committee is the High Level Committee on simultaneous elections constituted by the Union government on 2 September 2023 under the chairmanship of Ram Nath Kovind, former President of India. Its members included Home Minister Amit Shah, Fifteenth Finance Commission chairman N. K. Singh, former Lok Sabha Secretary General Subhash C. Kashyap, senior advocate Harish Salve, former Chief Vigilance Commissioner Sanjay Kothari, and former Rajya Sabha opposition leader Ghulam Nabi Azad. The committee consulted forty seven political parties, took evidence from former Chief Justices and former Chief Election Commissioners, and submitted its report to President Droupadi Murmu on 14 March 2024, recommending a two stage move to simultaneous elections.

### Q: When were the simultaneous elections proposed?

Simultaneous elections were the original practice of the republic, held in 1951 and 1952, 1957, 1962, and 1967, before premature dissolutions in 1968 and 1969 and the dissolution of the Lok Sabha in December 1970 broke the common cycle. The proposal to restore them was raised by the Election Commission in its annual report for 1983, developed by the Law Commission in its 170th Report of 1999, recommended in phased form by a parliamentary standing committee in December 2015 and a NITI Aayog paper of January 2017, drafted into an amendment scheme by the Law Commission in August 2018, and adopted as government policy on the Kovind Committee's report in 2024.

### Q: What constitutional amendments are needed?

The amendment package identified across the Law Commission reports and the Kovind Committee centres on Articles 83 and 172, which fix the duration of the Lok Sabha and the legislative assemblies, supported by Articles 85 and 174 on sessions and dissolution and by disciplines bearing on Article 356, so that no route of premature dissolution can restart a separate five year clock. The Kovind Committee proposed a new Article 82A to carry the synchronisation machinery and amendments to Article 327 on Parliament's election legislation power. A second stage, covering local bodies and a single electoral roll through a proposed Article 324A and changes to Article 325, was accepted to require ratification by the states under Article 368.

### Q: What is the 129th Amendment Bill?

The Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill 2024 is the Bill introduced in the Lok Sabha on 17 December 2024 by Law Minister Arjun Ram Meghwal to give effect to the first stage of the Kovind Committee's design. It inserts Article 82A, under which the President notifies an appointed date at the first sitting of a new Lok Sabha, assemblies constituted after that date end with the Lok Sabha's full term, and the Election Commission conducts the two general elections together. It amends Articles 83, 172, and 327, writes the unexpired term rule into the duration provisions, and was referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee on 20 December 2024.

### Q: Why is federal-balance affected?

Federal balance is affected because synchronisation aligns every state mandate to a term set by the national cycle. Alignment is achieved by curtailing assemblies elected after the appointed date so that they end with the Lok Sabha, while no device curtails the Lok Sabha to meet an assembly, so the adjustment runs one way. Critics argue that a common calendar also merges the state verdict into the national campaign, weakening the state electorate's ability to judge a state government on its state record, and closes the midterm channel through which state results have signalled discontent to Union governments between general elections. Supporters answer that the remainder rule binds the Lok Sabha symmetrically and that state subjects remain constitutionally untouched.

### Q: What is the Joint Parliamentary Committee?

The Joint Parliamentary Committee is the committee of thirty nine members drawn from both Houses of Parliament, chaired by P. P. Chaudhary, to which the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill 2024 and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill 2024 were referred on 20 December 2024. Joint committees of this kind examine a Bill clause by clause, take evidence from experts, officials, and stakeholders, and report recommendations back to both Houses, which may accept or reject them. Its examination of the simultaneous elections Bills has heard constitutional scholars, election administrators, and state representatives, and its tenure has been extended as the evidence widened beyond the original timetable.

### Q: What is the appointed date in the simultaneous elections design?

The appointed date is the date notified by the President, on the day of the first sitting of the House of the People after a general election, from which the synchronisation provisions of the proposed Article 82A take force. Every legislative assembly constituted in an election held after the appointed date ends on the expiry of the full term of that Lok Sabha, whatever its own five year span would otherwise have been. The device is the hinge of the transition: one notification converts a set of staggered terms into terms disciplined by a single expiry, and full simultaneity arrives at the first general election after the notification has worked through the assemblies.

### Q: What happens when a legislature is elected only for an unexpired term?

Under the remainder rule carried from the Law Commission's 2018 draft into the 2024 Bill, a House constituted after a premature dissolution serves only the unexpired term, the period between the dissolution and the date on which the dissolved House's five years would have expired. The new House does not begin a fresh five year clock. The rule keeps the common calendar intact through falls of government, and its advocates argue that the small prize of a truncated term discourages dissolutions. Its critics answer that electors asked to choose a government for a few months are denied a full mandate, and that a state unable to form a ministry for a short residue faces President's Rule instead.

### Q: What did the Law Commission recommend in its 170th Report of 1999?

The Law Commission's 170th Report on Reform of the Electoral Laws, submitted in May 1999 under Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy, concluded that the cycle of separate elections was wasteful and disruptive and that the goal should be one election to the Lok Sabha and all legislative assemblies in five years. It examined how the terms fixed by Articles 83 and 172 could be aligned, confronted the problem of Houses that fall mid-term, and proposed stabilising devices including the suggestion that a motion of no confidence should be accompanied by a proposal for the alternative government. The report began the documentary lineage that every later examination, including the Kovind Committee, cites as its foundation.

### Q: What did the NITI Aayog discussion paper of 2017 propose?

The NITI Aayog discussion paper of January 2017, written by Bibek Debroy and Kishore Desai as Analysis of Simultaneous Elections: The What, Why and How, proposed synchronisation in two windows within a five year cycle. The Lok Sabha and roughly half the assemblies would vote together, and the remaining assemblies would vote at the midpoint, about two and a half years later, on a rhythm compared in the paper to a midterm. The authors set out the expenditure of the staggered cycle, the governance cost of repeated Model Code periods, and the constitutional amendments a phased calendar required, and presented the two window design as a pragmatic route between full simultaneity and the rolling status quo.

### Q: What did the parliamentary standing committee recommend in 2015?

The Department Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, chaired by E. M. Sudarsana Natchiappan, recommended in its 79th Report of 17 December 2015 that simultaneous elections be introduced in two phases. In the first phase, assemblies whose terms ended within a defined window around a Lok Sabha election would be aligned to vote with it. In the second phase, the remaining assemblies would be aligned at the midpoint of the Lok Sabha term, so that significant elections would occur twice in five years rather than continuously. The report's phased approach became the template that the NITI Aayog paper of 2017 elaborated and that later designs adapted.

### Q: What is a constructive vote of no confidence?

A constructive vote of no confidence is a parliamentary device, drawn from Article 67 of the German Basic Law of 1949, under which a legislature can dismiss its government only by simultaneously electing a successor by majority vote. A purely negative majority cannot destroy a ministry and leave a vacuum. The Law Commission recommended the device for India in 1999 and again in its draft report of 2018, as a stabiliser for any system of fixed or synchronised terms, since it lets a House change its government without dissolving itself. The Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill of December 2024 did not include the device, an omission the committee examination of the Bill has repeatedly revisited.

### Q: Why does the design require a single electoral roll?

A single electoral roll is required in the design because synchronised and near synchronised elections across three tiers otherwise run on separate lists: the Election Commission prepares the rolls for Parliament and the assemblies under Article 325, while State Election Commissions prepare separate rolls for municipalities and panchayats under Articles 243K and 243ZA. Divergences between the lists can deny a voter one tier's ballot while allowing another. The Kovind Committee therefore proposed a single electoral roll and a single elector photo identity card, prepared by the Election Commission in consultation with the State Election Commissions through a new Article 324A, as its ratified second stage.

### Q: What did the Law Commission propose in its draft report of August 2018?

The Law Commission's draft report on simultaneous elections, released on 30 August 2018 under Justice B. S. Chauhan after an April working paper, held that simultaneous elections could be restored by amending the Constitution, the Representation of the People Act 1951, and the rules of procedure of the Lok Sabha and the assemblies. It proposed that a House elected after a premature dissolution serve only the unexpired remainder of the term, recommended the constructive vote of no confidence, suggested relaxing the anti-defection law's rigours for hung Houses, and proposed that the Prime Minister and Chief Ministers be elected by the whole House. Its amendment architecture anticipated the devices the 2024 Bill later carried.

### Q: What does the proposed Article 82A empower the Election Commission to do?

The proposed Article 82A obliges the Election Commission of India to conduct the general elections to the House of the People and all the legislative assemblies simultaneously once the appointed date has been notified. It also gives the Commission a measured discretion: where the Commission is of the opinion that the election to a particular legislative assembly cannot be held together with the general election, it may recommend to the President that an order be made declaring that the assembly election be held at a later date. An assembly elected under such a deferred poll still expires with the full term of the Lok Sabha, so the deferral bends the calendar without breaking the common expiry.

### Q: How does the Model Code of Conduct shape the efficiency argument?

The Model Code of Conduct shapes the efficiency argument because its restrictions on government announcements, transfers, and inaugurations apply in every state holding an election, and under the staggered calendar some part of India is under the code for a large part of most years, with committee documents citing an average in the region of two hundred to three hundred days annually. Each application freezes new policy announcements in the affected territory and diverts administrative attention. The efficiency position argues that synchronisation confines the code to one season in five years and returns long unimpeded stretches to governments at both levels. Critics reply that a synchronised season places the whole country under the code at once.

### Q: Why do critics say synchronised terms weaken accountability between elections?

Critics argue that a synchronised fixed calendar removes the occasions on which electorates hold governments to account between general elections. Under the staggered system, state verdicts function as midterm signals: the state elections of December 2018, which removed three incumbent state governments of the party ruling at the Centre, were read nationwide as a warning months before the 2019 general election and disciplined the campaign that followed. Under full synchronisation no comparable electoral reckoning occurs for five years, and a government that loses public confidence in its second year faces the voters only in its fifth. Defenders answer that accountability runs through the legislature continuously and that stability is itself a democratic good.

### Q: How would local body elections be synchronised within one hundred days?

Under the Kovind Committee's second stage, elections to municipalities and panchayats would be synchronised with the general elections to the Lok Sabha and the assemblies in such a way that the local polls are held within one hundred days of the general elections. The stage requires a new Article 324A, a single electoral roll and single elector photo identity card prepared by the Election Commission in consultation with the State Election Commissions, and ratification by the states, because local government is a state subject and the local rolls belong to the State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA. The 129th Amendment Bill covers only the first stage and leaves the local tier to later legislation.

### Q: What cost figures do supporters of simultaneous elections cite?

Supporters cite the official expenditure of conducting elections repeatedly under the staggered calendar, with estimates placed before successive committees putting a single Lok Sabha election cycle at approximately 4,500 crore rupees and assembly elections across a cycle adding an approximately equivalent sum, before counting security deployment, requisitioned staff time, and repeated Model Code disruption. They also cite the far larger unofficial economy of party and candidate spending, estimated by the Centre for Media Studies at roughly 60,000 crore rupees for the 2019 general election season. Against these figures they set the one time cost of additional voting machines and paper audit units that a synchronised poll requires, arguing that the consolidated exercise saves a substantial share of the duplicated overhead.
