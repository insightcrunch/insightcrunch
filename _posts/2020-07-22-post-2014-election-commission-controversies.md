The post-2014 Election Commission controversies are usually told as a string of unrelated quarrels, one schedule disputed here, one speech cleared there, one commissioner resigning somewhere else. Read in sequence and on the documentary record, the quarrels stop looking unrelated. Between October 2017 and December 2024 the Election Commission of India faced a schedule announcement in Gujarat that the opposition called partisan, a Karnataka poll date that reached the public before the Commission announced it, a general election in which a serving commissioner recorded dissents against clean chits given to the Prime Minister and the ruling party's president, a High Court that said the Commission's officers should probably face murder charges, a Constitution Bench judgment that rewrote how commissioners are chosen, a disclosure of political donations that the Commission had to be ordered twice to complete, and a Maharashtra counting dispute that ended with the Commission writing a long public rebuttal on Christmas Eve. This article argues one claim about that sequence. The post-2014 ECI controversies operate as a systemic stress pattern; [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/) operationalises across the long-arc trajectory. The claim is tested the only way the charter of this pillar allows, episode by episode, with each controversy recorded as a dated sequence of the event itself, the Commission's stated position or action on the record, the critics' stated charge in their own named words, and any court outcome stated by its holding.

## The post-2014 Election Commission controversies as a documented sequence

The inventory matters because the pattern claim stands or falls on the inventory being complete enough to test. The sequence opens with scheduling. On 12 October 2017 the Commission announced the Himachal Pradesh assembly election and deferred the Gujarat announcement to 25 October 2017, breaking the practice of announcing the two states together with a common counting day. In March 2018, during the Karnataka schedule announcement in New Delhi, the polling date of 12 May 2018 circulated on a television channel and on social media minutes before Chief Election Commissioner O. P. Rawat read it out. In May 2018 the Commission deferred polling in Bengaluru's Rajarajeshwari Nagar constituency after 9,746 electors photo identity cards were seized from a flat in the constituency. In December 2018, on polling day in Telangana, voters across the state found their names missing from the rolls, and the state's Chief Electoral Officer, Rajat Kumar, apologised on camera for what he conceded was a mistake.

The inventory also holds an early judicial reversal that set the decade's terms for how the Commission's quasi-judicial acts would be tested. In June 2015 a complaint had been filed alleging that 20 Aam Aadmi Party members of the Delhi assembly held an office of profit as parliamentary secretaries to ministers. The President referred the question to the Commission under Article 103, and on 19 January 2018 the Commission gave its opinion that the 20 members were liable to disqualification. President Ram Nath Kovind accepted the opinion, and the members stood disqualified on 21 January 2018. The members challenged the process in the Delhi High Court, arguing that the Commission had decided against them without the oral hearing that the principles of natural justice required on the facts it had to find. On 23 March 2018 a division bench of the High Court set the disqualification aside, holding that the Commission's opinion was vitiated by the failure to give the members a fair opportunity on the contested factual questions, and returned the matter to the Commission for a fresh hearing. The episode is the inventory's first instance of the form that recurs at its end: a Commission act of an adjudicatory kind, a challenge carried into court by the persons it hit, and a judgment that corrected the Commission on the procedure of its own decision-making rather than on any party's larger charge. It also fixed a public association, early in the period, between the Commission and a disqualification that had removed a fifth of an elected assembly at a stroke and then been undone, an association later episodes could cite without rehearsing the facts.

The sequence continues with adjudication inside the Commission itself. Through April and May 2019 the Commission decided a run of Model Code complaints against speeches by Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Bharatiya Janata Party president Amit Shah, clearing both men in case after case, and Election Commissioner Ashok Lavasa recorded dissents in five of those decisions. In May 2019 Lavasa wrote to Chief Election Commissioner Sunil Arora that he would keep away from Model Code proceedings until his minority decisions were recorded in the Commission's orders, and on 21 May 2019 the full Commission decided by majority that dissent in Model Code matters would not form part of the orders. In September 2019 the Income Tax Department issued notices to Lavasa's wife, Novel Singhal Lavasa, and newspapers reported scrutiny of companies connected with his son and daughter. On 15 July 2020 the Asian Development Bank announced Lavasa as a vice-president, and on 18 August 2020 he resigned from the Commission more than two years before his term would have ended.

The later entries in the inventory are judicial and electoral at once. On 26 February 2021 the Commission announced an eight-phase election for West Bengal, the longest schedule in the state's history, and Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee charged that the dates matched what the Bharatiya Janata Party had wanted. On 26 April 2021, hearing a petition about counting-day arrangements at Karur in Tamil Nadu, a Madras High Court bench led by Chief Justice Sanjib Banerjee said orally that the Commission was singularly responsible for the second wave of COVID-19 and that its officers should probably be put up on murder charges. On 6 May 2021 the Supreme Court refused the Commission's plea to expunge the remarks or to restrain the media from reporting them. On 2 March 2023 a five-judge Constitution Bench decided Anoop Baranwal v Union of India and prescribed a selection committee for commissioners that included the Chief Justice of India. Parliament answered with the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, which received presidential assent on 28 December 2023 and replaced the Chief Justice with a cabinet minister nominated by the Prime Minister. On 15 February 2024 a Constitution Bench struck down the electoral bond scheme and ordered disclosure of the donation record; the disclosure ran through March 2024 in two tranches after the Supreme Court rejected the State Bank of India's plea for more time and then its omission of the bonds' identifying numbers. On 9 March 2024 Election Commissioner Arun Goel resigned, days before the general election schedule was announced on 16 March 2024, and two new commissioners were appointed on 14 March 2024 under the contested Act while the Supreme Court declined to stay it. On 20 November 2024 Maharashtra voted, on 23 November 2024 the ruling Mahayuti alliance won 235 of 288 seats, and the defeated Congress carried a documented dispute about turnout figures and roll additions to the Commission, which answered with a detailed clarification dated 24 December 2024.

Three framings compete for this material. The first treats each entry as a discrete incident, a controversy of the week, closed when the news cycle closes. The second treats the entries as moves in a political contest, evidence arranged by each side against the other. The third, defended here, treats the entries as a pattern with shared features that can be named, mapped and checked against the record. Students and researchers who assemble the episode files from the Commission's press notes, orders and published data, including the slide decks that parties attach to their complaints, can open those decks in a browser through a [PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and keep the whole trail in one place. The pattern reading does not ask the reader to excuse any episode, and it does not ask the reader to condemn any episode twice. It asks a narrower question with a documentary answer. Do the controversies share features, do they aggregate into something larger than their sum, and do they continue a trajectory that begins long before 2014? The sections that follow take the episodes in order before the pattern is drawn.

### What power does the Election Commission use to fix an election schedule?

The Commission fixes schedules under Article 324 of the Constitution, which vests the superintendence, direction and control of elections in the Commission, read with the Representation of the People Act 1951, whose sections govern the notifications that set polling in motion. No statute prescribes the number of phases an election must have.

Nor does any statute say when two states must be announced together, so the schedule is the Commission's own administrative judgment, defended in public by reasons the Commission chooses to state, and it is that combination of complete discretion and stated reasons that the Gujarat and Bengal disputes put to work.

## Schedules as decisions: Gujarat in 2017 and Bengal in 2021

The Gujarat episode is the cleanest early test of the pattern because both sides stated their positions on the record within days. The event is dated. The Commission announced the Himachal Pradesh schedule on 12 October 2017, with polling on 9 November 2017, and said the Gujarat schedule would follow. It followed on 25 October 2017, with polling in two phases on 9 and 14 December 2017 and counting for both states together on 18 December 2017. The Commission's stated position came from Chief Election Commissioner Achal Kumar Joti, who had himself served as Gujarat's chief secretary earlier in his career, a biographical fact the opposition press noted at the time. Joti said the delinking was done because of the flood situation in Gujarat and for no other reason. He put the July 2017 flood toll at 229 lives across seven north Gujarat districts, said the Gujarat chief secretary had written twice asking that the announcement not be hurried because the same state employees who were carrying out relief and restoration would have to be diverted to election duty, and argued that announcing the election would have frozen restoration work under the Model Code while the officials were reassigned. He also said the Commission had kept the interval between announcement and polling within the roughly three-week discipline that a 2001 law ministry memorandum, noted by the Supreme Court, commended to it.

The critics' charge was stated in equally plain words. The Congress party's P. Chidambaram wrote that the Commission would be recalled from its extended holiday after the Gujarat government had announced all its concessions and freebies, and Congress spokesmen charged that the delay gave the state government and the Prime Minister a window, between the two announcements, in which projects could still be launched in Gujarat without breaching a code that was not yet in force there. The record shows the window was used as windows of that kind usually are. The Prime Minister visited Gujarat twice in the interval and launched projects reported at about Rs 3,600 crore, including the inauguration of the Ghogha to Dahej ferry service on 22 October 2017, none of which breached any rule, because the Model Code binds only from announcement. Joti rejected the charge of connivance directly, saying there was no question of connivance with anyone for the delay and that the charge that the Commission had given up its impartiality was not acceptable. No court was asked to rule on the Gujarat schedule, and none did. The episode therefore closes in the documentary form that recurs across the decade. A discretionary decision, stated reasons of an administrative kind, a partisan charge stated at its strongest by a named opponent, a denial on the record, and no adjudication, because schedule-making sits inside Article 324 discretion that courts are reluctant to invade mid-process.

The Bengal episode repeats the form with the polarities of the charge reversed in one respect and identical in another. On 26 February 2021 Chief Election Commissioner Sunil Arora announced elections to five assemblies. Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry would vote in a single phase on 6 April 2021, Assam in three phases, and West Bengal in eight phases running from 27 March to 29 April 2021, with counting everywhere on 2 May 2021. The Commission's stated position was security and sensitivity. Arora noted that Bengal would have one more phase than the seven of 2016, pointed to the deployment of central armed police forces across sensitive and vulnerable polling stations, and placed the schedule within the Commission's practice of phasing large and violence-marked elections so that forces can move from one region to the next. The critics' charge came from the incumbent chief minister whose state received the longest schedule. Mamata Banerjee said, with all due respect to the Commission, that questions were being raised on why the election would be held in so many phases in Bengal while other states voted in one phase, and she asked whether the dates had been announced according to the suggestions of the Prime Minister and the Home Minister, saying she had information from her sources that the poll dates were similar to the ones the Bharatiya Janata Party wanted. The Bharatiya Janata Party's Bengal leadership welcomed the eight phases as necessary for a fair election in a state whose local administration it accused of partisanship. The Commission did not alter the schedule, did not shorten it when the second wave of the pandemic built through April 2021, and completed all eight phases, after which the Trinamool Congress won 213 seats and formed the government for a third term.

The Gujarat precedent also entered the Commission's own later practice, which is aggregation working inside the institution. In October 2022, announcing the next round of assembly elections, the Commission again announced Himachal Pradesh first, on 14 October 2022, and Gujarat separately afterwards, on 3 November 2022. Asked why the two states had not been announced together, Chief Election Commissioner Rajiv Kumar answered that the Commission goes by the convention in announcing schedules and had gone by the convention last followed, meaning the delinked convention of 2017, and he added that the Model Code period had in fact been shortened against both 2017 and 2012. The opposition's 2022 charge repeated the 2017 charge in substance: that announcing Gujarat later gave the incumbent government more time outside the code. An episode that the incident framing would file as closed in December 2017 had become, five years on, the Commission's cited convention and the opposition's cited grievance in the same press cycle.

Two features of the pattern are visible already in the scheduling pair. The first is that the contested object is never the result. Nobody alleged that Gujarat voted wrongly in December 2017 or that Bengal's count was falsified in May 2021. The contested object is a discretionary institutional act, the timing and phasing of an election, which the Constitution leaves to the Commission and which therefore cannot be settled by pointing to a rule the Commission broke. The second is that the charge and the defence both had to be conducted in public statements, because no forum existed in which the charge could be tried. A schedule announcement is not an order appealable anywhere. The controversies over schedules are thus controversies about the Commission's judgment and motives, carried on through press conferences and reported speech, and they recur in 2017 and 2021 with different parties holding the centre and the state, which is the first sign that the pattern is a property of the institution's position rather than of any single government's term.

## Karnataka 2018: the date that arrived before the announcement

The Karnataka episode of 27 March 2018 is the decade's purest case of a controversy about the Commission's custody of its own information. The Commission had called a press conference in New Delhi for 11 a.m. to announce the Karnataka assembly schedule. Chief Election Commissioner O. P. Rawat spent the first part of the conference on preparedness, facilities and the paper trail before turning to the dates. At 11.06 a.m., by the account the Bharatiya Janata Party later filed with the Commission, an English news channel broadcast the schedule: polling on 12 May 2018, counting on 18 May 2018. At 11.08 a.m. Amit Malviya, the head of the Bharatiya Janata Party's information technology department, tweeted the same two dates, and a Karnataka Congress social media functionary, Srivatsa, tweeted them at about the same time from the same broadcast. When reporters put the tweet to Rawat mid-conference, his first recorded response treated the dates as conjecture that nobody could stop. Minutes later, announcing the schedule himself, Rawat confirmed polling on 12 May 2018 but counting on 15 May 2018, which meant the leaked pair was half right and half wrong. His second recorded response was different in kind. He called the leak a very serious issue, said the Commission would probe it, and promised stringent action, legally and administratively.

The Commission's institutional action followed the same day. Its press statement recorded that the tweets had been brought to its notice, noted the error in the counting date, and constituted a committee of senior officers, under Senior Deputy Election Commissioner Umesh Sinha, to investigate and report within seven days and to suggest steps against recurrence. The committee sought information from the media organisations and individuals concerned. Malviya filed a written explanation with screenshots and timestamps, attributing his tweet to the channel's broadcast and pointing out that the Congress functionary had tweeted the identical dates at the identical minute. The Commission's follow-through then produced the episode's second controversy. Its inquiry order did not name Malviya for questioning, and the Congress charged that the Bharatiya Janata Party's officer had been absolved before any probe while the Congress functionary faced the committee. The Congress spokesman Randeep Singh Surjewala had already put the party's charge in its strongest form on the day itself, writing that the Bharatiya Janata Party had become the super Election Commission, that the credibility of the Commission was on test, and demanding to know whether the Commission would issue notice to the party president and register a first information report against its information technology head for leaking confidential information. The Bharatiya Janata Party's stated position, delivered by a delegation led by Union minister Mukhtar Abbas Naqvi, was that the tweet rested on the channel's report, that Malviya should not have tweeted, and that there was no intention to undermine the Commission's authority. No prosecution followed, no finding of a leak from inside the Commission was published, and the episode's documentary residue is the press statement, the committee's constitution, the written explanation and the competing charges.

Six weeks later Karnataka produced the inventory's second 2018 entry, this one about the Commission's coercive powers rather than its information. On the night of 8 May 2018, officials seized 9,746 electors photo identity cards, along with laptops, a printer and counterfoils of overseas elector forms, from a flat in Jalahalli in the Rajarajeshwari Nagar constituency of Bengaluru, four days before polling. The Bharatiya Janata Party demanded that the election in the constituency be countermanded and accused the Congress candidate, Munirathna, of assembling the cards to rig the poll. The Congress answered that the flat belonged to a person linked to the Bharatiya Janata Party and that the seizure was a staged drama. The Commission sent the state's chief electoral officer and the Bengaluru police commissioner to report, despatched a deputy election commissioner to examine the facts, and on 11 May 2018 deferred the poll in the constituency to 28 May 2018, with counting on 31 May 2018. Its order, issued under Article 324 and the Representation of the People Act 1951, recorded the deputy election commissioner's conclusion that there appeared to be a planned design to induce voters, thus vitiating the poll process. The deferral was an exercise of the plenary power the Supreme Court had recognised in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner in 1978, and it was the kind of assertive intervention that an earlier generation of commentary treated as the Commission's signature. In the pattern reading, the two Karnataka entries belong together. In the first, the Commission appeared unable to keep its own secret for the length of a press conference and then declined to publish a finding. In the second, it acted within three days on a field report and stopped an election. The stress pattern is not a story of an institution that always fails or always bends. It is a story about which of the Commission's acts attract trust and which attract suspicion, and about how uneven that distribution became.

## The Model Code in 2019: complaints, sanctions and clean chits

The 2019 general election, held in seven phases from 11 April to 19 May 2019, generated the decade's densest run of Commission adjudications, and it is the episode on which the pattern claim most depends, because here the record includes the Commission's own internal division. The background is the Model Code of Conduct, the consensual code whose architecture and limits are treated in [the soft-law-architecture-and-limits framework](/2020/07/06/model-code-conduct-soft-law-india/). During the 2019 campaign the Commission worked under its own advisories of 9 and 19 March 2019, which told parties and candidates not to display photographs of defence personnel and not to refer to the activities of the armed forces in seeking votes, advisories issued after the February 2019 Pulwama attack and the Balakot air strike had entered campaign speech. The Congress filed a series of complaints against speeches by the Prime Minister and by Amit Shah. Three of the Prime Minister's speeches carried the argument. At Wardha on 1 April 2019, Modi attacked Rahul Gandhi for choosing to contest from Wayanad in Kerala, in terms the Congress complaint described as an appeal framed around the constituency's religious composition. At Latur on 9 April 2019, Modi asked first-time voters to dedicate their votes to the soldiers who carried out the Balakot air strike and to the martyrs of Pulwama. At Patan on 21 April 2019, he spoke of having kept Pakistan on its toes until Wing Commander Abhinandan Varthaman, captured after the 27 February 2019 aerial engagement, was released. Complaints against Shah included his Nagpur speech, in which the Congress alleged he had equated the election with a religious contest.

Two further decisions of the same weeks belong in the record because the critics' asymmetry charge has to be tested against them. On 10 April 2019, a day before polling opened, the Commission invoked Article 324 to stop the release of a biographical film on the Prime Minister, PM Narendra Modi, holding that biographical material on a candidate or leader, certified for display during the election period, had the effect of disturbing the level playing field, and the film was released only after the election ended. Through April 2019 it also dealt with NaMo TV, a channel that had appeared on direct-to-home platforms carrying the Prime Minister's campaign content without the certification the code requires, and it ruled that the channel's content was subject to the code's certification and silence-period provisions, after which the channel was removed from the platforms. The Commission was therefore willing, in the same campaign, to act against vehicles of the ruling party's publicity that it found outside the code. The critics' charge, to be stated precisely, is not that the Commission never acted against the ruling side. It is that its boldest actions were directed at films and channels while its slowest and most divided decisions concerned the speeches of the two men at the top of that side's campaign.

Concern about the Commission's standing was also placed on a formal record before the clean chits, from outside party politics. On 8 April 2019 a group of 66 retired civil servants, diplomats and police officers wrote to President Ram Nath Kovind expressing concern at what they described as the Commission's perceived partisanship and its handling of complaints and of machine verification, and asking the President to act to restore confidence in the institution. The Commission's officers responded in the press that the institution's record spoke for itself. The letter matters to the pattern reading because of its authors and its date: a body of retired officials with no election to fight recorded, before the first vote of 2019 was cast, the same aggregation argument this article makes, that the episodes were being read by the public as a set and that the set was costing the Commission the presumption of neutrality on which its authority rests.

The Commission's decisions came in a cluster at the end of April and the start of May, after the Supreme Court, hearing a petition by Congress parliamentarian Sushmita Dev, set 6 May 2019 as the deadline for deciding the pending complaints against the Prime Minister and the party president. On 30 April 2019 the Commission held that the Wardha speech did not violate the Model Code or its advisories. On 1 May 2019 it reached the same conclusion on the Latur speech. On 3 May 2019 it cleared Shah in two cases, and on 5 May 2019 it cleared the Prime Minister in the Patan case, bringing the clearances of the Prime Minister to six cases in that week. The formula in the orders was that the Commission, in its considered view, found no violation of the code or the advisories in the matter complained of. What the published orders did not show, and what reporting established within days, was that the decisions were not unanimous. Lavasa had dissented in the Wardha and Latur decisions and in three further decisions in the same run, five dissents in all, four on speeches by the Prime Minister and one on a speech by Shah, and the decisions had been carried by a two to one majority of Arora and Sushil Chandra over his dissent.

The same Commission, in the same weeks, sanctioned speakers from several parties, and the pattern reading requires that record to be stated with the same care. On 15 April 2019 the Commission barred Uttar Pradesh chief minister Yogi Adityanath from campaigning for 72 hours and Bahujan Samaj Party leader Mayawati for 48 hours over speeches each had made that the Commission found in breach of the code's provisions on religious appeals. It barred Samajwadi Party leader Azam Khan for 72 hours in the same round and Union minister Maneka Gandhi for 48 hours days later. It censured other speakers and issued notices across parties through the campaign. The critics' charge was therefore not that the Commission enforced nothing. The charge, stated by Congress spokesmen and by former officials who wrote in the press, was asymmetry at the top: that the enforcement ladder reached chief ministers, former chief ministers and ministers, but stopped at the two men whose speeches drew the largest audiences, and that the Commission took weeks to decide those complaints while deciding others in days. The Commission's stated position was institutional rather than argumentative. Arora said publicly that the members of the Commission were not expected to be templates or clones of each other, defended majority decision-making as the prescribed method under the Commission's transaction of business rules, and declined to engage the merits of the dissents in public. The merits question, whether the Latur appeal to first-time voters crossed the March advisories, was never answered by any court, because Model Code decisions of this kind are treated as closed once the election ends. [The post-2019 election political-architectural context](/2019/08/16/2019-election-verdict-explained/) supplies the verdict within which these decisions sat: a second consecutive single-party majority, won while the Commission's internal disagreement was still unpublished.

### How does the Election Commission record a decision when its members disagree?

The Commission transacts business by majority under its own rules, and its orders issue in the Commission's name alone. In quasi-judicial matters such as party symbol disputes, a member's separate opinion can accompany the order and enter the public record.

In Model Code matters the full Commission decided on 21 May 2019, by majority over a recorded dissent on the procedural question itself, that minority views would not form part of the final orders, which is why the 2019 clean chit orders read as unanimous.

## Ashok Lavasa and the architecture of a dissent

Ashok Lavasa arrived at the Commission by the ordinary route and left it by an extraordinary one, and both facts belong to the record. A retired Indian Administrative Service officer of the 1980 batch from the Haryana cadre, he had been finance secretary, environment secretary and civil aviation secretary in the Union government before the President appointed him Election Commissioner on the government's advice; he took office on 23 January 2018, joining a Commission headed first by O. P. Rawat and then, from December 2018, by Sunil Arora. By seniority and the convention of succession, he stood next in line for the chief election commissionership when Arora's term ended in April 2021, and his own term ran to October 2022, when he would have retired as Chief Election Commissioner. Nothing in his appointment or his first year marked him out from the institution's long line of administrator-commissioners, the line that runs from [the founding-template architecture](/2020/06/08/sukumar-sen-1951-1952-election/) through the assertive years treated in [the assertive-commissioner-architectural-shift](/2020/06/14/tn-seshan-eci-reforms-india/).

The dissent sequence is documented in his own words and in the Commission's procedural decisions. Through late April and early May 2019, as the clean chit decisions were taken, Lavasa recorded minority decisions in five cases. When the orders issued without them, he wrote to Arora. The letter's contents entered the public record on 17 and 18 May 2019, two days before the final phase of polling on 19 May 2019. In the sentence most often quoted from it, he wrote that minority decisions recorded by him in several cases continued to be suppressed in a manner contrary to well-established conventions observed by multi-member statutory bodies. He wrote that it appeared futile for him to participate in the deliberations of the Commission until its lawful functioning was restored in terms of including the minority decisions recorded by him, and that in the present circumstances he was left with no option but to keep away from such proceedings. He accordingly recused himself from Model Code meetings from the first week of May 2019. Arora's recorded response denied any controversy in the Commission's internal functioning and repeated the line that the three members were not expected to be templates or clones of each other. On 21 May 2019, two days after polling closed, the full Commission met on the procedural question and decided by majority that dissent notes in Model Code violation cases would not be recorded in the Commission's orders, Lavasa dissenting on that question too. When a Right to Information applicant later sought the dissent notes, the Commission refused disclosure, citing the exemption for material whose release could endanger the safety of persons or identify sources who had assisted in confidence, pointing to the field officers' inputs inside the files. The Central Information Commission, in an order of Chief Information Commissioner Bimal Julka, upheld the refusal and held that no further intervention was required.

The second half of the Lavasa sequence is documented in the acts of other state agencies and in his own exit, and the attribution discipline this article follows matters most here. In September 2019 the Income Tax Department issued notices to Novel Singhal Lavasa, the commissioner's wife, concerning her tax filings for earlier assessment years, and newspapers reported that companies associated with his son and his daughter had also come under official scrutiny for alleged non-declaration of income and disproportionate assets. The family denied wrongdoing in their responses to the notices. No agency stated any connection between the tax proceedings and the dissents, and no document in the public record establishes one. The critics' charge of a connection was stated in print by opposition politicians and by commentators who described the timing, months after the dissents became public, as retaliatory in character; the charge is recorded here as a charge, with its evidentiary status stated plainly, because the pattern reading depends on not confusing sequence with proof. On 15 July 2020 the Asian Development Bank announced that Lavasa would join it as Vice-President for Private Sector Operations and Public-Private Partnerships, an appointment the bank's statement grounded in his experience of public-private partnerships and infrastructure, and which reporting at the time said had been finalised with the concurrence of the Union government, as India's nominee appointments to the bank ordinarily are. On 18 August 2020 he submitted his resignation to President Ram Nath Kovind and asked to be relieved on 31 August 2020, surrendering the chief election commissionership he would otherwise have assumed the following April. His exit was the second premature departure in the Commission's history; the first was Chief Election Commissioner Nagender Singh, who resigned in 1973 to become a judge of the International Court of Justice.

What the Lavasa sequence contributes to the pattern is architectural rather than personal. A three-member Commission decides by majority, and majority decision-making is the design, not a defect in it. The stress point the episode exposed was narrower and more durable: the design had never settled whether a minority decision in an adjudicatory matter is part of the Commission's order or an internal input that the order may silently absorb. In symbol disputes between party factions, the Commission's practice had long accommodated separate opinions on the record. In Model Code adjudication, the 21 May 2019 decision chose absorption, and the institution thereby made its most contested orders of the decade read as unanimous at the moment they were least so. The dissent question also outlived the man. When the appointment of commissioners itself became the contested question after 2023, the procedural issue of how disagreement inside the Commission is recorded returned in a new form, on the selection side rather than the adjudication side, and it is treated in that setting below.

## Rolls and machines: the evidentiary controversies of 2018 to 2024

A second family of controversies in the inventory concerns the evidentiary base of elections, the roll and the machine, and its documentary form differs from the adjudication cases. Here the Commission's acts are administrative, the critics' charges are about competence and design rather than motive, and the corrective, when it comes, often comes from a court. The Telangana episode is the family's largest entry. Telangana voted for its assembly on 7 December 2018. Through the day, voters in Hyderabad and across the state reported at polling stations that their names, present in earlier elections, were missing from the rolls. That evening the state's Chief Electoral Officer, Rajat Kumar, apologised publicly and conceded that a mistake had been made; he said the Commission would review the matter comprehensively and revise the rolls aggressively before the 2019 general election. Kumar had earlier conceded that about 22 lakh names had been removed from the state's rolls between the 2.83 crore electors recorded before September 2018 and the 2.61 crore in the draft rolls of 10 September 2018, a reduction he attributed to the deletion of duplicates, the dead and the shifted, carried out under the National Electoral Roll Purification and Authentication Programme of 2015, which had matched rolls against Aadhaar data until the Supreme Court stopped the seeding in August 2015. A public interest petition filed by the Hyderabad researcher Srinivas Kodali alleged that deletions in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh had been made by software without the notice and hearing the rules require. The Commission's later clarification stated that deletions and inclusions follow the enrolment and revision process, that objections are invited before deletions, and that the final rolls used on polling day, published on 19 November 2018 with a supplementary list, contained 2.806 crore electors after about 26 lakh enrolments during the revision. The two accounts, a chief electoral officer's apology and concession on polling day and a procedural clarification afterwards, are both on the record, and the space between them is where the controversy lived: not whether names were deleted, which nobody disputed, but whether a deletion drive conducted at that scale, by matching databases, could carry the individual notice that the law of enrolment promises each elector.

The roll problem also recurred inside the window. On 11 April 2019, the first day of the general election, voters in Hyderabad reported the Telangana experience repeating itself, names missing at stations where they had voted for years, and the reporting of that day connected the deletions to the same purification programme by name. The Srinivas Kodali petition's allegation, that deletions on this scale had been executed by software matching without the individual process the rules prescribe, was therefore not an argument about one state's housekeeping. It named a method the Commission had run nationally in 2015 and whose consequences surfaced at polling stations in two elections five months apart, in a state election the Commission supervised and in the general election that followed.

The machine controversies run on a parallel track, and [the technological-architectural-trajectory](/2020/06/19/evm-vvpat-story-explained-india/) carries their full history. Two court outcomes bound the post-2014 phase of the argument. In the general election of 2019, opposition parties demanded that the paper slips of the voter verifiable paper audit trail be counted for half the polling stations in each assembly segment and that the count prevail over the electronic count in case of mismatch. On 8 April 2019 the Supreme Court raised the physical verification from one polling station to five polling stations per assembly constituency or segment, and declined the larger demand. On 7 May 2019 the Court dismissed a review plea seeking a further increase to half the stations. The Commission conducted the 2019 count on that footing, and the argument resumed through the next cycle. On 26 April 2024, in Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India, the Supreme Court rejected the pleas for a return to paper ballots and for counting every paper slip, and held, as the operative directions of the judgment, that the symbol loading units used to load candidate symbols onto the machines be sealed and stored after the loading process, and that candidates placed second or third in a constituency could seek verification of the burned memory of the microcontrollers in five percent of the machines per assembly segment, on a written request made within the period the judgment prescribed. The judgment is the family's clearest instance of the documentary form stated at the outset: a charge about the machine's integrity, the Commission's stated position that the machines are stand-alone and tamper-proof and that the audit trail already provides verification, and a court outcome that adjusted the verification protocol without accepting the charge. Between the two judgments the Commission also faced repeated demands, after state results that surprised the losers, for the release of booth-level turnout data in final form faster than its aggregation process produced it, a demand that returned in Maharashtra in 2024 in the form treated below.

### What is Form 17C and why does it matter in a counting dispute?

Form 17C is the statutory record of the votes cast at a polling station, prepared by the presiding officer at the close of poll and handed to the polling agents of the candidates present, station by station, before the materials are sealed.

Because each station's account of votes polled is fixed in copies held by rival camps before counting begins, the Commission's stated position in turnout disputes is that the final figures cannot be altered afterwards without the alteration showing against the agents' copies.

## A High Court, a pandemic and the limits of rebuke

The Bengal schedule controversy of February 2021 acquired its second act in April 2021, in a courtroom in Chennai rather than Kolkata, and the act is the inventory's sharpest instance of a constitutional institution rebuking the Commission in terms the Commission could not answer in kind. The setting was the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, which built through the very weeks in which Bengal was voting in phases and four other assemblies completed their polls. The Commission had issued COVID protocols for the 2021 round, governing rally sizes, roadshow permissions and counting-day arrangements, and it had banned victory processions for counting day, 2 May 2021, by an order of 27 April 2021 as the wave peaked. On 26 April 2021, a division bench of the Madras High Court led by Chief Justice Sanjib Banerjee, with Justice Senthilkumar Ramamoorthy, heard a petition by the Tamil Nadu transport minister R. Vijayabhaskar about arrangements for counting at the Karur constituency. In the hearing, as reported by every major news organisation from the courtroom, the Chief Justice said that the Commission was the institution singularly responsible for the second wave, that no action had been taken against political parties holding rallies despite the court's orders, and that the Commission should probably be put up on murder charges. The remarks were oral observations made in the course of the hearing. They did not appear in the bench's written order, which dealt with the counting arrangements sought by the petitioner.

The Madras remarks were the sharpest, but they were not the only judicial words of that month. In Kolkata, a Calcutta High Court bench led by Chief Justice Thottathil B. Radhakrishnan, hearing petitions about campaigning in Bengal during the wave, recorded in the same weeks its dissatisfaction with the enforcement of the Commission's own COVID circulars on the ground and called for reports on compliance from the Commission and the state's chief electoral officer. The two courts spoke in different registers, one in oral remarks that never entered an order and one in recorded directions, and the difference between the registers became the legal question of the episode.

The Commission's response was procedural and documented. It first applied to the Madras High Court itself, asking that media reports be confined to observations recorded in orders and judgments and that oral observations made during hearings not be reported. The High Court declined to entertain that application. The Commission then appealed to the Supreme Court, arguing that the remarks were baseless, had tarnished the image of an independent constitutional authority, and would demoralise the officials conducting elections across five states and a union territory. The Supreme Court heard the appeal on 3 May 2021 and decided it on 6 May 2021, in a judgment by a bench of Justices D. Y. Chandrachud and M. R. Shah in Election Commission of India v M. R. Vijayabhaskar. The holdings, stated from the judgment, are these. The Court refused to expunge the remarks, holding that a formal opinion of a judicial institution is reflected through its judgments and orders and not its oral observations during a hearing, so that oral remarks are not part of the official record and the question of expunging them does not arise. It refused to restrain the media from reporting court proceedings, holding that the freedom of speech and expression covers the reporting of what is said in open court and that such reporting holds the judiciary itself accountable. It recorded that the High Court's remarks were harsh and the metaphor improper, that the High Court had not sought to attribute culpability for the spread of the infection to the Commission, and that a degree of caution and circumspection by the High Court would have allayed a grievance of the kind the Commission had urged. And it closed by recording the Commission's track record as an independent constitutional body carrying a significant burden in securing electoral democracy, expressing the hope that the matter could rest with a sense of balance.

The episode's place in the pattern is precise. The charge against the Commission, that it had permitted mass campaigning through a lethal wave in the name of completing a schedule it had itself fixed, was stated by a constitutional court in the strongest language any court used about the Commission in the decade. The Commission's defence, that its protocols existed and that the schedule predated the wave's peak, was never adjudicated on the merits, because the Supreme Court's judgment was about the status of oral remarks and the rights of the press, not about whether the Commission had enforced its protocols well. The outcome therefore neither vindicated nor condemned the Commission's conduct of the 2021 round. What it settled was the terms on which the Commission can be publicly rebuked by a coordinate institution: freely, in open court, in language the Supreme Court itself found harsh, with no remedy available to the Commission beyond the balance the same judgment chose to record. An institution whose authority rests on being seen to stand above the contest found, in May 2021, that its reputation was a subject on which other institutions could speak without its leave and without appeal.

### What happens when a court criticises the Election Commission during an election?

The criticism stands in the public record even when it never enters a written order. In May 2021 the Supreme Court held that oral observations in a hearing are not part of the official record, cannot be expunged, and may be reported freely by the media covering open court.

The Commission's remedies are therefore political and institutional rather than legal: compliance statements, protocol orders and the closing words of the same judgment that refused it relief.

## Appointments: from Anoop Baranwal to the Act of 2023

Every controversy treated so far concerns what the Commission did. The appointment controversy concerns who chooses the people who do it, and it is the inventory's one entry in which the decisive acts belong to a court and a Parliament rather than to the Commission itself. The constitutional text had left the question open since 1950. Article 324(2) makes the appointment of the Chief Election Commissioner and the Election Commissioners subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament, and for over seven decades Parliament made no such law, so appointments were made by the President on the advice of the government of the day. The asymmetry inside the institution that this produced, protected tenure at the top and executive-chosen membership beneath it, is the subject of [the architectural-asymmetry-question framework](/2020/06/30/tenure-security-election-commission/). The design the Court eventually prescribed had been on the table for three decades. The Dinesh Goswami Committee on electoral reforms had recommended in 1990 that appointments be made after consultation beyond the executive, and the Law Commission's 255th report of March 2015 recommended a selection committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition and the Chief Justice of India, the same composition the Constitution Bench would adopt in 2023. Successive governments left both recommendations unimplemented, which is why the question reached the Court as a vacuum to be filled rather than a statute to be reviewed. The litigation that closed the vacuum began as a public interest petition by the advocate Anoop Baranwal and others seeking an appointment process insulated from executive control. On 2 March 2023 a five-judge Constitution Bench, in a unanimous judgment delivered by Justice K. M. Joseph, held that until Parliament enacted a law, appointments would be made by the President on the advice of a committee consisting of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha or the leader of the largest opposition party there, and the Chief Justice of India. The Court grounded the direction in the Constituent Assembly's expectation of an independent commission and in the premise that a commission chosen by the executive alone could not be structurally independent of it.

Parliament enacted the law within the year, and the law's design became the controversy. The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act was passed by Parliament in December 2023 and received the President's assent on 28 December 2023, coming into force on 2 January 2024. Its selection committee consists of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and a Union cabinet minister nominated by the Prime Minister, which is to say it replaced the Chief Justice of India in the Court's interim design with a second member of the executive side of the table. A search committee headed by the law minister prepares a panel of names for the selection committee, and the Act provides that a selection is not invalid for a vacancy in the committee. Petitions challenging the Act were filed by, among others, the Congress leader Jaya Thakur and the Association for Democratic Reforms, contending that the substitution destroyed the balance the Constitution Bench had designed and returned appointments to executive dominance. The procedural state of that litigation, which is the form in which this article is required to state it, is as follows. The petitions came before the Supreme Court in January 2024; a bench of Justices Sanjiv Khanna and Dipankar Datta issued notice on 12 January 2024 and orally declined to stay the Act's operation. The question of a stay became concrete in March 2024. On 9 March 2024 Election Commissioner Arun Goel resigned, a little over a year into a term that ran to December 2027 and days before the announcement of the general election, giving personal reasons as his stated ground; media reporting at the time recorded speculation about differences within the Commission, which neither Goel nor the Commission confirmed. Two vacancies then stood open. The selection committee met on 14 March 2024 and the President appointed Gyanesh Kumar and Sukhbir Singh Sandhu as Election Commissioners the same day; they took office on 15 March 2024. The Leader of the Opposition in the selection committee, Adhir Ranjan Chowdhury, recorded his dissent in writing and said publicly that he had been given a shortlist of six names at the meeting itself, from a search process whose panel of over two hundred names he had received the previous night, and that the procedure reduced the opposition's role to presence without choice. On 15 March and again on 21 March 2024 the Supreme Court heard the stay applications against the Act and the new appointments and declined interim relief, holding the balance against unsettling appointments already made on the eve of a general election, and posted the challenges for hearing on their merits. The general election schedule was announced on 16 March 2024, polling ran from 19 April to 1 June 2024, and the challenges to the Act remained pending, undecided on their merits, through the period this article covers.

The appointment sequence contributes a feature the earlier episodes lack. In the schedule, code and roll controversies, the Commission acted and others judged. Here the Commission is the object of the act: its composition was redesigned by a judgment, redesigned again by a statute that answered the judgment, and staffed under the statute over a recorded opposition dissent, while the constitutional challenge to the redesign waited for a hearing. The stress in this entry is not on the Commission's conduct but on the chain of custody of the Commission itself, and it connects backward to everything before it, because the authority with which a commission announces schedules, signs clean chits and answers High Courts depends in the end on how it came to be composed. That dependence is the reason the pillar treats appointment law here, in a controversies article, rather than only in the tenure article: after March 2024, every act of the Commission in the general election that followed was performed by a body whose manner of composition was itself under constitutional challenge.

## 2024: the disclosure the Commission had to complete twice, and the Maharashtra count

The electoral bond sequence placed the Commission in a position it had not occupied in the earlier episodes: custodian of a record it had not created, publishing under judicial direction a dataset whose completeness became the controversy. The background belongs to [the anonymity-architecture-tested-and-struck-down framework](/2020/06/25/electoral-bonds-political-finance-india/). On 15 February 2024 the Constitution Bench in Association for Democratic Reforms v Union of India struck down the electoral bond scheme of 2018 as violative of the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a), ordered the State Bank of India to stop issuing bonds, and directed the bank to submit to the Commission, by 6 March 2024, the details of bonds purchased since the Court's interim order of 12 April 2019, with the Commission to publish the information on its website within a week of receiving it. The bank asked for time until 30 June 2024, pleading the labour of matching donors to recipients from data held in separate silos. On 11 March 2024 the Court dismissed the application, holding that the bank's plea was unsustainable on the record of its own systems, and directed submission by 12 March 2024. The bank submitted data on 12 March 2024 and the Commission published it on 14 March 2024. What the first tranche lacked was the unique alphanumeric number printed on each bond, the very field that connects a purchaser to a redeeming party. On 15 March 2024 the Court issued notice on the omission, and on 18 March 2024 it directed the bank to disclose all details in its possession, including the alphanumeric numbers, observing that the bank could not be selective in its disclosure, and required the bank's chairman to file an affidavit by 21 March 2024 confirming that nothing had been withheld. The bank complied on 21 March 2024, the Commission published the completed record, and analysts in the press and in civil society organisations such as the Association for Democratic Reforms spent the following weeks matching purchasers to parties in public, doing in the open the matching the bank had said would take until June. The completed record, as the Association for Democratic Reforms documented from the Commission's own published files, showed redemptions running to over Rs 12,000 crore across the disclosure window, with the Bharatiya Janata Party the largest recipient, followed by the Trinamool Congress and the Congress, and it allowed individual purchasers to be connected to individual parties for the first time since the scheme began in 2018. The Commission also published, after obtaining their return from the Court's registry, the sealed-cover filings that parties had made with it under the interim order of 12 April 2019, so that the disclosure the judgment ordered was completed from the Commission's own custody as well as the bank's.

The Commission's own part in the sequence was narrow, and its narrowness is the point the pattern reading draws from the episode. The Commission had administered the bond scheme's disclosure end for six years under the law as it stood, had put on the Court's record during the litigation its own letter of 26 May 2017 to the law ministry flagging the scheme's implications for transparency in political funding, and in 2024 it published what it was given, when it was given, including an incomplete first tranche whose missing field was identified in court by the petitioners rather than flagged by the publisher. The critics' charge, stated by the petitioners and by commentators including the constitutional lawyer Suhrith Parthasarathy in his essays on the case, was that the disclosure had to be extracted in stages from institutions that complied at the minimum each order allowed. The counter-record is also documentary: the Commission sought and obtained from the Court the return of the sealed documents it had itself filed under the 2019 interim order, digitised them, and published the fuller record, and no court found the Commission in default of any direction. The episode therefore shows the stress pattern operating on the transparency function itself. The institution charged with making political money visible became fully transparent in 2024 only at the pace of judicial orders addressed to a bank.

The general election of April to June 2024, conducted by the Commission as reconstituted in March, generated its own entries in the same documentary form. On 18 March 2024 the Commission ordered the removal of the home secretaries of six states and of the director general of police of West Bengal, Rajeev Kumar, an exercise of its administrative authority over election machinery that state governments protested and the Commission defended as necessary to a level playing field. During the campaign it issued notices and censures across parties under the Model Code, and it also became a litigant over its own data. The Association for Democratic Reforms petitioned the Supreme Court during polling for a direction that the Commission publish, within 48 hours of each phase, the absolute numbers of votes recorded at each polling station from Form 17C, arguing that the rising gap between the Commission's polling-day turnout percentages and its later figures was feeding suspicion. The Commission's affidavit resisted the plea, arguing that Form 17C is already given to candidates' agents at the close of poll, that releasing scanned copies of the handwritten forms mid-election invited morphing and confusion, and that the statutory scheme assigns the final figures to the counting process. On 24 May 2024 a vacation bench of the Supreme Court declined to issue the direction while the election was in progress, holding that the plea could be taken up with the pending petitions after the election. The turnout-data question thus travelled the full circuit of the pattern within one season: administrative act, public charge, litigation, and a procedural outcome that left the underlying design question open.

Maharashtra closed the inventory nine months later, and it closed it in the documentary form of 2017 rather than of 2019: a charge, a rebuttal, and no adjudication. Maharashtra voted on 20 November 2024 in a single phase and the results of 23 November 2024 gave the Mahayuti alliance of the Bharatiya Janata Party, the Shiv Sena and the Nationalist Congress Party 235 of the assembly's 288 seats, against 46 for the opposition Maha Vikas Aghadi, a result whose scale surprised the losers and much of the commentary. The Congress's documented case, filed with the Commission in a memorandum of 29 November 2024, had two limbs. The first was turnout arithmetic: the Commission's own releases put turnout at about 58.22 percent at 5 p.m. on polling day and the final figure at about 66 percent, and the party asked how roughly 76 lakh additional votes had materialised in the closing hours and in the aggregation that followed, pointing also to differences between votes polled and votes counted in a set of constituencies. The second limb was the rolls: the party alleged arbitrary additions, pointing to constituencies where the elector count had risen by about 50,000 between July and November 2024 and noting that most such seats had gone to the ruling alliance. The Commission answered in stages. An interim response invited the party to a meeting, held on 3 December 2024, and a detailed clarification followed on 24 December 2024. Its stated position, from the clarification, is that comparison between the 5 p.m. figure and the final figure is a misconception: the 5 p.m. figure is a running estimate assembled under procedural pressure at the close of poll, the press note issued around 11.45 p.m. on polling night is an additional disclosure introduced during the 2024 general election, and the final turnout is compiled from Form 17C, the statutory account of votes cast that presiding officers hand to candidates' agents at the close of poll, against which no later alteration can pass undetected. On the rolls, the clarification said only six constituencies had recorded increases above 50,000 electors in the period, that additions and deletions had followed the due process of claims and objections in which the parties themselves participate, and that there had been no arbitrary addition or deletion. The clarification also took up the differences the memorandum had listed between votes polled and votes counted in individual constituencies, and described them as bona fide but inconsequential, arising from the structure of the count itself: the station accounts record the votes cast on the machines, while the counting totals add postal ballots and are compiled at a later stage of the same process, so small divergences between the two series are a feature of the paperwork rather than evidence of interference with it. The Commission closed the document by repeating that candidates and their agents are present at every stage of the process, from the sealing of machines before polling to the declaration of results, and that the record those agents hold in their own hands is the standing answer to any charge about the figures. The Congress did not withdraw its charges, and no adjudication followed. In the same weeks, defeated candidates in Maharashtra filed applications under the April 2024 judgment's protocol for verification of the burned memory of machines in their constituencies, and in the village of Markadwadi in the Malshiras constituency of Solapur district, where the winning candidate's local vote had fallen far below the village's past pattern, residents organised a mock poll on paper ballots for 3 December 2024 to test the machine count; the district administration declined permission, deployed police, and the exercise was called off, with the administration's stated ground being that a parallel poll had no legal sanction and threatened public order. No election petition arising from the Maharashtra result had been decided on its merits within the period this article covers, and the counting controversy therefore stands where the Commission's clarification left it: a documented charge, a documented rebuttal resting on Form 17C, and a verification protocol whose applications were pending.

## The three features of the systemic stress pattern

The inventory is complete enough to be mapped, and the map is this article's findable artifact. It is labelled here so that it can be cited by name: the Three-Feature Systemic Stress Pattern Map. The map takes the controversies treated above and sorts them by the three features that the pattern reading claims they share. Feature One is feature-sharing: the episodes contest the same small set of institutional acts, the fixing of schedules, the adjudication of campaign speech, the keeping and publication of the electoral record, and the composition of the Commission itself, and they contest them through the same mechanisms, public criticism, litigation after the decision, civil society documentation and opposition engagement. Feature Two is aggregation: the episodes are cited against each other, so that the meaning of any one of them in public argument depends on the accumulated set, which is why a 2024 argument about turnout figures drew its force from a 2019 argument about clean chits rather than from its own arithmetic alone. Feature Three is continuity: the contested acts are performed under an architecture built long before 2014, the improvised founding template, the assertive-commissioner turn of the 1990s, the three-member structure settled in 1993 and tested in T. N. Seshan v Union of India in 1995, and the post-2014 episodes test that architecture without yet altering its constitutional text.

| Feature of the pattern | What the feature names | Documented controversies populating the feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feature One: feature-sharing across episodes | The same institutional acts contested through the same mechanisms | The Gujarat schedule dispute of October 2017 and the Karnataka date leak of March 2018 (schedules and information custody); the Model Code clean chits and the Lavasa dissents of April and May 2019 (adjudication of speech); the Telangana roll deletions of December 2018 (keeping of the record) |
| Feature Two: aggregation into pattern-content | Episodes cited against each other until the set, not the episode, carries the argument | The Madras High Court remarks of April 2021 read through the 2019 clean chits; the electoral bond disclosure of March 2024 read through the Commission's transparency record; the Maharashtra counting dispute of November and December 2024 argued from the accumulated set |

One further regularity belongs in the map's margin, because it is visible only across the whole set: the Commission's own manner of answering changed under the accumulation. In 2017 the answer was a chief election commissioner's voice at a press conference, reasons given orally and a charge of connivance refused in a sentence. In 2019 the answer on the most contested decisions was institutional silence, orders that read as unanimous and a refusal, later upheld by the Central Information Commission, to release the file that would have shown otherwise. In 2021 the answer was litigation, an application to one court and an appeal to another to contain what had been said about the institution. In 2024 the answer was documentary and detailed: affidavits resisting a data plea on stated grounds, and a Christmas Eve clarification that took the turnout charge apart figure by figure and rested the rebuttal on Form 17C. The progression from voice to silence to litigation to documentation is itself pattern-content. It records an institution learning, episode by episode, that its answers would be preserved and compared, and shifting its defence from authority asserted to record produced. Whether the shift is read as adaptation or as retreat depends on the framing the reader brings, but the shift is in the record either way, and neither the incident framing nor the strategic framing predicts it.

The map disciplines the claim in both directions. It shows what a reader must find in the record for the pattern reading to hold: episodes in each row, dated and documented, sharing the feature the row names. It also shows the reading's exposure. An episode that contested none of the four institutional acts, or that was settled on its own terms without being absorbed into later argument, would sit outside the map, and the reading would have to shrink accordingly. The episodes in the inventory do not sit outside it, including the ones that cut against any simple story of institutional decline: the Rajarajeshwari Nagar deferral, an assertive use of plenary power of the kind the assertive-commissioner era made famous, and the December 2024 clarification, a detailed public rebuttal of a kind the Commission of the clean chit period did not issue. Both are in the map because the pattern is a claim about the structure of contestation around the Commission, not a verdict that the Commission loses every contest.

## Adjudicating the framings: incident, strategy, pattern

The named disagreement the brief assigns this article can be put in its three positions and judged. The specific-incident framing holds that the inventory is a list, not a set. Its strongest form is the reference record itself: the standard reference treatment of the Commission organises the post-2014 material under discrete controversies, each with its own facts, and the framing's defence is that the facts really are discrete. The Gujarat schedule turned on floods and a chief secretary's letters. The Karnataka leak turned on a television broadcast. The Lavasa resignation turned on one man's career. On this reading, aggregation is an artefact of commentary, and the Commission that deferred Rajarajeshwari Nagar is simply not the same story as the Commission that cleared the Latur speech. The political-strategic framing holds the opposite: the episodes are one story because one political configuration produced them. Its strongest published forms are the charges recorded in this article, Chidambaram on the Gujarat schedule, Banerjee on the Bengal phases, Surjewala on the Karnataka leak, Chowdhury on the appointment committee, together with the argument, made by opposition parties after each episode, that an institution chosen by the executive will predictably resolve discretionary questions in the executive's favour. On this reading, the architecture is scenery and the cause is power.

The pattern reading defended here accepts one element from each rival and rejects the conclusion of each. From the incident framing it accepts that every episode has local facts that must be recorded in their local form, which is why this article has refused to let any episode borrow evidence from another. From the strategic framing it accepts that the episodes have political causes and political beneficiaries, and the complication the brief assigns is real: a reading that dissolved those causes into architecture would underweight them. What the pattern reading adds is the observation the other two cannot make. The local causes differ in every episode, a flood, a broadcast, a pandemic, a statute, and yet the institutional acts contested are the same four, the mechanisms of contest are the same four, and the episodes enter each other's arguments as precedent and as accumulated suspicion. E. Sridharan's essays on the post-2014 institutional order make the scholarly version of the point: the stress on India's referee institutions in this period is systemic in character, a property of how the institutions are positioned between a strong executive and a polarised party contest, rather than a sum of accidents befalling each institution separately. M. R. Madhavan's analyses for PRS Legislative Research track the same episodes from the procedural side, the appointment bill's design, the Model Code's statutory limits, the verification protocols, and they show the stress registering precisely where the procedures are thinnest. Suhrith Parthasarathy's essays on the bond and appointment cases argue the legal edge of the question: that the disclosure and the selection committee are constitutional issues about the conditions of electoral democracy, not administrative footnotes. The three bodies of commentary differ in discipline and reach different conclusions about remedies. They agree, against the incident framing, that the episodes must be read together to be read correctly, and they agree, against the purely strategic framing, that what is being tested is an architecture that any configuration of parties would stress in partially predictable ways, because the architecture concentrates discretion at exactly the points the episodes contested.

The adjudication therefore runs as follows. Where a framing explains the evidence with fewer leftovers, it wins. The incident framing leaves the aggregation unexplained: it cannot say why the Maharashtra argument of December 2024 was conducted in the vocabulary of 2019, or why the appointment statute of 2023 was debated as the sequel to a dissent of 2019. The strategic framing leaves the counter-episodes unexplained: it cannot comfortably place the Rajarajeshwari Nagar deferral, the 2021 schedule that ended in the incumbent's third victory, the December 2024 rebuttal that answered the turnout charge on the merits of Form 17C, or the fact that the sharpest single rebuke of the decade came from a High Court about a pandemic rather than from any party about a result. The pattern reading places all of them, because its claim is about the structure of contestation: a commission armed with plenary discretion over schedules, speech, rolls and records, composed by a process the political executive long controlled, will generate controversies that share features and aggregate, whoever holds power, and the post-2014 decade is the period in which the aggregation became dense enough to name. That is the sense, and the only sense, in which the controversies operate as a systemic stress pattern.

## The pillar synthesis: the long arc from Sen to the stress pattern

This article closes its pillar, and the pillar's long arc is the pattern's deepest evidence. The arc begins with [the founding-template architecture](/2020/06/08/sukumar-sen-1951-1952-election/): Sukumar Sen built the first general election of 1951 and 1952 out of an improvised secretariat, borrowed staff and the plenary text of Article 324, and the template he set, a small permanent commission directing a vast borrowed machinery, is the machinery that every later episode in this article ran on. The arc's second station is [the assertive-commissioner-architectural-shift](/2020/06/14/tn-seshan-eci-reforms-india/), when T. N. Seshan between 1990 and 1996 converted the Code and the plenary power into instruments of public confrontation, survived the government's answer of a three-member commission, and was upheld in substance by the Supreme Court in 1995 in a judgment that also confirmed the Chief Election Commissioner's primacy within the multi-member body. The assertive turn normalised exactly the discretionary visibility that the post-2014 controversies contested: a commission that announces itself as a protagonist will be judged as one. The third station is [the technological-architectural-trajectory](/2020/06/19/evm-vvpat-story-explained-india/), in which the machine count replaced the ballot count and the contest moved to verification protocols, where the courts of 2019 and 2024 settled the terms. The fourth is the money trajectory closed by the bond judgment. The fifth is [the architectural-asymmetry-question framework](/2020/06/30/tenure-security-election-commission/), the protected chief and the removable commissioners, the asymmetry the appointment litigation finally forced into statute in 2023. The sixth is [the soft-law-architecture-and-limits framework](/2020/07/06/model-code-conduct-soft-law-india/), the code that governs campaigns without statutory force and whose adjudication produced the decade's only internal dissent crisis. Around these stations the pillar ranged wider: [the three-substantive-positions framework](/2020/07/11/simultaneous-elections-debate-india/) on the proposal to synchronise the electoral cycle, which would rewrite the scheduling discretion at the heart of the 2017 and 2021 disputes, and [the architectural-substantive-tension framework](/2020/07/17/anti-defection-tenth-schedule-india/) on the Speaker's parallel adjudicatory office, a reminder that the Commission is one referee among several in the constitutional design. All of it sits inside the wider frame of [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) and [the post-2014 PMO architecture context](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/), the renegotiation of centre, institution and movement that the post-2014 decades performed across every referee body at once.

The pillar's analytical contribution, stated once and plainly, is the distinction between the Commission's procedural-administrative content, the conduct of polls, which no episode in the inventory shows failing at scale, and its institutional-architectural content, the discretionary and compositional questions on which every controversy in the inventory fastened. The founding generation built the first content and improvised the second. Seshan dramatised the second without settling it. The post-2014 decade is when the unsettled second content became the standing subject of Indian electoral politics, argued by parties, courts, a dissenting commissioner and a High Court in turn, and aggregated into the pattern this article has mapped. The institution under stress framework, which the pillar's opening article built from the founding settlement outward, thus reaches its closing operationalisation: the stress is not an episode in the Commission's history. Read across the long arc, it is the recurring condition of an architecture that concentrated discretion faster than it built the conventions, recorded reasons, published dissents and insulated appointments that would let the discretion be trusted at face value. The questions this pillar leaves undecided pass onward in the series' argument: the court that designed the appointment committee of March 2023, refused the stays of March 2024 and adjusted the verification protocol of April 2024 has been throughout this pillar the referee of the referee, and the politics of that court's own composition and doctrine is the ground the argument crosses next.

## What the documented sequence settles, and what it leaves open

The sequence settles the descriptive question. Between 2017 and 2024 the Commission's schedules, its Model Code adjudications, its custody of the roll and the donation record, and its own composition were each contested through the same mechanisms and in each other's vocabulary, and that is a fact about the record, not an interpretation laid over it. The sequence also settles the analytical question this article was assigned, in favour of the pattern reading over its two rivals, for the reasons given in the adjudication above. What the sequence leaves open is everything that would count as remedy. Whether selection committees should include a judge is the question the Constitution Bench answered one way in March 2023 and Parliament answered another way in December 2023, and it awaits a court. Whether a dissent in a Model Code order should be published is a question the Commission answered for itself in May 2019 and could revisit by its own rules. Whether turnout data should be published in final form on polling night is a question of administrative design the Maharashtra exchange left exactly where it found it, with the Commission defending its aggregation process and its critics unconvinced. The pattern reading predicts, on the evidence of the decade, that these open questions will be taken up again at the next discretionary act that draws fire, and that when they are, the argument will cite the whole inventory and not merely the act of the day. An institution's controversies are the minutes of its unsettled questions. The post-2014 minutes are long, they are documented, and they are best read as one record.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the post-2014 ECI controversies?

They are the documented set of disputes that gathered around the Election Commission of India between 2017 and 2024 and that this article reads as one pattern. The set includes the separate announcement of the Gujarat schedule in October 2017, the Karnataka poll date that appeared on television and social media before the Commission announced it in March 2018, the deferral of polling at Rajarajeshwari Nagar after the seizure of 9,746 voter identity cards in May 2018, the missing names in the Telangana rolls in December 2018, the Model Code clean chits of April and May 2019 and the dissents they provoked, the eight-phase Bengal schedule and the Madras High Court remarks of 2021, the appointment litigation of 2023 and 2024, the staged electoral bond disclosure of March 2024, and the Maharashtra counting dispute of late 2024. Each entry is recorded in the article as an event, the Commission's stated position, the critics' stated charge and any court outcome.

### Q: Who is Ashok Lavasa?

Ashok Lavasa is a retired Indian Administrative Service officer of the 1980 batch from the Haryana cadre who served as Election Commissioner of India from 23 January 2018 to 31 August 2020. Before his appointment he had been finance secretary, environment secretary and civil aviation secretary in the Union government. By seniority he stood next in line to become Chief Election Commissioner when Sunil Arora's term ended in April 2021, and his own tenure would have run to October 2022. He became the most publicly discussed member of the Commission in its history to that point because of the dissents he recorded during the 2019 general election against majority decisions clearing Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Bharatiya Janata Party president Amit Shah of Model Code complaints. In July 2020 the Asian Development Bank announced him as a vice-president, and on 18 August 2020 he resigned from the Commission, surrendering the chief election commissionership he would otherwise have assumed the following year.

### Q: What was the Lavasa dissent?

The Lavasa dissent was the set of minority decisions that Election Commissioner Ashok Lavasa recorded in the spring of 2019 against the Election Commission's majority decisions on Model Code complaints. In five cases, four concerning speeches by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, including the Wardha speech of 1 April 2019 and the Latur speech of 9 April 2019, and one concerning a speech by Amit Shah, the full Commission cleared the speaker by a two to one majority of Sunil Arora and Sushil Chandra, and Lavasa's disagreement was not carried in the published orders. In May 2019 he wrote to the Chief Election Commissioner that minority decisions recorded by him continued to be suppressed in a manner contrary to the conventions of multi-member statutory bodies, and he withdrew from Model Code meetings. On 21 May 2019 the Commission decided by majority that dissent in Model Code matters would not form part of its orders. The dissent's substance was never published by the Commission, and a later refusal to disclose the notes under the Right to Information Act was upheld by the Central Information Commission.

### Q: What are the post-2024 ECI controversies?

The post-2024 controversies are the entries in the sequence that fall in and after the 2024 general election year and that the brief for this article groups as the pattern's continuation. They include the electoral bond disclosure of February and March 2024, in which the Supreme Court struck down the bond scheme on 15 February 2024, refused the State Bank of India more time on 11 March 2024, and on 18 March 2024 ordered the bank to disclose the bonds' alphanumeric numbers that the first published tranche had omitted, after which the Commission published the completed record on 21 March 2024. They include the resignation of Election Commissioner Arun Goel on 9 March 2024 and the appointment of two commissioners under the contested Act of 2023 on 14 March 2024 while the Supreme Court declined to stay the Act. They include the litigation over publishing booth-level turnout data during the 2024 general election, and the Maharashtra counting dispute of November and December 2024 over turnout figures, roll additions and machine verification.

### Q: What is the systemic-stress-pattern?

The systemic stress pattern is the analytical reading this article defends against the specific-incident framing and the political-strategic framing of the post-2014 controversies. It holds that the episodes share three features, set out in the Three-Feature Systemic Stress Pattern Map. The first feature is feature-sharing: the episodes contest the same institutional acts, schedules, Model Code adjudication, the keeping and publication of the electoral record, and the Commission's own composition, through the same mechanisms of public criticism, post-decision litigation, civil society documentation and opposition engagement. The second feature is aggregation: the episodes are cited against one another, so the set rather than the episode carries the public argument, as when the Maharashtra dispute of 2024 was argued in the vocabulary of 2019. The third feature is continuity: the contested acts are performed inside an architecture built before 2014, the founding template of 1950 to 1952 and the three-member design settled in 1993, which the post-2014 decade tested without amending. The reading is a claim about the structure of contestation, not a verdict on any single episode.

### Q: How is the post-2014 ECI different?

The post-2014 Commission differs from its predecessors less in its legal powers, which remained the plenary powers of Article 324 throughout, than in the density and documentation of the contest around those powers. Earlier commissions faced sharp episodes, the Seshan years being the standing example, but the controversies of those years were usually carried by one institution at a time and closed with the episode. In the post-2014 period the contested acts recurred across every function the Commission performs, scheduling, adjudication, roll-keeping, disclosure and composition, and each episode was preserved in a public record of press statements, court filings, published data and a dissenting commissioner's letter that later arguments could cite. The Commission's own manner of answering changed across the decade, from oral justification in 2017, through institutional silence over the 2019 orders, to litigation in 2021 and detailed written rebuttal in 2024. The difference is therefore one of accumulation and record: the institution's authority came to be argued over as a standing question of Indian politics rather than as an occasional quarrel, and the arguments aggregate into the pattern this article names.

### Q: What is the Maharashtra 2024 counting controversy?

The Maharashtra 2024 counting controversy is the documented dispute that followed the state assembly election of 20 November 2024, whose results on 23 November 2024 gave the ruling Mahayuti alliance 235 of 288 seats. The defeated Congress filed a memorandum with the Election Commission on 29 November 2024 raising two charges: that the turnout figures had risen from about 58.22 percent at 5 p.m. on polling day to about 66 percent in the final figure, a gap the party quantified at roughly 76 lakh votes, and that electors had been added arbitrarily in a set of constituencies between July and November 2024, most of them won by the ruling alliance. The Commission met the party on 3 December 2024 and issued a detailed clarification on 24 December 2024, holding that the 5 p.m. figure is a running estimate, that final turnout is compiled from Form 17C accounts held by candidates' agents, and that only six constituencies had added more than 50,000 electors in the period, through due process. Defeated candidates separately sought machine verification under the Supreme Court's April 2024 protocol.

### Q: Why did the Election Commission announce the Gujarat and Himachal Pradesh schedules separately in October 2017?

The Commission announced the Himachal Pradesh schedule on 12 October 2017 and the Gujarat schedule on 25 October 2017, and its stated reason was the Gujarat floods of July 2017. Chief Election Commissioner Achal Kumar Joti said the floods had killed 229 people across seven north Gujarat districts, that the Gujarat chief secretary had written twice asking that the announcement not be hurried, and that the state employees conducting relief and restoration were the same employees who would be diverted to election duty once the Model Code began. He said the delinking was done because of the flood situation and for no other reason, and he rejected the charge of connivance with the state government directly. The Congress charged that the gap gave the Gujarat government and the Prime Minister time to announce concessions and launch projects outside the code, and the Prime Minister did visit Gujarat twice in the interval, launching projects reported at about Rs 3,600 crore. No court ruled on the schedule, so the episode rests on the Commission's stated reasons against the opposition's stated charge.

### Q: What did the Election Commission's committee examine after the Karnataka poll dates appeared on television in March 2018?

The committee examined how the Karnataka assembly schedule reached the public before the Commission announced it on 27 March 2018. During the Commission's own press conference, an English news channel broadcast polling on 12 May 2018 and counting on 18 May 2018, and the Bharatiya Janata Party's information technology head Amit Malviya tweeted the same dates at 11.08 a.m., as did a Karnataka Congress social media functionary at about the same time. Chief Election Commissioner O. P. Rawat first treated the dates as conjecture, then, on announcing polling for 12 May and counting for 15 May, called the leak a very serious issue and promised stringent action. That evening the Commission constituted a committee of senior officers under Senior Deputy Election Commissioner Umesh Sinha, to report within seven days and suggest safeguards. Malviya filed a written explanation with timestamps attributing his tweet to the channel's broadcast. The Commission's inquiry order did not name him for questioning, which drew the Congress's charge that he had been absolved before any probe. No finding of an internal leak was published and no prosecution followed.

### Q: Why was polling in Rajarajeshwari Nagar deferred in May 2018?

Polling in the Rajarajeshwari Nagar constituency of Bengaluru was deferred because the Election Commission concluded that the poll process there had been vitiated by a planned design to induce voters. On the night of 8 May 2018 officials seized 9,746 electors photo identity cards, laptops, a printer and overseas elector form counterfoils from a flat in Jalahalli in the constituency, four days before the state voted. The Bharatiya Janata Party demanded the election be countermanded and accused the Congress candidate of assembling the cards to rig the poll; the Congress answered that the flat was linked to the other side and the seizure staged. The Commission had the state's chief electoral officer and the Bengaluru police commissioner report, sent a deputy election commissioner to examine the facts, and on 11 May 2018 deferred the poll to 28 May 2018 with counting on 31 May 2018. Its order, issued under Article 324 and the Representation of the People Act 1951, recorded the deputy election commissioner's conclusion that there appeared to be a planned design to induce voters, thus vitiating the poll process.

### Q: Which speeches by Narendra Modi and Amit Shah did the Election Commission clear in April and May 2019?

The Commission cleared a run of speeches in a single week at the end of the 2019 campaign. On 30 April 2019 it held that the Prime Minister's Wardha speech of 1 April 2019, attacking Rahul Gandhi's decision to contest from Wayanad, did not violate the Model Code or its advisories. On 1 May 2019 it cleared the Latur speech of 9 April 2019, in which first-time voters were asked to dedicate their votes to the Balakot air strike and the Pulwama martyrs. On 3 May 2019 it cleared Amit Shah in two cases, including his Nagpur speech, and on 5 May 2019 it cleared the Prime Minister's Patan speech of 21 April 2019, bringing his clearances to six cases that week. The decisions followed a Supreme Court direction, on a petition by Congress parliamentarian Sushmita Dev, to decide the pending complaints by 6 May 2019. Reporting established that the Wardha and Latur decisions and three others in the run were majority decisions, carried two to one over Election Commissioner Ashok Lavasa's dissent, though the published orders did not say so.

### Q: Why did Ashok Lavasa stop attending Model Code meetings in May 2019?

Lavasa stopped attending because the Commission's orders on the 2019 Model Code complaints were being issued without his recorded minority decisions. He had dissented in five decisions in the run of clearances given to speeches by the Prime Minister and Amit Shah, and when the published orders read as the Commission's single view, he wrote to Chief Election Commissioner Sunil Arora in May 2019. The letter, whose contents became public on 17 and 18 May 2019, said that minority decisions recorded by him in several cases continued to be suppressed in a manner contrary to well-established conventions observed by multi-member statutory bodies, that it appeared futile for him to participate until the Commission's lawful functioning was restored by including them, and that he was left with no option but to keep away from such proceedings. Arora replied publicly that the Commission's members were not expected to be templates or clones of each other. On 21 May 2019 the full Commission decided by majority, Lavasa dissenting, that dissent notes in Model Code cases would not be recorded in the orders.

### Q: What did the Madras High Court say about the Election Commission in April 2021?

Hearing a petition about counting arrangements at the Karur constituency on 26 April 2021, during the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, a Madras High Court bench led by Chief Justice Sanjib Banerjee said orally that the Election Commission was the institution singularly responsible for the second wave, that no action had been taken against political parties holding rallies despite the court's orders, and that the Commission should probably be put up on murder charges. The remarks were made in the hearing and reported from the courtroom; they did not appear in the bench's written order. The Commission applied to the High Court to confine media reporting to recorded observations and was declined, then appealed to the Supreme Court. On 6 May 2021 a bench of Justices D. Y. Chandrachud and M. R. Shah refused to expunge the remarks, holding that oral observations are not part of the official record and cannot be expunged, and refused to restrain the media from reporting open court proceedings. The judgment recorded that the remarks were harsh and the metaphor improper, while noting the Commission's track record as an independent constitutional body.

### Q: How many phases did the West Bengal election of 2021 have, and why was the schedule criticised?

The West Bengal election of 2021 had eight phases, running from 27 March to 29 April 2021, the longest schedule in the state's history and one phase more than in 2016. Announcing it on 26 February 2021, Chief Election Commissioner Sunil Arora grounded the phasing in security: sensitive and vulnerable polling stations had been identified, and central armed police forces had to be deployed and moved across the state in strength. Assam voted in three phases and Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry in a single phase on 6 April 2021 in the same round. The criticism came from Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee, who asked why Bengal alone needed so many phases while other states voted in one, and whether the dates had been announced according to the suggestions of the Prime Minister and the Home Minister, saying her information was that the dates matched what the Bharatiya Janata Party wanted. The Bharatiya Janata Party's Bengal leadership welcomed the eight phases as necessary for a fair poll. The Commission held the full schedule through the pandemic's second wave, and the Trinamool Congress won 213 seats.

### Q: Why were names missing from the electoral rolls in Telangana in December 2018?

Names were missing because of the deletions carried out in the roll revision that preceded the election, which the state's election machinery conceded had removed electors on a very large scale. Telangana voted on 7 December 2018, and through polling day voters across the state found their names absent from rolls on which they had voted before. The Chief Electoral Officer, Rajat Kumar, apologised that evening and conceded a mistake had been made. He had earlier said about 22 lakh names had been removed between the 2.83 crore electors recorded before September 2018 and the 2.61 crore in the draft rolls of 10 September 2018, attributing the deletions to the removal of duplicates, the dead and the shifted under the National Electoral Roll Purification and Authentication Programme of 2015, which had matched rolls against Aadhaar data until the Supreme Court stopped the seeding in August 2015. A public interest petition alleged deletions by software without the notice the rules require. The Commission's clarification said deletions follow due process with objections invited, and that the final rolls of 19 November 2018 held 2.806 crore electors.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court order on VVPAT slip counting in April 2019?

On 8 April 2019 the Supreme Court ordered the Election Commission to raise the physical verification of voter verifiable paper audit trail slips from one polling station to five polling stations per assembly constituency or segment in the 2019 general election. Opposition parties had demanded that slips be counted for half the polling stations in each segment and that the slip count prevail in case of a mismatch with the electronic count, and the Court declined the larger demand while increasing the sample fivefold over the Commission's existing practice. On 7 May 2019 the Court dismissed a review plea seeking the increase to half. The 2019 count proceeded on the five-station footing. The question returned in the next cycle, and on 26 April 2024, in Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India, the Court rejected pleas for counting every slip and for a return to paper ballots, while directing that symbol loading units be sealed after use and that candidates placed second or third could seek verification of the burned memory of microcontrollers in five percent of machines per assembly segment on written request.

### Q: When did Ashok Lavasa resign from the Election Commission, and what post did he take up?

Ashok Lavasa resigned on 18 August 2020 and asked to be relieved on 31 August 2020, to take up the post of Vice-President for Private Sector Operations and Public-Private Partnerships at the Asian Development Bank in Manila. The bank had announced his appointment on 15 July 2020, grounding it in his experience of public-private partnerships and infrastructure, and reporting at the time said the appointment had been finalised with the concurrence of the Union government, as India's appointments to the bank ordinarily are. The resignation surrendered more than two years of his term. Had he stayed, he would have succeeded Sunil Arora as Chief Election Commissioner in April 2021 and would have retired from that office in October 2022, conducting the assembly elections of Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Punjab, Uttarakhand, Goa and Manipur in between. His departure was the second premature exit in the Commission's history. The first was Chief Election Commissioner Nagender Singh, who resigned in 1973 to become a judge of the International Court of Justice. Sushil Chandra moved up in the line of succession after Lavasa left.

### Q: What investigations of Ashok Lavasa's family were reported in 2019?

In September 2019, months after Lavasa's dissents became public, the Income Tax Department issued notices to his wife, Novel Singhal Lavasa, concerning her tax filings for earlier assessment years, and newspapers reported that companies associated with his son and his daughter had also come under official scrutiny for alleged non-declaration of income and disproportionate assets. The family denied wrongdoing in their responses to the notices. No agency stated any connection between the tax proceedings and the dissents of May 2019, and no document in the public record establishes one. Opposition politicians and commentators stated the charge of a connection in print, pointing to the timing; the charge is recorded in this article as a charge, with that evidentiary status, because the sequence is documented while the causation is not. The reporting of the notices formed the middle term of the Lavasa sequence as the public received it: the dissents of April and May 2019, the notices of September 2019, and the resignation of August 2020 that followed the Asian Development Bank's announcement of his appointment as a vice-president.

### Q: What did the Election Commission tell the Congress about Maharashtra turnout figures in December 2024?

In its detailed clarification of 24 December 2024, answering the Congress memorandum of 29 November 2024, the Commission held that the party's comparison of turnout at 5 p.m. with the final figure rested on a misconception. It said the 5 p.m. figure is a running estimate assembled by presiding officers who are performing several statutory duties at the close of poll, that the press note issued around 11.45 p.m. on polling night was an additional disclosure introduced during the 2024 general election, and that final turnout is compiled from Form 17C, the statutory account of votes cast at each polling station, copies of which are handed to candidates' authorised agents at the close of poll, so that the actual turnout cannot be altered afterwards without detection. On the rolls, it said only six constituencies had recorded increases of more than 50,000 electors between July and November 2024, that additions and deletions had followed the due process of claims and objections in which parties participate, and that there had been no arbitrary addition or deletion of voters in Maharashtra.

### Q: Why did Election Commissioner Arun Goel resign in March 2024?

Arun Goel resigned on 9 March 2024, and the only reason placed on the record by Goel or by the Commission was personal reasons. He had taken office on 21 November 2022 and his term ran to December 2027, so the resignation surrendered more than three and a half years, and it came a week before the general election schedule was announced on 16 March 2024, leaving the Commission with a single member, Chief Election Commissioner Rajiv Kumar, until two new commissioners took office on 15 March 2024. Media reporting at the time recorded speculation about differences between Goel and the Chief Election Commissioner over the conduct of the coming election; neither man confirmed any difference, and the Commission issued no statement beyond the fact of the resignation and the President's acceptance of it. The vacancy's consequences were institutional rather than personal. Two commissioners had to be appointed within days, under the Act of 2023 whose validity was under challenge, by a selection committee whose opposition member recorded a written dissent about the procedure, and the Supreme Court declined to stay either the Act or the appointments when the matter was heard on 15 and 21 March 2024.
