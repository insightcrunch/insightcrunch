The 1991 reforms in detail are usually told as an atmosphere: a crisis, a collapse of reserves, a new government, a famous budget. Told that way, July 1991 becomes weather, something that happened to India rather than something named officials did, clause by clause, on dated paper. The documents tell a tighter story. Between 1 July 1991 and 24 July 1991, a government eleven days old when the sequence opened executed five distinct instruments: the Reserve Bank of India lowered the rupee against its currency basket by roughly 9 per cent on 1 July and by roughly 11 per cent more on 3 July; the Ministry of Commerce announced a trade policy on 4 July that replaced import licensing with tradable eximscrips; a gold operation already under way shipped 47 tonnes of the Reserve Bank's stock to the Bank of England in July after an earlier sale of 20 tonnes to the Union Bank of Switzerland; and on a single day, 24 July 1991, the government laid a Statement on Industrial Policy before Parliament that abolished industrial licensing for all but eighteen industries, hours before Finance Minister Manmohan Singh presented a budget that cut the peak customs duty from above 300 per cent to a ceiling of 150 per cent and quoted Victor Hugo to the Lok Sabha. Each instrument had an author, a legal form and an operative text. Each can be read. This article reads them.

The claim defended here is the brief's, stated plainly: the July 1991 reforms operate as detailed political content, and their operative clauses, read closely, reveal more than either the policy-history framing or the crisis-response framing captures. The policy-history framing, the standard textbook chronicle, lists the measures in sequence and explains each by the one before it. The crisis-response framing, pressed hardest by the reforms' left critics in 1991 and after, reads the same documents as a dictated text, drafted in Washington and signed in New Delhi under the duress of an empty treasury. The detail reading defended in this article takes a third path. It treats the five instruments of July 1991 as designed objects: their percentages, their annexes, their choice of legal form and their timing carry the politics. A minority government that could not rely on Parliament for a legislative programme chose instruments that mostly required no vote at all: a central bank adjustment, a commerce ministry announcement, a policy statement laid on the table of the House, and one budget, the single text that had to survive a division. The fertiliser fight of August 1991, when the Congress Parliamentary Party forced the finance minister to trim his own budget in public, marks the exact boundary of that strategy. What follows reconstructs each instrument from its text and its documented contest, and then adjudicates the three readings against the evidence the texts supply.

### What did the Rao government actually change in July 1991?

In twenty four days the Rao government devalued the rupee twice, replaced the import licensing system with tradable eximscrips, abolished industrial licensing for all but eighteen industries, ended the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices asset test, opened thirty four industries to automatic foreign equity up to 51 per cent, and capped peak customs duty at 150 per cent.

The five instruments and the gold operation that financed the interval between them can be mapped before they are argued. The table below, the Five-Feature Map of July 1991, sets out each instrument with its date, its author and its operative content, and the article returns to it in the adjudication that closes the argument.

## An Inheritance of Weeks: Reserves, Gold and a Government Eleven Days Old

The government that executed the July instruments had been formed in the middle of the emergency it inherited. Foreign exchange reserves had fallen through 1990 under the pressure of the Gulf crisis of August 1990, which raised the oil import bill and interrupted remittances from Indian workers in the Gulf, and under the weight of a fiscal deficit that the Union government had run at 8.4 per cent of GDP in 1990-91. By June 1991 the Reserve Bank's foreign currency assets stood a little above one billion dollars, roughly two weeks of import cover, and the question in New Delhi was not the direction of policy but the arithmetic of default: which payments, falling due in which week, the treasury could still meet. The Chandra Shekhar government, a minority administration kept in office by Congress support until March 1991, had already crossed the thresholds that later commentary assigns to its successor. In January 1991 that government drew about 1.8 billion dollars from the International Monetary Fund under the Compensatory and Contingency Financing Facility and a first credit tranche arrangement, and in the spring of 1991 it approved the sale, with a repurchase option, of 20 tonnes of confiscated gold to the Union Bank of Switzerland, a transaction executed through the State Bank of India that raised about 200 million dollars. The crisis-response instruments, in other words, predate the reform government. What the Rao government added was not the discovery of the crisis but a set of structural texts laid over it.

The inheritance can be stated in the ledger form the crisis itself imposed. The current account deficit had swollen to about 3 per cent of GDP in 1990-91, the widest in two decades, financed increasingly by short term commercial borrowing and by non-resident deposits whose holders could leave, and some did leave, withdrawing funds through the autumn of 1990 as the Gulf crisis and two changes of government in a year unsettled them. External debt stood at about 72 billion dollars by March 1991. Total reserves including gold had fallen to about 5.8 billion dollars by March, and the spendable component, foreign currency assets, slid through the spring to a little above one billion dollars by June, the two weeks of import cover that fixed the season's timetable. The credit rating agencies had marked India down through 1990 and into 1991, commercial banks had shortened their exposure, and the Reserve Bank's weekly statistics had become the document the whole government read first. A crisis narrated as atmosphere cannot be governed; a crisis stated as a ledger can be answered line by line, and the July instruments are best introduced as the lines of an answer: the devaluation for the export line, the scrips for the import line, the gold for the payments line falling due before either could work, and the budget for the deficit line that had driven the borrowing.

The political calendar compressed everything. Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated on 21 May 1991, midway through the general election his killing interrupted. The Congress emerged from the completed polling as the largest party in the Lok Sabha with 232 seats, short of a majority, and P. V. Narasimha Rao, who had not contested the election and had been preparing to retire from active politics, was sworn in as Prime Minister on 21 June 1991. Rao kept the industries portfolio in his own hands, a retention whose significance the detail reading stresses: the industrial policy that would abolish licensing was drafted inside a ministry the Prime Minister personally held. His finance minister, Manmohan Singh, was a former Governor of the Reserve Bank and a former head of the Planning Commission machinery, appointed on the strength of a reform design that already existed on paper. Montek Singh Ahluwalia, then Commerce Secretary, had prepared in 1990, as Special Secretary to the Prime Minister, the memorandum known in the literature as the M document, which had sketched trade liberalisation, a tradable replenishment instrument and industrial delicensing a full year before the treasury emptied. The existence of that 1990 text matters to the adjudication later in this article, because it dates the design before the dictation alleged by the crisis-response framing. The instruments of July 1991 were drafted in days, but they were not improvised in days; they were drawn from a file that predated the government, the election and the assassination.

Eleven days separate the swearing-in of 21 June from the first instrument of 1 July, and the interval was filled with a negotiation the public record only partly shows. The government had to satisfy the Fund that a standby arrangement would be supported by measures, and the Fund's own record fixes the sequence: the larger standby arrangement, SDR 1,656 million, roughly 2.2 billion dollars, was approved on 31 October 1991, after the July instruments were already in force, and India drew the full amount before the arrangement expired on 30 June 1993. Stabilisation money followed structural texts rather than preceding them, a chronology the crisis-response reading must strain to explain. Domestically, the government faced a Parliament in which it lacked a majority and a party in which the old guard read devaluation and delicensing as a repudiation of four decades of Congress economics. The instrument choices of July carry that constraint in their form. None of the first three instruments required a vote. The fourth, the budget, required several, and it is the budget, precisely because it had to pass, that absorbed the only public retreat of the season.

## The Design File: What Was Already on Paper Before the Treasury Emptied

The detail reading stands or falls on a documentary question: did the July instruments have authors and drafts that predate the emergency, or were they composed under dictation in the weeks after 21 June 1991? The file answers clearly, and its layers reach back a decade. The trade reform layer begins with the committee reports of the early 1980s, the Abid Hussain committee on trade policies of 1984 above all, which had recommended the replacement of discretionary import licensing by tariff based protection and tradable entitlements, recommendations the Rajiv Gandhi government implemented only at the margin between 1985 and 1988. The industrial layer begins with the Long Term Fiscal Policy of December 1985 and the budget of that year, in which Finance Minister Vishwanath Pratap Singh raised the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices asset threshold from 20 crore rupees to 100 crore, the first breach in the asset gate that July 1991 would demolish, and with the delicensing of a first set of industries in 1985 and 1986, which established that the licensing system could be cut in pieces without the sky falling. Each layer was partial, contested and reversible, and each taught the officials who would staff 1991 which clauses provoked which lobbies, knowledge that no dictated programme could have contained.

The decisive layer is the memorandum Montek Singh Ahluwalia prepared in 1990. Ahluwalia, then Special Secretary to Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi, set out a programme of trade liberalisation built on a tradable replenishment instrument, industrial delicensing, a corrected exchange rate and a phased move toward convertibility, and the paper circulated at the top of the government in the months before the Gulf crisis turned a chronic payments weakness into an emergency. The M document was not adopted in 1990. The Chandra Shekhar government that followed spent its short life on stabilisation, drawing on the Fund in January 1991, pledging gold in the spring and presenting, through Finance Minister Yashwant Sinha, an interim budget that promised a full budget later; the structural file stayed in the drawer. When Rao formed his government on 21 June 1991, the file came out of the drawer with its author attached, Ahluwalia having moved to the Commerce Secretary's chair from which the trade package would be run. Sitapati's Half-Lion (2016) and Jairam Ramesh's To the Brink and Back (2015), written from opposite ends of the Prime Minister's Office, agree on the essential point: the new finance minister did not commission a design in June 1991, he selected one. Manmohan Singh's own formation closed the circuit, since the finance minister of 1991 had been Governor of the Reserve Bank from 1982 to 1985 and had chaired the committee whose 1985 report on the monetary system had argued for the market oriented financial reforms the trajectory later enacted. The July instruments were the third attempt to execute a design whose first two attempts, in 1985 and in 1990, had died of political exposure. The design's survival strategy in 1991, the choice of instruments that needed no votes and of a sequence that front loaded the irreversible acts into the government's first month, was the lesson its authors drew from those two deaths, and it is legible in the texts themselves.

This prehistory also disciplines the strongest version of the dictation charge. The Fund's January 1991 financing to the Chandra Shekhar government and the negotiations of the summer were real constraints on magnitudes: the deficit had to fall by a stated amount, reserves had to be rebuilt on a stated path, and the standby of 31 October 1991 wrote those magnitudes into performance criteria after the fact. But the design file shows the Indian state arguing with itself about instruments for a decade before the Fund's negotiators arrived, and the instruments chosen in July bear the marks of those internal arguments, the scrip from the Planning Commission discussions of 1990, the eighteen item annex from the security establishment's reservations, the 51 per cent ceiling from a negotiation between the liberalisers and a party that would not say the words majority foreign ownership without a cap. A dictated text does not carry the fingerprints of its recipient's internal quarrels. The July texts are covered in them.

## Gold, Pledged and Redeemed: The Operation That Financed the Interval

The gold operation is the hinge between the crisis the Rao government inherited and the instruments it chose, and it deserves reconstruction as an executed transaction rather than as a symbol. The first leg belonged to the predecessor government. With reserves sliding and the January 1991 Fund drawing insufficient, the Chandra Shekhar government approved the use of confiscated gold held by the state: 20 tonnes were sold to the Union Bank of Switzerland with a repurchase option in the spring of 1991, raising about 200 million dollars, by some accounts 234 million, through the State Bank of India. The second leg belonged to the Rao government and to July itself. In July 1991 the Reserve Bank shipped 47 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England in four tranches, pledged as collateral for loans of about 405 million dollars arranged with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan. The physical movement of the metal, crated and flown under security from Mumbai, was the most politically combustible act of the season: opposition speakers in Parliament and outside it described the shipments as the pawning of the national honour, and the phrase attached itself to the reforms in public memory more durably than any clause of any policy statement.

The detail reading asks what the operation's terms, rather than its theatre, disclose. Three features of the execution matter. First, the gold was pledged and sold under repurchase arrangements, not dissipated: the state retained a contractual route back to its metal, and it used that route. The pledges were redeemed within months as reserves rebuilt through the autumn of 1991, the Bank of England metal repurchased by November 1991 and the Swiss transaction closed by the end of the year, well inside the periods the loans allowed. Second, the sums were bridge sums, calibrated to weeks rather than to solvency: about 605 million dollars across the two legs, against a standby arrangement of roughly 2.2 billion that arrived only in October. The gold bought the interval in which the July texts could be issued and begin to work on export orders, remittance channels and creditor nerves. Third, the decision structure was bipartisan in fact whatever the rhetoric: a Janata Dal (Secular) government approved the first leg and a Congress government executed the second, and the instruments of stabilisation ran continuously across the change of regime. That continuity is the first hard datum against a pure dictation account of 1991. The Fund did not need to instruct two successive Indian governments of opposed composition to pledge gold; the arithmetic of reserves instructed both, and both answered with the same metal. What differed between the governments was everything built on top of the bridge: the Chandra Shekhar administration pledged gold to survive, the Rao administration pledged gold to buy time for a redesign of the trade, industry and exchange regimes. The redesign is in the texts, and the texts are where the detail reading keeps the argument.

The gold's political afterlife inside the season confirms the distinction between the bridge and the building. In the monsoon session debates of July and August 1991, opposition speakers returned to the shipments as the emblem of national humiliation, and the government's replies, made on the floor and in the finance minister's budget defence, never contested the metal or the tonnage; they contested the inference, pointing to the repurchase terms written into both transactions and to the redemption schedule the Reserve Bank then executed ahead of the loans' outer dates. When the Bank of England metal was repurchased by November 1991, the government treated the fact as a state paper in the argument, evidence that the pledge had been a bridge with a return ticket rather than a sale of the patrimony. The opposition's emblem survived the redemption, as emblems do, and gold remained the word with which the reforms' critics taxed the government in the elections of the decade; but the documentary record the detail reading follows had already separated into two files, the bridge file, closed within the year with the metal home, and the building file, the statutes and statements that stayed open. Histories of 1991 that begin with the gold and end with the gold mistake the season's most perishable act for its most durable one.

## Feature One: The Two-Step Devaluation of 1 and 3 July 1991

The first instrument of the Rao government was executed by the Reserve Bank of India under Governor S. Venkitaramanan, who had taken office in December 1990 and had already managed the gold decisions of the spring. On 1 July 1991 the Reserve Bank lowered the value of the rupee by approximately 9 per cent against the basket of five major currencies, the dollar, the pound sterling, the Deutsche mark, the yen and the French franc, to which the rupee was pegged. On 3 July 1991 the Reserve Bank lowered it again, by approximately 11 per cent against the same basket. The two steps compounded to a downward adjustment of roughly 18 to 19 per cent; the rupee moved from about 17.9 to the dollar to about 23 to the dollar across the two announcements. The devaluation was the indispensable companion of the trade policy that followed on 4 July, because a licensing reform that freed imports while the exchange rate still overvalued the rupee would have spent the reserves the government did not have, and because exporters, whose earnings the new trade policy proposed to reward with tradable scrips, needed the rate itself to carry part of the incentive that the abolished cash subsidies had carried before.

Why two steps, and why forty eight hours apart? The documentary accounts converge on a political answer executed through a technical form. A single adjustment of the full amount would have announced itself as a programme and invited a parliamentary and market storm before the trade policy existed to give it meaning; a first step of about 9 per cent could be read, and was briefed, as a technical correction of the basket rate of a kind the Reserve Bank had made before. The interval allowed the government to measure the reaction of markets, of the Fund and of its own party, and it allowed the second step to be folded into the trade policy announcement of the following day, so that the completed devaluation arrived in public as one element of a package rather than as a confession standing alone. The secrecy was correspondingly tight. The decision circle was confined to the Prime Minister, the finance minister and the Reserve Bank's leadership; senior cabinet colleagues learned of the adjustment after it had been executed, a bypass that Jairam Ramesh, who served as an aide in the Prime Minister's Office in those months, records in To the Brink and Back (2015) as a deliberate protection against the Congress old guard's capacity to mobilise against the measure in advance. Ramesh also records the fragility of the second step: Rao wavered on the morning of 3 July, and the hesitation is part of the documentary record of how narrow the political margin under the instruments ran. The devaluation that textbooks present as a statistic was, in its execution, a two day act of nerve management inside a minority government.

### Why was the rupee devalued twice in three days?

The devaluation was split so that the first step, about 9 per cent on 1 July 1991, could pass as a technical adjustment of the basket rate, while the second, about 11 per cent on 3 July, completed the correction beside the trade policy of 4 July, which gave the cheaper rupee its export machinery.

The politics of the adjustment ran in both directions. The opposition parties of the left charged in Parliament that the devaluation had been dictated by the International Monetary Fund as the price of its loans, and the charge had a documented foundation in the Fund's general advocacy of exchange rate correction, which Indian officials did not disguise. The government's defence, made by Manmohan Singh in the budget debate three weeks later, rested on the export arithmetic: an overvalued rupee taxed every exporter and subsidised every import, and the reserves position made the correction a condition of solvency rather than a preference. Jagdish Bhagwati and Arvind Panagariya, in India's Tryst with Destiny (2012), treat the July adjustment as the overdue correction of a rate that had drifted into overvaluation through the late 1980s as domestic inflation outran the inflation of India's trading partners, and they note the design choice that followed from it: because the devaluation was paired within a day with eximscrips, exporters received both a price signal and a tradable asset, a pairing the Fund's standard template did not specify and the M document of 1990 had anticipated. The two-step form, the one day pairing and the documentary prehistory in a 1990 Indian memorandum are the details on which the crisis-response reading of this instrument breaks down; the size of the adjustment was crisis arithmetic, but its form was authored in New Delhi.

The mechanics deserve their paragraph, because the basket system concealed the instrument's hand. The rupee's rate was not a market price in 1991; the Reserve Bank announced it, by reference to the basket whose composition and weights were undisclosed, and commercial banks dealt within a narrow margin around the announced rate. A devaluation inside such a system is a signature, not a slippage: an official in the Reserve Bank's exchange control department recomputed the rate on instruction, and the banking system opened on 1 July and again on 3 July at numbers the state had chosen in private. The unofficial market supplied the verdict on the chosen numbers. The premium commanded by the dollar in the hawala channels through which remittances and trade payments had leaked for years, and which had widened sharply through the crisis winter as holders of rupees paid to exit them, narrowed markedly after the two steps, evidence recorded in the Reserve Bank's own reporting of the period and in the Fund's reviews: the parallel rate and the official rate, whose gap had measured the overvaluation, converged toward the level the devaluation had set. The convergence mattered beyond its arithmetic, because the remittance flows the Gulf crisis had interrupted returned through official channels when the parallel premium ceased to reward the unofficial route, and the reserves recovery of the autumn was built materially on that return. The detail reading files the episode under the same heading as the scrips and the annexes: an instrument aimed at a price, the unofficial premium, that no chronicle of the reforms thinks to name, and whose movement in July 1991 is among the cleanest measurements of the devaluation's credibility with the audience that mattered most, the holders of the country's foreign earnings.

## The Conditionality File: What the Fund Actually Held

Between the devaluation and the trade policy sits the question the whole season argues about, and the conditionality file, read as a file, settles more of it than the polemics on either side allow. The file has three documents. The first is the January 1991 package negotiated by the Chandra Shekhar government: a drawing under the Compensatory and Contingency Financing Facility for the oil shock and a first credit tranche stand-by approved on 18 January 1991 at SDR 551.93 million, together worth about 1.8 billion dollars, obtained on representations about fiscal correction that the interim budget of the spring repeated. The second document is the memorandum of economic policies the Rao government transmitted in August 1991, the letter of intent behind the larger arrangement: it committed the government to the deficit path the budget had already announced, to credit ceilings, to the trade and industrial measures the July texts had already issued, and to further steps in pricing and the financial sector, and it is dated after every one of the four July instruments it is supposed to have dictated. The third document is the arrangement itself, the stand-by approved by the Fund's board on 31 October 1991 for SDR 1,656 million, roughly 2.2 billion dollars, which India drew in full before its expiry on 30 June 1993, alongside a World Bank structural adjustment loan of 500 million dollars approved in December 1991. The World Bank's own programme document of August 1991, prepared to support the borrowing, records the sequence with bureaucratic candour: the authorities had taken the measures, and the institutions were arranging finance behind them.

The file supports a precise conclusion about what conditionality did in 1991. It policed magnitudes after the fact and it supplied the external guarantee that made creditors wait; it did not compose the instruments. The Fund's leverage was real, and the detail reading gains nothing by denying it: without the January drawing, the gold would have been pledged in worse circumstances, and without the October standby, the reserves recovery of the following spring would have run slower and the government's room to refuse the Bombay Club two years later would have been narrower. But leverage over outcomes is not authorship of texts, and the distinction is the one the actors themselves observed. Manmohan Singh's budget speech never cited the Fund as authority for a single clause; the clauses were defended on Indian arithmetic, the deficit, the reserves, the frozen fertiliser prices of a decade. The opposition's dictation charge, pressed in the House in 1991 and in print for years after, had to treat the August memorandum as a script because treating it as a report, a government informing its creditor of measures already taken for reasons already published, dissolved the charge. On the documents, the memorandum is a report. The conditionality file, assembled in order, is the crisis-response framing's best evidence, and read closely it convicts that framing of a dating error: it mistook the guarantee for the deed.

## Feature Two: The Trade Policy of 4 July 1991 and the Invention of the Eximscrip

On 4 July 1991, the day after the devaluation was completed, P. Chidambaram, Minister of State for Commerce, announced the trade policy changes in Parliament. The drafting itself belongs to the detail record: Montek Singh Ahluwalia, the Commerce Secretary, recounted in his memoir Backstage (2020) that he and Chidambaram finalised the proposals in a matter of hours on 3 July, signed the file in quick succession and carried it to the Prime Minister that evening, overruling a senior finance ministry bureaucracy that had not been consulted into agreement, with Manmohan Singh's backing and Rao's trust in his finance minister doing the institutional work that consultation would have delayed. The instrument they produced dismantled the replenishment licensing system through which imports had been rationed since the 1950s and replaced it with a market instrument of a kind Indian trade administration had never operated.

The eximscrip was the centre of the design. Exporters would receive tradable scrips with a face value of 30 per cent of the value of their exports, 40 per cent for a set of favoured products, and those scrips could be used to import any item on the restricted list up to that value, or sold to an importer who needed them. Because the scrips were tradable, a market premium formed on them at once, and the premium did three kinds of work simultaneously. It rewarded the exporter in proportion to scarcity, without a charge on the budget, since the cash compensatory support that exports had previously drawn was abolished alongside, a substitution the budget speech of 24 July priced precisely: the provision for export subsidies fell from an estimated requirement of 4,200 crore rupees to 1,224 crore, a saving of about 3,000 crore. It rationed imports by price rather than by file, since the total value of scrips was tied to export earnings and excess import demand raised the premium instead of exhausting a quota. And it created, inside a still controlled regime, a discoverable price for foreign exchange access, a halfway house the policy's authors openly described as transitional: the government announced its intention to move to full convertibility of the rupee on the current account within three to five years, a horizon the event outran, since the scrips were replaced by the dual exchange rate of March 1992 and the rate was unified a year after that.

Around the scrip, the 4 July package cut the administrative apparatus down to a fraction of its former reach. Imports that had required licences became freely importable against scrips for the bulk of the restricted list, which shrank to a short negative list of specified goods. Decanalisation freed sixteen export items and fourteen import items from the canalising agencies through which trade in them had been compelled to pass. Advance licensing, the duty exemption route for export production, was simplified with licences to issue within fifteen days and the supporting documents reduced from nine to about four. Trading houses received an enlarged role. Each of these clauses is small; together they reversed the operating principle of Indian trade administration, from prior permission to post facto price. The principle had been designed, in the M document and in the Planning Commission discussions of 1990 that Ahluwalia's memoir and the scholarly literature record, before the Fund's standby existed, and it was defended in those terms by its authors when the dictation charge was pressed: the Fund supported the scrips after announcement, Ahluwalia notes, and had not asked for them. For the detail reading, the trade policy is the strongest single exhibit, because its central instrument is unintelligible as a dictated measure. Conditionality specifies outcomes, a deficit number, a rate correction, a ceiling. It does not specify a tradable certificate at 30 per cent with a premium mechanism and a convertibility horizon, and the presence of that authored machinery in the text is what a reading of the reforms must explain.

The scrips traded at a premium from their first weeks, and the premium's behaviour is itself evidence in the record, reported in the financial press of July and August 1991 and analysed in the scholarly literature the episode generated. A premium in the range of a fifth to a third of face value told exporters that the market priced access to restricted imports far above the official tariff structure's pretensions, and it told the government that the quantity of scrips, yoked to export earnings, would expand or contract with trade rather than with a bureaucrat's estimate of need. Importers who had queued for licences from the Chief Controller of Imports and Exports discovered that the binding constraint on their purchases had become a price they could pay or decline, and the corruption market that licensing had sustained, in which the licence itself was the asset, lost its underlying instrument. The decanalisation clauses worked more quietly to the same end: petroleum products, fertilisers, edible oils and metals had moved through canalising agencies such as the State Trading Corporation and the Indian Oil Corporation by administrative right, and the removal of sixteen export items and fourteen import items from that compulsion in a single announcement redrew the boundary between the state's trading arm and the private trader without a debate on the principle, because the announcement was drafted as a schedule rather than as a doctrine. The detail reading lingers on that drafting choice. Schedules are how the July government moved principles past a Parliament and a party that would have fought the principles under their own names.

## Feature Three: The Statement on Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991, Clause by Clause

The third instrument was laid before Parliament on 24 July 1991, the same day as the budget, and its placement in the calendar was itself a decision: the industrial statement carried the structural headline of the day while the budget carried the fiscal arithmetic, and the two texts were debated as one event. The Statement on Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 is the most quoted and least closely read of the July documents. Its operative clauses repay enumeration, because the delicensing it announced was bounded, and the bounds are the politics.

Industrial licensing was abolished for every industry except the eighteen listed in the statement's second annex, retained on grounds the statement grouped as security, strategic concern and social protection. The eighteen were coal and lignite; petroleum other than crude and its distillation products; distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks; sugar; animal fats and oils; cigars and cigarettes of tobacco and manufactured tobacco substitutes; asbestos and asbestos based products; plywood, decorative veneers and other wood based products including particle board and block board; raw hides and skins, leather, chamois leather and patent leather; tanned or dressed furskins; paper and newsprint except bagasse based units; electronic aerospace and defence equipment; industrial explosives, including detonating fuse, safety fuse, gun powder, nitrocellulose and matches; hazardous chemicals; drugs and pharmaceuticals, governed by the drug policy; entertainment electronics; white goods such as domestic refrigerators, dishwashers, washing machines, microwave ovens and air conditioners; and arms, ammunition and allied defence equipment together with atomic energy and the minerals named in the schedule to the Atomic Energy Order of 1953. For every industry outside that list, the licence was replaced by a filing: the entrepreneur submitted an Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum to the Secretariat for Industrial Approvals and could proceed. Location rules survived in softened form, with units in cities of more than a million people steered to sites 25 kilometres from the municipal boundary except for electronics, software and printing. The phased manufacturing programmes, which had compelled new projects to indigenise components on a state dictated timetable, were abolished outright, and the mandatory convertibility clause in term lending, which had let financial institutions convert loans into equity by right, was dropped for new loans.

The statement's second surgery was on the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act of 1969. What the statement abolished was not the Act but its asset gate: the requirement that companies crossing an asset threshold, set at 100 crore rupees since 1985, obtain prior government clearance for expansion, diversification, new undertakings, amalgamation and takeover. That pre-entry scrutiny, the statement announced, would go, and the Act would be amended to remove the relevant provisions and to concentrate the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Commission on investigating monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices after the fact. The precision matters to the detail reading. A government abolishing the MRTP Act wholesale would have needed a bill, a debate and a majority it did not possess. A government announcing the removal of an asset test, with amending legislation to follow in due course, changed investment behaviour from the day of the statement, because no board any longer priced an expansion against a clearance that the government had declared dead. The annexes reward a slower reading, because they are a political cartography of what the government of 1991 believed it could not yet touch. Alcoholic drinks stayed licensed because state excise on liquor was a fiscal pillar no Union statement could spend. Sugar stayed because the sugar cooperatives of Maharashtra and the mill politics of Uttar Pradesh were load bearing walls of the ruling party's rural organisation. White goods and entertainment electronics stayed, the colour televisions and refrigerators of the swadeshi speeches, because the consumer goods industries were the visible front line of the foreign invasion the opposition had promised to resist, and the government chose to hold that line on goods whose import content was symbolic while freeing the capital goods whose import content was productive. Hazardous chemicals stayed in the shadow of Bhopal, seven years after the disaster, when no Indian government could have delicensed the chemical industry and survived the headline. Defence equipment, atomic energy and the strategic minerals stayed because the security state's core was not negotiable in a statement of industrial policy. Each retention is dated, explicable and political, and the list as a whole is the negative image of the delicensing it bounds: the reform's authors freed what they could defend in the House and fenced what they could not, which is why the annex has eighteen entries rather than none or eighty. The third annex shows the same hand in positive form. Its thirty four priority industries for automatic foreign equity were concentrated in the economy's input producing core, metallurgical industries, boilers and prime movers, electrical and transport equipment, industrial machinery, chemicals and fertilisers, drugs and pharmaceuticals, and agro based and food processing industries, the sectors whose expansion the planners had once pursued by licence and the reformers pursued by invitation. What the annexes did not touch is as instructive: the reservation of hundreds of products for the small scale sector survived the statement intact, and delicensing proceeded for a decade alongside a reservation regime that the Abid Hussain committee of 1997 would later recommend dismantling, a bound the detail reading records because the reform's incompleteness was chosen in the same clauses as its reach.

The foreign investment clauses worked the same way. Automatic approval, routed through the Reserve Bank, was granted for foreign equity up to 51 per cent in the thirty four high priority industries of the statement's third annex; proposals beyond the annex or the ceiling went to a reconstituted Foreign Investment Promotion Board charged with negotiation rather than mere scrutiny. Foreign technology agreements in the priority industries received automatic approval within set royalty ceilings, 5 per cent on domestic sales and 8 per cent on exports with a lump sum limit, and the hiring of foreign technicians and the testing of Indian products abroad were freed from clearance altogether.

For the public sector, the statement cut the list of industries reserved for state ownership from seventeen to eight: arms and ammunition and allied defence items, atomic energy, coal and lignite, mineral oils, the mining of the principal metallic ores and of gold and diamond, the minerals of the Atomic Energy schedule, and railway transport. Chronically sick public enterprises were to be referred to the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction under an amended Sick Industrial Companies Act, on the same discipline the private sector faced. Government shareholding in selected public enterprises would be disinvested up to 20 per cent, offered to mutual funds, financial institutions, workers and the general public. The statement, read whole, is not a withdrawal of the state but a re-specification of its instruments, from permission to referral, from reservation to a shortened list, from scrutiny before entry to investigation after conduct. Its lineage runs through the pillar's earlier settlements: against [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/), in which licensing had been the planning system's hand on industry, and against [the political-acts framework](/2020/11/26/indira-nationalisations-political-acts/) of the nationalisation era, in which the state had reached for ownership itself. The 1991 statement reversed both gestures with a document of a few pages, laid on the table of a House its authors did not control, and it survived because its clauses mostly executed themselves. Researchers who read the statement in the form in which it circulates, as scanned gazette and ministry files, often work through document tools such as a [ppt viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) to move between the statement and the budget speech of the same day; the pairing is the right instinct, because the two texts of 24 July were designed as one act in two registers, and the industrial statement is unintelligible at full strength without the fiscal text that funded it.

### Did the July 1991 statement abolish the licence raj in a single day?

The statement abolished the industrial licence for every industry outside its eighteen item annex, and replaced the licence with a memorandum filing, but the licence raj as a system of permissions in trade, foreign exchange and finance was dismantled across the following decade rather than on 24 July 1991 alone.

## Feature Four: The Budget of 24 July 1991 and Its Fiscal Architecture

The budget of 24 July was the second budget exercise of the calendar year, and the fact belongs to its detail. The Chandra Shekhar government, caretaker by March 1991, had taken a vote on account and left the full budget unpresented; the election and the assassination then consumed the spring. The Rao government therefore inherited a fiscal year already a third spent, financed by ordinances and interim appropriations, with the subsidy bills of the winter and the interest bills of the borrowing decade falling due inside its own first quarter. A July budget is a truncated instrument: its tax changes run for eight months, its expenditure plans inherit four months of commitments it did not make, and its author must ask a House elected on another government's record to underwrite a correction whose pain is immediate and whose authorship is contested. Singh's speech acknowledges the truncation in its structure, front loading the measures that needed no collection machinery, the customs ceilings, the subsidy notifications issued the same evening, and deferring the tax system redesign to a promised exercise in structural reform of taxation that the Chelliah committee, appointed the following month under Raja Chelliah, would supply in its reports of 1992 and 1993. The detail reading notes the appointment because it completes the budget's pattern: even where the July text could not finish a reform, it installed the instrument, a dated committee with a named chairman and a reporting deadline, through which the reform would arrive.

The budget Manmohan Singh presented to the Lok Sabha on the afternoon of 24 July 1991 was the only July instrument that had to be voted, and its text carries the double character of a fiscal plan and a survival document. It opened with the stabilisation arithmetic. The Union government's fiscal deficit had reached 8.4 per cent of GDP in 1990-91; the budget proposed to bring it to 6.5 per cent in 1991-92, through expenditure restraint and the subsidy reductions detailed below, and Singh told the House that the alternative to correction was a default whose costs would fall hardest on the poor. The speech is explicit that the correction was to be borne substantially by the state itself: the provision for export subsidies, as noted in the trade policy discussion, fell from an estimated requirement of 4,200 crore rupees to 1,224 crore because the exchange rate adjustment and the eximscrip expansion had replaced cash support with price support, a saving of about 3,000 crore booked inside the deficit target.

The customs proposals carried the budget's trade signature. Indian import duties, Singh told the House, had risen over decades of protection and revenue hunger until prescribed rates for some items stood above 300 per cent. The budget proposed to cut the ad valorem rate of basic plus auxiliary customs duty to a maximum of 150 per cent wherever it stood higher, eliminating every tariff peak above that ceiling, with only imported alcoholic beverages and passenger baggage excepted, at a revenue cost of 132 crore rupees in a full year. It also repaired an anomaly of the crisis winter: the auxiliary duty increases imposed across the board on 15 December 1990 by the predecessor government had raised some rates by twenty points and others by five, and the budget gave a relief of ten percentage points to nearly every item that had taken the twenty point increase, with full rollback to pre-December rates for a set of items tied to environment, exports and fuel saving. One correction of the record belongs here, because the budget's customs numbers are often misstated in summary accounts: the 24 July 1991 budget set the peak at 150 per cent, and the further reduction of the peak to 110 per cent came a year later, in the budget of 1992-93. Compressing the two steps into the single famous day flatters the day and falsifies the sequence; the detail reading exists to resist exactly that compression.

The subsidy clauses carried the budget's domestic signature, and they were drafted as instruments rather than as gestures. Low analysis fertilisers, calcium ammonium nitrate, ammonium chloride, ammonium sulphate and sulphate of potash, were freed from price and movement controls with effect from that evening, the first decontrol of a fertiliser category in the postwar system. The prices of all other fertilisers rose by an average of 40 per cent, the first increase of any kind since July 1981, and for single super phosphate a ceiling on the subsidy payable per tonne was imposed to push high cost units toward efficiency and eventual deregulation. Singh justified the increase in the speech by the decade of frozen prices against continuously rising procurement prices for paddy and wheat, and he promised compensation to farmers through those procurement prices. Petrol and cooking gas prices were raised in the same fiscal package. Direct taxes were left substantially alone; the budget's revenue work was done by the customs rationalisation being deliberately revenue negative, by the subsidy cuts and by the deficit target's discipline on expenditure, an architecture that inverted the usual budget logic of taxing more to spend as before. Over the whole text stood the closing passage that fixed the speech in public memory: Singh quoted Victor Hugo's dictum that no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come, proposed the emergence of India as a major economic power as one such idea, and ended with a declaration that India was wide awake and would prevail and overcome. Vinay Sitapati's Half-Lion (2016), the biography of Rao built on the Prime Minister's papers, treats the speech as the moment the government's programme acquired a public philosophy, and the detail reading concurs with a qualification: the philosophy persuaded because the clauses beneath it were real, dated and priced, and a House that had heard rhetoric for four decades could check every number in the printed speech against the annexes.

Two further clauses of the budget deserve extraction from the annexes, because they show the fiscal text thinking institutionally about its own consequences. The first is the safety net promise. Singh told the House that structural adjustment would displace workers in sick industry and that the government would build a protective mechanism for them, a commitment institutionalised in February 1992 as the National Renewal Fund, financed in part from the budget and charged with retraining and redeployment assistance and with the voluntary retirement settlements that the public sector's restructuring required. A finance minister cutting subsidies in a crisis budget took care to write the compensation instrument into the same fiscal architecture, and the fund's existence, however inadequate its later record, marks the boundary the government drew between stabilisation it would defend and abandonment it would not. The second clause is the budget's candour about monetisation. The speech acknowledged that deficits of the inherited scale had been financed by the Reserve Bank's absorption of government paper through ad hoc treasury bills, converting fiscal excess into money creation and inflation, and the deficit target of 6.5 per cent was framed as the first step in breaking that circuit, a commitment the decade completed when the ad hoc treasury bill system was phased out by agreement between the government and the Reserve Bank in 1994 and abolished in 1997. The budget's fiscal architecture, in short, aimed at the machine that had produced the deficit and not only at the deficit, which is the detail reading's claim about the July instruments stated at the level of a single text.

### Was the budget of July 1991 dictated by the International Monetary Fund?

The standby arrangement that formalised Fund support was approved on 31 October 1991, three months after the budget, and the design documents for the July measures, Ahluwalia's memorandum of 1990 and the trade package drafted in the Commerce Ministry, predate the programme and were written by Indian officials.

The budget's author understood that a voted text lives or dies in its public defence, and the record of the defence is part of the instrument's history. On 25 July 1991, the day after the presentation, Manmohan Singh made an unscheduled appearance at the press conference on his own budget, an irregular step Jairam Ramesh records was taken to keep the budget's message from being distorted by officials less than enthusiastic in its support, and he defended the proposals there under the description he gave them, a budget with a human face, walking the assembled correspondents through the fertiliser increase, the petroleum prices and the deficit arithmetic clause by clause. The phrase and the performance together mark the settlement's rhetorical strategy: the government would concede the pain in public, price it in public, and argue in public that the alternative was worse, reserving its silence for the machinery, the sequencing, the bypassed consultations and the Prime Minister's management of his own party, where silence was load bearing. A reform programme remembered for a single quotation is usually remembered wrong; the quotation was the advertisement, and the clauses were the product, and Singh's conduct in the week of the budget, peroration in the House on the twenty fourth and arithmetic at the press table on the twenty fifth, shows the author keeping the two in their assigned places.

## The Fertiliser Fight: Where the Instrument Strategy Met Its Limit

The budget's authority lasted eleven days before its own party tested it, and the test is the most instructive episode in the July record. The 40 per cent fertiliser increase struck the constituency the Congress least wished to tax in an election year barely concluded, and the disquiet inside the Congress Parliamentary Party was organised enough to force a sequence of meetings that Jairam Ramesh chronicles in To the Brink and Back (2015) from inside the Prime Minister's Office. Rao convened the parliamentary party on 1 August 1991 and, in Ramesh's account, stayed away from the first meeting to let members vent their anger freely at the finance minister, who faced the criticism largely alone; only two members of Parliament, Mani Shankar Aiyar and Nathuram Mirdha, defended the budget wholeheartedly across the meetings of 1, 2 and 3 August. The Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs met on 4 and 5 August to settle the form of retreat. On 6 August 1991 Singh made a statement in the Lok Sabha: the fertiliser increase was scaled down from 40 per cent to 30 per cent, a rollback of ten percentage points, the interests of small and marginal farmers were protected within the scaled structure, an exemption the Agriculture Ministry confirmed in Parliament the following month, and the decontrol of the low analysis fertilisers and the increases in petrol and cooking gas prices stood untouched.

The episode is usually filed as a footnote of weakness. The detail reading files it as the control case that proves the argument about instrument choice. Every July measure that could be executed by a central bank adjustment, a ministry announcement or a statement laid on the table survived intact, because none of them returned to the House for a decision. The one measure embedded in a votable text, carried by the finance bill through a Parliament in which the government held 232 seats and depended on abstentions and absences, was trimmed within a fortnight by the government's own benches. The rollback's shape confirms the reading: what the party forced was not the abandonment of the price correction, which remained at 30 per cent against a decade of frozen prices, nor of the decontrol, which survived whole, but a visible concession priced at ten points, calibrated to let Congress members tell their districts that the party had been heard. Prices, percentages and exemptions were the currency of the negotiation because the reforms had been written as prices, percentages and exemptions from the start. A reading of 1991 that begins from the documents predicts the fertiliser fight's outcome in advance; a reading that begins from ideology, whether of conversion or of surrender, is surprised by it every time.

## Four Offices: The Men Who Signed the Instruments

The detail reading's insistence on authorship requires a roll of the authors, institution by institution, because the July settlement was executed by four offices whose incumbents' formation shaped the clauses they signed. The Reserve Bank of India was held by S. Venkitaramanan, a career administrator who had been finance secretary before his appointment as Governor in December 1990, and whose tenure, running to December 1992, spans the entire crisis arc from the first Fund drawing to the unified exchange rate's eve. Venkitaramanan executed the gold operations under two governments, executed the two-step devaluation under the second, and defended the Bank's conduct before the Joint Parliamentary Committee that investigated the securities scandal; the continuity of his signature across the regime change is the institutional form of the article's argument that stabilisation was a state act, not a party act. The finance ministry was held by Manmohan Singh, the only finance minister of the century who had previously governed the central bank, and whose budget speech reads as it does, priced, dated and annexed, because its author had spent a career inside the machinery his clauses rearranged. The commerce ministry, which produced the season's most original instrument, was held at the political level by P. Chidambaram as Minister of State, a lawyer in his first ministerial tenure, and at the official level by Montek Singh Ahluwalia as Commerce Secretary, the M document's author carrying his own 1990 draft into execution; the pairing of a new minister with the design's draftsman is why the trade policy took hours to finalise on 3 July rather than weeks. Above the four offices sat the Prime Minister's, where Rao, holding the industries portfolio himself, performed the settlement's indispensable political labour: absorbing the party's anger in the parliamentary party meetings of August, protecting the finance minister's text against amendment by exhaustion, and timing the instruments so that each fait accompli raised the cost of reversing the next. Vinay Sitapati's account of Rao in Half-Lion (2016) documents the method in the Prime Minister's own papers: silence in public, arithmetic in private, and a refusal to spend the government's thin majority on any vote that an executive instrument could spare it. The four offices and the chair above them are the answer the detail reading gives to the question of agency. The reforms were not made by the crisis, or by the Fund, or by an abstraction called the government; they were made by a governor, two ministers, a secretary and a prime minister, each of whose prior formation is recoverable from the record, and each of whose choices is preserved in a text a reader can open.

## The Contest in Parliament and Industry: Reception as Evidence

The July texts were contested from the benches and from the boardrooms, and the contest's documentary residue is evidence a detail reading can weigh, because every objector objected to clauses, and the clauses they chose reveal what the texts actually did. In the Lok Sabha budget debate of late July and August 1991, the parties of the left, the Communist Party of India (Marxist) and its allies, pressed the dictation charge in its parliamentary form: the programme, their speakers argued, had been negotiated with the Fund and the World Bank before it was presented to the House, and the devaluation and the subsidy cuts were its instalments. The Bharatiya Janata Party's economic spokesmen pressed a different objection, anchored in the party's swadeshi doctrine: the budget and the industrial statement, they argued, surrendered the commanding ground of the economy to foreign capital at the moment of maximum national weakness, and the 51 per cent automatic route was named as the clause that proved it. Within the Congress, the opposition took the form chronicled in the fertiliser fight, an objection not to the programme's direction but to its incidence, pressed by members who had to defend a 40 per cent price rise in districts where the party's organisation ran through the cooperative and procurement networks the price rise taxed. The government's defence, conducted by Singh in the debate and by Rao in the party forums, stayed on the documents: the deficit figure, the reserves figure, the export subsidy substitution, the Hugo peroration. No participant in the debate described the programme as a mood or a conversion. They fought over percentages, annexes and exemptions, and a reading of the reforms that fights over the same objects is aligned with the actors' own understanding of what had been done to them.

Industry's reception supplies the second body of evidence, and its most cited artifact arrived two years later. The large industrial houses that had grown inside the licensing system discovered in 1991 that delicensing cut both ways: it freed their own expansion from the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices asset gate, and it freed foreign competitors and new domestic entrants from the licence barrier that had kept the incumbents' markets closed. By November 1993 the ambivalence had acquired a name and a document. A group of senior industrialists, among them Rahul Bajaj of Bajaj Auto and Hari Shankar Singhania, presented the finance minister with the memorandum the press named for the city of its signatories, the Bombay Club memorandum, asking for a level playing field against multinational entrants: time to adjust, protection against predatory acquisition, and joint venture disciplines that would let Indian houses negotiate from strength. Singh received the delegation and declined the memorandum's core request, and the exchange is a fixed point in the record because it dates the moment Indian capital understood that the July settlement was not a favour done for it but a regime imposed on it, under which incumbency itself had lost its state guarantee. The Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry's resolutions of the period oscillate in the same direction, welcoming delicensing and the exchange correction while pleading for sequencing on imports, and the oscillation is the sound of a constituency being re-founded by a text. For the adjudication that follows, the reception evidence performs a precise service: a programme dictated from outside for the benefit of foreign capital would not have drawn a defensive memorandum from domestic capital within twenty eight months, and a programme that was mere chronicle would not have reorganised the political demands of every constituency it touched. The detail reading predicts both the memorandum and the oscillation, because it holds that the clauses were real transfers of advantage, legible to the transferred.

## Feature Five: The Trajectory in Statute, from SEBI to FEMA

The fifth feature is the July settlement's conversion into durable law, and its three stations show the same instrument intelligence at work over a longer arc. The first station was the securities market. The Securities and Exchange Board of India had existed since 12 April 1988 as an administrative body without statutory power, a referee whose whistle no statute backed, at precisely the moment when delicensed industry was about to raise capital from the public on an unprecedented scale. The government gave SEBI statutory force by ordinance on 30 January 1992, and the Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, Act 15 of 1992, received presidential assent on 4 April 1992 and took effect from 30 January 1992, with G. V. Ramakrishna, who had chaired the board since 1990, as its first statutory chairman. The Capital Issues (Control) Act, the 1947 statute under which the Controller of Capital Issues had fixed the price of every public issue, was repealed in May 1992, and issuers moved to free pricing under SEBI's disclosure regime. Two corrections of the compressed record belong to this station, and the detail reading makes them without embarrassment, since its authority rests on dates. The SEBI Act belongs to the ordinance of 30 January 1992, not to any January the twelfth; and the chairman whose name belongs to the statutory founding is Ramakrishna, the securities regulator of the founding years, while C. B. Bhave's chairmanship of SEBI belongs to 2008 to 2011, a later consolidation. The sequence of form also deserves notice: ordinance first, statute after, the executive instrument again preceding the parliamentary one by the interval a minority government needed.

The statutory founding was stress tested within weeks. The securities scandal that broke in April 1992, when the journalist Sucheta Dalal reported in the Times of India that the broker Harshad Mehta had financed a share price ramp with funds diverted from the banking system's ready forward deals in government securities, detonated inside the market the July reforms had just deregulated. Prices that SEBI's disclosure regime was meant to discipline had been rigged through the interstices of the banking system, and the political charge wrote itself: liberalisation had licensed a scam. The government's answer ran through the new statutory machinery rather than around it. A Joint Parliamentary Committee under Ram Niwas Mirdha investigated through 1992 and 1993 and reported in December 1993, distributing blame across the Reserve Bank's supervision, the finance ministry's oversight and the banks' internal controls; the Reserve Bank tightened the securities transaction rules the scandal had exposed; and SEBI, under Ramakrishna and from 1995 under D. R. Mehta, used the crisis to extract the regulatory depth the founding Act had sketched, over brokers, over depositories in due course and over the takeover code of 1994. The episode belongs in the fifth feature's evidence because it demonstrates the property the detail reading attributes to the July settlement as a whole: its institutions were built to be used under fire, and the fire, when it came, was fought with the instruments the settlement had created rather than with a restoration of the control regime the scandal's critics demanded. The Controller of Capital Issues was not recalled to fix prices. That refusal, repeated at each stress point of the decade, is the trajectory's real content.

The second and third stations concern the foreign exchange statute whose initials frame the era. The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973 had been written for scarcity: it treated the holding of foreign exchange as a privilege under licence, criminalised violations, and pressed companies with substantial foreign equity, the so-called FERA companies, toward dilution to 40 per cent. The July 1991 statement's automatic route to 51 per cent foreign equity in thirty four industries was already a contradiction of FERA's spirit operating inside FERA's letter, managed through Reserve Bank permissions. The Foreign Exchange Regulation (Amendment) Act of 1993 regularised the contradiction, stripping out much of the 1973 Act's restrictive core, easing the treatment of foreign companies, of non-resident investment and of property holding, and retiring the operative force of the FERA company category. The replacement came at the decade's end: the Foreign Exchange Management Act, Act 42 of 1999, received assent in December 1999 and came into force on 1 June 2000, repealing FERA outright. Its preamble states the inversion in so many words, an Act to consolidate and amend the law relating to foreign exchange with the objective of facilitating external trade and payments and promoting the orderly development of the foreign exchange market, where FERA's stated object had been the conservation of a scarce resource. Under FEMA, violations became civil offences, compoundable on payment, where FERA had sent inspectors and prosecutors; regulation gave way to management in the statute's very title. The trajectory from the July permissions to the 1993 amendments to the 2000 replacement is the fifth feature's content, and it completes the pattern the first four features establish: the political decision of July 1991 was executed first through instruments that needed no statute, and the statutes arrived afterward, across three parliaments and two changes of governing coalition, without any of them reversing the direction. The exchange rate stations mark the same rhythm: the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of 1 March 1992, announced in the budget of 1992-93, surrendered 40 per cent of export and remittance receipts at the official rate and let 60 per cent convert at the market rate; the unified, market determined rate followed on 1 March 1993; and India accepted the obligations of Article VIII of the Fund's Articles in August 1994, formalising the current account convertibility the trade policy of 4 July 1991 had promised within three to five years and delivered in three.

## The Settlement in the States and the Questions It Deferred

One consequence of delicensing escaped the July texts' own framing and belongs to the record of their operation: the relocation of industrial politics from New Delhi to the state capitals. Under licensing, the Union government had allocated industrial location as an act of policy, steering plants to backward districts and balancing states against each other through the licensing committee's discretion. When the licence died, the discretion died with it, and the states discovered that investment they had once petitioned the Centre to send them was theirs to attract. Through 1992 and 1993 the larger industrial states answered with policy documents of their own, packages of sales tax deferrals, power tariff concessions, land banks and single window clearance agencies, and the competition among Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka for the memoranda being filed in Delhi became a standing feature of the decade's federal politics. The detail reading counts the shift among the settlement's largest effects, because it was produced by a deletion: the July statement created a federal market for industrial location by removing the central allocator, and no subsequent government, whatever its federal rhetoric, restored the allocator's chair. The three tensions framework catches the change precisely: who governs whom was renegotiated not only between the state and the market but between the Union and the states, inside a clause that said nothing about federalism at all.

The deferred questions are equally legible in the texts. The July statement left the small scale reservation untouched, left labour law untouched, and left the exit of unviable firms to the slow machinery of the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction, whose queues became the settlement's acknowledged scandal through the 1990s. The budget's safety net, the National Renewal Fund of February 1992, carried the human cost the programme admitted, financing voluntary retirement and retraining for workers in restructured public enterprises from budgetary grants and external assistance, and its settlements ran into tens of thousands of workers across the decade's first half. Critics on the left named the pattern exactly: a reform that moved at speed where capital was concerned and at the Board's pace where labour was concerned had chosen its velocities, and the choice was political. The detail reading concedes the observation and locates it where it belongs, in the clauses: the July authors wrote fast instruments for the constraints that threatened the state's solvency and slow instruments for the constraints that threatened its coalition, and the asymmetry is the settlement's most honest self description. A reading that begins from the documents has no need to deny it, because the documents announce it.

## What the Detail Changed on the Ground, 1991 to 1993

A reading that rests on texts must show the texts biting into outcomes, and the two years after July supply the bite in measurable form. The reserves figure is the first control. Foreign currency assets, a little above one billion dollars in June 1991, had recovered to above five billion dollars by March 1992, and the pledged gold, the emergency measure, had been redeemed inside the same interval, because the standby of October 1991, the remittance recovery as Gulf workers' earnings resumed, and the export earnings the devaluation and the scrips had priced in, refilled the treasury the July instruments had emptied of obligations. The second control is the collapse of the licensing workload itself. The Directorate General of Technical Development's queues and the Secretariat for Industrial Approvals' licensing files, which had measured industrial life in clearances granted and refused, ceased to measure anything for the delicensed majority: entrepreneurs filed memoranda in the thousands in the months after 24 July, and the state's knowledge of the industrial economy, formerly a ledger of permissions, became a register of filings. Foreign investment approvals tell the same story from the other side of the annex: proposals that would have died in the Foreign Investment Board's discretionary file before July arrived in the automatic route for thirty four industries after it, and foreign direct investment, negligible in the licence years, began its climb through the mid 1990s toward a scale the 1991 texts made imaginable rather than merely possible.

The complications of the record must not be smoothed. Inflation, which had run in double digits through 1991 and peaked in the autumn, fell only slowly, and the government of 1991-92 spent much of its first budget year defending the subsidy structure it had left standing after the fertiliser rollback. Export earnings in dollar terms actually fell in 1991-92, because the Soviet Union's dissolution in December 1991 destroyed the rupee payment trade that had carried a large share of Indian engineering exports, a shock no July text had priced, and because the devaluation's export response lagged the import compression it imposed at once. The current account deficit, which had run at 3 per cent of GDP in 1990-91, narrowed only gradually, and the fiscal deficit target of 6.5 per cent was met in the breach more than in the letter as the fertiliser settlement and the safety net commitments ate into the correction. A detail reading welcomes these complications, because they are specified rather than atmospheric: the export fall has a date and a cause, the deficit slippage has a price in percentage points, and neither contradicts the mechanism the texts describe, which predicted adjustment costs and priced them in the budget's own annexes. What the complications do not support is the counter-reading that the July instruments failed on their own terms, since the terms themselves were institutional rather than conjunctural: the memorandum filing survived the inflation, the scrips gave way to the exchange rate on schedule, the tariff ceiling moved down on schedule, and the statutes arrived on schedule. Outcomes wobbled. Instruments held. The two budgets that followed the famous one executed the first budget's schedule in the open, and the schedule's survival under annual parliamentary scrutiny is the strongest single reply to the claim that July 1991 was an emergency improvisation. The budget of 1992-93 cut the peak customs rate from 150 per cent to 110 per cent, the step summary accounts misassign to 1991, and announced the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System that replaced the eximscrips on 1 March 1992; the budget of 1993-94 accompanied the unified exchange rate of 1 March 1993 and the acceptance, the following year, of current account convertibility, retiring the transitional instruments in the order the trade policy of 4 July 1991 had forecast. An improvisation does not keep its own timetable across three budgets and two finance bill debates; a design does, and the timetable was kept by the same hands, Rao's government and Singh's finance ministry, through the no confidence motion of July 1993 that the government survived by the narrowest margin of its tenure. The detail reading rests its case on that continuity as much as on the July texts themselves.

The approval statistics of the first full year complete the control set. Foreign investment proposals, which under the FERA era's discretion had arrived in a thin file and waited on interministerial circulation, arrived after August 1991 in volumes the Secretariat for Industrial Approvals had to publish as running statistics: approvals for foreign collaboration and equity in the first year of the settlement exceeded, several times over, the annual averages of the late 1980s, and the reconstituted Foreign Investment Promotion Board, housed initially in the Prime Minister's Office so that large proposals met a single negotiating table rather than a circulation of files, cleared cases the automatic route did not reach. The pattern held through the change of finance ministers that never came and the change of governments that did: the Board outlived the Rao government, migrating to the finance ministry under its successors, and the automatic route's annex grew by amendment as industries the statement had fenced were opened in the later 1990s, each amendment a small dated instrument of the same family as the parent text. The detail reading's claim is finally an archival one. The settlement of 1991 can be audited, because its authors wrote it in instruments whose operation generated statistics, files and statutes, and the audit, conducted on the documents assembled in this article, returns the verdict the five features promised: a designed programme, executed by named officials through chosen forms, inside a crisis it did not choose and against opponents it answered clause by clause.

## Three Readings Adjudicated: Chronicle, Dictation and Detail

The named disagreement this article must adjudicate can be stated with the evidence assembled. The policy-history framing, the default of textbooks and of the reference accounts against which this article competes, narrates the July measures as a sequence of correct responses and evaluates them by their outcomes; its weakness is that a chronicle cannot say why the measures took the forms they took, why a devaluation arrived in two steps of 9 and 11 rather than one of 19, why a trade policy invented a tradable scrip, or why the only retreat of the season fell on the one instrument that had to be voted. The crisis-response framing, stated at its strongest by the reforms' parliamentary and scholarly critics in 1991 and after, among them Prabhat Patnaik in his Economic and Political Weekly interventions of the period, reads the programme as Fund conditionality executed under duress, and points to real evidence: the January 1991 drawings, the gold, the standby of October 1991 with its performance criteria. Its weakness is chronological and textual. The design file predates the duress, in the M document of 1990; the standby postdates the July instruments; the scrip, the two step form and the annexed bounds of delicensing bear no conditionality fingerprint, since Fund programmes specify fiscal and monetary magnitudes, not annexes of eighteen industries; and the dictation reading cannot explain why the same crisis produced gold pledges from two governments but structural texts from only one. Bhagwati and Panagariya's India's Tryst with Destiny (2012) supplies the defence's strongest form, that the ideas were Indian, long prepared and long blocked by politics rather than by ignorance, and Sitapati's Half-Lion (2016) supplies the political machinery, Rao's protection of his finance minister and his management of a party that had not been consulted into consent. On the primary evidence assembled in this article, the adjudication goes to the detail reading: crisis set the magnitudes, dictation is not required to explain them, and the forms, which are the politics, were authored by named Indian officials working a prepared design through the narrow institutional channels a minority government commanded. That is the sense in which the July 1991 reforms operationalise [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/) at the level of the clause, and in which their detail, rather than their legend, is the durable object of study, continuous with [the post-1991 institutional-political context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/) that later governments inherited and with [the political-architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/11/20/nehruvian-planned-economy-reanalysed/) against which the planned economy's own instruments were built.

The scholarly literature that grew around the episode divides along the same seam, and two of its strongest interventions sharpen the verdict. Rob Jenkins, in Democratic Politics and Economic Reform in India (1999), named the government's method reform by stealth: the Rao administration, on his account, deliberately depoliticised its own programme, dispersing contentious measures across committees, state governments and executive instruments, and compensating threatened interests quietly rather than confronting them in open legislative battle. The stealth thesis is the detail reading's closest scholarly kin and its necessary correction: where this article reads the instrument choices as design, Jenkins reads them as camouflage, and the two readings divide on intention rather than on evidence. On the evidence assembled here, the adjudication between them goes by a date. A government practising pure stealth does not publish its programme in a budget speech ending with Victor Hugo and a declaration of national awakening, debated on the floor of the House within the month; the July instruments were camouflaged in form and advertised in philosophy, and the combination, executive execution with public peroration, is what the detail reading alone predicts. Atul Kohli's assessments, from his work on the politics of economic liberalisation in the 1990s onward, press from the other side: the reforms, on his account, were a business friendly turn executed by a state that had aligned itself with organised capital, and their detail is the record of that alignment. The reception evidence assembled in this article answers him at one point: the Bombay Club memorandum of November 1993 is not the document of an aligned constituency but of a displaced one, and a state captured by its incumbent industrial houses does not refuse their first collective request. Where Kohli's reading stands is on incidence rather than authorship, on who gained as the growth years came, and the detail reading does not dispute incidence; it disputes only the claim that incidence explains the texts, which were drafted before the gains could be known and against the stated preference of the houses best placed to collect them.

One more named position completes the adjudication's map, because it judges the texts by a standard neither chronicle nor dictation nor design supplies. Amartya Sen and Jean Dreze, in India: Economic Development and Social Opportunity (1995), granted the July reforms their stabilisation logic and indicted their silence: the instruments of 1991 addressed the state's licensing and fiscal machinery while the complementary investments in literacy, public health and land relations that East Asia had paired with market opening went unbuilt, and the growth the reforms released would run ahead of the human capabilities needed to share it. The indictment is the strongest of the three critical positions precisely because it concedes authorship and disputes priorities, and the detail reading can meet it on the texts: the budget's own clauses show a government aware of the charge in advance, holding half of plan resources for agriculture and the rural sector by the speech's own declaration and writing the National Renewal Fund into the fiscal architecture, provisions the Sen and Dreze standard judges inadequate in scale rather than absent in intent. The adjudication records the exchange because it fixes the reforms' true frontier of dispute. By 1995 the argument about July 1991 was no longer whether the texts were dictated or designed, but what they had omitted, and an argument about omission is an argument the detail reading is built to host, since omissions, like clauses, can be enumerated.

The complication must be faced at full strength before the argument closes. The crisis-response reading survives in one narrowed form: without the reserves collapse there would have been no July at all, since the same design had sat in files since 1990 and earlier, through the Chandra Shekhar months and the last Rajiv Gandhi year, without a government willing or able to issue it. The detail reading concedes the point and absorbs it. Crisis was the condition of the instruments' issuance, not the author of their content, and the proof is the counterfactual the record itself supplies: the gold operation, the one instrument that was pure crisis response, is also the one instrument that left no institution behind it, while the authored instruments, the scrip, the annexes, the 150 per cent ceiling, the statutory regulators and the management statute of 1999, built the framework inside which every later Indian government, of every composition, has conducted economic policy. Read against [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), the July texts renegotiated who may produce, who may import, who may own and who may hold foreign exchange, the second tension of the series, who governs whom, answered for a generation by five documents of a single month and the statutes that ratified them. Read against [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), the devaluation and the Fund standby belong to the same family as the era's diplomacy, external acts whose operative content was domestic. And read against [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), July 1991 stands as the economic counterpart of the constitutional moments, a founding executed without a constituent assembly, in annexes and budget paragraphs, which is precisely why its detail repays the closest reading this article has attempted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 24 July 1991 Manmohan Singh budget?

The budget presented to the Lok Sabha on 24 July 1991 by Finance Minister Manmohan Singh was the fiscal half of the July reform package, delivered on the same day as the Statement on Industrial Policy. It targeted a cut in the Union fiscal deficit from 8.4 per cent of GDP in 1990-91 to 6.5 per cent in 1991-92, capped the peak rate of customs duty at 150 per cent where prescribed rates had run above 300 per cent, cut the provision for export subsidies from an estimated 4,200 crore rupees to 1,224 crore, raised fertiliser prices by an average of 40 per cent while decontrolling low analysis fertilisers, and closed with the Victor Hugo line that no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come.

### Q: What was the July 1991 Industrial Policy?

The Statement on Industrial Policy laid before Parliament on 24 July 1991 was the structural half of the July package. It abolished industrial licensing for all industries except the eighteen listed in its second annex, replaced the licence with an Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum filing for the rest, removed the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices asset test that had subjected large companies to prior clearance for expansion, granted automatic approval for foreign equity up to 51 per cent in thirty four high priority industries, cut the list of industries reserved for the public sector from seventeen to eight, referred chronically sick public enterprises to the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction, and announced disinvestment of up to 20 per cent of government shareholding in selected public enterprises.

### Q: How did the 1991 reforms end industrial licensing?

The reforms ended industrial licensing by announcement and filing rather than by a licensing statute's repeal. The Statement on Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 declared that a licence would no longer be required to establish or expand an industrial undertaking outside its eighteen item annex of security, strategic and social concern industries, and it substituted an Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum, a simple filing with the Secretariat for Industrial Approvals, for the permission companies had previously begged from the Directorate General of Technical Development and the licensing committee. Location conditions survived in softened form for large cities, and the phased manufacturing programmes that had compelled local content on a state timetable were abolished in the same text.

### Q: What is FERA-FEMA shift trajectory?

The FERA to FEMA trajectory is the movement of India's foreign exchange law from the conservation regime of the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973 to the management regime of the Foreign Exchange Management Act of 1999. FERA treated foreign exchange as a scarce licensed privilege, criminalised violations and pressed foreign controlled companies toward dilution. The July 1991 reforms breached its spirit through automatic approvals, the Amendment Act of 1993 stripped out much of its restrictive core, and FEMA, assented to in December 1999 and in force from 1 June 2000, repealed it entirely, redefining violations as civil offences and stating facilitation of external trade and orderly market development as the law's objective.

### Q: Who was Montek Singh Ahluwalia in 1991?

Montek Singh Ahluwalia was Commerce Secretary in July 1991 and one of the reform design's principal authors. As Special Secretary to Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi he had prepared in 1990 the internal memorandum known as the M document, which sketched trade liberalisation, a tradable replenishment instrument and industrial delicensing a year before the crisis broke. In the first week of July 1991 he co-drafted the trade policy package with Commerce Minister of State P. Chidambaram, finalising its details within hours on 3 July before it was carried to the Prime Minister, and his later career, Finance Secretary from 1993 and Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission from 2004 to 2014, kept him at the centre of the settlement he had helped design.

### Q: What was the post-1991 trade liberalisation architecture?

The trade architecture built after July 1991 rested on four supports. The exchange rate support replaced the fixed basket peg by stages: the two-step devaluation of July 1991, the dual rate of the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System from 1 March 1992, and a unified market determined rate from 1 March 1993, capped by acceptance of current account convertibility in August 1994. The tariff support cut the peak customs rate from 150 per cent in the 1991 budget to 110 per cent in 1992-93 and downward thereafter. The procedural support abolished import licensing outside a shrinking negative list and retired the eximscrip into the exchange rate itself. The institutional support gave the system statutory regulators, above all SEBI under its Act of 1992.

### Q: How did the 1991 reforms shape post-1991 political content?

The reforms reset the terrain on which Indian politics was fought after 1991. Because the July instruments were executed through executive channels, later governments of opposed coalitions, the United Front, the National Democratic Alliance and the United Progressive Alliance, extended rather than reversed them, and economic argument shifted from whether the state should license production to how markets should be regulated, taxed and opened sector by sector. The reforms also redistributed political initiative: state governments began competing for investment the Centre no longer allocated, industry bodies argued over annexes and rates instead of permissions, and the parties of the left, having lost the licensing state, rebuilt their economic case around disinvestment, labour and land, the contests that structured the following decades.

### Q: By how much was the rupee devalued on 1 July 1991 and 3 July 1991?

The Reserve Bank of India lowered the rupee by approximately 9 per cent against its five currency basket on 1 July 1991 and by approximately 11 per cent more on 3 July 1991, under Governor S. Venkitaramanan. The two adjustments compounded to a downward movement of roughly 18 to 19 per cent, taking the rupee from about 17.9 to the dollar to about 23 to the dollar. The percentages were calculated against the basket of the dollar, the pound sterling, the Deutsche mark, the yen and the French franc to which the rupee was then pegged, and the figures commonly quoted are the Reserve Bank's announced adjustments of the basket rate on the two dates.

### Q: Why was the devaluation of July 1991 carried out in two steps?

The split was a political technique executed as a monetary measure. A first step of about 9 per cent could be presented, and defended, as a technical correction of the basket rate of a kind the Reserve Bank had made before, while a single announced fall of about a fifth would have declared a programme and provoked a parliamentary storm before any supporting measures existed. The forty eight hour interval let the government test the reaction of markets, of the International Monetary Fund and of the Congress party, and it let the completed adjustment be unveiled beside the trade policy of 4 July, which supplied the export machinery, the eximscrips, that gave the cheaper rupee an institutional purpose.

### Q: How much gold did India pledge abroad in 1991, and when was it redeemed?

India moved 67 tonnes of gold abroad in 1991 across two operations. In the spring, under the Chandra Shekhar government, 20 tonnes of confiscated gold were sold to the Union Bank of Switzerland with a repurchase option through the State Bank of India, raising about 200 million dollars. In July, under the Rao government, the Reserve Bank shipped 47 tonnes to the Bank of England in four tranches, pledged for loans of about 405 million dollars arranged with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan. Both operations were closed out as reserves recovered in the autumn: the Bank of England pledge was repurchased by November 1991 and the Swiss transaction was settled by the end of that year.

### Q: How did eximscrips work under the trade policy of 4 July 1991?

Eximscrips were tradable certificates issued to exporters at a face value of 30 per cent of the value of their exports, 40 per cent for a set of favoured products, under the trade policy announced by P. Chidambaram on 4 July 1991. A scrip entitled its holder to import items on the restricted list up to its face value, and because scrips could be sold, an exporter who did not import could sell the entitlement to an importer at a market premium. The premium rewarded exporters without a charge on the budget, replacing the abolished cash compensatory support, and it rationed restricted imports by price, since the total scrip supply was tied to export earnings. The instrument was retired when the dual exchange rate replaced it in March 1992.

### Q: Which industries still required a licence after the policy statement of 24 July 1991?

Eighteen industries retained compulsory licensing under the statement's second annex, grouped by security, strategic and social grounds. They were coal and lignite; petroleum other than crude and its distillation products; distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks; sugar; animal fats and oils; cigars and cigarettes of tobacco; asbestos and asbestos based products; plywood, decorative veneers and other wood based products; raw hides and skins and leather in its specified forms; tanned or dressed furskins; paper and newsprint except bagasse based units; electronic aerospace and defence equipment; industrial explosives; hazardous chemicals; drugs and pharmaceuticals under the drug policy; entertainment electronics; white goods; and arms, ammunition and allied defence equipment together with atomic energy and its scheduled minerals.

### Q: What happened to the MRTP asset threshold in July 1991?

The Statement on Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 abolished the asset threshold of the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act of 1969 in its operative form. Since 1985, companies whose assets crossed 100 crore rupees had needed prior government clearance to expand, diversify, establish new undertakings or merge, a pre-entry scrutiny the statement declared at an end, with amending legislation to remove the governing provisions. The Act itself was not repealed in 1991; its commission was redirected from vetting size before investment to investigating monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices after conduct occurred. The distinction mattered to investors, because behaviour changed from the date of the statement, while the statute book caught up through amendment.

### Q: What did the budget of 24 July 1991 do to the peak rate of customs duty?

The budget capped the peak rate of customs duty at 150 per cent. Manmohan Singh told the Lok Sabha that prescribed import duties for some items had risen above 300 per cent under decades of protection and revenue raising, and he proposed to reduce the ad valorem rate of basic plus auxiliary duty to a maximum of 150 per cent wherever it exceeded that level, abolishing every tariff peak above the ceiling except for imported alcoholic beverages and passenger baggage, at a revenue cost of 132 crore rupees in a full year. The further reduction of the peak to 110 per cent, sometimes folded into accounts of the famous day, in fact came in the budget of 1992-93.

### Q: What fiscal deficit did the 1991 budget inherit, and what target did it set?

The budget inherited a Union fiscal deficit of 8.4 per cent of GDP, the outturn Manmohan Singh reported for 1990-91, a year of Gulf crisis oil prices, interrupted remittances and election season expenditure, and it set a target of 6.5 per cent of GDP for 1991-92. The correction was to be achieved mainly on the expenditure side and through subsidy redesign rather than new taxation: the export subsidy provision fell by about 3,000 crore rupees as the devaluation and the eximscrips replaced cash support, fertiliser and petroleum prices were raised, and the customs rationalisation was accepted as deliberately revenue negative. Singh presented the target to the House as the minimum correction consistent with avoiding a default on external payments.

### Q: What replaced the industrial licence for industries outside the licensed list?

For every industry outside the eighteen item annex, the Statement on Industrial Policy substituted a filing for a permission: the Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum, submitted to the Secretariat for Industrial Approvals in the Ministry of Industry, after which the entrepreneur could proceed without a licence, a letter of intent or a conversion of one into the other. The memorandum was an intimation, not an application, and no official could refuse it. Residual conditions survived at the edges, most visibly the location rule that steered units in cities of more than a million inhabitants to sites 25 kilometres from the municipal limits, with exemptions for electronics, computer software and printing, and the abolished phased manufacturing programmes removed the local content timetable that had followed the licence.

### Q: What did the budget of 1991 do to the provision for export subsidies?

The budget cut the provision for export subsidies from an estimated requirement of 4,200 crore rupees to 1,224 crore rupees for the remainder of 1991-92, a saving of about 3,000 crore that Singh presented as a direct yield of the month's other instruments. The reasoning in the speech was substitution, not abandonment: the two-step devaluation had cheapened Indian exports in foreign currency terms, and the expansion of the replenishment system into tradable eximscrips at the start of July rewarded exporters through a market premium, so the cash compensatory support that the exchequer had previously paid could be largely withdrawn. The cut was one of the largest single savings inside the deficit target of 6.5 per cent of GDP.

### Q: When did SEBI become a statutory body, and under which Act?

The Securities and Exchange Board of India became a statutory body through an ordinance promulgated on 30 January 1992, replaced by the Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, Act 15 of 1992, which received presidential assent on 4 April 1992 and took effect from 30 January 1992. SEBI had existed since 12 April 1988 as an administrative body without statutory powers, and the Act gave it authority to register and regulate market intermediaries, protect investors and develop the securities market, under its first statutory chairman G. V. Ramakrishna. The statutory founding was paired with the repeal of the Capital Issues (Control) Act in May 1992, which ended state fixing of share issue prices.

### Q: What was the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of March 1992?

The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System, announced in the budget of 1992-93 and effective from 1 March 1992, was the dual exchange rate that succeeded the eximscrips as the bridge between the pegged rupee and a market rate. Exporters and recipients of inward remittances surrendered 40 per cent of their foreign exchange receipts to the Reserve Bank at the official rate, which the state used for essential imports such as petroleum, fertiliser and life saving drugs, and converted the remaining 60 per cent at a market determined rate. The arrangement priced most trade at the market rate while protecting priority imports, and it lasted exactly a year: a unified, market determined exchange rate replaced it on 1 March 1993.

### Q: How did FEMA change the treatment of foreign exchange violations when it replaced FERA?

The Foreign Exchange Management Act changed a violation of foreign exchange law from a crime into a civil liability. Under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973, contraventions attracted prosecution, imprisonment and an enforcement directorate built for a scarcity regime in which holding foreign exchange without permission was itself suspect. FEMA, in force from 1 June 2000, reclassified contraventions as civil offences punishable by monetary penalty, and it allowed compounding, the settlement of an admitted contravention on payment without prosecution. The reclassification expressed the statute's stated object, facilitating external trade and payments and promoting the orderly development of the foreign exchange market, in place of FERA's object of conserving a scarce resource under state direction.
