## How the Mandatory Minimum Machinery Works

A federal mandatory minimum is a number that Congress writes directly into a criminal statute, and the number matters because a judge has no lawful authority to sentence beneath it. The minimum attaches when the elements the statute names are established: most often a drug type and a threshold quantity under 21 U.S.C. 841(b), the use or carrying of a firearm during and in relation to a federal drug or violent crime under 18 U.S.C. 924(c), or qualifying prior convictions formally noticed through the procedure at 21 U.S.C. 851. Once those elements are charged and either admitted or proved, the floor is fixed before the judge's sentencing discretion begins, and no ordinary reason can move the sentence below the statutory line, not sympathy for the defendant's role, not disagreement with the severity of the range, and not a guideline calculation pointing lower.

![Mandatory minimum sentencing law triggers and exits - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-37.webp)

Only three exits lead beneath the floor, and the identity of the person holding each key is the mechanism that matters most. The substantial assistance motion under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e) can take any federal defendant below a minimum, but only the government may file it, so the most frequently used route is unavailable unless the prosecutor chooses to open it. The safety valve under 18 U.S.C. 3553(f) excuses qualifying drug defendants from the minimum entirely, and the judge decides whether the five statutory criteria are satisfied, with no government motion required. The 2018 expansion of that valve widened the criminal history gate and extended coverage to maritime drug offenses. Of the three, only the substantial assistance exit is one the defendant cannot initiate, a structural asymmetry that shapes every plea negotiation conducted under the threat of a minimum.

The discretion Congress meant to remove from judges did not vanish; it relocated. Charging decisions, which quantity to allege, whether to file an 851 notice, how many firearm counts to bring, are committed to the prosecution, and judges appointed by presidents of both parties described the arrangement as a transfer of sentencing authority to the charging office. And the modern minimums rest on a history almost no one recites: Congress enacted the first federal drug minimums in 1951, stiffened them in 1956, then repealed nearly all of them in 1970 through the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act after a bipartisan judgment that they were unrealistic and inflexible, only to reinstate quantity driven minimums sixteen years later in the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, a statute drafted in a midterm election season without hearings on the thresholds themselves.

## The Quantity Trigger in the Federal Drug Penalty Statute

A federal drug mandatory minimum almost always begins with a number on a laboratory scale rather than with a description of what the defendant did. Congress separated the crime from its punishment in the Controlled Substances Act. Subsection 841(a) of Title 21 defines the offense: it is unlawful for any person knowingly or intentionally to manufacture, distribute, or dispense a controlled substance, or to possess one with intent to manufacture, distribute, or dispense it. Subsection 841(b) then sets the penalty, and it sets that penalty by drug type and weight. Simple possession for personal use is a different offense entirely, codified at 21 U.S.C. 844, and it does not use the quantity ladder. The moment the government charges manufacturing, distribution, or possession with intent to distribute, the weight of the drug becomes the dominant sentencing fact, because subsection 841(b) converts grams and kilograms into years of required imprisonment.

The penalty architecture inside subsection 841(b) is a staircase with three main treads for the eight drugs that Congress assigned to Schedules I and II and singled out for quantity driven punishment: heroin, powder cocaine, cocaine base in the form of crack, PCP, LSD, fentanyl, methamphetamine, and marijuana. The Congressional Research Service describes these as the substances Congress regarded as most susceptible to abuse and least appropriate for medicinal use without tight control. The staircase works the same way for each of them. A very large quantity triggers subparagraph (b)(1)(A), which carries a ten year mandatory minimum and a maximum of life imprisonment. A substantial but smaller quantity triggers subparagraph (b)(1)(B), which carries a five year mandatory minimum and a maximum of forty years. Any lesser quantity of those drugs, or any other Schedule I or II controlled substance, falls into the catch-all subparagraph (b)(1)(C), which carries no mandatory minimum and a maximum of twenty years. The thresholds separating the tiers are fixed in the statute to the gram and the kilogram, and no sentencing judge can slide a case from one tier to another by reasoning about blameworthiness. Only the charged and proven weight can place the case on a particular tread.

### How is the federal drug penalty ladder structured?

Subsection 841(a) defines the trafficking offense, and subsection 841(b) grades the punishment by drug type and weight. Subparagraph (b)(1)(A) imposes a ten year minimum for the largest quantities, subparagraph (b)(1)(B) imposes a five year minimum for midrange quantities, and subparagraph (b)(1)(C) covers lesser amounts of the listed drugs and all other Schedule I or II substances with no minimum and a twenty year maximum.

The top tier, subparagraph (b)(1)(A), is reached at one kilogram or more of heroin, five kilograms or more of powder cocaine, two hundred eighty grams or more of crack cocaine, fifty grams or more of actual methamphetamine or five hundred grams or more of a mixture containing methamphetamine, four hundred grams or more of fentanyl, and one thousand kilograms or more of marijuana or one thousand or more marijuana plants. The statute also sets top tier thresholds for the remaining listed drugs: one hundred grams or more of pure PCP or one kilogram or more of a mixture containing PCP, ten grams or more of LSD, and one hundred grams or more of a fentanyl analogue. Crossing any one of those lines moves the ordinary sentencing range to ten years to life, and the range cannot drop below ten years for a defendant without a qualifying prior record absent one of the three statutory exits that later sections of this article address.

The middle tier, subparagraph (b)(1)(B), uses thresholds set at exactly one tenth of the top tier. One hundred grams of heroin, five hundred grams of powder cocaine, twenty eight grams of crack cocaine, five grams of actual methamphetamine or fifty grams of a mixture containing methamphetamine, forty grams of fentanyl, and one hundred kilograms of marijuana or one hundred marijuana plants each trigger a five year mandatory minimum with a maximum of forty years. The remaining listed drugs scale down the same way: ten grams of pure PCP or one hundred grams of a mixture containing PCP, one gram of LSD, and ten grams of a fentanyl analogue. The one to ten relationship between the two tiers is deliberate statutory design, and it means that a defendant charged just above the lower line and a defendant charged just below the higher line face sharply different floors even when their quantities are close.

Below both quantity tiers sits the catch-all, subparagraph (b)(1)(C). It applies to lesser amounts of the eight listed drugs, to every other Schedule I or II controlled substance, and to certain analogues and date rape drugs. A first offense under this subparagraph carries no mandatory minimum and a maximum of twenty years. A smaller marijuana provision, subparagraph (b)(1)(D), covers offenses involving less than fifty kilograms of marijuana with a maximum of five years. The absence of a floor in the catch-all tier is what makes the tier boundaries so consequential. A case charged at ninety nine grams of heroin lives in a world with no required minimum and a twenty year ceiling, while the same conduct charged at one hundred grams lives in a world with a five year floor and a forty year ceiling, and the difference between those two worlds is one gram on the scale.

The statute then layers two enhancements onto the quantity floors. If death or serious bodily injury results from the use of the drug, the mandatory minimum rises to twenty years under both the (A) and (B) tiers, and the catch-all (C) tier gains a twenty year mandatory minimum where it previously had none. A qualifying prior conviction raises the floors as well. Under the (B) tier, a prior conviction for a serious drug felony or a serious violent felony moves the floor from five years to ten years, with a maximum of life. Under the (A) tier, such a prior conviction moves the floor from ten years to fifteen years. A mandatory term of life imprisonment applies when death or serious bodily injury results and the defendant has a qualifying prior conviction. The First Step Act of 2018 revised the recidivist ladder inside subsection 841(b): where two or more prior qualifying convictions once produced a mandatory life term, the minimum became twenty five years, and the statute uses the terms serious drug felony and serious violent felony rather than the older felony drug offense language. There is no life minimum in subsection 841(b) that attaches to quantity alone, however large the amount. The life terms all require the added facts of death, serious bodily injury, or prior convictions.

How the weight itself is measured matters as much as the thresholds. For most listed drugs, the statute counts the weight of the entire mixture or substance containing a detectable amount of the drug, not the weight of the pure chemical. Methamphetamine is the notable exception, because the statute provides separate thresholds for actual methamphetamine, meaning the pure drug, and for a mixture containing it. Marijuana can be measured by weight or by plant count, with one thousand plants treated the same as one thousand kilograms. Conspiracy and attempt to commit a subsection 841(a) offense carry the same penalties as the completed offense under 21 U.S.C. 846, so the quantity attributed to the conspiracy determines the tier. Courts have held that quantities from separate substantive counts are not added together to reach a higher tier, while quantities attributable to a single conspiracy are aggregated. The measurement rules decide which tread of the staircase the case stands on before any other sentencing question is asked.

### How does a mandatory minimum attach to a case?

A quantity based minimum attaches only when the drug type and threshold weight are charged in the indictment and either admitted or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The Supreme Court held in Apprendi v. New Jersey, decided in 2000, that any fact increasing the statutory maximum is an element that must be charged and proved to a jury. The Court initially declined to extend that rule to facts raising only the floor, but in Alleyne v. United States, decided in 2013, it overruled that distinction and held that any fact increasing a mandatory minimum is likewise an element for the jury. Because a threshold quantity raises the floor from zero to five years or from five to ten years, the weight is an element of the aggravated offense, not a mere sentencing detail for the judge. The one surviving exception is the prior conviction, which under long standing precedent need not be charged in the indictment or proved to a jury, a point the Alleyne majority expressly preserved.

Before Alleyne, the governing rule was Harris v. United States, decided in 2002, which had refused to extend Apprendi to facts that raised only the mandatory minimum. Under Harris, a judge could find by a preponderance of the evidence that a defendant had brandished a firearm during a drug trafficking crime and impose the higher seven year floor even though the jury had found only carrying, which carried a five year floor. An older decision, McMillan v. Pennsylvania, decided in 1986, had taken the same approach to a state firearm minimum. Alleyne overruled Harris on this point and repudiated the McMillan distinction between facts that raise the ceiling and facts that raise the floor. The reasoning drew on the historic link between the charged crime and its punishment: both kinds of facts alter the range of sentences to which a defendant is exposed, and both aggravate the punishment, so both are elements. After 2013, no federal judge could do what the Harris trial judge had done, find a minimum raising fact on the lower civil standard while the jury verdict alone would have supported the lower floor.

In practice, most minimums attach through plea rather than trial. The government drafts the indictment to allege a specific drug and a weight at or above a threshold, and the defendant pleads guilty to that indictment, admitting the quantity and waiving any jury finding on it. Plea agreements routinely stipulate the attributable weight, and the stipulation is what locks in the tier. This is also where the charging decision shows its full force. The prosecutor chooses whether to allege one hundred grams of heroin or ninety nine, whether to charge the conspiracy quantity that crosses the ten year line or the individual transaction that does not, and whether to include the weight at all. A decision to charge below a threshold, or to charge the offense without a quantity allegation, leaves the case in the catch-all tier with no floor. Judges appointed by presidents of both parties have described this dynamic as moving sentencing authority from the bench to the prosecutor, because the tier is fixed before the judge ever sees the case.

When the government charges a threshold quantity but fails to prove it at trial, the fallback is the catch-all tier. The conviction stands as a subsection 841(a) offense, but the sentence is governed by subparagraph (b)(1)(C), with no mandatory minimum and a twenty year maximum. The same fallback applies when the government deliberately charges a lesser included quantity. The quantity element therefore operates as a one way ratchet in the government's hands: alleging and proving it raises the floor, while failing to allege or prove it leaves the lower range in place. Nothing in the statute permits a judge to disregard a properly charged and proved quantity in order to sentence below the floor, which is why the proof rules carry so much weight in every drug prosecution.

### What is the role of the drug importation penalty provision?

Section 960 provides the parallel penalty structure for drug importation and exportation, mirroring the quantity ladder of section 841. The offense provisions sit at 21 U.S.C. 952 for importation and 21 U.S.C. 953 for exportation, which prohibit the knowing and intentional movement of controlled substances across the border, while subsection 960(b) grades the punishment by the same drug types and threshold weights. Subparagraph 960(b)(1) carries the ten year mandatory minimum at the same quantities that trigger subsection 841(b)(1)(A), subparagraph 960(b)(2) carries the five year minimum at the same quantities that trigger subsection 841(b)(1)(B), and subparagraph 960(b)(3) is the catch-all with no minimum. The Sentencing Commission has described the two penalty structures as parallel, and attempt and conspiracy to import or export carry the same penalties under 21 U.S.C. 963. The practical effect is that a trafficking case built on border seizure faces the same quantity driven floors as a domestic distribution case, with the same thresholds, the same enhancements, and the same proof requirements.

### What did the Fair Sentencing Act change about crack thresholds?

The Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 raised the crack cocaine thresholds from five grams to twenty eight grams for the five year minimum and from fifty grams to two hundred eighty grams for the ten year minimum, replacing the 100 to 1 crack to powder disparity with an 18 to 1 ratio. It also eliminated the five year mandatory minimum for simple possession of crack cocaine, leaving the powder cocaine thresholds unchanged.

The original crack thresholds came from the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, which set the five year floor at five grams of crack against five hundred grams of powder cocaine and the ten year floor at fifty grams of crack against five kilograms of powder. That 100 to 1 ratio drew sustained criticism from the Sentencing Commission, from federal judges, and from legislators in both parties, and the Commission documented its racially disparate application across four reports to Congress between 1995 and 2007. The Fair Sentencing Act, signed by President Obama on August 3, 2010, was the legislative answer. It raised the crack lines to twenty eight and two hundred eighty grams while leaving the powder lines at five hundred grams and five kilograms, producing the approximately 18 to 1 ratio that remains in the statute. It also repealed the provision that had imposed a five year mandatory minimum for simple possession of five grams of crack, conforming crack possession to the treatment of other drugs. The Act applied to cases sentenced after its effective date but not to sentences already imposed, and the Supreme Court confirmed that timing rule for pending cases in Dorsey v. United States, decided in 2012. The First Step Act of 2018 later made the Fair Sentencing Act retroactive, permitting defendants sentenced under the old crack thresholds to seek resentencing, and the Commission reported thousands of granted reductions under that provision.

The eighteen to one ratio was itself a legislative compromise rather than a scientific finding. The House of Representatives had passed a bill eliminating the crack to powder disparity entirely, while the Senate preferred a narrower adjustment, and the final figure emerged from negotiation between the two chambers. The bill passed both chambers by voice vote, a sign of how broadly the old ratio had lost its defenders, and the powder thresholds were left untouched because no coalition existed to change them. The result was a statute that narrowed the gap without closing it, and the remaining disparity in trafficking thresholds continues to distinguish crack from powder in every quantity tier.

### Why is the quantity trigger the most common minimum in the federal system?

The quantity trigger is the most common federal mandatory minimum because every drug trafficking case has a weight, while the other minimums require additional facts that many cases lack. A firearm minimum requires a gun, a recidivist minimum requires qualifying prior convictions, but the quantity floor needs nothing beyond the drugs themselves. The Sentencing Commission identified sections 841 and 960 as the most commonly prosecuted drug offenses carrying mandatory minimum penalties in its 2011 report to Congress on mandatory minimums. An earlier Commission report, issued in 1991, found that four statutes, sections 841, 844, and 960 together with the firearm provision at 18 U.S.C. 924(c), accounted for approximately 94 percent of all mandatory minimum cases. A former Commission chair told a House Judiciary subcommittee in 2007 that 82.9 percent of federal prisoners serving mandatory minimum sentences were drug offenders. The thresholds are also set low enough to reach ordinary distribution cases: twenty eight grams of crack cocaine, one hundred grams of heroin, and five hundred grams of powder cocaine are street level quantities, not cartel level ones, and the guidelines themselves were originally pegged to the statutory minimums. Because the quantity element attaches in case after case without any special conduct finding, the drug weight has become the workhorse minimum of the federal system, the floor that appears most often and shapes the most sentences.

The design also explains why the quantity trigger resists the usual criticisms aimed at the other minimums. A firearm enhancement can be challenged on the facts of possession or use, and a recidivist enhancement depends on the validity and timing of prior convictions, but a scale reading is difficult to contest once the laboratory report is in. The Commission's 2011 report noted that the quantity thresholds operate mechanically, producing the same floor for defendants whose roles in the distribution chain differ widely. That mechanical quality is what makes the trigger both the most predictable and the most frequently applied minimum in the federal code: it asks a single factual question, and the answer maps directly onto a number of years.

## The firearm trigger and the tack-on sentence

Section 924(c) of title 18 functions as a tack-on, a mandatory minimum that attaches to another federal crime and is served on top of it. The statute punishes anyone who, during and in relation to a federal crime of violence or drug trafficking crime, uses or carries a firearm, or who, in furtherance of such a crime, possesses a firearm, and it directs that the resulting term be imposed in addition to the punishment provided for the underlying offense. The provision sits outside the drug quantity schedules that produce most federal mandatory minimums, yet the Sentencing Commission's report on firearms offenses describes firearms offenses as the second most common offenses carrying a mandatory minimum, after drug offenses, accounting for 16.8 percent of offenses carrying such a penalty in fiscal year 2016. The structure explains the provision's reach. A single prosecution can yield a drug sentence, a robbery sentence, or another predicate sentence, and then a separate consecutive firearm term fixed by statute, with no room for the judge to fold the two together.

The predicate side of the trigger is defined in the statute itself. Section 924(c)(2) defines a drug trafficking crime as any felony punishable under the Controlled Substances Act, the Controlled Substances Import and Export Act, or chapter 705 of title 46, so nearly the entire federal drug code qualifies. Section 924(c)(3) defines a crime of violence as a felony that has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, or that by its nature involves a substantial risk that such force may be used in committing the offense. The firearm count therefore depends on the predicate conviction standing first; without a qualifying underlying federal offense, there is nothing for the minimum to attach to.

### What conduct triggers the firearm minimum?

A 924(c) minimum triggers when the government proves that the defendant used or carried a firearm during and in relation to a federal crime of violence or drug trafficking crime, or possessed a firearm in furtherance of such a crime. The predicate offense must be one that may be prosecuted in a court of the United States, and the firearm conduct must connect to it in the manner the statute specifies.

The statute uses two different connecting phrases, and the difference matters. Use and carry must occur during and in relation to the predicate crime, while possession must occur in furtherance of it. Courts have read the in relation to language to require that the firearm facilitate the crime or at least have the potential to facilitate it, so that a gun that merely happens to be nearby during an unrelated act does not satisfy the statute. The in furtherance of language, added later, requires a specific nexus between the possession and the crime, a link that goes beyond coincidence of time and place. The practical consequence is that the government must prove more than the presence of a gun at the scene; it must prove the relationship the statute names.

The two phrases arrived by different routes. In Bailey v. United States, decided in 1995, the Supreme Court read the word uses narrowly, holding that it required active employment of the firearm rather than mere possession near drugs or proceeds. Congress responded with the 1998 amendment, Public Law 105-386, titled An Act to Throttle Criminal Use of Guns, which rewrote the subsection. The amendment added possession in furtherance of a crime as a freestanding basis for liability, so that the government no longer needed to squeeze possession into the word use. The same amendment created the brandishing and discharge tiers and divided the subsection into the subparagraphs in force under the current text. In the same era, Muscarello v. United States, decided in 1998, read the word carries broadly, holding that it covers knowingly conveying a firearm in a vehicle, including in a locked glove compartment, and not only carrying on the person. Together the 1998 legislation and the Muscarello reading produced the current trigger: use or carry with a during and in relation to connection, or possession with an in furtherance of connection.

### How do the five, seven, and ten year terms work?

The base offense carries a term of not less than five years. If the firearm was brandished, the floor rises to not less than seven years, and if the firearm was discharged, to not less than ten years. Each tier replaces rather than adds to the lower one, and each is imposed on top of whatever sentence attaches to the underlying crime.

The statute defines brandish at section 924(c)(4): to display all or part of the firearm, or otherwise make its presence known to another person, in order to intimidate that person, regardless of whether the firearm is directly visible. The definition turns on intimidation rather than sight lines, so a gun mentioned to frighten can qualify even when it stays hidden. Discharge, by contrast, requires no such mental component. In Dean v. United States, decided in 2009, the Supreme Court held that the ten-year discharge enhancement applies without separate proof that the defendant intended to fire the weapon; the fact of discharge is enough. The tiered structure thus grades the firearm conduct by its danger and its use for intimidation, while keeping the factual showings modest.

Who decides whether the higher tier applies has changed. In Harris v. United States, decided in 2002, the Court treated brandishing as a sentencing factor that a judge could find by a preponderance of the evidence. In Alleyne v. United States, decided in 2013, the Court overruled Harris and held that any fact that increases the mandatory minimum, including brandishing and discharge, is an element of the offense that must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The government must therefore charge the tier and prove it to the jury, and a judge may not lift a five-year case to seven or ten on the judge's own finding.

### What weapon types raise the minimum further?

When the firearm is a short-barreled rifle, short-barreled shotgun, or semiautomatic assault weapon, the minimum is ten years. When it is a machinegun or a destructive device, or is equipped with a firearm silencer or firearm muffler, the minimum is thirty years, rising to life imprisonment on a qualifying repeat violation.

These weapon-type enhancements sit in section 924(c)(1)(B) and operate independently of the use tiers. A defendant who merely possesses a machinegun in furtherance of a drug crime faces the thirty-year floor even without brandishing or firing it. The classification of the weapon is itself a question for the jury. In United States v. O'Brien, decided in 2010, the Supreme Court held that the machinegun provision states an element of a separate offense rather than a sentencing factor, so the government must prove the weapon's character to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The 1998 amendment wrote these terms into the modern form of the statute, replacing an older scheme that had set ten years for short-barreled weapons and thirty years for machineguns, silencers, and destructive devices without the brandishing and discharge gradations.

## The consecutive sentence rule

### Why must a 924(c) sentence run consecutively?

Section 924(c)(1)(D)(ii) provides that no term of imprisonment imposed under the subsection may run concurrently with any other term of imprisonment, including any term imposed for the predicate crime of violence or drug trafficking crime. Section 924(c)(1)(D)(i) separately bars the court from placing on probation any person convicted of a violation of the subsection. The command applies notwithstanding any other provision of law, which closes the usual avenues by which judges align sentences.

The consecutive requirement is what makes the provision a true tack-on rather than a sentencing factor folded into the predicate offense. A defendant convicted of both a drug trafficking crime and a 924(c) count serves the drug sentence and then the firearm sentence, back to back, with no overlap. The sentencing guidelines reinforce the result: under section 2K2.4 of the guidelines manual, the guideline sentence for a 924(c) conviction is the statutory minimum itself, and the guidelines instruct that it run consecutively. The numbers show the compounding effect. The Sentencing Commission's quick facts for fiscal year 2025 report that the average sentence for 924(c) offenders rose from 136 months in fiscal year 2021 to 149 months in fiscal year 2025, that 89 percent of 924(c) offenders were also convicted of another offense, and that the most common of those other offenses was drug trafficking. Almost a third of 924(c) offenders in some years were convicted of another offense that itself carried a mandatory minimum, so two floors can stack inside a single judgment before the consecutive rule even begins its work.

## Stacking multiple counts in one case

### What was 924(c) stacking?

Before 2018, prosecutors could charge multiple 924(c) counts in a single indictment, and under Deal v. United States, decided in 1993, each count after the first counted as a second or subsequent conviction carrying a much longer term. A defendant with no prior record could therefore receive decades of consecutive firearm time from one prosecution, because the first count carried the base term and every later count drew the enhanced repeat term.

The mechanics came from the phrase second or subsequent conviction in section 924(c)(1)(C). In Deal, the Supreme Court held that the phrase attached not only when a defendant was convicted in a later case after an earlier conviction, but also when a jury returned sequential guilt findings on multiple 924(c) counts within a single indictment. Each finding after the first was treated as subsequent to the ones before it, so the enhanced minimum applied to every count but the first. At the time of Deal the enhanced term was twenty years; the 1998 amendment raised it to twenty-five years, and added life imprisonment where the firearm involved was a machinegun, destructive device, silencer, or muffler. A prosecution with three counts could thus produce five years on the first count and twenty-five years on each of the next two, all consecutive to each other and to the predicate sentences. Prosecutors controlled the outcome entirely through the charging decision, because the number of counts charged fixed the number of enhanced terms.

### How did the First Step Act change stacking?

Section 403 of the First Step Act, enacted December 21, 2018, rewrote section 924(c)(1)(C) so that the twenty-five-year term applies only to a violation that occurs after a prior conviction under the subsection has become final. The enhancement is reserved for true recidivists, and multiple 924(c) convictions obtained in a single prosecution no longer trigger it. Section 403(b) extended the reform to offenses committed before the date of enactment where a sentence for the offense had not yet been imposed as of that date.

## The related homicide provision

### What happens under 924(j) when death results?

Section 924(j) punishes a person who, in the course of a 924(c) violation, causes the death of a person through the use of a firearm. If the killing is murder as defined in section 1111 of title 18, the punishment is death or imprisonment for any term of years or for life. If the killing is manslaughter as defined in section 1112, the punishment is as provided in that section, which sets a maximum of fifteen years for voluntary manslaughter and eight years for involuntary manslaughter.

Section 924(j) is a related but separate offense, and one structural difference sets it apart from the provision it builds on. The Supreme Court addressed the point in Lora v. United States, decided in 2023. The defendant had been convicted under 924(j) and argued that the trial court wrongly applied 924(c)'s ban on concurrent sentences to his 924(j) sentence. The Court agreed with the defendant, holding unanimously that the consecutive-sentence and no-probation commands of section 924(c)(1)(D) govern only sentences imposed under 924(c) itself and do not control sentencing for a 924(j) conviction. A district judge sentencing under 924(j) therefore retains the ordinary discretion to run the term concurrently with or consecutively to another sentence. Justice Jackson wrote the opinion, emphasizing that the two subsections are distinct provisions in the statute rather than a single offense with an aggravated form.

## Frequency and the missing safety valve

### Why is there no safety valve for 924(c)?

The safety valve in section 3553(f) of title 18 applies only to enumerated drug offenses, including sections 841, 844, 846, 960, and 963 of title 21 and sections 70503 and 70506 of title 46, and 924(c) is not among them. The First Step Act's 2018 expansion of the valve loosened the criminal history limits, but it did not extend coverage to firearms offenses. A defendant who meets every safety valve criterion on the drug counts still faces the full consecutive firearm term, because the valve simply has no application to the 924(c) count.

That gap makes 924(c) the mandatory minimum with the fewest exits. The substantial assistance motion under section 3553(e) remains available, since it can reach any mandatory minimum on the government's motion, but it belongs to the prosecutor alone. The Sentencing Commission's data show how rarely any relief arrives: in its 924(c) quick facts, substantial assistance departures appear in only about six to seven percent of cases involving 924(c) alone, with average reductions around half of the otherwise applicable term. No judge-made departure, no safety valve finding, and no guidelines adjustment can move the number. The charging decisions that trigger the minimum, the number of counts charged, and the choice whether to file a substantial assistance motion all sit with the prosecution, while the judge's role at sentencing is limited to selecting among the tiers the jury's findings support and running the resulting term consecutively as the statute commands. That allocation of authority is the structural point the provision illustrates better than any other federal minimum: the discretion the statute removes from the bench reappears in the charging and motion decisions that precede the sentencing hearing.

### Why does the provision matter so much in practice?

It matters because of volume and because of the company it keeps. The Commission's fiscal year 2025 quick facts put the five-year possessing or carrying minimum at 69 percent of 924(c) cases, the seven-year brandishing minimum at 21 percent, and the ten-year discharge minimum at 9 percent, with the remaining cases drawing the twenty-five-year, thirty-year, or life terms. More than half of 924(c) offenders were also convicted of drug trafficking, and nearly nine in ten were convicted of at least one other offense, so the consecutive rule routinely converts a drug sentence plus a firearm count into a total term far above either component alone. Firearms offenses, dominated by 924(c), stand behind only drug offenses among federal offenses carrying mandatory minimums. The result is a provision that generates thousands of consecutive sentences each year, offers no safety valve, reserves its one reliable exit for the government's motion, and leaves the judge to impose a total the statute has already fixed.

## The Prior Conviction Enhancement Procedure at 21 U.S.C. 851

The drug trafficking statute does not raise a sentence by itself when a defendant has a criminal record. A separate step must happen first, and the statute assigns that step to one office alone. Section 851 of title 21, titled Proceedings to Establish Prior Convictions, lays out the notice and hearing procedure that governs every federal drug sentence increased because of prior convictions. Congress enacted it as section 411 of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, the same statute that repealed the earlier generation of drug minimums, and the procedure has operated in substantially the same form ever since. (The full story of that repeal appears in the companion explainer on the [Controlled Substances Act of 1970](/2018/05/01/controlled-substances-act-1970-guide/).) The mechanics of section 851 explain why the prior conviction enhancement is the clearest illustration of this article's central claim: the discretion that the mandatory minimum removes from the judge reappears, intact, in the hands of the prosecutor. Practitioners who track these filings often use companion worksheet tools such as VaultBook to monitor the filing deadline and to test whether each listed conviction satisfies the current predicate definitions.

### What must the government file before a prior conviction enhancement applies?

Before trial, or before the entry of a guilty plea, the United States attorney must file a written information with the court and serve a copy on the defendant or defense counsel, listing each prior conviction the government intends to rely on to increase the sentence. Without that filing, no prior conviction enhancement is lawful.

The command is phrased as a prohibition. No person convicted of an offense under the drug enforcement part of title 21 may be sentenced to increased punishment by reason of prior convictions unless the filing and the service occur first. The timing is the point of the provision. Because the information must arrive before trial or before the plea, the defendant learns the exact sentencing stakes of the case before choosing how to resolve it. When the prosecutor cannot obtain the prior conviction facts through due diligence in time, the statute permits the court to postpone the trial or the taking of the plea for a reasonable period; the delay exists so the notice can be prepared, not so the notice can be skipped. Clerical mistakes in the information may be corrected at any time before sentence is pronounced, which leaves room to fix errors in dates or docket numbers but no room to add entirely new convictions after the deadline has passed. A separate clause provides that no information may be filed when the increased punishment would exceed three years of imprisonment unless the defendant was afforded prosecution by indictment, a grand jury safeguard that preserves the historic protection against recidivist punishment imposed on thin process.

The decisive structural feature is who files and who does not. Only the United States attorney may file the information. The statute gives the court no authority to impose the enhancement on its own motion, and nothing in the text authorizes the defendant to demand or consent to the filing. Filing is also optional in the other direction: the government may hold eligible prior convictions and never file, in which case the enhancement never enters the case. The decision to seek the enhancement and the decision to leave it alone are both exercises of charging discretion that no other actor in the courtroom can replicate or review.

The statute also shields the government against its own omissions. The failure of the United States attorney to include the complete criminal record in the information is not grounds for invalidating the notice. The prosecutor may therefore list a chosen subset of the defendant's eligible convictions, calibrating the threatened sentence, without risking the enhancement on the ground that some convictions were left out.

### What happens when a defendant denies a prior conviction listed in the information?

After conviction but before sentencing, the court must ask the defendant whether each listed prior conviction is affirmed or denied, and must warn that any challenge not raised before sentencing is waived. A denial triggers a written response and a hearing before the judge, without a jury, on the disputed convictions.

The affirm or deny colloquy required by section 851(b) is the statute's built-in safeguard against mistaken identity and mistaken records. The judge inquires directly of the defendant, not of counsel, and must give the waiver warning on the record. When the defendant denies a conviction or challenges its validity, section 851(c) requires a written response that states the claim and its factual basis with particularity. The allocation of proof then splits along a line the statute draws between factual disputes and constitutional attacks. For an ordinary dispute, for example that the named person is not the defendant or that the conviction does not qualify as a predicate, the United States attorney carries the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt at a hearing before the court without a jury, at which either side may introduce evidence and either side may request written findings of fact and conclusions of law. For a claim that the prior conviction was obtained in violation of the Constitution, the burden reverses: the defendant must prove the violation by a preponderance of the evidence. A constitutional challenge not raised in the response before the enhanced sentence is imposed is waived unless the defendant shows good cause for the failure to raise it.

Two further provisions shape how much of a contest a defendant can realistically mount. Under section 851(d), when the judge determines that the defendant is not subject to increased punishment as a matter of law, the government may ask the court to postpone sentencing so the United States can appeal that determination, an interlocutory appeal right that belongs to the prosecution alone. Under section 851(e), no defendant may challenge the validity of a prior conviction that occurred more than five years before the date of the information. The five year window means that older convictions are insulated from collateral attack at sentencing even when the defendant believes they were constitutionally defective, a limitation that defense organizations have criticized for decades and that Congress has never repealed.

The consequence of a late or defective filing divides the circuit courts, and the majority rule runs against the common assumption. Only the Eleventh Circuit treats the pre trial filing requirement as jurisdictional, meaning a missed deadline strips the court of power to impose the enhancement even when the defendant never objected. Every other circuit to consider the question treats the requirement as a claims processing rule: a late or defective information is an error the defendant must raise, subject to waiver and to plain error review on appeal, not a defect that automatically voids the sentence. The practical difference matters at sentencing. In most of the country, a defendant who fails to raise an 851 defect keeps the enhancement, while in the Eleventh Circuit the enhancement fails regardless of whether anyone objected. Practitioners should therefore treat the filing deadline as a hard one in every district, since the friendlier rule in most circuits still depends on someone noticing the error in time.

### How did the enhancement ladder work before the First Step Act?

Under the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, a defendant convicted at the largest drug quantity tier in 21 U.S.C. 841(b)(1)(A) after one prior felony drug conviction faced a minimum of 20 years; after two or more such convictions, the statute required a mandatory term of life imprisonment without release.

The ladder was rigid and steep. The base offense carried a 10 year minimum; a single prior felony drug conviction raised the floor to 20 years; a second qualifying conviction converted the sentence to life, with no parole. The lower quantity tier, 841(b)(1)(B), worked the same way on a smaller scale: a 5 year base minimum rose to 10 years after one prior felony drug conviction. (The 1986 statute that built this structure is covered in the companion explainer on the [Anti-Drug Abuse Acts of 1986 and 1988](/2018/06/15/anti-drug-abuse-acts-1986-1988/).) The triggering category was the statutory term felony drug offense, defined in the definitions section of the Controlled Substances Act to include any federal, state, or foreign drug offense punishable by imprisonment for more than one year. That definition swept broadly. A decades old state possession conviction with a theoretical maximum above one year could qualify even when the defendant had served no jail time for it, and the statute placed no limit on how old the conviction could be. Because the 1986 law gave the judge no authority to go below the enhanced minimum except through the two narrow exits available in drug cases, the filing of the 851 information effectively fixed the sentence years before the judge pronounced it. The advisory guidelines, the system created by the statute discussed in the [Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 guide](/2018/01/01/sentencing-reform-act-1984-guide/), could recommend a lower range, but the guidelines yielded to the statutory floor whenever the enhancement applied.

### How did prosecutors use the prior conviction notice as plea leverage?

Because only the prosecutor decides whether to file, the information became a bargaining instrument: file it and threaten 20 years or life, or withhold it and offer the unenhanced minimum in exchange for a plea. Leadership of the Department of Justice repeatedly intervened to regulate that practice across administrations.

The leverage was built into the design. A defendant facing trial with two qualifying priors knew that a guilty verdict after an 851 filing meant mandatory life, while a plea without the filing could mean the 10 year base minimum. The gap between those outcomes gave the prosecutor enormous influence over the defendant's choice, and judges across the ideological spectrum described the arrangement as a transfer of sentencing power from the bench to the charging office. The dynamic drew the attention of two attorneys general. In August 2013, Attorney General Eric Holder issued a department wide policy directing prosecutors to decline to seek an 851 enhancement unless the defendant's conduct made the case appropriate for severe sanctions, identifying low level nonviolent offenders for restraint. In September 2014, Holder followed with guidance addressed specifically to plea negotiations, instructing prosecutors that the enhancement should not be used for the sole or predominant purpose of inducing a defendant to plead guilty. Holder also extended the 2013 policy backward in part: in a separate memorandum issued the same month, he directed prosecutors to apply the new policy to defendants charged but not yet convicted and, where consistent with the policy, to move to withdraw 851 informations already filed, while providing that defendants already sentenced would not benefit. In May 2017, Attorney General Jeff Sessions rescinded both memoranda and ordered prosecutors to charge and pursue the most serious readily provable offense, restoring the broad filing discretion the earlier policies had cabined. The sequence shows what the statute itself makes plain: the 851 decision is a policy choice made inside the executive branch, and its scope expands or contracts with whoever leads the department.

### What did the First Step Act change about prior conviction enhancements?

Section 401 of the First Step Act, signed in December 2018, replaced the broad felony drug offense trigger with two narrower categories, serious drug felony and serious violent felony, and lowered the 841(b)(1)(A) ladder to 15 years for one prior qualifying conviction and 25 years for two or more.

The new definitions do the narrowing work. A serious drug felony is an offense of the kind described in the federal armed career criminal definition at 18 U.S.C. 924(e)(2), for which the offender served more than 12 months of imprisonment, and from which the offender was released within 15 years before the instant offense began. Each of those conditions has teeth. The 10 year maximum predicate excludes minor state drug offenses that qualified under the old definition. The 12 month time served requirement excludes convictions that produced only a short sentence, no matter how high the statutory maximum. The 15 year release window excludes old convictions, so a stale record no longer triggers the enhancement. The second category, serious violent felony, covers the violent offenses listed at 18 U.S.C. 3559(c)(2) plus felony assaults of the kind described in 18 U.S.C. 113 when committed in the special maritime and territorial jurisdiction, in each case requiring more than 12 months served; unlike the drug category, the violent category carries no time window, so an old violent conviction still qualifies. The reduced ladder applies across the quantity tiers. The amended statute runs 10 years at the base tier, 15 years after one qualifying prior, and 25 years after two or more, replacing the old 10, 20, and life structure; the 841(b)(1)(B) tier keeps its 10 year enhanced minimum but applies the same narrower predicates. The notice procedure itself did not change: the government must still file the 851 information before trial or plea, but the convictions it lists must satisfy the new definitions. Congress also limited the reach of the change. The amendments applied to defendants whose cases were still pending when the statute took effect, not to defendants already sentenced, a line that distinguished section 401 from the fully retroactive crack cocaine provisions of section 404. (The full account of the 2018 amendments appears in the [First Step Act of 2018 guide](/2018/01/15/first-step-act-2018-guide/).)

### Why do prior convictions skip the jury under Apprendi and Alleyne?

In Almendarez-Torres v. United States, decided in 1998, the Supreme Court held that the fact of a prior conviction is a sentencing factor rather than an element of the offense, so the Sixth Amendment jury right does not attach to it. Both Apprendi and Alleyne expressly preserved that narrow exception.

The exception is the reason the 851 scheme is constitutional without a jury finding on the priors. Apprendi v. New Jersey, decided in 2000, required every fact, other than a prior conviction, that increases the penalty beyond the statutory maximum to be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Alleyne v. United States, decided in 2013, extended the rule to facts that increase a mandatory minimum. Each decision left the Almendarez-Torres exception intact by name. The prior conviction is treated differently because it was established through proceedings with their own procedural safeguards, ordinarily a guilty plea or a jury trial in the earlier case. The 851 procedure functions as the substitute safeguard at the later sentencing. Written advance notice, the affirm or deny colloquy, the right to contest identity and validity, and the burden of proof rules at the denial hearing give the defendant process at sentencing in place of a jury determination of the prior itself. The Supreme Court has since narrowed the exception's reach without closing it. In Erlinger v. United States, decided in 2024, the Court held that only the fact of a prior conviction falls within the exception, so a separate factual inquiry, such as whether earlier offenses occurred on different occasions for purposes of the Armed Career Criminal Act, must go to a jury. The holding leaves 851 enhancements standing but draws the boundary: the judge may find that a conviction exists, not how to characterize the conduct behind it.

### Who actually decides whether the prior conviction enhancement applies?

The prosecutor. The statute gives the filing power to the United States attorney alone, the judge has no authority to seek or block the enhancement, and the defendant can challenge only the convictions the government chose to list, not the choice to list them.

This is the purest illustration of this article's central claim. Congress wrote the mandatory minimums to take sentencing discretion away from judges, and the 851 procedure relocated that discretion rather than eliminating it. The prosecutor decides whether the defendant's record warrants an information, which prior convictions to list, and whether to withdraw a filed information during negotiations. The judge decides only the questions the statute reserves for the court: whether the listed convictions are real, whether they satisfy the predicate definitions, and whether a constitutional challenge survives the five year window. None of those rulings touches the threshold choice, which is unreviewable and which the defendant cannot appeal. The same structure explains the rest of the minimums system. The decision to charge a drug quantity above a threshold, the decision to add a firearm count under 18 U.S.C. 924(c), and the decision to file the substantial assistance motion under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e) all sit with the government, and each one fixes the sentencing floor before the judge opens the file. Discretion moved. It did not disappear.

## The Three Exits Below a Minimum

A federal mandatory minimum leaves the sentencing judge with a single number and no lawful way beneath it, except through three exits written into the statute. The first is a government motion based on substantial assistance under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e). The second is the safety valve of 18 U.S.C. 3553(f), which excuses qualifying drug defendants from the minimum altogether. The third is the 2018 expansion of that valve by section 402 of the First Step Act. Each exit has a different gatekeeper, and the identity of the gatekeeper is the mechanism that matters most. The substantial assistance exit opens only when the prosecutor files the motion. The safety valve opens when the judge finds the statutory criteria satisfied.

### Who controls the substantial assistance exit?

Only the prosecutor controls it. Section 3553(e) of title 18 gives the court authority to impose a sentence below a level established by statute as a minimum sentence only upon motion of the government, reflecting the defendant's substantial assistance in the investigation or prosecution of another person who has committed an offense. The Supreme Court held in Melendez v. United States, decided in 1996, that a guidelines departure motion alone cannot open that door.

The provision entered the code through section 1007(a) of the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, the same statute that reinstated the drug minimums discussed in the article's account of the 1986 legislation. A companion directive in 28 U.S.C. 994(n) told the Sentencing Commission to make sure the guidelines reflected the appropriateness of a lower sentence, including one below a statutory minimum, to account for substantial assistance. The guidelines answered with section 5K1.1, permitting the court to depart from the guideline range upon government motion. That created two separate motions with two separate effects, and the distinction between them is the entire content of the Melendez rule.

Melendez himself illustrated the distinction. The government filed a motion under section 5K1.1 describing his assistance in a letter to the court and asking for a sentence below the otherwise applicable guideline range, but it did not request a sentence below the statutory minimum. The district court granted the guidelines departure yet imposed the ten year minimum required by 21 U.S.C. 841(b)(1)(A), ruling that it had no authority to go further without a motion under section 3553(e), and the Supreme Court agreed. A 5K1.1 motion asks the court to move below the guideline range. Only a 3553(e) motion asks the court to move below the statute. The motion, not the guidelines, is the gate.

The Court had defined the scope of that discretion four years before Melendez. In Wade v. United States, decided in 1992, the Court described the filing decision as a power, not a duty. Even when a defendant has plainly provided substantial assistance, the government may decline to move based on its rational assessment of the costs and benefits of moving, and that judgment is unreviewable unless it rests on an unconstitutional motive such as race or religion, or was not rationally related to any legitimate government end. The Court analogized the choice to other core prosecutorial decisions, such as what charges to file. Assistance is a necessary condition for the exit, never a sufficient one.

Once the government files the motion, the court decides how far below the minimum to go. Section 5K1.1(a) lists the considerations the court may weigh: the significance and usefulness of the assistance, taking into account the government's evaluation; the truthfulness, completeness, and reliability of any information or testimony provided; the nature and extent of the assistance; any injury suffered, or any danger or risk of injury to the defendant or the defendant's family, resulting from the assistance; and the timeliness of the assistance. Appellate review of what follows is narrow. A discretionary refusal to depart is generally not reviewable on appeal, and a granted departure is reviewed for abuse of discretion under the standard the Court set in Koon v. United States, decided in 1996.

The same substantial assistance logic extends past sentencing through a separate procedural door. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 35(b) permits the government to move, after sentencing, to reduce a sentence to reflect substantial assistance rendered after the sentence was imposed, and the rule expressly provides that the court may reduce the sentence below the minimum established by statute. Like the 3553(e) motion, the Rule 35(b) motion is filed only by the government.

### What must a defendant prove to use the safety valve?

The defendant must persuade the sentencing judge, by a preponderance of the evidence, that five statutory conditions are met: limited criminal history, no violence or firearms connected with the offense, no death or serious bodily injury resulting from it, no leadership role in the offense, and complete truthful disclosure of everything the defendant knows. No government motion is required. The statute directs the court to act after the government has been afforded the opportunity to make a recommendation, which is an invitation to be heard, not a motion the court must receive.

The valve entered the code through section 80001 of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, eight years after the minimums it relieves were reinstated. Its heading states the design plainly: limitation on the applicability of statutory minimums in certain cases. The operative sentence begins with the words notwithstanding any other provision of law, and it applies in the case of an offense under section 401, 404, or 406 of the Controlled Substances Act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 841, 844, and 846, or section 1010 or 1013 of the Controlled Substances Import and Export Act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 960 and 963. When the court finds the five criteria satisfied at sentencing, it imposes a sentence under the guidelines without regard to any statutory minimum sentence. A law review analysis in the Columbia Journal of Law and Social Problems captured the structural difference between the two exits: the substantial assistance provision is a departure from a minimum that remains legally applicable, while the safety valve is an excusal from the minimum, so that for a qualifying defendant the floor ceases to exist for sentencing purposes.

The five criteria each do independent work. The criminal history criterion, discussed in detail in the next section, originally barred any defendant with more than one criminal history point. The violence criterion bars any defendant who used violence or credible threats of violence, or who possessed a firearm or other dangerous weapon, or induced another participant to do so, in connection with the offense. Courts read that criterion as focusing on the defendant's own conduct and conduct the defendant aided or abetted, counseled, commanded, induced, procured, or willfully caused, as the commentary to guideline section 5C1.2 explains. The injury criterion bars any defendant whose offense resulted in death or serious bodily injury to any person. The role criterion bars any defendant who was an organizer, leader, manager, or supervisor of others in the offense, as determined under the guidelines, or who was engaged in a continuing criminal enterprise as defined in section 408 of the Controlled Substances Act. The disclosure criterion requires that, not later than the time of the sentencing hearing, the defendant has truthfully provided to the government all information and evidence the defendant has concerning the offense or offenses that were part of the same course of conduct or of a common scheme or plan. The statute adds that the defendant's lack of useful information, or the fact that the government already knew it, does not by itself defeat compliance. A defendant who genuinely has nothing to offer can still satisfy the fifth criterion by telling the truth about having nothing to offer.

Three features of the valve's procedure distinguish it from the substantial assistance exit. First, the burden rests on the defendant, who must convince the court by a preponderance of the evidence that each criterion is met, as the Ninth Circuit held in United States v. Ajugwo in 1996. Second, the finding is made by the judge at sentencing, not by a jury. The Supreme Court held in Alleyne v. United States in 2013 that any fact increasing a mandatory minimum is an element for the jury, but the courts of appeals have held that Alleyne does not reach the valve, because the valve removes a minimum rather than increasing one. Third, the valve's coverage is offense specific. It reaches only the five listed drug statutes. A conviction under 21 U.S.C. 860 for drug trafficking near schools, which carries twice the penalties of simple trafficking, is outside the valve, as several circuits have held. A conviction under 18 U.S.C. 924(c) for using or carrying a firearm during a drug trafficking crime is outside the valve as well, and the 924(c) term runs consecutively to any other sentence, so a defendant who qualifies for the valve on the drug count still serves the firearm minimum in full. The guideline implementation of the valve sits at section 5C1.2, and a qualifying defendant also receives a two level reduction in offense level under section 2D1.1(b)(18).

### What did the valve's expansion change?

Section 402 of the First Step Act, signed on December 21, 2018, replaced the old criminal history bar with a graduated three part test, extended the valve to maritime drug offenses, and shielded a defendant's disclosures from being used to increase the sentence. The other four criteria were left exactly as they were.

The old criminal history rule was the valve's narrowest gate. It disqualified any defendant with more than one criminal history point as calculated under the guidelines, so a single prior sentence of more than a year, or a handful of minor prior sentences, was enough to close the exit. Congress described that low threshold as producing mandatory minimum sentences many members regarded as unnecessary and harsh, and the amendment was designed to give district courts more flexibility for defendants with relatively minor records. The new paragraph 3553(f)(1) disqualifies only a defendant who has more than four criminal history points, excluding any points resulting from a one point offense; or a prior three point offense; or a prior two point violent offense, each as determined under the sentencing guidelines.

The meaning of the word and connecting those three subparagraphs produced a circuit split that the Supreme Court resolved in Pulsifer v. United States, decided on March 15, 2024. Mark Pulsifer had pleaded guilty to distributing at least fifty grams of methamphetamine and faced a fifteen year mandatory minimum reflecting his record. He sought the valve, arguing that the conjunctive and meant he was disqualified only if he had all three listed characteristics in combination, and his record lacked a two point violent offense. The government argued that each subparagraph stated a separate disqualifying condition. The Ninth Circuit had adopted Pulsifer's reading in United States v. Lopez in 2021, while the Eighth Circuit had adopted the government's reading in Pulsifer's own case. The Supreme Court affirmed the Eighth Circuit by a vote of six to three, with Justice Kagan writing for the majority: paragraph (f)(1) creates an eligibility checklist with three necessary conditions, so a defendant is eligible only if the defendant does not have more than four criminal history points, does not have a prior three point offense, and does not have a prior two point violent offense. Any one of the three disqualifies. Justice Gorsuch dissented, joined by two colleagues.

The 2018 amendment made two further changes. It added offenses under 46 U.S.C. 70503 and 70506, the Maritime Drug Law Enforcement Act provisions covering drug trafficking on vessels subject to United States jurisdiction, closing a gap that had left maritime smuggling defendants outside the relief available to land based traffickers. And it added a use protection provision: information disclosed by a defendant under the valve subsection may not be used to enhance the sentence of the defendant unless the information relates to a violent offense as defined in section 16 of title 18.

The amendment's reach into pending cases was deliberately limited, which is why it is best described as retroactivity adjacent rather than retroactive. Section 402(b) provides that the amendments apply only to a conviction entered on or after the date of enactment. Courts have treated a conviction as entered when the judgment of conviction and sentence are entered on the district court's docket, so the expanded valve reached defendants who had pleaded guilty or been found guilty before December 21, 2018, but had not yet been sentenced, a reading the Sixth Circuit applied in United States v. Havens in 2019 and the Department of Justice adopted as policy for pending sentencings. Section 402 contains no instruction extending the valve to defendants whose cases were already final on collateral review. The amendment helped the pipeline of cases moving through the courts at enactment while leaving closed cases under the old one point rule. The article's companion discussion of the 2018 statute places this limited reach alongside the act's other sentencing provisions.

### Why does it matter who holds each key?

Because the defendant cannot earn the most used exit without the prosecutor's cooperation, while the valve's exit belongs to the judge, the system produces two distinct economies. One is an economy of cooperation, traded for motions. The other is an economy of proof, in which the defendant establishes statutory criteria in open court. Neither economy reaches a 924(c) defendant.

The cooperation economy begins with the proffer. A defendant seeking a substantial assistance motion typically sits with prosecutors and agents and answers questions about the offense and about other participants, suppliers, and customers. The proffer is followed by continued cooperation, which may include testifying before a grand jury or at trial, and the prosecutor then memorializes the assistance in a letter to the court, the practitioner shorthand calls it a 5K1.1 letter, describing what the defendant provided and requesting the departure. Plea agreements often formalize the arrangement in advance: the government promises to consider filing a section 3553(e) motion, preserving the defense's ability to challenge a bad faith refusal under the standards Wade set. Every step of this sequence runs through the prosecutor's office.

That structure creates the incentive pattern the literature calls the cooperation paradox. Professor Stephen Schulhofer gave the phenomenon its name, and a Federal Judicial Center study by Barbara Vincent and Paul Hofer documented its operation: because substantial assistance departures reward defendants who can provide substantial information, higher ranking traffickers, who are more deeply embedded in the distribution network, are better positioned to earn them than low level couriers and street dealers, who often know little worth trading. The valve was Congress's answer to that paradox in 1994, aimed at the low level, nonviolent, first time offender who had nothing to sell, and the 2018 expansion extended the same logic to defendants with modest records. But the paradox persists within the substantial assistance exit itself, because the value of cooperation is measured by its usefulness to the government, which is the first of the five considerations listed in section 5K1.1(a), and usefulness correlates with rank.

The asymmetry also explains why neither exit helps the defendant convicted under 18 U.S.C. 924(c). The firearm statute carries its own mandatory minimums and requires that the term run consecutively to any other term of imprisonment. The safety valve does not list 924(c) among its covered offenses, so a judge cannot use it to go below the firearm minimum. The substantial assistance motion could in principle reach a 924(c) count, since section 3553(e) speaks of a minimum sentence without limiting the statute of conviction, but the motion remains the prosecutor's to file or withhold. A defendant with both a drug count and a 924(c) count who qualifies for the valve on the drug count still faces the consecutive firearm term.

Booker v. United States, decided in 2005, changed none of this. The decision rendered the sentencing guidelines advisory, but the Court left statutory minimums untouched, and the courts of appeals held that Booker does not affect the requirement of a government motion as a prerequisite for going below a statutory minimum. The Eighth Circuit so held in United States v. Mullins in 2005, and the other circuits followed. The guidelines sections that implement the two exits, 5K1.1 for substantial assistance and 5C1.2 for the safety valve, continue to track their parent statutes rather than the other way around. An advisory guideline range cannot authorize a sentence below a number Congress wrote into a statute. Only the three exits can, and each opens only for the person holding its key.

The minimums were enacted to remove sentencing discretion from judges, and their operative effect was to relocate that discretion to prosecutors through charging and motion decisions that courts barely review. The three exits do not restore the judge to the center of the system; they ration the judge's authority through gates that others control, which is why the identity of the decisionmaker matters more than the number in the statute.

## The forgotten history

The story of the federal mandatory minimum sentence is routinely told as a straight line from the 1950s to the present, as though the minimums of the modern drug code have operated without interruption since their first enactment. That telling is wrong in a way that matters. Congress created the first federal drug minimums in 1951, stiffened them in 1956, and then repealed nearly all of them in 1970 after a bipartisan judgment that they were ineffective and inflexible. The minimums in force in federal courtrooms from 1986 onward are a second creation, built by a different Congress for different reasons, and they have been narrowed in turn by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 and the First Step Act of 2018. The 1970 repeal is the hinge of the whole account, and the sixteen years between repeal and reinstatement are the least understood stretch in the history of federal sentencing.

### Why did Congress create federal drug minimums in the first place?

Congress created them to take a perceived drug problem away from the discretion of trial judges. The Boggs Act of 1951, Public Law 82-255, signed by President Harry Truman on November 2, 1951, was the first federal statute to set mandatory minimum prison terms for drug offenses, and it barred judges from suspending sentences or granting probation and parole.

Sponsored by Representative Hale Boggs, a Louisiana Democrat, the act amended the Narcotic Drugs Import and Export Act and imposed a two year minimum for a first violation, a five year minimum for a second, and a ten year minimum for a third. The drive behind it was postwar anxiety about narcotics reaching young people, amplified by the testimony of Harry Anslinger, the commissioner of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, who warned Congress that marijuana use led adolescents onward to heroin. Witnesses at the Boggs hearings repeatedly connected marijuana to eventual heroin addiction, and Anslinger testified that more than half of young addicts had started with marijuana before graduating to heroin and the needle. The act grouped cannabis with heroin and cocaine under a single penalty scheme, so that the sentence turned on the number of prior convictions rather than on the drug, the quantity, or the circumstances of the offense.

### What did the later fifties narcotics law change?

It raised the penalties, widened the offenses covered, and introduced death as a possible punishment. The Narcotic Control Act of 1956, Public Law 84-728, approved on July 18, 1956, grew out of a 1955 Senate subcommittee investigation into narcotics trafficking led by Senator Price Daniel of Texas.

For possession, the act kept the 1951 architecture but raised the rungs: two years for a first offense, five for a second, and ten for a third. For sale and trafficking, the minimums moved to five years for a first offense and ten years for a second, and parole and probation were made unavailable to nearly all offenders, with only first time possessors retaining eligibility. The provision that drew the most attention authorized death for an adult who sold heroin to a person under eighteen: the text directed imprisonment for life, or for not less than ten years, but permitted the offender to suffer death if the jury in its discretion so directed. The Senate debate made the policy explicit. Senator Daniel described marijuana as a drug that started most addicts in the use of drugs and led them to the heroin habit and to their final destruction, and the federal role was framed as the suppression of trafficking through punishment rather than the treatment of addiction through medicine.

### Why did the minimums come under bipartisan fire in the sixties?

Because the promised deterrence did not materialize, and because the sentences were plainly too rigid for the range of conduct they covered. President John F. Kennedy assembled the President's Advisory Commission on Narcotic and Drug Abuse in 1961, chaired by Judge E. Barrett Prettyman, and its 1963 report rejected the punitive logic of the fifties statutes.

The commission concluded that penalties should fit both the offender and the offense, that rehabilitation rather than retributive punishment was the necessary aim, and that the long sentences mandated by law had shown little or no deterrent effect on drug use. The report became the intellectual anchor for a broader shift. The Narcotic Addict Rehabilitation Act of 1966 created civil commitment for treatment as an alternative to prosecution for addicts, and the Drug Abuse Control Amendments of the mid-sixties moved parts of federal drug policy toward a medical framework. The United States Sentencing Commission's 2011 Report to Congress on mandatory minimum penalties records that by the late 1960s, as the minimums became increasingly unpopular, the Nixon administration proposed a sweeping reform of the controlled substance sentencing laws. The consensus was no longer the fear-driven unanimity of 1951. It was that fixed floors had failed to reduce drug use, had prevented judges from distinguishing the serious offender from the minor one, and had cluttered the law with penalties that neither fit the crime nor deterred it.

### How did the repeal of the drug minimums happen?

Congress repealed nearly all federal drug mandatory minimums in a single comprehensive statute, and the vote was not close. The Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, Public Law 91-513, signed by President Richard Nixon on October 27, 1970, replaced the old patchwork with a new architecture, Title II of which became the Controlled Substances Act.

The House passed the bill 341 to 6, and the Senate passed it 54 to 0. The Sentencing Commission's 2011 report states that the act repealed nearly all mandatory minimum penalties for drug offenses, and the statute's history records Congress's reasoning: that eliminating mandatory minimums would establish a more realistic, more flexible, and therefore more effective system of punishment and deterrence for federal narcotics violations. The repeal was not a blanket amnesty for all minimums in federal law. The same 1970 act retained and created mandatory minimums for other offenses, including a one year minimum under 18 U.S.C. 924(c) for using or carrying a firearm during the commission of a federal felony, and a one year minimum for using or carrying explosives while committing certain crimes. The target was specifically the drug minimums built by the Boggs and 1956 regimes. Congress kept the underlying offenses, the graduated penalty ranges, and the recidivist enhancements, but returned to judges the authority to sentence below the old floors. The repeal reflected no general policy against mandatory minimums as such. It reflected a considered judgment, reached by an overwhelming bipartisan majority and signed by a Republican president, that mandatory minimums had proved the wrong instrument for drug offenses.

### What filled the sixteen year gap?

A sentencing system designed to make judges, not prosecutors or legislators, the central decisionmakers. Concern about disparities among judges, voiced most prominently by District Judge Marvin Frankel, had produced a bipartisan push for a different kind of uniformity: one achieved through structured judicial discretion rather than fixed legislative floors.

The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, enacted as Title II of the Comprehensive Crime Control Act, Public Law 98-473, approved October 12, 1984, created the United States Sentencing Commission and directed it to promulgate guidelines that would govern the exercise of judicial discretion while preserving it. The guidelines were mandatory in their original design, the commission was told to narrow the ranges within which judges could choose, and parole was abolished for new federal sentences so that the time imposed would be the time served. The commission transmitted its initial guidelines to Congress in 1987, and they took effect that November. In other words, the 1984 Congress had chosen a path toward uniformity that worked through the judge, through structured discretion and appellate review, while the 1986 Congress would choose the older path of removing the judge's discretion entirely for designated offenses. The two mechanisms overlapped uneasily from the start. Congress directed the commission to keep the guidelines consistent with the new statutes, and the commission incorporated the 1986 minimums into the guideline drug quantity table, so that the guidelines designed to regularize judicial choice became the transmission belt for legislative floors. A mandatory minimum overrides the guidelines whenever the two point to different sentences, and that hierarchy was established the moment the 1986 thresholds entered the code.

### What did the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of the eighties actually do?

It rebuilt mandatory minimums around drug type and quantity, in a statute drafted and passed in a midterm election season after the cocaine overdose death of star Len Bias. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, Public Law 99-570, signed by President Ronald Reagan on October 27, 1986, set five year and ten year minimums for drug trafficking quantities.

For cocaine powder, the five year floor attached at 500 grams and the ten year floor at five kilograms. For crack cocaine, the same five year floor attached at five grams and the same ten year floor at fifty grams. The hundredfold difference between the crack and powder thresholds created the 100 to 1 quantity ratio that would define federal drug sentencing for the next quarter century. The statute passed the House by 392 votes to 16 and the Senate by 97 votes to 2, a margin that reflected how little dissent the political moment allowed. The catalyst was the death of Len Bias, the University of Maryland basketball star selected by the Boston Celtics in the 1986 professional draft, who died of a cocaine overdose in June of that year. Media coverage of the death, and of the spread of crack through American cities, converted drug policy into the dominant domestic issue of the midterm campaign. The legislative record contains no clear account of why the thresholds were fixed where they were. The House version had initially set a 50 to 1 ratio between powder and crack, and the Senate, citing the supposed harmfulness of crack, raised it to 100 to 1 during deliberations. No hearing record explains how the committees settled on five grams of crack as the trigger for five years, or fifty grams as the trigger for ten. Evidence reported later suggested that Bias had overdosed on powder cocaine rather than crack, the form of the drug for which Congress was writing the harshest penalties in the bill that came to be associated with his name. The 1986 act also extended the minimums to conspiracies and attempts, which had previously carried the floors only when the substantive offense was completed, and established the principle that would become the modern regime's signature: the minimum attaches to the drug type and the weight involved, regardless of the defendant's role, history, or circumstances.

### What did the follow-up antidrug law add?

It pushed the minimums into simple possession and into the death penalty. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988, Public Law 100-690, signed by President Reagan on November 18, 1988, created a five year mandatory minimum for simple possession of five grams or more of crack cocaine.

That provision made crack the first and only drug subject to a federal mandatory minimum for a first offense of simple possession. Possession of any other drug, including powder cocaine, remained punishable by no more than one year for a first offense. The 1988 act also restored the federal death penalty for certain drug offenses, including large scale trafficking by so called kingpins and killings committed in furtherance of a continuing criminal enterprise. With those additions, the modern minimum regime was complete in its architecture: quantity driven floors for trafficking, a possession floor unique to crack, conspiracy and attempt liability at the same floors, and capital punishment at the top of the scale. Where the 1970 Congress had concluded that fixed floors were unrealistic and inflexible, the 1988 Congress was using them as its default instrument, applying them to conduct as minor as holding a small quantity of crack for personal use. The demographic consequences were documented by the Sentencing Commission in the years that followed: federal crack defendants were overwhelmingly Black, while powder cocaine defendants were far more likely to be white or Hispanic, so the steepest minimums fell on the defendants least able to absorb them.

### How did the Fair Sentencing Act begin the narrowing?

It cut the crack to powder ratio from 100 to 1 to 18 to 1 and eliminated the possession minimum that had made crack unique. The Fair Sentencing Act of 2010, signed by President Barack Obama on August 3, 2010, raised the crack triggers for the five and ten year minimums to 28 and 280 grams, leaving powder thresholds unchanged.

The act also repealed the five year mandatory minimum for simple possession of crack that the 1988 act had created, removing the only federal minimum for a first possession offense. It increased financial penalties and raised guideline ranges for major traffickers and for drug cases involving violence, pairing the relief at the bottom of the scale with added severity at the top. The 2010 act passed the Senate by unanimous consent and the House by voice vote, a legislative ease that contrasted sharply with the political frenzy of 1986 and that registered how thoroughly the consensus had shifted. The Sentencing Commission had spent years documenting the basis for reform: its reports found no scientific support for treating crack as a hundred times more dangerous than powder, since both contain the same active ingredient, and found that the ratio had produced stark racial disparities without delivering the promised deterrence. As originally introduced, the 2010 bill would have eliminated the disparity entirely, but the drafters settled on 18 to 1 to secure bipartisan support, because some members remained convinced that crack was more addictive and more closely tied to violent crime. The new law applied to future cases and to cases pending on the date of enactment, but it did not reach backward to prisoners already sentenced under the old thresholds. That unfinished business waited eight years.

### What did the First Step Act change?

It narrowed the minimums at four points and made the 2010 crack reform retroactive. The First Step Act, signed by President Donald Trump on December 21, 2018, devoted its Title IV to sentencing reform, and sections 401 through 404 addressed mandatory minimums directly. Section 401 reduced and restricted the enhanced minimums for repeat drug offenders.

The mandatory minimum for a high level drug offense after one prior qualifying conviction fell from twenty years to fifteen, and the minimum after two or more prior convictions fell from life imprisonment to twenty five years. The section also narrowed the trigger: the enhanced floors apply only where the prior convictions are for a serious drug felony or a serious violent felony, rather than for any felony drug offense, which confined the enhancements to defendants with genuinely serious records. Section 402 broadened the safety valve, the statutory exception that permits a judge to sentence below a drug mandatory minimum. Before the act, only defendants with effectively spotless criminal records, limited to one criminal history point, could qualify. The expanded valve reaches defendants with up to four criminal history points, with disqualification rules calibrated to the type of offense, which made low level nonviolent offenders with minor records eligible for sentences below the floors. Section 403 addressed the stacking of firearm enhancements under 18 U.S.C. 924(c). Before the act, prosecutors could charge multiple 924(c) counts in a single indictment and trigger the twenty five year enhanced minimum for a second or subsequent conviction even when the defendant had no prior conviction at all. The act clarified that the enhancement applies only where the defendant has a prior conviction that is already final, restricting the harshest firearm minimum to true repeat offenders. Section 404 made the Fair Sentencing Act retroactive. Prisoners sentenced under the old 100 to 1 crack thresholds before August 3, 2010, could petition the court for resentencing as if the 2010 thresholds had applied at the time of their offenses. The relief was not automatic, and the court retained discretion over whether to reduce the sentence. According to figures from the United States Sentencing Commission cited by the Congressional Research Service, courts granted 3,705 sentence reductions under section 404, closing the circle that the 2010 act had left open.

### Why does the error about continuity matter?

Because the shape of the history determines what conclusions it can support. If mandatory minimums are described as an unbroken tradition running from the 1950s to the present, the policy looks like a settled national commitment, and its survival looks like evidence of its success. The actual record supports neither inference.

The first generation of minimums was tried, found wanting by a commission appointed to study it, rejected by a Republican administration, and repealed by an overwhelming bipartisan majority that said plainly the floors were unrealistic and inflexible. The second generation was built in a different political emergency, on thresholds chosen without a hearing record to explain them, and it produced the racial disparities that later Congresses spent decades partially undoing. They are a choice that Congress made, unmade, and made again, and the periods when they were absent are as instructive as the periods when they were in force.

## The leverage points that fix the sentence before sentencing

A federal mandatory minimum is often described as a rule that ties the judge's hands. The fuller account is that it moves the hands. Long before a judge reads a presentence report, three decisions made by the prosecutor have already fixed the floor beneath the sentence: which drug quantity to charge, whether to file a prior conviction notice under 21 U.S.C. 851, and how many firearm counts to charge under 18 U.S.C. 924(c). Each of these is a charging decision, each is committed to prosecutorial discretion, and each is functionally unreviewable, which is why the operative effect of mandatory minimums is not the disappearance of sentencing discretion but its relocation from the courtroom bench to the prosecutor's office.

### Who decides whether a mandatory minimum attaches to a case?

The prosecutor decides, through charging choices that the judge cannot revisit. The quantity alleged in the indictment determines whether the drug thresholds of 21 U.S.C. 841(b) are crossed; the filing of an 851 information triggers the prior conviction enhancements; and each 924(c) count carries its own consecutive term.

The quantity decision is the most consequential lever in drug cases. The statute sets a ten year floor at one threshold and a five year floor at a lower one, with the exact amounts varying by substance, and the amount the indictment attributes to the defendant is the amount that counts. A quantity just below the threshold leaves the judge the full advisory range; a quantity just above it removes the bottom of that range entirely. Nothing requires the prosecutor to charge the largest quantity the evidence supports or the smallest, and the choice is binary in effect.

The 851 filing is the second lever. Section 851 requires the government to file a formal information before trial or plea if it seeks to rely on a prior conviction to enhance a drug sentence, and the enhancements it triggers were historically severe, including doubled minimums and, in the configuration that stood before the 2018 narrowing, a mandatory life term for a third qualifying offense. Prior convictions are established by records rather than testimony, so the decision to file or withhold an 851 notice is almost purely discretionary: it can be deployed during plea negotiation and withdrawn as part of a deal, or filed to lock in the higher floor, and the judge has no independent authority to disregard it once properly filed.

The third lever is the number of 924(c) counts. Each count of using or carrying a firearm during and in relation to a drug trafficking crime or crime of violence carries its own mandatory term that must run consecutively to any other sentence, and before the First Step Act of 2018, a second 924(c) count charged in the same indictment could trigger the statute's twenty five year subsequent conviction provision even without a prior conviction on the defendant's record. The scale appears in the commission's Quick Facts for fiscal year 2020: 2,500 offenders were convicted under 924(c), 81.2 percent remained subject to the mandatory minimum at sentencing, and their average sentence was 147 months, falling to 97 months for those who received relief. Prosecutors decide how many counts to charge, and therefore how many consecutive terms the judge must stack.

## The charging directives, from one attorney general to the next

Because these decisions are discretionary, the internal policies of the Department of Justice matter as much as the statutes. Successive attorneys general have issued written charging memoranda, and their directives on mandatory minimums have swung between constraint and command.

### What did each attorney general's charging memo direct about mandatory minimums?

Ashcroft's 2003 memorandum ordered prosecutors to charge the most serious, readily provable offense. Holder's 2013 Smart on Crime memorandum told them not to charge triggering quantities for low level, nonviolent offenders. Sessions's 2017 memorandum restored the Ashcroft rule, and Garland's 2022 memoranda reserved mandatory minimum charges for cases where lesser charges would not reflect the offense's seriousness.

The Ashcroft memorandum, titled Department Policy Concerning Charging Criminal Offenses, Disposition of Charges, and Sentencing, was the high water mark of the command model. It directed prosecutors to charge and pursue the most serious, readily provable offense, defined as the one generating the most substantial sentence under the guidelines, unless a mandatory minimum or consecutive count would generate a longer sentence, and it strongly encouraged 851 filings in every case where the prior conviction was readily provable. Exceptions required supervisory approval, and prosecutors were told to oppose downward departures and appeal those that occurred. The memorandum followed the PROTECT Act of 2003 and its Feeney Amendment, which Congress had passed amid concern about sentencing leniency.

The Holder memorandum of August 12, 2013, titled Department Policy on Charging Mandatory Minimum Sentences and Recidivist Enhancements in Certain Drug Cases, was issued as part of the Smart on Crime initiative and broke with the Ashcroft approach for a defined class of defendants. For low level, nonviolent drug offenders without significant criminal histories, prosecutors were instructed to decline to charge the drug quantity necessary to trigger a mandatory minimum, effectively capping the exposure through the charging decision itself. A follow up guidance memorandum of September 24, 2014, directed that prosecutors not use the filing of a prior conviction notice as leverage to induce a guilty plea. The 2013 memorandum functioned as a charging safety valve, and commission data showed that fewer mandatory minimum charges were brought in the years it governed.

The Sessions memorandum of May 10, 2017, titled Department Charging and Sentencing Policy, expressly rescinded both Holder memoranda. It ordered prosecutors to charge and pursue the most serious, readily provable offense, defined as the offense carrying the most substantial guideline sentence including mandatory minimums, and it required supervisory approval with documented justification for any departure from the policy. Sessions defended the policy as the enforcement of the laws as passed by Congress and attributed a rise in violent crime to Holder era leniency, a claim his critics disputed. An interim memorandum from Acting Attorney General Monty Wilkinson in January 2021 rescinded the Sessions memorandum while longer term policy was formulated.

The Garland memoranda of December 16, 2022, came in two parts, a general memorandum on charging, pleas, and sentencing and a companion drug cases memorandum. Together they directed that charges carrying mandatory minimums should ordinarily be reserved for instances where the remaining charges would not sufficiently reflect the seriousness of the conduct, the danger to the community, and the purposes of punishment, deterrence, and rehabilitation. The drug memorandum applied that standard with particular force to drug type and quantity minimums, which it described as having produced disproportionately severe sentences and racial disparities. It instructed prosecutors not to automatically charge a mandatory minimum but to weigh factors including leadership, firearm possession, gang ties, and violent history, and it required supervisory approval and reporting to the Executive Office for United States Attorneys for any mandatory minimum charge pursued. It also directed prosecutors to treat crack cocaine offenses as powder cocaine offenses for charging and sentencing recommendations, on the ground that the disparity had no scientific basis and drove unwarranted racial disparities.

## What judges appointed by presidents of both parties said about the shift

The structural criticism, that mandatory minimums transfer sentencing authority from judges to prosecutors, has been voiced from the bench by judges whose appointments span the partisan divide.

Judge Paul G. Cassell, nominated to the United States District Court for the District of Utah by President George W. Bush and confirmed in 2002, became one of the most prominent judicial critics after he was compelled to impose a fifty five year mandatory sentence in a drug and firearms case. Testifying in 2007 before the House Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime, Terrorism, and Homeland Security as chair of the Judicial Conference's Committee on Criminal Law, Cassell said of the 924(c) stacking provision, "I have found the application of one particularly egregious mandatory minimum sentencing provision to result in sentences that are cruel and unusual, unwise and unjust." He added that it was hard to explain why a judge must give a longer sentence to a first offender who carried a gun to several marijuana transactions than to a person convicted of murder. Cassell resigned from the bench in 2007 and coauthored with Erik Luna the article Mandatory Minimalism, which stated the structural point directly: "Mandatory minimums frequently result in disparate and unduly harsh sentences, but they also transfer the authority and discretion granted to sentencing judges to the hands of prosecutors."

Judge Mark W. Bennett, appointed to the United States District Court for the Northern District of Iowa by President Bill Clinton in 1994, pressed the same structural criticism in a 2017 public radio interview. Responding to the argument that mandatory minimums promote consistency and therefore fairness, Bennett answered that the opposite was true, saying, "Mandatory minimums support unwarranted uniformity by treating everyone alike even though their situations are dramatically different." He illustrated the point with two nonviolent methamphetamine offenders, one selling for profit and one trading to support an addiction, asking whether it made sense to treat them identically when Congress had directed judges to consider the nature and circumstances of the offense and the history and characteristics of the defendant.

Institutionally, the Judicial Conference of the United States, the policymaking body of the federal judiciary, has opposed mandatory minimums for decades. At a 2008 congressional hearing, testimony noted that the Conference had reiterated its opposition more than a dozen times, on the grounds that mandatory minimums distort the sentencing process, undermine the guideline system Congress created to promote fairness and proportionality, and encourage charge and fact bargaining that damages honesty in sentencing.

## What the Sentencing Commission measured about application

The United States Sentencing Commission's periodic reports to Congress are the authoritative statistical record of how mandatory minimums apply, to whom, and with what results. The commission issued a comprehensive report in October 2011 covering fiscal year 2010, and a drug focused report in October 2017 covering fiscal year 2016, along with annual Quick Facts summaries. The figures below are drawn from those reports and are identified by year.

The October 2011 report, analyzing fiscal year 2010, found that more than one quarter of the 73,239 offenders sentenced in federal court, 27.2 percent or 19,896 offenders, were convicted of an offense carrying a mandatory minimum. Over three quarters of those convictions, 77.4 percent, were for drug trafficking offenses. Hispanic offenders were the largest group at 38.3 percent, followed by Black offenders at 31.5 percent, White offenders at 27.4 percent, and other offenders at 2.7 percent. More than 90 percent, 90.3 percent, were men, and 73.6 percent were United States citizens. On relief, almost half, 46.7 percent, were relieved from the minimum's application at sentencing through substantial assistance, the safety valve, or both, but relief was not distributed evenly: Black offenders received relief least often, in 34.9 percent of their cases. Offenders convicted of a mandatory minimum offense pleaded guilty at a slightly lower rate, 94.1 percent, than offenders not convicted of such an offense, 97.5 percent, while offenders eligible for the safety valve pleaded guilty at 99.4 percent, higher than both groups.

The October 2017 report, Mandatory Minimum Penalties for Drug Offenses in the Federal Criminal Justice System, analyzing fiscal year 2016, organized its conclusions into ten findings. Over half, 52.8 percent, of offenders convicted of a drug mandatory minimum offense faced a minimum of ten years or more, and the average sentence for those offenders was 94 months, more than double the 42 month average for drug offenders not convicted of such an offense. Offenders who received relief averaged 64 months against 126 months for those who remained subject to the minimum at sentencing. The guidelines compounded the effect: nearly three quarters, 72.6 percent, of offenders who remained subject to a drug minimum had a guideline minimum exceeding the statutory floor, and a majority, 58.8 percent, received sentences above the statutory minimum. The report found that drug minimums were used less often than before, with the number and share of offenders convicted of such offenses declining since fiscal year 2010, while the offenses of those convicted tended to be more serious, and it concluded that the penalties applied more broadly than Congress may have anticipated. On demographics, among the 8,342 offenders subject to a federal mandatory minimum at sentencing in fiscal year 2016, Black offenders were the largest group at 35.5 percent, followed by White offenders at 31.1 percent and Hispanic offenders at 30.9 percent, figures drawn from congressional findings citing the commission's work.

Finding seven stated that drug mandatory minimums appear to give defendants a significant incentive to provide substantial assistance to the government, while finding eight cautioned that neither the safety valve nor the substantial assistance provision fully ameliorated the impact on relatively low level offenders. Finding ten reported that drug trafficking offenders convicted of a drug minimum had a lower recidivism rate than comparable offenders not convicted of one, while noting that the difference was likely due in part to an older age at release.

## The defenders' case, with its evidence

The claim that mandatory minimums have no serious defenders does not survive contact with the record. Three arguments have been advanced in their favor, each with an empirical literature, and the commission's own data supplies material for both sides of the dispute.

### Do mandatory minimums deter crime?

Supporters cite economist studies of sentence enhancements, notably Kessler and Levitt's 1999 analysis of California's Proposition 8, which found eligible crimes fell 4 percent in the first year and 8 percent within three years, consistent with deterrence. Critics cite a 1997 RAND study finding minimums not cost effective for reducing cocaine use, expenditures, or drug crime.

The uniformity argument holds that mandatory minimums advance the central purpose of the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, which was to reduce unwarranted disparity among similarly situated offenders sentenced by different judges. Supporters have argued that a statutory floor guarantees that like cases produce like sentences regardless of the courtroom, and that this consistency is itself a form of fairness. The critics' empirical answer comes from the commission's relief data: in fiscal year 2010, the 46.7 percent overall relief rate masked wide variation, with Black offenders relieved in only 34.9 percent of their cases, which critics present as evidence that discretion did not disappear but moved to charging and motion decisions where it operated less visibly and less evenly. The 2011 report also documented geographic concentration, with seven of ninety four districts accounting for 27.0 percent of mandatory minimum cases, a pattern critics cite as evidence that application depends heavily on local charging practice.

The cooperation argument is the one the commission's data supports most directly. Defenders argue that the leverage of a certain, severe floor is what induces lower level participants to provide substantial assistance against higher level offenders, making the minimum a tool for dismantling organizations rather than merely punishing individuals. The 2017 report's seventh finding, that drug minimums give defendants a significant incentive to cooperate, is the empirical anchor for this claim, and the relief statistics show the mechanism working at scale: nearly half of fiscal year 2010 mandatory minimum offenders received sentences below the floor through cooperation, the safety valve, or both. Critics respond with the report's eighth finding, that the relief provisions did not fully reach relatively low level offenders, and with the structural objection that cooperation leverage is valuable only to defendants who possess information the government wants, which tends to favor participants with more rather than less culpability.

## What the courts settled about constitutionality

The constitutional questions most readers ask about have settled answers. Mandatory minimum sentences have been upheld against Eighth Amendment proportionality challenges. In Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957 (1991), the Supreme Court sustained a mandatory sentence of life imprisonment without parole for possession of more than 650 grams of cocaine, with the controlling opinions holding that the Eighth Amendment's proportionality principle did not forbid the sentence. Courts have likewise held that the prosecutor's discretion to charge a triggering quantity, to file or withhold an 851 notice, and to file or withhold a substantial assistance motion under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e) is an exercise of executive authority that judges may not second guess, with relief available only on narrow constitutional grounds such as a showing that the decision rested on race or religion.

The statute sets the number, but the number attaches only when the prosecutor's charging decisions place the case within the statute's terms, and the only widely used path below the number requires the prosecutor's motion. The commission's reports, across 1991, 2011, and 2017, document who bears that structure and how often its exits open. The defenders' case for uniformity, deterrence signaling, and cooperation leverage has evidence behind it, and the critics' case on discretion, proportionality, and uneven application has evidence behind it too. ## The Decisionmaker Matters More Than the Number

The mandatory minimum is one of the few federal sentencing devices whose decisive choices are made outside the sentencing hearing. By the time a judge opened a presentence report, the prosecutor's charging decisions had already fixed the floor: the quantity alleged in the indictment, the 851 information filed or withheld, and the number of 924(c) counts charged. The three statutory exits rationed what the judge could do next. The substantial assistance motion belonged to the government, the safety valve belonged to the judge's finding, and the 2018 expansion widened only the valve's gate. The structure explains why the strongest criticism of the system was structural rather than sentimental: the discretion the statutes removed from the bench reappeared in charging and motion decisions that courts barely reviewed, exercised by prosecutors whose policies swung between constraint and command as successive attorneys general issued new memoranda.

That criticism never stood alone, and the record gives the defenders' case its due. Uniformity mattered to the Congress that wrote the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, and a statutory floor guarantees that like offenses produce like floors regardless of courtroom. Deterrence signaling drew on studies such as Kessler and Levitt's 1999 analysis of California sentence enhancements, while critics answered with the 1997 RAND finding that minimums were not cost effective against cocaine use. Cooperation leverage had the commission's own 2017 finding behind it, that drug minimums gave defendants a significant incentive to assist the government, with nearly half of fiscal year 2010 mandatory minimum offenders receiving relief through cooperation or the valve. The counter evidence appeared alongside it in the same reports: Black offenders received relief least often, in 34.9 percent of their cases in fiscal year 2010, and the 2017 report cautioned that neither exit fully reached relatively low level offenders.

The history supplies the final frame. The first generation of federal drug minimums, enacted in 1951 and stiffened in 1956, was tried, judged ineffective by a presidential commission, rejected by a Republican administration, and repealed by an overwhelming bipartisan vote in 1970. The second generation, enacted in 1986 on quantity thresholds chosen without a hearing record to explain them, produced the racial disparities that later Congresses spent decades partially undoing through the 2010 crack reform and the 2018 amendments. The through line is not the number of years in any statute but the identity of the decisionmaker, because a minimum was never only a floor. It was a transfer of authority, and the authority landed with the prosecutor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How is a mandatory minimum written into a statute?

Congress writes the minimum into the penalty subsection of the offense as a floor that the sentencing judge may not go below. The offense definition and the penalty live in different subsections: 21 U.S.C. 841(a) defines the trafficking offense, while 841(b) converts drug type and weight into punishment tiers expressed as a term "which may not be less than" a stated number of years. The structure is deliberately mechanical. Once the triggering facts are established, the floor applies to every defendant whose conduct fits the description, regardless of role, motive, or personal circumstances. The minimum is therefore not a guideline recommendation that a judge may set aside with reasons; it is part of the statute that creates the offense, and departing from it without a statutory exit would be an unlawful sentence. Other provisions follow the same pattern, with 18 U.S.C. 924(c) directing that its firearm term be imposed "in addition to" the punishment for the underlying offense and served consecutively.

### Q: What triggers a federal mandatory minimum sentence?

A federal mandatory minimum triggers when the elements the statute names are established in the case. For drug cases under 21 U.S.C. 841(b), the trigger is a drug type and a threshold quantity, such as one hundred grams of heroin or five hundred grams of powder cocaine. For firearms under 18 U.S.C. 924(c), the trigger is using, carrying, or possessing a firearm during and in relation to or in furtherance of a federal crime of violence or drug trafficking crime. For repeat offenders, the trigger is qualifying prior convictions noticed through the 21 U.S.C. 851 procedure. Under Apprendi v. New Jersey and Alleyne v. United States, the facts that raise the floor are elements of the offense and must be charged in the indictment and either admitted by the defendant or proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, with prior convictions as the narrow exception. In practice most minimums attach through guilty pleas, where the plea agreement stipulates the quantity and locks in the tier.

### Q: Can a judge go below a mandatory minimum?

A judge can go below a mandatory minimum only through one of the three statutory exits, and through no other route. The exits are the government's substantial assistance motion under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e), the safety valve under 18 U.S.C. 3553(f) for qualifying drug defendants, and the 2018 expansion of that valve. Ordinary sentencing discretion does not reach the floor: a judge may not depart below it because the defendant's role was minor, because the guideline range points lower, or because the judge considers the minimum unjust in the particular case. The Supreme Court's decision in Booker v. United States rendered the sentencing guidelines advisory but left statutory minimums untouched, and the courts of appeals held unanimously that Booker does not authorize a sentence below a number Congress wrote into a statute. Sympathy, proportionality concerns, and disagreement with the policy of the minimum are all legally inert at sentencing unless one of the three exits opens.

### Q: What is the safety valve for a mandatory minimum?

The safety valve, at 18 U.S.C. 3553(f), is a statutory exception that excuses a qualifying defendant from the mandatory minimum entirely, so that the judge sentences under the guidelines without regard to the floor. Congress created it in the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, eight years after the drug minimums were reinstated. To qualify, the defendant must persuade the judge by a preponderance of the evidence that five conditions are met: limited criminal history, no violence or firearms connected with the offense, no death or serious bodily injury resulting from it, no leadership role, and complete truthful disclosure of everything the defendant knows. No government motion is required, which distinguishes the valve from the substantial assistance exit. Its coverage is offense specific: it reaches only enumerated drug offenses, and a 924(c) firearm count is outside the valve, so a defendant who qualifies on the drug count still serves the consecutive firearm minimum.

### Q: Who decides whether a mandatory minimum applies, the judge or the prosecutor?

The prosecutor decides whether a mandatory minimum applies, through charging choices the judge cannot revisit. The quantity alleged in the indictment determines which drug tier the case falls into; the decision to file or withhold an 851 information determines whether prior conviction enhancements attach; and the number of 924(c) counts charged determines how many consecutive firearm terms apply. The judge's role begins after those decisions are made: the judge applies the floor that the charges support, determines whether the defendant has carried the burden for the safety valve, and decides how far below the minimum to go once the government files a substantial assistance motion. The one discretionary decision the judge owns outright is the valve finding. Every other gate, the charging tier, the prior conviction notice, and the motion to go below for cooperation, belongs to the prosecution, and those decisions are functionally unreviewable except on narrow constitutional grounds.

### Q: Did Congress ever repeal drug mandatory minimums?

Congress repealed nearly all federal drug mandatory minimums in 1970, a fact that the standard telling of sentencing history often omits. The Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, signed by President Richard Nixon on October 27 of that year, replaced the penalty architecture built by the Boggs Act of 1951 and the Narcotic Control Act of 1956. The House passed the bill 341 to 6 and the Senate 54 to 0. Congress reasoned that eliminating the floors would produce a more realistic, more flexible, and therefore more effective system of punishment and deterrence. The repeal followed the 1963 report of the President's Advisory Commission on Narcotic and Drug Abuse, which had found that the long mandated sentences showed little or no deterrent effect, and a bipartisan consensus that fixed floors were ineffective and inflexible. The repeal lasted sixteen years. In 1986 Congress reinstated quantity driven minimums in the Anti-Drug Abuse Act, building the modern regime on thresholds chosen without hearings on the quantities themselves.

### Q: What is an 851 enhancement in mandatory minimum cases?

An 851 enhancement is a prior conviction increase to a federal drug sentence under the notice procedure at 21 U.S.C. 851, titled Proceedings to Establish Prior Convictions. Before trial or plea, the United States attorney must file a written information listing each prior conviction the government intends to rely on; without that filing, no enhancement is lawful. Only the government may file, the court cannot impose the enhancement on its own motion, and the defendant can contest only the convictions listed, not the choice to list them. After conviction the court asks the defendant to affirm or deny each listed conviction, and denials are resolved at a hearing before the judge without a jury. Under the First Step Act of 2018, the predicates narrowed to serious drug felonies and serious violent felonies, and the ladder runs fifteen years for one qualifying prior and twenty five years for two or more, replacing the older structure that had included a mandatory life term.

### Q: Are mandatory minimums constitutional?

Mandatory minimums have been upheld against the main constitutional challenges. In Harmelin v. Michigan, decided in 1991, the Supreme Court sustained a mandatory sentence of life imprisonment without parole for possession of more than 650 grams of cocaine, with the controlling opinions holding that the Eighth Amendment's proportionality principle did not forbid the sentence. The Court has also sustained the procedural structure: under Almendarez-Torres v. United States, the fact of a prior conviction need not be charged in the indictment or proved to a jury, an exception that Apprendi v. New Jersey and Alleyne v. United States each expressly preserved while requiring jury findings for other minimum raising facts. Prosecutorial discretion over charging and motions has been treated as an exercise of executive authority that judges may not second guess, with relief available only on narrow constitutional grounds such as a showing that a decision rested on race or religion. The advisory guidelines created by Booker do not authorize sentences below statutory minimums.

### Q: How did Alleyne v. United States change who finds minimum raising facts?

Before Alleyne, decided in 2013, the rule from Harris v. United States, decided in 2002, treated facts that raised only the mandatory minimum as sentencing factors a judge could find by a preponderance of the evidence. Under Harris a judge could find that a defendant brandished a firearm during a drug trafficking crime and impose the seven year floor even though the jury had found only carrying, which carried a five year floor. Alleyne overruled that distinction, holding that any fact increasing a mandatory minimum is an element of the offense that must be charged in the indictment and proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, just as Apprendi v. New Jersey had required for facts increasing the statutory maximum. The reasoning drew on the historic link between the charged crime and its punishment: both kinds of facts alter the range of sentences to which a defendant is exposed, so both aggravate the punishment. After 2013 the threshold drug quantity, the brandishing and discharge tiers of 924(c), and the weapon type enhancements all had to go to the jury.

### Q: What is 924(c) stacking, and how did the First Step Act limit it?

Stacking was the practice of charging multiple 924(c) firearm counts in a single indictment and treating each count after the first as a second or subsequent conviction carrying the statute's enhanced term. In Deal v. United States, decided in 1993, the Supreme Court held that the phrase second or subsequent conviction attached when a jury returned sequential guilt findings on multiple counts within one indictment, so each finding after the first drew the longer minimum. After the 1998 amendment that term was twenty five years, and the counts ran consecutively, so a defendant with no prior record could receive decades of firearm time from one prosecution. The number of counts charged fixed the outcome entirely, and the charging decision belonged to the prosecutor. Section 403 of the First Step Act, enacted December 21, 2018, rewrote section 924(c)(1)(C) so that the twenty five year term applies only to a violation committed after a prior 924(c) conviction has become final, reserving the enhancement for true recidivists and ending single prosecution stacking.

### Q: What is a Melendez motion in mandatory minimum cases?

A Melendez motion is a government motion under 18 U.S.C. 3553(e) asking the court to impose a sentence below a statutory minimum based on the defendant's substantial assistance, named for Melendez v. United States, decided in 1996. The case resolved a confusion between two different motions. A guidelines motion under section 5K1.1 asks the court to depart below the guideline range, while a 3553(e) motion asks the court to depart below the statutory minimum, and the Supreme Court held that the guidelines motion alone cannot open the second door. In Melendez the government had filed a 5K1.1 motion describing the defendant's cooperation but had not requested a sentence below the ten year minimum, and the Court agreed with the district court that it lacked authority to go further. The holding makes the motion, not the fact of assistance, the gate. Even a defendant who plainly cooperated cannot receive a below minimum sentence unless the government files the 3553(e) motion, and the decision whether to file is committed to the prosecutor under Wade v. United States.

### Q: What are the five safety valve criteria?

The safety valve at 18 U.S.C. 3553(f) opens only when the defendant proves five conditions to the sentencing judge by a preponderance of the evidence. First, criminal history: after the 2018 expansion, the defendant must not have more than four criminal history points, must not have a prior three point offense, and must not have a prior two point violent offense, a three part test the Supreme Court construed conjunctively in Pulsifer v. United States. Second, no violence or firearms: the defendant must not have used violence or credible threats, possessed a firearm or other dangerous weapon, or induced another participant to do so, in connection with the offense. Third, no death or serious bodily injury resulting from the offense. Fourth, no leadership role: the defendant must not have been an organizer, leader, manager, or supervisor of others, and must not have engaged in a continuing criminal enterprise. Fifth, truthful disclosure: not later than sentencing, the defendant must have truthfully provided all information and evidence about the offense and related conduct, though having nothing useful to offer does not itself defeat the criterion.

### Q: How does Booker v. United States affect mandatory minimums?

Booker v. United States, decided in 2005, rendered the federal sentencing guidelines advisory rather than mandatory, but it changed nothing about statutory minimums. The Court's remedy addressed the guidelines system created by the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984, in which judicial factfinding under mandatory guidelines raised sentences; it did not address floors that Congress wrote directly into criminal statutes. The courts of appeals held uniformly after Booker that the decision does not authorize a sentence below a statutory minimum and does not alter the requirement of a government motion as a prerequisite for going below one. The Eighth Circuit so held in United States v. Mullins in 2005, and the other circuits followed. The guideline provisions implementing the exits, 5K1.1 for substantial assistance and 5C1.2 for the safety valve, continue to track their parent statutes rather than the other way around. An advisory guideline range cannot override a number Congress enacted, so Booker expanded judicial discretion only within the space above the floor.

### Q: What procedure does section 851 require for prior conviction enhancements?

Section 851 of title 21 requires the United States attorney to file a written information with the court before trial or before the entry of a guilty plea, listing each prior conviction the government intends to rely on to increase the sentence, and to serve a copy on the defendant or defense counsel. No enhancement is lawful without that filing. The statute phrases the command as a prohibition: no person convicted of a drug offense under the relevant part of title 21 may be sentenced to increased punishment by reason of prior convictions unless the filing and service occur first. Clerical mistakes may be corrected before sentence is pronounced, but new convictions cannot be added after the deadline. After conviction and before sentencing, the court must ask the defendant whether each listed conviction is affirmed or denied, with a warning that unraised challenges are waived. Denials trigger a hearing before the judge without a jury, where the government must prove the conviction beyond a reasonable doubt unless the defendant raises a constitutional attack, which the defendant must prove by a preponderance.

### Q: What is section 924(j) of the federal firearm statute?

Section 924(j) punishes a person who, in the course of a 924(c) firearm violation, causes the death of a person through the use of a firearm. If the killing is murder as defined in 18 U.S.C. 1111, the punishment is death or imprisonment for any term of years or for life; if it is manslaughter as defined in 18 U.S.C. 1112, the punishment follows that section, which sets a maximum of fifteen years for voluntary manslaughter and eight years for involuntary manslaughter. The provision is a related but separate offense from 924(c), and one structural difference sets it apart. In Lora v. United States, decided in 2023, the Supreme Court held unanimously that the consecutive sentence and no probation commands of section 924(c)(1)(D) govern only sentences imposed under 924(c) itself and do not control sentencing for a 924(j) conviction. A judge sentencing under 924(j) therefore retains the ordinary discretion to run the term concurrently with or consecutively to another sentence, a flexibility the firearm provision deliberately withholds.

### Q: What is the cooperation paradox in mandatory minimum sentencing?

The cooperation paradox describes the incentive pattern in which the substantial assistance exit rewards the defendants who need it least. Professor Stephen Schulhofer gave the phenomenon its name, and a Federal Judicial Center study by Barbara Vincent and Paul Hofer documented its operation. Because substantial assistance departures reward defendants who can provide substantial information useful to the government, higher ranking participants in a trafficking organization, who are more deeply embedded in the distribution network and know more about suppliers and customers, are better positioned to earn the exit than low level couriers and street dealers, who often know little worth trading. The most culpable offenders thus hold the best currency for buying their way below the minimum, while minor participants facing the same floor have nothing to sell. Congress designed the safety valve in 1994 as the answer to this paradox, creating a judge controlled exit for the low level, nonviolent offender with nothing to offer, and the 2018 expansion extended that logic to defendants with modest criminal records.

### Q: How did the Fair Sentencing Act change crack cocaine thresholds?

The Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 raised the crack cocaine quantity thresholds that trigger mandatory minimums, narrowing but not eliminating the disparity with powder cocaine. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 had set the five year minimum at five grams of crack against five hundred grams of powder, and the ten year minimum at fifty grams of crack against five kilograms of powder, a 100 to 1 ratio assembled without hearings on the quantities. The 2010 act raised the crack lines to twenty eight grams for the five year floor and two hundred eighty grams for the ten year floor while leaving the powder lines unchanged, producing an approximately 18 to 1 ratio. It also eliminated the five year mandatory minimum for simple possession of crack cocaine, which had been the only federal minimum for simple possession of any drug. The act passed the Senate by unanimous consent and the House by voice vote, and the Supreme Court held in Dorsey v. United States, decided in 2012, that it applied to defendants sentenced after enactment. Defendants sentenced before that date remained under the old thresholds until the First Step Act of 2018 made the change retroactive.

### Q: How does the statute measure drug weight, mixture or actual drug?

For most listed drugs the statute counts the weight of the entire mixture or substance containing a detectable amount of the drug, not the weight of the pure chemical. A diluted substance therefore produces a higher weight reading than the active ingredient alone, and the threshold tier follows the scale rather than the purity. Methamphetamine is the notable exception: the statute provides separate thresholds for actual methamphetamine, meaning the pure drug, and for a mixture containing methamphetamine, with the pure drug thresholds set ten times lower. Marijuana can be measured by weight or by plant count, with one thousand plants treated the same as one thousand kilograms. Conspiracy and attempt carry the same penalties as the completed offense under 21 U.S.C. 846, so the quantity attributable to the conspiracy determines the tier, and courts aggregate quantities within a single conspiracy while refusing to add quantities across separate substantive counts to reach a higher tier.

### Q: Must a 924(c) sentence run consecutively to the underlying sentence?

Section 924(c)(1)(D)(ii) provides that no term of imprisonment imposed under the subsection may run concurrently with any other term of imprisonment, including any term imposed for the predicate crime of violence or drug trafficking crime. Section 924(c)(1)(D)(i) separately bars the court from placing on probation any person convicted under the subsection. The command applies notwithstanding any other provision of law, which closes the usual avenues by which judges align sentences. The consecutive requirement is what makes the provision a true tack on rather than a sentencing factor folded into the predicate offense: the defendant serves the predicate sentence and then the firearm sentence, back to back, with no overlap. The sentencing guidelines reinforce the result, directing under section 2K2.4 that the guideline sentence for a 924(c) conviction is the statutory minimum itself, run consecutively. In Lora v. United States, decided in 2023, the Supreme Court confirmed that these commands govern only 924(c) sentences, not the related homicide provision at 924(j).

### Q: Did the Supreme Court uphold mandatory minimums against constitutional challenge?

The Supreme Court upheld mandatory minimums against the principal constitutional challenges. In Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957 (1991), the Court sustained a mandatory sentence of life imprisonment without parole for possession of more than 650 grams of cocaine, with the controlling opinions holding that the Eighth Amendment's proportionality principle did not forbid the sentence, and lower courts have followed that holding across decades of proportionality claims. On procedure, the Court required in Apprendi v. New Jersey and Alleyne v. United States that facts raising the statutory maximum or the mandatory minimum be charged and proved to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, while expressly preserving the Almendarez-Torres exception for the fact of a prior conviction. On separation of powers, the Court has treated prosecutorial control over charging and substantial assistance motions as an exercise of executive authority that judges may not second guess, limiting relief to narrow constitutional showings such as a decision resting on race or religion. No decision has struck down the minimums structure itself.
