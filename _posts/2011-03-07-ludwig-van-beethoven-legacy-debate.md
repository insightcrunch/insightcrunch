## What remains after the familiar portrait is removed

The Beethoven heroic narrative debate begins with an act of subtraction. Take away every anecdote that descends from the man who served as his secretary in his last years and then wrote his biography. Take away every inference that starts from the deafness and works backward into the scores. Take away every phrase that nineteenth century criticism coined and the twentieth century repeated until repetition felt like fact. Then ask what is left standing, and how much of the composer most listeners carry in their heads survives the removal. The answer is the subject of this article. A great deal survives, but it is a different shape from the familiar portrait, and the case for the music turns out to rest on grounds the portrait never mentioned.

<!--more-->

![Ludwig van Beethoven legacy debate, the heroic portrait tested against the documentary record - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-61.webp)

What survives the subtraction is not nothing. The scores survive, with their sketchbooks behind them, thousands of pages in his own hand showing themes drafted, rejected and rebuilt. His letters survive, more than fifteen hundred of them, covering money, health, publishers, pupils, family quarrels and the ordinary comedy of a working life. A private testament from 1802 survives, written at Heiligenstadt in his own words at the lowest point of his early despair. Financial records survive, contracts, receipts, the archives of the publishers who bought his work in three countries. The conversation books survive in part, the notebooks in which visitors wrote their side of the talk once speech had become useless to him, and they survive even after the entries forged by his own secretary are struck out of them. The life those papers describe is set out in full in [the account of Beethoven's life and career](/2011/01/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-life-and-career/), and this article takes that narration as read, because its concern is not the life but the argument that has been built on top of it.

The thesis of what follows can be stated in one sentence. The figure most people mean by Beethoven is a nineteenth century critical construction erected on a biographical record that its chief custodian deliberately contaminated, and the strongest case for the composer survives the removal of that construction only by changing what the case is about, from the suffering hero to the documented workshop. That is neither the worshipful version nor the debunking one, and the difference matters. A debunking would merely install a second portrait in place of the first, smaller where the first was grand, cynical where the first was reverent, and just as dependent on selection. The position argued here is that both portraits are constructions, that constructions are judged by the evidence underneath them, and that when the evidence is allowed to adjudicate, what emerges is not a diminished composer but a relocated claim.

One question opens in this section and will not be answered until the sources themselves have been audited. If the anecdotes are unreliable, what do we actually know about the man? The question is harder than it sounds, because the anecdotes are not decorative additions to the record. They are the load-bearing walls of the popular portrait: the defiance, the rages, the prophetic sayings, the deaf titan shaking his fist at fate. Remove them and the reader is entitled to wonder whether anything with a pulse remains, or only paper. Section eleven answers the question with an inventory of what the uncontaminated record contains, and the answer is more interesting than the legend, because the man in the documents was funnier, shrewder, pettier, more generous and more ordinary than the titan, and his ordinariness in the documents is precisely what makes the scores beside them extraordinary.

The plan of the argument is the plan of the removal. First the heroic narrative is stated at full strength, as its best advocates would state it, and then traced to the identifiable writers who built it and the purposes it served them. The deafness is examined next, not as a fact of the life, which is undisputed, but as an explanation of the music, which is a different and much weaker thing. Then the Schindler problem is faced directly: what he forged, what that forgery touches, and what a cleaned record looks like. The late works are argued both ways, the transcendent reading and the failed-experiment reading, before the greatest-composer claim is tested on criteria stated in advance, against Bach and against Mozart, and the charges of dominance and bombast are weighed. The article closes by saying what a fair assessment would require and what would have to be true for the grand claim to fail, because an argument that cannot state its own defeat conditions is not an argument at all.

## The heroic narrative stated properly

Before the narrative can be audited it has to be stated the way its strongest advocates state it, without caricature, because a construction this durable was not built out of nothing. In the heroic narrative, a provincial musician of Flemish descent arrives in Vienna as Haydn's pupil and Mozart's heir, and proceeds to break the classical settlement open from inside. Deafness descends on him in his late twenties, the cruellest affliction that can befall a composer, and instead of retreating he converts the affliction into fuel. The music becomes a record of struggle overcome: the Third Symphony tears up the dedication to a political hero and remakes the symphony as a drama of heroic action on a scale nobody had attempted; the Fifth moves from hammering darkness to blazing light across four movements, an itinerary of the soul that listeners have mapped onto their own lives ever since; the Ninth, written in near total deafness, crowns the journey by bringing the human voice into the symphony to sing of universal brotherhood. The artist of this story is not a servant of courts, as his predecessors had been, but a free and suffering sovereign, answerable to his genius alone, careless of money, contemptuous of princes, dictating works whole from some inner hearing that the loss of the outer ear only purified.

Stated that way, the narrative has genuine explanatory power, and it is dishonest to pretend otherwise. It noticed real things. The Eroica really is roughly twice the length of any symphony that preceded it, and its first movement really does stage conflict, crisis and hard-won resolution with a violence that was new. The Fifth really does end in a major key blaze reached from minor key turmoil, and the trajectory is a fact of the score, not of the commentary. The Ninth really was unprecedented, and a deaf man really did stand on the platform at its first performance in May 1824, beating time the audience could see did not match the performance, until a singer turned him round to face the applause he could not hear. That last scene, whatever its precise transmission, captures something the documents independently confirm: by 1824 the isolation was nearly complete, and the work written inside it was the most public utterance of his career. The narrative's error is not invention from whole cloth. It is the conversion of these facts into a psychology, a moral and a theory of how the music was made, none of which the facts by themselves contain.

The scholar who has anatomised this conversion most precisely is Scott Burnham, whose book Beethoven Hero, published in 1995, remains the standard account of how the heroic reading organised two centuries of listening. Burnham's point is often misreported as an attack on the music, and it is the opposite. He shows that the works of the middle period, with the Eroica as paradigm, became the measure of musical value itself, so that listeners learned to prize whatever resembled struggle and triumph, and to hear other kinds of excellence, Mozart's ease or the late works' strangeness, as lesser or as puzzles. On Burnham's analysis the narrative did not merely describe Beethoven. It set the terms on which all orchestral music after him would be judged, which is why dismantling it feels to many readers like an attack on value as such. It is not. It is a claim about where the terms came from.

There is also a moral architecture in the narrative that deserves to be named, because it explains the narrative's grip better than any musical detail. Suffering, in this story, is not random. It is the price and the proof of the achievement, and the achievement in turn redeems the suffering, so that the deafness becomes meaningful and the listener's own hardships are dignified by the pattern. That is a religious shape wearing secular dress, and the nineteenth century, which was losing the first and building the second, had urgent uses for it. To audit the narrative is therefore to audit a piece of consolation as well as a piece of criticism, and the audit has to be careful, because consolation is not refuted by being identified. What can be shown is narrower and stronger: that specific men wrote this story, at specific dates, for specific reasons of their own, and that the story's fit with the documents is partial in ways that can be enumerated. The narrative was made. What was made can be examined.

## Who built the heroic narrative and when

The first rung of the argument is a matter of dates, and dates are unfriendly to legends. Ludwig van Beethoven died in March 1827. The heroic narrative, as a complete portrait rather than a tendency, was assembled after that date by writers who can be named, in publications that can be dated, for purposes that can be reconstructed from their own situations. This does not make the portrait false. It makes it authored, and an authored portrait can be checked against its subject in a way that a timeless truth cannot. Scott Burnham's work on the heroic style is valuable precisely here, because he treats the narrative as a historical artefact with a career of its own, rising with the nineteenth century concert world and hardening into common sense as that world built its institutions.

### Who created the image of Beethoven as a hero?

No single writer created it. E. T. A. Hoffmann's 1810 review of the Fifth Symphony supplied the metaphysical frame, the early biographers supplied anecdotes, A. B. Marx supplied the programme readings, and Richard Wagner's centenary essay of 1870 completed the portrait of the deaf prophet.

The frame came first, and it came during the composer's lifetime. When E. T. A. Hoffmann reviewed the Fifth Symphony in the Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung in 1810, he argued that instrumental music was the most romantic of the arts precisely because it had no subject but the infinite, and he placed Beethoven at the summit of that art as the composer whose music awakened longing, terror and awe beyond the reach of words. Hoffmann was writing about the music, not the man, and his review is a legitimate critical achievement. But it supplied the vocabulary, the priestly tone, the assumption that this music traffics in metaphysical ultimates, into which the later biography would pour its anecdotes. The hero was sketched before the life was written.

The life followed quickly. Johann Aloys Schlosser produced the first book-length biography in Prague in 1828, a year after the funeral. In 1838 Franz Wegeler, a Bonn friend of the composer's youth, and Ferdinand Ries, his former pupil, published their biographical notices in Koblenz, a volume of recollections whose independence matters greatly to this article and will matter again in section eleven. Anton Schindler's biography appeared in 1840, and although it was the third account in print, it became the most influential by an enormous margin, because Schindler had been there, or said he had, and because his anecdotes were the most quotable. Adolf Bernhard Marx, the Berlin critic and theorist, spent the same decades teaching readers to hear the symphonies as dramatic programmes, attaching narrative itineraries to movements that their composer had left untitled, and his biography of 1859 codified the approach. Wilhelm von Lenz gave the construction its architecture in 1852 by dividing the output into three styles, a scheme that maps with suspicious neatness onto a life story: the young apprentice, the heroic master, the transcendent elder. A classification that fits the myth that well should be handled as part of the myth's machinery, not as a neutral finding.

Richard Wagner completed the portrait in 1870, the centenary of Beethoven's birth, in an essay simply titled Beethoven, written while Europe was at war and German cultural confidence was at flood tide. Wagner's deaf composer is a seer, a man cut off from the world of appearances and therefore admitted to the world of essences, whose Ninth Symphony proves by its recourse to the human voice that pure music had reached its limit and cried out for the word. It is a brilliant essay and a transparent act of annexation, since the limit it discovers in Beethoven is precisely the one Wagner's own music dramas claimed to surpass. The heroic narrative now had everything it needed: a metaphysical frame from Hoffmann, a body of anecdotes from the biographers, a listening method from Marx, a life-shaped architecture from Lenz, and a prophet's halo from the most commanding musical intelligence of the age. Every element postdates the man, and most postdate him by a generation. The composers and critics who built the image were not lying about their own experience of the music. They were building the ancestor their century required, and they built him out of Beethoven because Beethoven's music, and his conveniently dramatic affliction, could bear the weight.

## Wagner, Schumann and the critical construction

Two names dominate the construction's middle decades, and they used the same hero for opposite purposes, which is itself evidence that the hero was a construction, since a documented life rarely serves contradictory programmes with equal ease. Robert Schumann, editing the Neue Zeitschrift für Musik in the 1830s and 1840s, made Beethoven the fixed standard of his criticism. The symphonies after Beethoven, in Schumann's journal and in his reviews, were not simply pieces of music; they were attempts at a summit already occupied, and the young composers Schumann championed were measured, encouraged and found wanting against the dead man's achievement. Schumann's Beethoven licensed a conservative conclusion, that instrumental music had reached its classical perfection and that the task of the living was reverent continuation. His famous critical generosity was real, and so was the trap inside it, because a generation of symphonists grew up hearing a giant's footsteps behind them, as Johannes Brahms is reported to have put it when explaining why his own First Symphony took so long to finish.

Wagner's Beethoven licensed revolution instead. In his writings of 1849 and after, and supremely in the centenary essay, the Ninth Symphony becomes the last symphony, the point at which music confesses its need of poetry and drama, and Beethoven becomes the forerunner who points beyond himself to the artwork of the future, Wagner's own. The deafness, in Wagner's handling, is not a misfortune but a consecration: sealed away from the trivial sounding world, the composer heard the true one. Nothing in the documents supports that metaphysic, and Wagner did not pretend to be working from documents. He was working from need. His enterprise required a legitimating ancestor of unimpeachable greatness who could be shown to have authorised the succession, and Beethoven, dead for two decades when the campaign began and unable to object, was cast in the role. That the two great critical intelligences of the German nineteenth century could extract opposite morals from the same figure tells us the figure had become a resource, a quarry of authority, rather than a person being described.

Between them, Schumann's journal and Wagner's essays did something else that is easy to miss. They professionalised the narrative. Criticism was becoming an institution in these decades, with journals, professorships and a reading public for musical argument, and the heroic Beethoven was the institution's founding exhibit, the case on which critics demonstrated that music could sustain the kind of serious commentary previously reserved for literature and philosophy. Tia DeNora, the sociologist whose Beethoven and the Construction of Genius appeared in 1995, approaches the same construction from the other end of the life, arguing that the category of the musical genius itself was being built in the Vienna of the 1790s, and that Beethoven's early reputation was the product of an identifiable social machinery: aristocratic patrons competing for association with serious talent, a new ideology of autonomous art, and the composer's own refusal of the servant's place his predecessors had occupied. On DeNora's account, the construction did not begin at the funeral. It began in the drawing rooms, and the posthumous narrative inherited a figure already half built by the world that had paid for him. Her argument does not require the music to be anything less than it is. It requires only that genius be treated as something a society assembles around a talent, rather than a substance the talent secretes, and she documents the assembling in unusual detail.

The critical construction also hardened through a quieter channel, the programme note and the concert guide, where Marx's narrative itineraries for the symphonies were simplified for ticket buyers and repeated until they acquired the status of the composer's intentions. A listener told before the Eroica that its first movement enacts a hero's struggle will hear struggle, and will report the hearing as perception rather than instruction. Burnham's analysis is at its sharpest on this circularity: the heroic style became a hearing habit, the habit confirmed the narrative, and the narrative supplied the habit to the next generation. Breaking the circle does not require denying that the Eroica can be heard as struggle. It requires only remembering that the hearing was taught, by named teachers, at a datable period, and that other hearings were once available and were crowded out rather than refuted.

## What the narrative was useful for

A construction that survives for two centuries is not merely believed; it is employed. The heroic Beethoven earned his keep in at least four economies, and listing them explains why correction has always been an uphill trade. The first economy was national. The Bonn monument of 1845, raised by subscription and unveiled before assembled royalty with Franz Liszt as its chief fundraiser and benefactor, installed Beethoven as a German cultural monument in stone eighteen years after his death. The centenary of 1870 fell during the Franco-Prussian War, and Wagner's essay was not the only voice that year to present the deaf composer of Bonn as proof of the depth and seriousness of German art. A composer who had quarrelled with his Austrian patrons, despised the Viennese public in his letters and written much of his best music for a cosmopolitan market became, posthumously, a national asset, and the national use of him preferred the titan to the working musician, because nations do not put contractors on plinths.

The second economy was institutional. The nineteenth century built the modern concert world, subscription orchestras, conservatoires, collected editions, and all of them needed a fixed centre, a repertory sun around which programming, teaching and publishing could orbit. Beethoven served perfectly. His output was large enough to fill seasons and small enough to be mastered, difficult enough to justify professional institutions and popular enough to fill their halls. The collected edition of his works issued by Breitkopf and Härtel in the 1860s, among the first undertakings of its kind for any composer, was both scholarship and canonisation, an argument in print that this output was a national literature requiring preservation entire. Institutions do not maintain centres they believe to be fraudulent, and by the time the source criticism of the twentieth century arrived, the centre had a century of salaries, curricula and endowments resting on it.

The third economy was professional, and it belonged to composers. To succeed Beethoven was the century's definition of ambition, and the narrative told the ambitious exactly what succession required: not polish, not charm, not success in the theatre, but struggle, scale and the appearance of wrestling with fate in public. Composers as different as Berlioz, Liszt, Brahms and Mahler organised their symphonic careers in the space the narrative marked out, and some of them, Berlioz above all, said so in their memoirs with complete frankness. The fourth economy was the listener's own. The narrative gave difficult music a door. A first-time listener who cannot yet follow a development section can follow a story of suffering overcome, and millions entered the Eroica and the Ninth through that door and stayed to learn the music itself. Any honest audit has to record this benefit on the narrative's side of the ledger. The construction was not a conspiracy against the truth. It was a tool that nations, institutions, composers and audiences each found indispensable, and tools of that kind are not discarded because a scholar demonstrates that they were manufactured. They are discarded, if at all, when a better tool arrives, which is why this article's business is not demolition but replacement: a case for the music that does not need the mythology, assembled in the sections that follow.

## The deafness as explanation

Every part of the heroic narrative is load-bearing, but the deafness carries the most weight, because it converts a biography into an argument. The claim, in its familiar form, runs as follows. A composer begins to lose his hearing in his late twenties. The affliction deepens across thirty years until the isolation is total. Cut off from the sounding world, from performance, from conversation, from the salon culture in which his art had been social, he is driven inward, and the inwardness becomes the music: the increasing strangeness of the late works, their disregard for what fingers and voices can comfortably do, their long spans of motionlessness and violence, are the sound of a mind conversing with itself because it can converse with nothing else. The deafness explains the style. That is the claim, and it must be given its full force before it is tested, because it is not a foolish claim. It rests on a real catastrophe, documented in the man's own hand.

### Did deafness change Beethoven's music?

His style changed twice, and only the second change coincides with profound deafness. The heroic manner of 1803 arrived while he could still perform in public, and the late manner's techniques are documented in his sketches as deliberate construction, so historians treat deafness as the setting of the late works rather than their demonstrated cause.

The documentary foundation is the letter he wrote to Franz Wegeler in June 1801, in which he confided that his hearing had been failing for about three years, that the high tones of instruments and voices had gone first, and that he had withdrawn from society because he could not bring himself to tell people that he, of all men, was growing deaf. A year later, in October 1802, at Heiligenstadt outside Vienna, he wrote the document known as the Heiligenstadt Testament, a letter to his brothers that was never sent and was found among his papers after his death. It records despair at the affliction, an admission that he had come close to ending his life, and a resolution to live for his art instead. The Testament is genuine beyond dispute, in his hand, dated, and consistent with the correspondence. It is the single most powerful document in the whole Beethoven record, and the heroic narrative is built on it more than on any anecdote, because it appears to give the story an authentic voice: the sufferer himself, testifying that suffering was the furnace of the work.

The explanation also draws strength from the visible surface of the late career. From 1818 he conducted his side of conversations in writing, in the conversation books that visitors filled with their questions and remarks while he answered aloud. He conducted rehearsals he could not hear, with results that musicians of the Ninth's first performance described with a mixture of loyalty and dismay. He wrote a fugue for string quartet so resistant to performance that his publisher persuaded him to withdraw it from its quartet and publish it separately. He set metronome marks that pianists have argued about ever since. A critic assembling the deafness explanation does not lack exhibits. The inwardness thesis organises them elegantly, it honours the man's suffering by making it productive, and it flatters the listener, who becomes a witness to transfiguration rather than a consumer of entertainment. Its elegance is the reason it must be examined rather than merely admired, because an explanation that fits this beautifully is usually one the evidence was selected to fit.

## Why the deafness explains less than it appears to

Begin with what the deafness genuinely explains, because the counter-case fails if it overreaches. It explains the circumstances of the life: the withdrawal from the virtuoso career that had made his name in the 1790s, the end of his public performing in 1814, the humiliating comedy and worse of his attempts to conduct in the 1820s, the conversation books, the suspicion and irritability that isolation fed, the ear trumpets designed for him by Johann Nepomuk Mälzel that sit in museums as relics of a working problem. It explains why the most sociable of art forms was practised by a man increasingly sealed off from society. It may well explain certain practical choices: a composer who cannot test a passage at the piano in company, or hear an orchestra's balance from the hall, is deprived of feedback loops that other composers used without thinking. Nobody serious denies any of this, and the trap in the brief for this article is explicit: the deafness must not be minimised as a fact of the life. The question is only whether it explains the music, and explanation is a demanding word. An explanation of a style must identify the mechanism by which the cause produced these notes rather than others, and it must survive comparison.

Comparison is where the explanation weakens. Deafness in other composers did not produce the Beethoven late style. Bedřich Smetana lost his hearing entirely in 1874 and went on composing for a decade; his late works are masterly and sound nothing like an inward metaphysical withdrawal, because Smetana's musical personality was formed by other materials and other ambitions. Gabriel Fauré's hearing failed in his last years and his music grew sparer and more austere, a trajectory some listeners do compare with Beethoven's, but the austerity of Fauré is a distillation of a lifelong harmonic language, not a new world built in silence. Conversely, the qualities the deafness is summoned to explain, formal daring, motivic concentration, the treatment of silence and discontinuity, appear in the work of composers whose hearing was intact: the late quartets of Haydn, written in his sixties with his ears in working order, reinvent the form from inside, and Schubert's final works, composed at speed by a man of thirty-one with no affliction of the ear, contain spans of desolation and formal experiment that no deafness was available to explain. A cause that is present when the effect is absent, and absent when the effect is present, is not doing explanatory work. It is doing narrative work.

The explanation also fails a simpler test, the test of falsifiability. In the literature of the heroic narrative, the deafness is made to account for opposite features with equal facility. Passages of notorious awkwardness are attributed to the deafness: he could not hear how they sounded. Passages of visionary strangeness are attributed to the deafness: liberated from how things sounded, he wrote what he conceived. A hypothesis under which clumsiness and transcendence both count as confirmations is not a hypothesis. It is an incantation, and it protects itself from the only evidence that could test it, the evidence of the workshop, which shows the awkward passages worried over, revised and retained, and the visionary ones assembled by trial and rejection like anybody else's. Maynard Solomon, whose 1977 biography rebuilt the life from the documents and whose later essays on the late music remain central to this debate, consistently resists the short circuit between affliction and style, treating the late works as the products of a mind engaging musical problems it had been approaching for decades. The resistance is worth following. The deafness was the condition under which the late music was written. Between a condition and a cause there is a distance that the heroic narrative exists to conceal, and chronology, the subject of the next section, measures that distance exactly.

## What the chronology actually shows

The chronology is the cleanest evidence in this article, and it is worth setting out slowly, because the familiar explanation depends on the sequence being blurry. It is usually told as a single descent: the hearing fails, the style deepens, the two curves falling and rising together across thirty years. The documents give a different picture, in which the decisive stylistic events happen early, while the man could still function in the sounding world, and the profound deafness belongs to the last decade, long after the revolution it is supposed to have caused was complete. Beethoven's stylistic change begins before his hearing loss became severe, which means the familiar explanation reverses the chronology. That sentence is the shareable fact at the centre of this article, and everything in the present section is its documentation.

His own testimony dates the onset. In the letters of 1801, to Wegeler in June and to Karl Amenda in July, he describes a decline already three years old, which places the first symptoms around 1798, in his twenty-eighth year. The symptoms were real and worsening, and he concealed them professionally for years, but concealment was possible because function remained: through the first decade of the century he was the most celebrated pianist in Vienna, improvising in public, performing his own concertos, conducting rehearsals, negotiating with publishers across a table, teaching aristocratic pupils who did not report a deaf master. The crisis of the Heiligenstadt Testament in 1802 was a crisis of prognosis and shame, not of present incapacity. What he feared, and what the Testament records, was the trajectory, and it is the trajectory that the heroic narrative silently converts into an accomplished fact fifteen years early.

Now set the music beside the dates. The break that defines the heroic Beethoven, the enlargement of scale and the new rhetoric of struggle, arrives in the works of 1802 to 1804: the Third Symphony, composed in 1803, the great piano sonatas of those years, the triple concerto, the first version of his opera. His pupil Carl Czerny reported that around 1802 Beethoven told his friend Wenzel Krumpholz that he was dissatisfied with his work to date and intended to follow a new path. Whatever allowance is made for a pupil's later recollection, the works themselves date the change, and they date it to a period when their composer still stood at the summit of his profession's public life, a decade and more before the conversation books begin in 1818 and the isolation becomes total. The new path was chosen by a man who could still hear an orchestra, however imperfectly, and who was competing, with full professional equipment, against the memory of Mozart and the living presence of Haydn. Ambition explains the Eroica's scale in 1803. Deafness, at the severity the narrative requires, was not yet available to explain anything.

The late manner belongs to a different alignment. From about 1816 the output slows, changes character, and by the Hammerklavier Sonata of 1818 the late style is under way, in the same years that the conversation books begin and the ear trumpets fail. Here the correlation the narrative wants genuinely exists, and honesty requires granting it: the late works were written by a man profoundly deaf. But correlation in the last decade cannot explain a revolution accomplished in the first, and even within the last decade the chronology cuts against the simple story, because the late style's characteristic devices, fugue as a structural engine, variation as a philosophy, the compression of whole arguments into single movements, have their roots in studies and experiments that reach back into the years of working hearing. The deafness accompanied the late style, housed it, and darkened the life around it. The claim that it caused the style requires the Eroica to have been written by a man who could not hear, and he could, which is why this article treats the deafness explanation as the narrative's most successful sleight: it takes a true affliction, a true late correlation, and a true document of despair, and assembles from them a causal story that the dates will not carry.

## The Schindler problem

Anton Schindler was a violinist and conductor from Moravia who attached himself to Beethoven in the early 1820s and served, with one long estrangement, as his unpaid secretary, copyist, negotiator and factotum through the final years. He was present in the household during the period of the Ninth Symphony and the last quartets. He was also, after the composer's death, the man who took possession of the bulk of the conversation books and a mass of papers, who published in 1840 the biography that fixed the public image for the rest of the century, and who inserted into the conversation books, in his own hand imitating the hands of others, roughly 150 entries that Beethoven's visitors never wrote. That last fact is not an allegation. It is the established finding of the scholars who prepared the critical edition of the conversation books in Berlin in the 1970s, working from handwriting, ink and paper, and it has not been seriously contested since. Schindler is the central case in this series of a custodian who did not merely transmit the record of a musician's life but manufactured parts of it, and he must be treated throughout what follows as a subject to be examined, never as a witness to be believed.

His motives are not mysterious, and reconstructing them is not an act of malice. He had given years of unpaid service to a difficult man who had quarrelled with him, dismissed him and taken him back, and who died without rewarding him. His standing in Viennese musical life after 1827 rested almost entirely on his proximity to the dead giant. The forged entries systematically improve that proximity: they show the composer confiding in Schindler, praising his judgement, relying on his mediation, in conversations that never took place. Other entries settle scores with contemporaries Schindler disliked. The biography performs the same work at larger scale, presenting its author as the intimate who understood the master best, and salting the text with anecdotes of a kind no other witness supplies: sayings, confrontations and prophetic remarks whose only source is the book that profits from them. The most famous sentence ever attributed to Beethoven about his own music, the claim that the four opening notes of the Fifth Symphony represent fate knocking at the door, appears in Schindler's biography and nowhere else. It has been printed in a million programme notes. It should be treated as Schindler's composition until evidence independent of Schindler appears, and none has.

The damage is compounded by a loss that cannot be assigned with the same confidence. Of some four hundred conversation books kept across the last decade of the composer's life, about 139 survive. The books were in Schindler's custody for the years in which most of them disappeared, before he sold the remnant to the Royal Library in Berlin in 1846. Whether he destroyed the missing books, culled them for what they contained about himself, or simply lost them through the carelessness of a man moving house and changing countries, the record does not establish, and this article does not pretend otherwise. What is established is narrower and sufficient: while the books were his, entries were added to them by his hand, and the majority of them vanished. A custodian proved to have forged entries in a document is a custodian whose unsupported testimony about anything the document touched must be quarantined, and since Schindler's biography touches nearly every famous episode of the last fifteen years, the quarantine is extensive. Theodore Albrecht, the editor of the correspondence addressed to Beethoven, has spent a career in the archives on both sides of this problem, and the picture that emerges from the genuine incoming letters, written by publishers, patrons, family members and friends with no reason to perform for posterity, is the necessary control against which Schindler's performances can be measured.

None of this makes Schindler a villain of melodrama, and the stance of this article is that he is more useful understood than condemned. He was an early example of a recognisable modern figure, the intimate associate whose memoir becomes an industry, and the Beethoven literature of the nineteenth century, built by men who had never met the composer and needed the intimate's testimony, laundered his inventions into facts by the simple process of citing each other. Marx cites Schindler, Lenz cites Schindler, the programme notes cite them all, and by 1900 the forgeries had acquired the one property forgeries cannot survive without, respectability. The question the next section takes up is what remains of the famous personality when everything that rests on this single compromised foundation is set aside, and the answer is the third rung of this article's ladder: less, and different, and better documented than the legend allows.

## How much of the character portrait is compromised

Once Schindler's material is removed, the documented personality is thinner and less dramatic than the familiar one. That is the finding, and it should be stated without flinching, because a great deal of what the world calls Beethoven's character is Schindler's characterisation, repeated until it feels like memory. The test can be applied episode by episode, and the episodes sort themselves into three classes: those that collapse entirely, those that survive in reduced form, and those that stand on evidence Schindler never touched. The sorting is the substance of this section, and it doubles as a demonstration of the method any reader can apply to the next Beethoven anecdote they meet, in a programme note or a film.

The collapses are the quotable Beethoven. Fate knocking at the door goes, being Schindler's unaided invention. The prophetic utterances go, the table talk in which the composer conveniently pronounces the interpretation posterity was to adopt, because the conversation books that record them record them in the forger's imitated hands. The deathbed drama shrinks hardest of all: the famous raised fist against the storm, the defiance at the point of death that the nineteenth century engraved and reproduced, descends from reports by visitors with legends to build, and the documents of the final illness, the doctors' consultations and the correspondence of those weeks, describe a quieter and more squalid ending, a sickroom routine of treatment and decline, with no quotable gesture in it. This article offers no detail beyond that outline, because the manner of a death is not evidence about a life's work, and the legend's hunger for a theatrical exit is itself one of the things under audit. What can be said is that the exit the documents describe is a sickroom, not a stage.

The reductions are instructive in a different way. Beethoven's defiance of rank survives, because Ferdinand Ries, who had been his pupil and had no biography to sell in 1840, describes the fury over the Eroica's dedication with particulars a forger would not have invented, and the copyist's copy of the symphony itself, with the name Bonaparte scratched through the title page so violently that the paper tore, is a physical document anyone may inspect. But the episode in the documents is narrower than the legend: a working composer enraged that his intended dedicatee had crowned himself, in a Europe where dedications were currency, not a republican titan lecturing the age. His temper survives, in letters and in the testimony of the household, but it arrives accompanied by its context, the pain, the deafness, the lawsuits over his nephew, and it sits beside a wit the legend mostly omits, the puns and nicknames and household comedies that fill his letters to Nanette Streicher when she was managing his domestic chaos in 1817 and 1818. The misanthrope of the portrait is a selection. The correspondence shows a man who was exhausting, generous, funny, hypochondriac, litigious and kind by turns, which is to say a man, and a less operatic one than the titan who sells the concerts.

What stands untouched is considerable, and section eleven inventories it. For the character question the essential point is the direction of the correction. The Schindler portrait is not false in every particular; it is interested, the work of a man improving his own position in history, and its distortions all lean the same way, toward intimacy, drama and prophecy. Remove them and the personality does not vanish. It loses its press office. The composer who emerges from the uncontaminated papers planned his career with a strategist's care, drove hard bargains with three publishers at once, managed his image in his lifetime with a skill his secretary merely continued after his death, and wrote begging, joking, furious and tender letters in a hand that needs no intermediary. Whether that man is less lovable than the titan is a question of taste. That he is better evidence is not a question at all.

## What independent sources survive

The first loop of this article can now close. If the anecdotes are unreliable, what do we actually know about the man? We know what the surviving paper knows, and the paper, once its custodians are graded, is rich. Begin with his own outgoing correspondence, more than fifteen hundred letters preserved in publishers' archives, family collections and libraries, written across thirty years for practical purposes and never meant as literature, which is exactly why they can be trusted about the things they touch: the prices he demanded and accepted, the works in progress and their order, his illnesses and their management, his enthusiasms, Handel above all among the dead, his contempt for shoddy performance, his delight in a good joke at a publisher's expense. The letters give a mind at work in its own words, unbeautiful, repetitive, obsessed with money and bowels and deadlines, and entirely unlike the oracular voice Schindler ventriloquised for him.

Alongside the letters out stand the letters in, and their modern editor, Theodore Albrecht, performed a service whose value grows as the Schindler quarantine widens, because the incoming correspondence is a record Beethoven did not write and his secretary could not retrospectively compose. Publishers write about contracts and piracy. Patrons write about commissions and quartets for named occasions. His nephew's guardianship opponents write with legal menace. Friends write about everything else. A personality can be triangulated from the way the world addressed him, and the world addressed a formidable professional, sometimes a frightening one, never a helpless visionary. Add the diaries, his private journal of the years 1812 to 1818, with its resolutions, reading notes and copied sentences of moral self-instruction, and the Heiligenstadt Testament of 1802, and the inner record, though partial, is authentic where it exists.

The witnesses can then be graded, and grading witnesses is the whole method. Wegeler and Ries published in 1838, before Schindler, and Ries had been the man's pupil in Vienna in the first years of the century, close enough to describe the improvisation sessions and the dedication crisis, distant enough by 1838 to have no intimacy left to inflate. Gerhard von Breuning, who as a boy had been in and out of the Schwarzspanierhaus household in the final years and whose father had been Beethoven's Bonn friend, published his recollections in 1874, a memoir whose domestic detail has repeatedly checked against the documents. Fanny Giannatasio del Rio kept a diary in 1816 that records a visit and a hopeless attachment with a diarist's unguarded contemporaneity. The genuine entries of the conversation books, in the hands of the visitors who wrote them, show the traffic of the last decade, callers, musicians, publishers, lawyers, with a banality no forger would have bothered to invent, which is precisely their credential. None of these sources is innocent, memoirs never are, but their interests are known, their dates are fixed, and they corroborate and correct one another in ways Schindler cannot survive.

Finally there is the evidence that never passes through a witness at all: the sketchbooks, the autographs with their corrections, the contracts, the subscription lists, the publishers' ledgers, the copy of the Eroica with its ruined title page. Paper of this kind cannot tell us what he felt, and the honest conclusion of the inventory is that his feelings are the least recoverable part of him, the part the anecdotes pretended to supply. What the paper does supply is the work, the career, the money, the methods and the milieu, in greater density than survives for almost any earlier composer. A researcher assembling such an audit keeps the provenance of every claim attached to the claim itself, and the discipline is easier to maintain in a tool built for it, the offline encrypted notes app obtained from [the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/), where a source register can live beside the drafts it disciplines. We know the man's operations better than those of any titan has a right to expect. It is the operations, in the end, that the music came from, and the argument of the remaining sections is that the operations make the stronger case.

## The late-style mystique

A second question opens here, and it will take four sections to close. Do the late works succeed, or are some of them failed experiments? The question is harder to ask cleanly than the Schindler question, because there is no forgery to quarantine, only a halo to see through, and the halo has a name in the literature: the late-style mystique. The works in question were composed between about 1816 and his death in 1827: the Hammerklavier Sonata, the last piano sonatas, the Diabelli Variations, the Missa Solemnis, the Ninth Symphony, and the five final string quartets with the Grosse Fuge. Around this music there gathered, during the nineteenth century and with increasing intensity in the twentieth, an atmosphere of reverence distinct from ordinary admiration. The late works were not merely great. They were testamentary, the utterances of a deaf seer writing for a posterity that would one day be worthy of them, and their difficulty was read as the signature of that election: what contemporaries could not follow, the future would vindicate.

The mystique has identifiable components, and they should be separated before the two cases are argued. The first is the category itself. The three-period scheme that Lenz formalised in 1852 does not merely divide the output; it promises an ascent, apprentice, hero, sage, so that the final period arrives pre-interpreted as the summit before a note is examined. The second component is the anecdote of incomprehension, in which the first audiences' bewilderment is retold as proof of altitude: the remark that later tradition puts in the composer's mouth about the Razumovsky Quartets, that they were not for the present but for a later age, may or may not record anything he said, and its survival owes less to its documentation than to its convenience, since it converts every subsequent failure of communication into a prophecy fulfilled. The third component is the deafness, already audited, returning in a new costume: the late works' strangeness is certified in advance as the sound of a mind beyond hearing, so that criticism of any passage becomes a confession of the critic's earthbound ears. A mystique, in short, is an insurance policy taken out on a reputation, under which no claim can ever be presented. The next two sections present the claims anyway, at full strength, on both sides.

It matters to be fair about why the insurance was bought. The late works are, on any showing, extraordinary objects, and the first generations to perform them were working without a tradition, without models, often without adequate technique for what the scores demand. Reverence was a way of keeping them in circulation while the culture caught up, and it worked: the quartets were played, published, argued over, and eventually mastered, in a way that works merely respected are not. The mystique was a conservation strategy before it was an evasion. But a conservation strategy can outlive its usefulness and become a means of not listening, and the question the ladder of this article is climbing toward is whether the late works need their insurance any longer, or whether they are strong enough to be judged, with the possibility of failure left genuinely open, like any other music.

## The case that the late works are transcendent

State the affirmative case as its best advocates make it, because it is a strong case and this article has no interest in winning against a weakened opponent. The advocate begins with J. W. N. Sullivan, whose Beethoven: His Spiritual Development, published in 1927, gave the transcendent reading its classic form: the late works as the record of a consciousness that had passed through suffering into a condition beyond it, music whose serenity and strangeness alike testify to a vantage no other composer reached. Sullivan's vocabulary is dated and his psychology is speculative, but the reading endures because it is fastened to features of the scores that any analyst can verify without sharing the metaphysics. The Diabelli Variations take a waltz its composer privately dismissed and build from it thirty-three variations that survey the entire history of the variation principle, parody, fugue, lament and dance, an encyclopaedia constructed from a trifle. The String Quartet in C sharp minor, Op. 131, runs seven movements without a break through an arch of keys and characters that no earlier quartet attempts. The slow movement of Op. 132 is headed by the composer himself as a convalescent's holy song of thanksgiving, written after the illness of 1825 that he had reason to think final, and its alternation of archaic hymn and returning strength is as close as music comes to a documented spiritual autobiography with the documentation in the score rather than in an anecdote.

The advocate continues with the architecture. The late works do not merely decorate inherited forms; they interrogate them. The Hammerklavier fugue treats the fugue not as an academic exercise but as a terrain to be conquered at the edge of playability. The Missa Solemnis, five years in the making, was inscribed by its composer with the wish that it should go from the heart to the heart, and its setting treats the mass text with a symphonist's disregard for liturgical convenience, which is either presumption or a claim that the concert hall had become a place where such things could be meant. The Ninth's finale dissolves the boundary between symphony and cantata that the whole tradition had maintained. On the affirmative reading, these are not experiments, a word that implies the possibility of a null result. They are conquests, and the proof offered is their behaviour under two centuries of professional scrutiny: the quartets stand at the centre of every serious quartet's repertoire, the variations at the centre of the piano's, and each generation of performers reports finding more in them, not less. A failure does not behave that way. A failure is found out.

The advocate's closing move is the one the mystique usually makes on his behalf, and he should be allowed to make it cleanly rather than by halo. It is that the criteria by which these works were first judged failures, immediate intelligibility, ease of execution, the pleasure of an audience hearing them once, are criteria for a different kind of music, and that judging Op. 131 by them is a category error comparable to judging a proof by its entertainment value. The works propose their own criteria: reach, integration, the capacity to sustain an hour of attention at the highest level of demand. By those criteria, the advocate concludes, they are not experiments that happened to succeed. They are the standard by which success in their genres is defined, and the burden of proof belongs to whoever would call the Grosse Fuge a failure to explain its subsequent century.

## The case that the late works are experiments that sometimes fail

The other side deserves the same courtesy, and it begins not with malice but with a premiere. The Grosse Fuge, composed as the finale of the String Quartet in B flat, Op. 130, was first performed in Vienna in March 1826 by the Schuppanzigh Quartet, the finest players available to him. The audience received several movements warmly and encored two of them. The fugue they did not follow, and the bafflement was not philistine: these were the listeners and players who had carried his music for decades. His publisher then did an extraordinary thing, persuading the most unpersuadable composer alive to withdraw the movement and write a replacement finale, lighter, shorter and wholly different in character, and he agreed, the only such capitulation of his career. The fugue was published separately as Op. 133. On the experiment reading, this is the cleanest evidence available: a work that failed the test its composer himself accepted, communication with competent contemporaries, and was rescued from that failure first by his own compliance and then by posterity's reverence.

### Are the late quartets failures or masterpieces?

The question assumes the two verdicts exclude each other, and the record supports a narrower answer. The Grosse Fuge failed with its first audience and was withdrawn by its composer, while the quartets as a body have held the centre of the repertoire for a century under continuous expert scrutiny.

The case generalises, on this side, to a pattern of overreach. The Hammerklavier Sonata carries a metronome mark for its first movement so fast that pianists have disputed its practicability from the beginning, and a composer who cannot hear the instrument he is writing for may set demands in ignorance of their cost. The vocal writing of the Ninth and the Missa asks things of choruses that choirs still experience as assault rather than expression. In the 1930s Theodor Adorno, the most formidable critic ever to take the late style as his subject, refused the consoling version outright: what he heard in the late works was not transcendence but fracture, a music that leaves conventions standing like emptied shells, declines reconciliation, and registers catastrophe rather than victory. Adorno valued the works, which is what makes his testimony dangerous to the affirmative case: the discontinuities the affirmers call vision, he calls the refusal of a dying man to pretend that the world coheres, and the notes support both descriptions with equal ease. The sceptical case then closes with the insurance argument. Ensured by the mystique, a late work cannot fail, because failure is reclassified as altitude. Works by lesser names with the same features, the same awkwardnesses and discontinuities, are programmed once and allowed to lapse, and nobody calls the lapse a failure of the audience. If the Grosse Fuge is a masterpiece, the sceptic concludes, it should be possible to say so in terms that could, in principle, have come out the other way.

## Why both readings use the same evidence

The two cases have now been stated, and a reader comparing them will notice the peculiar fact that gives this article its fourth rung: the transcendent and the experimental readings of the late works rest on identical evidence, so the disagreement is about criteria. Both sides cite the Grosse Fuge's scale and difficulty, the one as conquest, the other as overreach. Both cite the discontinuities of Op. 131, the one as a form beyond forms, the other as fracture. Both cite the Hammerklavier's metronome mark, the one as vision indifferent to the present instrument, the other as a demand made in ignorance of it. Both cite the first audiences, the one as the predictable incomprehension that greets altitude, the other as the verdict of the only jury that heard the works without reverence. There is no fact in dispute between the parties. Every document is shared, every note agreed. What differs is the standard by which a note counts as success, and standards are chosen, not discovered in scores.

That observation closes the second loop structurally rather than by verdict, as the stance of this article requires. If success means the achievement of unprecedented structural ends, ends that later music had to reckon with whether it wished to or not, the late works succeed, and their century in the repertoire is the running confirmation. If success means reliable effect on an audience meeting the work without preparation, some of the late works fail, and fail by design, since their composer had ceased to write for the audience in the room and said as much in his dealings with publishers. The two meanings are both legitimate, and an assessment that does not declare which one it is using is not an assessment but an incantation, of exactly the kind the deafness explanation was shown to be. What the evidence does settle, and it is not nothing, is the durability question: the works have been tested by two centuries of unsentimental professional use, programmed by players who stake their reputations on them, and they have not been found out. Durability is not transcendence, and it is not nothing either. It is what remains when the mystique is removed and the experiment is allowed its results, and it is a firmer ground for the composer's standing than either the halo or the debunking, a ground the criteria sections that follow will now occupy.

## The greatest-composer claim stated on criteria

The claim that Beethoven is the greatest composer is the one assertion in this debate that is usually made without any apparatus at all, as if its meaning were obvious, and the first service an audit can perform is to refuse it in that form. Greatest is not a property a composer has, like a birthplace. It is the output of a measurement, and a measurement requires criteria declared in advance, applied to the whole of the evidence, and held constant across the candidates. The brief for this article fixes the procedure: three criteria that his defenders actually invoke are to be tested in turn, structural ambition, expressive range, and historical consequence, and the claim is then to be run against the two rivals whose names always enter this argument, Bach and Mozart, on the same three measures. The article will not arrive at a ranking, because the trap in its design is correct: a ranking is what you get when you smuggle the weights in unexamined, and the interesting question is which criterion is doing the deciding.

### Is Beethoven overrated?

Overrated against which measure is the only honest form of the question. Against the heroic legend, yes, since no documented life supports it. Against the criteria of structural ambition, expressive range and historical consequence tested here, he wins on consequence outright and shares the other two, which is not what overrating usually means.

Before the criteria are worked through in prose, the whole debate can be laid out in one view, because most of its confusions come from arguing several claims as if they were one. The state of each claim, with the strongest evidence on each side and the test that would settle it, is the article's findable artifact, and readers who consult it first will find the sections that follow easier to place.

This article's narrower task is to watch what happens to one name when the measurement is made honestly, and the first criterion, structural ambition, is the ground on which his defenders have always been most confident.

## Structural ambition as the criterion

Structural ambition, as a criterion, measures how much weight a work asks its inherited forms to carry, and whether the forms hold, transform, or break interestingly under the load. It is the criterion closest to the scores themselves, the least dependent on biography, and the one on which the documentary evidence of the sketchbooks bears most directly, because ambition of this kind cannot be faked in a finished score while being absent from the workshop. By this measure the Eroica remains the founding exhibit. Its first movement is built on a scale roughly double that of any symphony before it, its development treats a subsidiary figure, the new theme in E minor that arrives where no theme should be left, as material for the movement's crisis, and its finale rebuilds a dance tune he had already used twice, in a ballet and in a set of variations, into the symphony's destination. The sketchbook for the work, with its drafts and rejections, shows the scale was not an effusion but a decision, revised toward. Ambition, in the documents, looks like labour.

The measure holds across the output where it is most severely tested. The Diabelli Variations load a single waltz with thirty-three transformations and do not exhaust it. Op. 111 closes the piano sonatas with two movements, the second a set of variations that progressively dissolve the theme's metre until rhythm itself becomes the subject, an ending that generations of pianists have read as a farewell to the instrument and that the sketches show being reached through the same unglamorous process of trial. The late quartets load the four-movement scheme with seven-movement arches, with fugues as finales, with slow movements of a length that changes the genre's centre of gravity. Where the criterion has limits for him, honesty requires naming them: the ambition is concentrated at the peaks, and between the peaks lie works, competent, professional, occasionally routine, that ask little of their forms and receive it. Structural ambition measures the ceiling, not the average, and an assessment that cares about the average will find its candidate in the next section's rivals. But a ceiling is a real measurement, and on ceilings his stands with very few for company, a point the cases for Bach and Mozart will have to answer on the same terms rather than by changing the subject.

## Expressive range as the criterion

Expressive range measures the breadth of human states a body of music can render convincingly, and it is the criterion on which the heroic narrative has done its subject the most damage, because the narrative flattens him into a single register, struggle, and then calls the register depth. The documents and the scores describe a wider musician. The same output that contains the Eroica's conflicts contains the Pastoral Symphony's deliberate relaxation of conflict, the Eighth Symphony's wit, which is the wit of a man parodying his own heroic manner a decade after inventing it, and the finale of Op. 135, built around a motto he adapted from a joke about a household bill, asking whether it must be and answering that it must, philosophy conducted as comedy. Terror he commands completely, in the Coriolan and Egmont overtures, which compress a tragedy's worth of it into minutes. Consolation he commands in the slow movements that quartets have made famous. The Cavatina of Op. 130 and the hymn movement of Op. 132 stand at the far end of the range from Wellington's Victory, and the distance between them is the measure of the musician.

The range narrows, and the audit must say where. Sustained lyrical song, the long breathed melody that Schubert and Mozart produce as a native element, is not his centre: his themes are characteristically built from motifs, cells to be developed, rather than sung lines to be savoured, and when the criterion is melody in that sense the comparison turns against him. Opera narrows him further. One opera, Fidelio, achieved across a decade and three versions, against Mozart's sequence of masterpieces in the form; whatever the opera's greatness, and it is real, a career's range that includes the stage only once cannot match a career that includes it continuously. Sacred music outside the Missa Solemnis is respectable rather than central. The verdict on this criterion is therefore genuinely divided: depth of expression within the instrumental forms he made his own, unmatched; breadth across all the forms his age offered, exceeded by at least one contemporary and one predecessor. A defender who claims range entire has not listened to the whole output. A detractor who denies the depth has not listened to the quartets, and the next criterion, where his case is strongest, is the one the defenders should have been leading with all along.

## Historical consequence as the criterion

Historical consequence measures what changed because the work existed: what later composers had to do differently, what institutions reorganised themselves around, what became impossible to write afterwards with a clear conscience. It is the most objective of the three criteria, because its evidence is other people's documented behaviour, and it is the criterion on which the case for Beethoven does not merely compete but distances the field. The downstream of his music is the subject of its own study in [the account of Beethoven's influence on music](/2011/02/21/ludwig-van-beethoven-influence-on-music/), and what follows is the summary the debate requires. Schubert, who carried a torch in the funeral procession of 1827 and died the following year, spent his last works reaching for the symphonic scale Beethoven had set, and his Great C major Symphony is unthinkable without the precedent. Berlioz heard the symphonies in Paris under Habeneck from 1828 and rebuilt French music around the experience, saying in his memoirs that Beethoven had opened a new world. Liszt carried the nine symphonies into every drawing room in Europe in piano transcriptions of punishing fidelity, and built his symphonic poems in the space the Eroica and the Pastoral had opened.

The consequence runs deeper than imitation. After Beethoven, the symphony was no longer an entertainment genre but the form in which a composer was required to be serious, and that requirement, which is the narrative's doing as much as the music's, governed a century: Wagner built his entire enterprise on the reading of the Ninth examined in section four, Brahms delayed his First Symphony into his forties under the standard and heard it greeted by Hans von Bülow as a Tenth, and Mahler inherited the choral symphony and the problem of following the Ninth as the conditions of his career. Even the reactions are consequences: every composer who defined himself against Beethoven, from the neoclassicists onward, confirms the centrality they were resisting. Nicholas Cook, the musicologist who has examined how the Ninth's meanings were manufactured by its performers and interpreters, supplies the necessary caution from within the affirmative case: consequence is partly made by the receivers, and the Beethoven who changed history is a joint production of the scores and the institutions that amplified them. The caution refines the criterion without reversing it. No other composer of his century stands at the head of so many documented lines of descent, and on this criterion the greatest-composer claim, stated carefully, survives its audit.

## Where he wins and where he does not

The three criteria have now been applied separately, and their results can be gathered without pretending they sum to a single number. He wins historical consequence outright, by a margin no rival in his century approaches, and the win is documented in other men's careers rather than asserted by his admirers. He shares structural ambition at the ceiling, where his peaks stand in the smallest company, while conceding that the ambition is unevenly spread across the output and that the average page of Beethoven is not the Eroica. He divides expressive range, supreme in the instrumental registers he made central, surpassed in sung melody, in opera and in the sacred forms he touched rarely. Any weighted total of those three results is a decision about the weights, not a discovery about the music, and the two sections that follow exist to prove the point by showing the total change hands when the same criteria are applied, at full strength, to Bach and to Mozart.

It is worth pausing on what this pattern of results does to the original question. The greatest-composer claim, as popularly made, asserts a single summit. The criteria audit finds instead a profile: a composer who is first on the criterion that measures effect on others, joint first on the criterion that measures formal reach at its peaks, and beaten on breadth by the two men he is always compared with. A profile is not a refutation. It is what an honest measurement of any complex achievement looks like, and the discomfort it causes is the discomfort of discovering that the question was malformed. The defenders who insist on the summit and the detractors who insist on the demotion are answering the same malformed question from opposite ends, and both need the malformedness, because a profile cannot be worshipped or debunked. It can only be read. The reading, continued against the two rivals, is the fairest test available of whether the profile survives comparison, or whether it is merely what a diminished legend looks like after an audit.

## The case for Bach on those criteria

The case for Johann Sebastian Bach, made at full strength on the same three criteria, begins with the observation that he contests the one criterion Beethoven was supposed to own. Structural ambition in Bach is not a matter of scale but of system: the two books of the Well-Tempered Clavier undertake to demonstrate, in every key, what the keyboard can think; the Goldberg Variations build a cathedral on a bass line; the Musical Offering takes a single theme supplied by a king and exhausts its contrapuntal possibilities as if possibility itself were the subject; and the Art of Fugue, left unfinished at his death, attempts the total science of a single idea. Where Beethoven's ambition shows in the load placed on inherited forms, Bach's shows in the construction of complete worlds, each internally law-abiding, each inexhaustible by the performers who have lived in them since. The boy Beethoven was formed inside one of those worlds: his teacher Christian Gottlob Neefe reported in a music journal in 1783 that the twelve-year-old played most of the Well-Tempered Clavier, a feat Neefe took as the sign of a second Mozart in the making. The judge of the later ambition was present at the earlier one's education.

On expressive range, Bach's case is formidable in a register Beethoven barely entered. About two hundred cantatas survive, a fraction of the output, and across them the whole vocabulary of grief, terror, consolation, jubilation and quiet trust is set with a specificity that still startles singers meeting it for the first time. The two surviving Passions dramatise crowds, betrayal and desolation with a psychological precision that opera composers study. Against this, the instrumental works match Beethoven department for department, the suites, the concertos, the solo violin and cello works building complete expressive worlds without a word sung. On consistency, the third and unofficial criterion that follows the official three like a shadow, Bach's case is the strongest of the three composers examined here: the floor of his output, the routine Sunday cantata written against the calendar, sits at a level most composers reach in their best years, because his routine was itself a discipline of invention.

Historical consequence is where the Bach case changes character, because his consequence was delayed by a century and then became arguably the deepest of all. He died in 1750 regarded by the profession as a learned anachronism, and his public resurrection waited until 1829, when the young Felix Mendelssohn conducted the St Matthew Passion in Berlin, two years after Beethoven's death, in the heart of the Beethoven century. From that revival forward, Bach became the foundation of compositional training itself: Mozart had already studied him in manuscript in the 1780s, in the circle around Baron van Swieten, and reaped the fugal harvest in his own last works; Beethoven was raised on the Well-Tempered Clavier; and every serious composer since has served the same apprenticeship. Beethoven's consequence ran through the symphony and the idea of the composer. Bach's ran through the technique of composition itself, the grammar musicians think in. Which of those is greater depends, once again, on what the criterion is silently taken to mean, and a debate that reaches this point has stopped being about either man and started being about the measurer.

## The case for Mozart on those criteria

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart enters this argument with one disqualifying handicap in the popular mind, the appearance of ease, and the appearance is a trap this article has met before, in the parallel audit of [the Mozart myths and legacy](/2010/12/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-legacy-debate/), where the effortless genius proved to be as much a construction as the suffering one. On expressive range, the first criterion to apply, Mozart's case is the strongest of the three. He practised every genre his age offered at its highest level at the same time: the operas characterise through music with a precision no contemporary matched, so that in The Marriage of Figaro and Don Giovanni a whole society thinks aloud in ensembles; the piano concertos of the Vienna years arrive in a run of sustained invention that has no parallel for concentration; the chamber music, the symphonies, the sacred works and the songs all stand at the summit of their kinds simultaneously. Haydn, who had no interest to serve and the standing to judge, told the composer's father in February 1785, in words Leopold reported home in a letter written within days, that he knew no greater composer than the young Mozart. Contemporary testimony of that weight, from the reigning master of the age, is the kind of evidence this article has been demanding throughout, and it exists for Mozart in a way it exists for no one else.

On structural ambition his case is subtler and therefore often undervalued, because his structures conceal their engineering. The finale of the Jupiter Symphony fuses sonata form with a fugue of five themes and presents the fusion as brilliance rather than effort. The G minor String Quintet loads the intimate genre with an expressive weight it had not carried. Don Giovanni holds tragedy and comedy in a single dramatic mechanism for two acts without dropping either. The ambition is real; it is simply not advertised, and a criterion that measures visible strain will underrate the composer whose strain is invisible, which is a defect of the criterion, not of the composer. On historical consequence, Mozart's lines of descent are documented in Beethoven's own workshop: the young Beethoven wrote variations for cello and piano on themes from The Magic Flute and Don Giovanni, modelled his Piano Quintet, Op. 16, closely on Mozart's Quintet in the same key, and arrived in Vienna in 1792 publicly framed, by Count Waldstein's farewell entry in his album, as the heir appointed to receive Mozart's spirit from Haydn's hands. The whole nineteenth century then learned composition partly through Mozart's scores. Where his case yields is in the mythology of consequence: he founded no ideology, his century made no titan of him until later and then a lesser one, and the symphony as a public moral arena is Beethoven's creation rather than his. On range he wins. On consequence he places. On ambition he draws at the ceiling and wins on concealment, which is to say the Mozart case, like the Bach case, defeats the summit claim without diminishing the man it was made against, and the pattern across both rivals is the finding: the greatest-composer question does not survive its criteria, whoever is fed into them.

## The charge of canon dominance

A third question opens here, the last of the article's loops. Is his dominance of the repertoire something he caused? The charge itself needs stating plainly first. Orchestral life, the argument runs, is organised around a dead centre of which Beethoven is the fixed middle: the nine symphonies form the core cycle every orchestra must maintain, the concertos anchor the solo repertory, the overtures fill the short slots, and around this centre an economy of subscription selling, music education and recording has arranged itself so thoroughly that the dominance reproduces itself each season without anyone deciding that it should. Other music, the living, the unfashionable dead, the composers of his own time who were crowded out first by his shadow and then by his statue, competes for the margin that remains. The charge is not that the music is unworthy. It is that its share is disproportionate to any defensible measure, of output, of quality, even of demand, and that the disproportion is self-perpetuating.

### Does Beethoven dominate concert programmes unfairly?

Dominate, demonstrably: his symphonies anchor subscription seasons and anniversary years swell around him. Unfairly depends on the baseline chosen. Measured against living composers and his own contemporaries, the share is indefensible; measured against ticket demand, programmers answer that they give audiences the composer audiences reliably choose.

The historical depth of the dominance is the first thing to grasp, because it predates the recording age, the radio age and the modern orchestra itself. The music historian William Weber, who spent a career counting concert programmes across Europe's capitals, documented the transformation from which the canon was born: in the 1780s roughly a quarter of the works performed in the cities he studied were by composers already dead, while by the 1870s the proportion had risen to roughly three quarters, and within that new empire of the dead, Beethoven stood first among the residents. The canon, that is, was a nineteenth century invention, assembled by the same decades that assembled the heroic narrative, and the two constructions are one construction viewed from different ends: the story told the institutions whom to enthrone, and the enthronement taught the story to each new audience as if it were the audience's own discovery. What the twentieth century added was scale and machinery, the complete cycles on record, the anniversary industries, the school curricula, the film scores and advertisements that made four notes the most recognised door knock in the world. Whether the man in the Schwarzspanierhaus caused any of this is the question the loop holds open until section twenty-six, and the evidence must first be allowed to speak in both directions.

## Programming evidence for and against

The evidence for the charge is the evidence of any season brochure. Subscription series are sold on recognisable summits, and the Beethoven cycle is the most reliable summit in the catalogue, which is why orchestras return to it with a periodicity that owes nothing to scholarship. The repertoire reports published by the League of American Orchestras, season after season, have placed him at or near the top of the most performed composers in the United States, and the pattern in European houses is the same. Anniversary years convert the tendency into a flood: the bicentenary of his birth in 1970 filled seasons on several continents with cycles, festivals and complete editions, crowding the programmes of that year and, by displacement, of the years around it. Even the audition system reproduces the centre, since orchestral players are hired on excerpts drawn heavily from the same nine symphonies, so that the profession's gatekeeping and its programming are the same list viewed from two sides. A season planner laying those figures side by side in a browser-based presentation viewer, such as [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), confronts a distribution no one designed and everyone maintains.

The evidence against the charge begins with the box office, which is not an institution's invention. Audiences buy tickets for the Eroica and the Ninth in numbers they do not buy for worthier obscurities, and an orchestra that programmes against its audience's demonstrated appetite is spending a subsidy or courting a deficit, depending on its funding model. Demand of that kind is not manufactured from nothing; it is the deposit of two centuries of listeners finding the music equal to its reputation often enough to renew the reputation. The centre has also proved capable of movement, which a genuinely closed canon would not permit: Mahler travelled in two generations from specialist enthusiasm to the centre of the repertory, and Sibelius and Shostakovich hold places their first hearers would not have predicted, which suggests the margin is contested rather than sealed. Festivals that programme adventurously, the Proms in London among them, fill halls for unfamiliar names when the unfamiliar is framed with intelligence, evidence that audiences follow curation further than the subscription model assumes. The honest summary is two-sided in a way the charge's partisans dislike: the dominance is real, its mechanisms are institutional, and the demand underneath it is also real. Which of those facts carries the moral weight is the question section twenty-six exists to answer, and the displaced, who have waited longest for a hearing in this article, come first.

## Which composers the dominance displaced

Displacement is easiest to see in the generation that shared his century's opening and lost its place in the next one. Louis Spohr was, in the 1830s and 1840s, among the most performed composers in Europe, a symphonist, a violinist of the first rank and an opera composer whose works filled the stages Beethoven's single opera could not. His symphonies have since withdrawn to the specialist margin, played at festivals and recorded by the curious, while the centre he once shared is occupied by the colleague whose statue replaced him. Ferdinand Ries, Beethoven's own pupil and the witness this article relied on in section ten, wrote eight symphonies that the nineteenth century performed and the twentieth mislaid. George Onslow's symphonies and chamber music commanded French and German respect in his lifetime and survive as a connoisseur's enthusiasm. These were not mediocrities crushed by genius. They were the repertory, and the repertory changed its mind, under institutional pressures that had little to do with re-hearing their music and deciding against it.

The displacement cut deeper where the institutions were already closed. Louise Farrenc composed three symphonies that Paris performed in her lifetime, won from the Conservatoire the professorship her sex was not supposed to hold, and then watched the century's canon formation pass her by, her orchestral works unpublished in full and unperformed for generations, not because audiences rejected them but because the machinery of editions, cycles and curricula had no slot prepared for her. Fanny Hensel, whose talent her brother Felix's career throws into permanent relief, saw her large works confined to the family's Berlin Sunday concerts while the public orchestral world went unvisited by them. Emilie Mayer, described by contemporaries with the awkward compliment of a female Beethoven, wrote eight symphonies that her century performed and the next one shelved. The survey of [musicians ignored in their own time](/2026/04/07/musicians-ignored-in-their-own-time/) follows this pattern across the series, and the Beethoven case is its largest instance rather than an exception to it: a canon narrow enough to have a centre is narrow enough to have a margin, and the margin is populated by the unplayed.

The largest displaced party has no names at all. It is the living, in every generation since 1827, whose premieres were not given because the slot, the rehearsal hours and the marketing budget went to another cycle, and whose second performances, the ones on which a work's life depends, were rarer still. Against their silence the defence enters its strongest plea: that the same centre which crowds them also sustains the institutions that occasionally programme them, that the Eroica sells the subscription inside which the premiere hides, and that an orchestral culture without its anchor tenant might not be a fairer culture but a smaller one. The plea is genuine and unprovable in both directions. What the record establishes is the mechanism, a concentration that renews itself through education, audition lists, recording catalogues and risk management, and the mechanism, once seen, cannot be laid at a dead man's door, which brings the loop to its close.

## Is the dominance his fault or the institution's

His repertoire dominance is largely an institutional fact about programming and funding rather than a verdict delivered by audiences. That is the fifth rung of the ladder, and with it the third loop closes: the dominance is not something he caused in the sense the charge requires. A composer who died in 1827 does not programme the seasons of 1920 or 2010. The decisions are taken each year by living people with budgets: intendants who know which names renew subscriptions, education departments that teach the works their teachers were taught, record companies whose back catalogues earn most from the centre, examination boards whose syllabuses define the competent musician, and critics for whom the canonical performance is the unit of comparison. Each decision is locally rational. Their sum is a concentration nobody chose, maintained by the same circularity Burnham identified in the hearing of the Eroica: the audience knows the music because it is programmed, and it is programmed because the audience knows it. To call the result an audience verdict is to mistake the output of the machine for the preference of its customers, who are offered a menu drawn up elsewhere and then congratulated on their taste.

The composer's own contribution to his fame should be recorded, because the institutional case becomes a new mythology if it makes him passive. He was, in his lifetime, a formidably active manager of his own position: he negotiated with publishers in three countries, played them against one another, sold the same work more than once in different territories, cultivated aristocratic patrons with dedications calibrated to their vanity and their purses, quarrelled publicly with the economics of the servant's place, and understood, earlier than almost any musician, that a composer's income and standing were things to be engineered. The Eroica's ruined title page is an act of image management as well as rage. All of that engineered a career, and a career is what it engineered. It did not engineer two centuries of subscription seasons, any more than Bach engineered the conservatoire curriculum that enthroned him a hundred years after his death. The responsibility for dominance lies where the power to change it lies, with the institutions that renew it annually and could, at the price of real risk, renew it less. Whether they should is a question about what orchestras are for, and it cannot be answered by pointing at a tomb in Vienna. The tomb's occupant has had his audit. The institution is still taking its decisions, and it takes them in his name because his name is the one that costs least to defend.

## The charge of bombast

The oldest charge against him is not neglect but noise. Bombast, in the vocabulary of the accusation, is effect outrunning content: climaxes achieved by repetition and volume rather than by thought, rhetoric that insists where it should persuade, the fist shaken so often that the shaking becomes the manner. The charge has a pedigree as long as the fame. Reviewing the Eroica in the Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung in 1805, an anonymous critic allowed the work's power and begged for its shortening, suggesting that the symphony would gain immeasurably if its composer had pruned its excesses, which is the bombast charge in embryo: too much, too long, too loud an insistence on being overwhelmed. Later in the century John Ruskin, confessing his own tin ear with a wit that has outlasted his musical judgement, wrote to a friend that Beethoven's music sounded to him like a bag of nails being upset, with a hammer dropped in now and then. Ruskin is evidence about Ruskin, but the survival of the gibe testifies to a real constituency of listeners, in every generation since, for whom the heroic manner registers as hectoring.

The refined form of the charge is more damaging than the gibe, because it turns the article's own findings against the music. It runs as follows. Certain passages, the development of the Eroica's first movement with its hammered dissonances, the Ninth's finale worrying its theme through variation after variation of increasing volume, the coda that will not end, achieve their effects by the methods of oratory rather than of argument. In another composer, the charge continues, such passages would be called bluster and marked down. In Beethoven they are read as struggle, because the heroic narrative instructs the listener in advance what the noise means, and the narrative, as section four showed, was supplied by critics with programmes of their own. The bombast charge, properly understood, is therefore not an accusation about decibels. It is an accusation that the music's most famous effects are co-produced by a story, and that the story has been doing unpaid work for two centuries. There is enough truth in this to require an answer, and the answer does not lie in denying that the effects exist. It lies in the part of the output the charge cannot see, because programming hides it, which is the subject of the next section.

## The counter-case from the quartets

The counter-case begins with a fact of proportion. The works on which the bombast charge rests, the heroic symphonies and their loud progeny, are a minority of the output, and the majority includes a body of chamber music in which the prevailing virtues are economy, intimacy and wit. The String Quartet in F minor, Op. 95, marked by its composer as a serious quartet and written in 1810, at the height of the supposed heroic period, is the most compressed large statement he ever made, four movements of refusal: themes are stated and cut off, developments arrive like interruptions, and the whole work behaves like a man declining to make the expected speech. The slow movements of the Razumovsky Quartets unfold at a walking pace of sustained song that no orator could survive. The Cello Sonatas of Op. 102, written as the late period opens, conduct their arguments in a murmur, two instruments thinking together at the edge of audibility, and the Bagatelles of Op. 126 miniaturise the late style into pieces a child could carry. A charge that cannot account for a third of the evidence is not a judgement of the composer. It is a judgement of the programme planning that feeds the public the loud fraction and calls the fraction the man.

The last quartet makes the point in its most concentrated form. The finale of Op. 135 is headed by a motto adapted from a joke, a canon he had composed about a friend who owed money for a concert subscription, asking in mock despair whether it must be, and the quartet's finale turns the joke into a question genuinely asked and lightly answered, the heavy machinery of the late style set to produce, in the end, something close to a shrug transfigured. Listeners who meet Beethoven first in this movement, or in the Cavatina's intimacy, or in the Piano Sonata Op. 110, whose middle reaches a passage of recitative as exposed as anything in Bach, do not hear bombast, and they are not hearing a different composer. They are hearing the part of him that the heroic selection, administered by the institutions audited in section twenty-six, keeps in the inner rooms of the repertoire. The counter-case therefore converges with the dominance argument from the opposite side: the popular Beethoven is too loud for the same reason he is too central, because a selection has been mistaken for a whole. Restore the whole and the charge shrinks to a true remark about some famous passages, which the Eroica's first reviewer already made in 1805, and which no fair assessment needs to inflate into a character.

## The occasional and commercial works as evidence

Both parties to the debate reach, sooner or later, for the same embarrassing shelf of evidence: the works he wrote for money and occasions, which neither the heroic narrative nor its debunking quite knows how to hold. Wellington's Victory, the battle piece of 1813 written to celebrate a victory over the French and to fill the inventor Mälzel's concerts, was the greatest popular and financial success of his lifetime, orchestral gunfire and national anthems arranged for maximum effect, and its composer, in his correspondence, treated it with a frankness that embarrasses the prophet image, as a thing made for the occasion and the fee. Der glorreiche Augenblick, the cantata of 1814 for the assembled sovereigns of the Congress of Vienna, flatters the powerful in the accents the heroic narrative insists he despised. Across two decades he supplied the Edinburgh publisher George Thomson with more than 150 settings of Scottish, Irish and Welsh folk songs, harmonising tunes from a nation he never visited for fees negotiated in letters of exemplary hardness. The Namensfeier Overture, the ballet music of his Bonn years, the patriotic choruses of the wars: the shelf is long.

For the debunking party, the shelf is a verdict: the titan had a price list, the prophet took commissions from emperors, and the narrative of the sovereign artist answerable to genius alone collapses on the invoices. For the defence, the shelf proves the opposite charge false in its turn, since occasional music was the profession's normal economy, the weekly bread of every Kapellmeister who ever lived: Bach's cantatas were job work against a church calendar, Handel's fireworks music was a royal commission, and nobody's greatness has ever been assessed by their occasional shelf, because the assessment of a composer by his potboilers is a method no one applies consistently. The finding this article enters is narrower than either party's. The occasional works damage the myth, which required a man who never wrote to order, and they do not damage the music that matters, which was never the myth's to protect. Their real service to the debate is diagnostic: the canon quietly removed them from view, so that the public Beethoven could appear to consist only of summits, and the removal was the institution's work, performed for the narrative's benefit. A composer judged entire, potboilers included, looks less like a titan and more like what the documents have shown throughout: the most ambitious professional of his age, whose best work survives the demystification his worst work never needed.

## The sketch evidence against the inspiration myth

The last piece of the audit is the one that has been waiting since the opening section, and it carries the sixth and final rung. The inspiration myth, the gentlest and most persistent of the constructions, holds that the music arrived: that the deaf seer served as amanuensis to an inner voice, dictating works whose completeness in his imagination preceded their writing, so that composition was transcription and the manuscripts merely the paperwork of a vision. The myth survives because it flatters the same consolation as the heroic narrative, and it dies, as the narrative died, on contact with the documents, in this case the sketchbooks. Beethoven sketched compulsively, in bound books and on loose leaves, at desks and on walks, in pencil stubs he kept in his pockets, and the surviving material runs to thousands of pages. Gustav Nottebohm, the scholar who began publishing studies of the sketches in the 1860s, opened the workshop to inspection for the first time, and what inspection found was not dictation. It was engineering.

The Eroica finale's theme, the tune on which the symphony's whole argument lands, can be traced through three works and several years of the sketchbooks: composed for the Prometheus ballet in 1801, tried as the finale of the Op. 35 piano variations in 1802, and only then installed as the destination of a symphony. The Schiller ode he set in the Ninth was a project he contemplated for three decades before the symphony absorbed it. The movements of Op. 131 were reordered on paper, themes rejected and recalled, joins rebuilt; the Grosse Fuge's replacement exists because even this composer, at this altitude, could accept an editor's pressure and produce a different ending on request. Everywhere the record shows the same process: a motif worried across pages until its possibilities declare themselves, a movement dismantled and rebalanced, a work abandoned and its materials salvaged into another. The sketchbooks show the music was engineered through revision, which removes the inspiration myth and leaves a stronger case for him than the myth ever provided, because a case built on visitation can be withdrawn by the next fashion in biography, while a case built on documented labour, on the visible transformation of ordinary materials into the Eroica across three attempts, rests on paper that anyone may examine. The titan was a worker. The workshop is the biography, and it is a better one than the legend, because it is true in the way the legend only performed.

## Does the biography matter to the music at all

Every thread of the argument now runs into a single question, and it deserves a section of its own because it judges all the others. Suppose the audit is complete: the anecdotes quarantined, the narrative dated and attributed, the deafness reduced to a condition, the sketches installed as the true biography. Does any of it matter to the music? Two positions, each with a serious pedigree, divide the question, and the stance of this article is to give both their full height. The first is the formalist discipline, whose founding text is Eduard Hanslick's treatise On the Musically Beautiful of 1854, written in Vienna within living memory of its subject's death. On this view, the Eroica's content is its tonal argument and nothing else; the feelings it arouses are effects, not meanings; and the entire biographical apparatus, true or forged, is at best a distraction from the notes and at worst a corruption of judgement, since it teaches listeners to grade works by the pathos of their origins, excusing the late quartets their awkwardness by the deafness or exalting them by the same fact, in neither case hearing them. The discipline's austerity is its strength. It is the only position in the debate that cannot be fooled by a forged anecdote, because it never asked for anecdotes.

The second position belongs to the study of reception, and Nicholas Cook has been its most instructive modern voice in the Beethoven literature: no listener, Cook's work insists, hears unhistorically. What a listener hears in the Ninth is mediated by two centuries of performances, editions, programme notes, school lessons and national ceremonies, so that the biography, including its forged portions, is causally part of the music's existence in the world whether or not it ought to be. A forged anecdote that changes what millions of listeners attend to has changed the music's history as surely as a corrected misprint. On this view the audit matters enormously, not because it alters a single note, but because the notes reach every ear through the story, and a false story delivers a distorted hearing to listeners who have no reason to suspect the delivery.

The evidence assembled in this article suggests where the balance falls, and it falls in a place neither party quite occupies. Biography does not change the notes, and the formalists are right that the notes are where the value lives; the Op. 131 that a listener meets innocent of every legend is the same Op. 131. But biography demonstrably governs which notes get played, taught, funded, recorded and loved, and across two centuries that governance is itself a musical fact of the first order, since a work unplayed is a work in a drawer regardless of its value. The deafness story did not write the late quartets. It decided that the late quartets would be approached as scripture rather than as repertoire, which altered their performing tradition, their tempo choices, their programme placement and their price. The biography matters to the music the way weather matters to a building: it is not the architecture, and nothing about the architecture can be understood in the long run without it. That is the hinge on which the closing sections turn, from the evidence to the assessment the evidence can support.

## What a fair assessment would require

A fair assessment of Beethoven, on the record as it now stands, would have to meet conditions that neither the worshipful tradition nor the debunking one habitually meets, and they can be enumerated as plainly as the evidence was. It would rank its sources by provenance before quoting them, treating the letters, the journal, the testament and the graded witnesses as the foundation, and everything descending from Schindler as advocacy requiring corroboration. It would declare its criteria before delivering its verdicts, and it would apply those criteria to whole outputs rather than to peaks: to the occasional works and the routine ones as well as to the summits, to the song cycle An die ferne Geliebte of 1816, the first true cycle of songs and a landmark of a genre the heroic image has no use for, as well as to the symphonies the image is built from. It would compare like with like, Bach entire against Beethoven entire, Mozart entire against both, and it would treat the canon itself as evidence about institutions rather than as a verdict on worth. And it would include in its listening the works the selection has hidden, the cello sonatas, the Mass in C, the violin sonata he called Spring, the bagatelles, hearing the composer whole before pronouncing on the parts.

It would also, and this is the condition most often skipped, permit individual works to fail. An assessment in which the Grosse Fuge cannot fail is as rigged as one in which it cannot succeed, and the fair instrument is the one the table in section sixteen modelled: each claim carrying its strongest evidence on both sides and the test that would settle it, openly displayed. Judged by that instrument, the assessment that emerges is neither the titan nor the fraud. It is a working composer of Vienna in the decades around 1800, formed by Haydn's teaching and Mozart's scores and the Well-Tempered Clavier, who managed his career with unprecedented shrewdness, lost his hearing across thirty years while revolutionising the symphony and the quartet, wrote a shelf of occasional music without shame, left a workshop archive of unmatched richness, and was posthumously rebuilt by critics, nations and institutions into the figurehead of an idea of music they needed. The music survives the rebuilding, most of it, and the best of it survives triumphantly. The survival is the fact. The figurehead was the packaging, and a fair assessment is simply the discipline of never again mistaking the one for the other.

## What would have to be true for the claim to fail

An argument that cannot describe its own defeat is a creed, and the greatest-composer claim, to be treated as an argument at all, must state the conditions under which it would collapse. They are three, and each is testable in principle, which is what distinguishes them from the moods of fashion. The claim would fail, first, if the documentary audit of the 1803 break were to show that the break was an inheritance mislabelled as an invention: if the toolkit of the heroic manner, the scale, the motivic development, the drama of struggle and resolution, were demonstrated to stand complete in the late quartets and symphonies of Haydn, so that the Eroica enlarged a rhetoric it did not create. The sketches and the scores make this test performable, and partial performances of it have always found Haydn closer than the narrative admits and the enlargement larger than the sceptics allow, which is why the claim survives the first condition bruised but standing.

The claim would fail, second, if the late works' reputation proved to be an artefact of the insurance audited in section twelve: if, played without their legend to listeners without the preparation, they repeatedly failed to hold attention that comparable works by contemporaries held. This experiment is run informally every time a quartet programmes Farrenc or Spohr beside the late Beethoven, and the running result, across two centuries of unsentimental professional use, is that the late works have not been found out; players stake reputations on them, audiences return to them, and the repertoire's centre holds them without subsidy of reverence alone. The claim would fail, third, if the criteria were legitimately reweighted so that the centre of musical value moved to the genres he neglected: if opera, song and sacred music were agreed to outrank the symphony and the quartet in the definition of greatness, the summit would pass to Mozart or to Bach by the same measurement that now sustains the argument, and the honest defender must accept that outcome in advance as the price of having criteria at all. None of the three conditions has been met. Two of the three are not close to being met. The claim's survival, however, is conditional in a way its popular form never admits, and stating the conditions is the assessment, since a greatness that specifies how it could be lost is a greatness that can finally be believed.

## Beethoven heroic narrative debate: what the argument is actually about

Step back from the evidence and a final pattern is visible, the one the whole audit has been approaching. The argument about Beethoven has never chiefly been about Beethoven. It is the standing proxy war for the questions music cannot settle directly: whether music is an autonomous architecture or a human document, whether genius is a substance or a social achievement of the kind Tia DeNora documented in the Vienna drawing rooms, whether the canon is a discovery or an institution's self-portrait, whether suffering is a credential or a circumstance. Every party to the war needs a battlefield large enough to matter, and since the nineteenth century built the modern idea of the composer on this one name, this name is where the war is fought. Wagner needed the prophet and found him. The formalists needed the pure architect and found him. The debunkers need the manufactured titan and find him. The documents, unglamorously, contain a working musician, and the working musician keeps failing to be the emblem each army requires, which is why the armies keep returning to Schindler's version, or to the version Schindler's version provoked, rather than to the papers.

The thesis stated in the first section can now be given its final form. The figure most people mean by Beethoven is a nineteenth century construction on a contaminated record, and the strongest case for him survives the construction's removal by changing its subject: from the suffering hero to the documented workshop, from visitation to labour, from a greatness that must be believed to a greatness that can be checked. The checked greatness is smaller in one dimension, the anecdotal, and larger in every dimension that matters, because it rests on scores, sketches, letters and the recorded behaviour of two centuries of musicians who had every opportunity to find the music out and instead built their art on it. What would have to be true for that case to fail has been stated, and the statement stands open. The argument, in the end, is about whether listeners want a god or a colleague at the origin of their art. The documents have already voted, in their thousands of pages of crossings out, for the colleague, and the colleague is the more remarkable figure: a deafened, difficult, funny, calculating professional who wrote, in full view of his own workshop, the music his century needed a titan to explain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the heroic narrative about Beethoven?

The heroic narrative is the story, assembled by nineteenth century critics and biographers, of Beethoven as a suffering titan: a composer struck deaf in his prime who converted affliction into triumph, broke the classical forms open by force of will, and made the symphony a drama of struggle overcome. Scott Burnham, in his 1995 book Beethoven Hero, showed that this story became more than a biography. It became the standard by which orchestral music itself was judged, so that works resembling struggle and victory were valued highest and other kinds of excellence were heard as lesser. The narrative rests on some genuine documents, above all the Heiligenstadt Testament of 1802, and on a large body of anecdote of much weaker provenance.

### Q: Who invented the heroic Beethoven?

No single writer invented him, and the construction can be dated in stages. E. T. A. Hoffmann's review of the Fifth Symphony in 1810 supplied the metaphysical frame, presenting Beethoven as the composer who opened the realm of the infinite. The early biographies followed: Schlosser in 1828, Wegeler and Ries in 1838, and Anton Schindler in 1840, whose anecdotes proved the most durable and are now known to be contaminated by his forgeries in the conversation books. A. B. Marx taught readers to hear the symphonies as dramatic programmes, Wilhelm von Lenz divided the output into three ascending styles in 1852, and Wagner's centenary essay of 1870 completed the portrait by casting the deaf composer as a prophet. Each builder had identifiable reasons of his own.

### Q: Why was the heroic image useful to later composers?

The image worked as a licence and a legitimation. Wagner used it to present himself as Beethoven's appointed successor, arguing that the Ninth Symphony's turn to voices proved pure music had reached its limit and summoned the music drama he alone was writing. Brahms worked under the same image from the other side, delaying his First Symphony for years because the standard it set made every attempt feel like a public examination. Between those poles, the heroic Beethoven gave ambitious composers a moral warrant: difficulty, scale and the appearance of struggle were certified in advance as signs of seriousness, while charm and polish were demoted. Tia DeNora's research adds that the usefulness began earlier, in the Vienna of the 1790s, where patrons and critics were already constructing the category of genius around him for purposes of their own.

### Q: Did deafness cause Beethoven's late style?

The chronology argues against the simple version of the claim. Beethoven's hearing began to fail around 1798, by his own account in letters of 1801, but the decisive stylistic break, the heroic manner of the Eroica, arrived in 1803, while he was still performing in public, a career he continued until 1814. The conversation books, the clearest sign of profound deafness, begin only in 1818. The late style does coincide with near total deafness, so the correlation is real; what is missing is the mechanism, and comparison cuts against one, since Smetana, completely deaf in his last decade, produced a late manner entirely unlike Beethoven's. The careful conclusion is that deafness was the condition under which the late works were written, shaping the life around them, while their techniques are documented in the sketchbooks as deliberate construction.

### Q: When did Beethoven's style actually change?

The documentary answer is that it changed twice. The first change is the famous one: around 1802 his pupil Carl Czerny reported him saying that he was dissatisfied with his work so far and intended to take a new path, and the works confirm the report, with the Eroica of 1803 enlarging the symphony's scale and rhetoric beyond anything by Haydn or Mozart. The second change begins around 1816, as the output slows and turns inward toward the Hammerklavier Sonata of 1818, the Missa Solemnis, the Ninth Symphony and the last quartets. Wilhelm von Lenz formalised the pattern in 1852 as three styles, youth, hero and sage, and the scheme's neatness should be treated with suspicion, since it maps perfectly onto the life story the nineteenth century wanted. The dates of the changes themselves, however, are fixed by the works and are not in dispute.

### Q: How much of Beethoven's personality record is reliable?

The record divides sharply by source. His own letters, more than fifteen hundred of them, his private journal of 1812 to 1818, and the Heiligenstadt Testament are authentic documents and support a detailed portrait: funny, shrewd, obsessive about money and health, generous and litigious by turns. The testimony of Wegeler and Ries, published in 1838 by men who had known him, is broadly reliable. What cannot be relied on is the layer descending from Anton Schindler, his secretary and first influential biographer, who forged roughly 150 entries in the conversation books, as the Berlin critical edition established in the 1970s, and whose biography supplies most of the famous sayings, including the claim that the Fifth Symphony's opening represents fate knocking at the door. The broad outline of the personality is sound. The quotable one-liners are the least reliable part of it.

### Q: What sources about Beethoven can be trusted?

Trust follows provenance, and a workable hierarchy exists. At the top stand documents in his own hand or generated by business: the autograph scores and sketchbooks, the letters, the journal, the Heiligenstadt Testament, contracts and publishers' records. Next come contemporary reports by named witnesses whose interests are known and who had nothing to sell: Ferdinand Ries among the pupils, Fanny Giannatasio's diary of 1816, the genuine conversation book entries in the visitors' own hands, and the letters written to him, edited in modern times by Theodore Albrecht, which he could not retrospectively compose. Posthumous memoirs stand lower, graded by their distance from the events and their authors' stakes. Schindler stands in a category of his own: present at the scene, demonstrably a forger within it, and usable only where a better source independently confirms him.

### Q: Are the late quartets considered successful by scholars?

The scholarly mainstream treats them as the summit of the quartet literature, and the position is not mere piety. Joseph Kerman's study The Beethoven Quartets, published in 1967 and still a standard point of reference, reads the five late quartets as the centre of the whole repertoire, the works against which the genre's possibilities are measured. The dissent that survives is about meaning rather than standing: Theodor Adorno heard fracture and refusal in the late style where others hear transcendence, and the first audiences of 1826, who encored two movements of Op. 130 and failed to follow the Grosse Fuge, remind every reader that the verdict was earned over time rather than delivered at birth. Between Kerman's centrality and Adorno's fracture there is a genuine critical argument, conducted, unusually, over works neither side proposes to drop.

### Q: Why do people call Beethoven's music bombastic?

Three causes combine. Some passages earn the charge on their own terms: climaxes built from repetition and volume, codas that insist past the point of arrival, effects that work by oratory rather than argument, and the earliest critics already complained, the Eroica's first reviewer in 1805 begging for cuts. A second cause is selection: the works orchestras programme most are the heroic symphonies, so the loudest fraction of the output stands for the whole in the public ear, while the compressed Op. 95 quartet and the murmuring cello sonatas of Op. 102 stay in the inner repertoire. The third cause is the narrative itself, which teaches listeners to hear insistence as struggle, protecting passages that in another composer would be marked down as bluster. John Ruskin's famous confession that the music sounded like a bag of nails being upset is the charge at its least thoughtful and most durable.

### Q: How often is Beethoven programmed compared with others?

By every repertoire count available for modern orchestral life, he stands at or near the top. The repertoire reports published by the League of American Orchestras have repeatedly placed him first or among the first two or three most performed composers in the United States, and European seasons show the same pattern, with his nine symphonies forming the core cycle against which orchestras define themselves. The historian William Weber's counts of earlier programmes show the dominance is not a modern invention: the share of works by dead composers in Europe's capitals rose from roughly a quarter in the 1780s to roughly three quarters by the 1870s, with Beethoven first among the enthroned. Anniversary years intensify the pattern measurably, the bicentenary of his birth in 1970 filling seasons with complete cycles across several continents at once.

### Q: Which composers are crowded out of concert programmes?

The clearest casualties are his own contemporaries and the generation after. Louis Spohr, among the most performed composers in Europe in the 1830s and 1840s, has withdrawn to the specialist margin; Ferdinand Ries, Beethoven's pupil, wrote eight symphonies his century played and the next one mislaid; George Onslow's orchestral works commanded respect in France and Germany and survive as a connoisseur's cause. The institutional exclusions run deeper: Louise Farrenc's three symphonies were performed in Paris in her lifetime and then dropped by the canon-forming machinery for over a century, Fanny Hensel's large works stayed inside her family's Berlin Sunday concerts, and Emilie Mayer's eight symphonies were shelved by the century that had applauded them. The largest party has no names: the living composers of every generation whose premieres, and still more whose second performances, lost their slots to another canonical cycle.

### Q: Is canon dominance a composer's own responsibility?

No, and the distinction matters for where reform could ever come from. Beethoven managed his own career with exceptional energy, negotiating with publishers in three countries, calibrating dedications to patrons, and engineering his standing as deliberately as any musician of his age. That activity explains a successful lifetime. It cannot explain two centuries of subscription seasons, because dead men do not programme orchestras. The concentration is renewed annually by living decision-makers: intendants protecting subscription income, education systems teaching what their teachers were taught, examination syllabuses built from the same symphonies used in orchestral auditions, and record catalogues whose profits concentrate at the centre. Each decision is locally rational and their sum is a dominance nobody voted for. Responsibility follows power, and the power to programme otherwise sits with institutions, not with a tomb in Vienna.

### Q: Does knowing a composer's life change how we hear the music?

It changes the hearing without changing a single note, and both halves of that sentence have serious advocates. The formalist discipline descending from Eduard Hanslick's 1854 treatise holds that the content of the Eroica is its tonal argument, and that biographical knowledge adds sentiment rather than understanding, excusing or exalting works by their origins instead of hearing them. Nicholas Cook's work on reception answers that no listener hears unhistorically: programme notes, teachers, editions and ceremonies mediate every encounter, so the biography, including its forged layers, is causally part of what the music does in the world. The practical evidence sides with Cook about attention and with Hanslick about value. Knowing the deafness story demonstrably changes what listeners notice and forgive. Whether it should is a question each listener answers by deciding what kind of attention the music deserves.

### Q: Is Beethoven greater than Bach?

On the criteria this debate can state, the answer divides. Bach wins on consistency, the floor of his routine output standing where other composers peak, and on systematic ambition: the Well-Tempered Clavier, the Goldberg Variations and the Art of Fugue build complete worlds out of single principles. His expressive range across some two hundred surviving cantatas and the two Passions covers territory Beethoven barely entered. Beethoven wins on historical consequence inside the culture of the symphony, where his effect on Schubert, Berlioz, Liszt, Wagner, Brahms and Mahler is documented in their works and words, while Bach's consequence, delayed until the revival of 1829, ran deeper into compositional technique itself, the grammar every composer since has studied. A single verdict requires declaring which criterion outranks the others, and that declaration, not the evidence, is what produces the winner.

### Q: What criteria should decide the greatest composer question?

Criteria that are declared in advance, applied to whole outputs, and held constant across candidates: that discipline alone separates measurement from loyalty. The three used in this article are structural ambition, meaning how much weight a work asks its inherited forms to carry; expressive range, meaning the breadth of human states the output renders convincingly; and historical consequence, meaning what later composers and institutions demonstrably did differently because the work existed. Scott Burnham's research carries a warning for any such exercise: the default criteria are themselves inherited from the nineteenth century's Beethoven criticism, so a measurement that feels neutral may simply be grading every candidate in the home subject of one of them. The remedy is not to abandon criteria but to declare them, test the ranking's sensitivity to their weighting, and report the profile rather than the podium.

### Q: How do I check whether a Beethoven anecdote is genuine?

Four questions dispose of most cases. First, where does the story appear in print for the first time, and how long after the event: an anecdote surfacing decades later starts with a burden. Second, whose testimony carries it: if the chain runs through Anton Schindler alone, treat the story as his composition, since his forgeries in the conversation books are established fact. Third, does an independent source corroborate it: a letter, a business document, or a witness with no stake, such as Ferdinand Ries writing in 1838, before Schindler's biography existed. Fourth, does the story conveniently pronounce the interpretation posterity wanted: sayings in which the composer explains his own symbolism are the most suspect class of all. The fate knocking at the door account of the Fifth Symphony fails the second and fourth tests, which is why careful writers attribute it to Schindler rather than to Beethoven.

### Q: How do I evaluate competing readings of a late quartet?

Begin by separating the observations from the verdicts, because the rival readings of the late quartets agree on nearly every observable fact: the discontinuities, the extreme registers, the archaic counterpoint, the spans of stillness. They differ on the criteria by which those features count as success. Ask each reading what it predicts you will hear on a second and third encounter, and test the predictions in performance, since readings that survive repeated listening earn their standing differently from readings that merely flatter a theory. Joseph Kerman's The Beethoven Quartets is the model of the method, arguing from the notes outward and treating the works' strangeness as a problem to be accounted for rather than a halo to be polished. A reading that cannot state what evidence would weaken it is not a reading but a loyalty, on whichever side it stands.

### Q: Which works make the strongest case for Beethoven?

Each criterion names its own exhibits. For structural ambition, the Eroica, whose first movement doubles the symphony's previous scale and whose finale rebuilds a twice-used dance theme into a destination, and the Diabelli Variations, thirty-three transformations of a waltz their composer thought little of. For expressive range at its deepest, the String Quartet in C sharp minor, Op. 131, seven movements played without a break, and the Piano Sonata Op. 110, whose songful recitative movement is as exposed as anything he wrote. For historical consequence, the Ninth Symphony, which every successor from Wagner to Mahler had to answer. The Missa Solemnis belongs on any such list as the largest single act of ambition, five years in the making. A case built on these works does not need the heroic legend, which is precisely the point.

### Q: Where should a skeptical listener start with Beethoven?

Start where the legend is thinnest and the workmanship is densest. The String Quartet in F minor, Op. 95, eleven minutes of compression and refusal from the supposed heroic period, shows a composer declining every expected gesture. The Piano Sonata Op. 110 offers the intimate register, melody, wit and a slow movement of naked song. The Eighth Symphony is the comedian's exhibit, a deliberate parody of his own heroic manner written a decade after he invented it. The two Cello Sonatas of Op. 102 conduct their arguments in a murmur, and the last quartet, Op. 135, turns a household joke into philosophy. A listener who begins with these works meets the documented musician rather than the titan, and can approach the Eroica and the Ninth afterwards with the mythology already disarmed.

### Q: What would change the verdict on Beethoven?

Three findings would do it, and the claim's health is shown by the fact that all three are testable. The verdict would change if the audit of the 1803 break showed the heroic toolkit already complete in Haydn's late works, reducing the Eroica to an enlargement rather than an invention; the scores and sketches make that test performable, and it has so far found enlargement and invention together. It would change if the late works failed under legend-free conditions, repeatedly losing listeners they were supposed to hold; two centuries of professional use have returned the opposite result. And it would change if the criteria themselves were legitimately reweighted toward opera, song and sacred music, the genres where Mozart and Bach press their strongest claims. A greatness that can name its defeat conditions is a claim. One that cannot is a creed, and creeds are what this debate began by removing.
