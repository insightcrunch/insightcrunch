## Commodity Title and Nutrition Title Compared

The commodity title and the nutrition title compared side by side present the central puzzle of the farm bill, because the two halves share one statute, one pair of authorizing committees, and one vote on the floor of each chamber while serving different populations through different mechanisms. Students encountering the statute in a classroom, staffers drafting amendments during a reauthorization, journalists explaining a failed vote to readers, and advocates preparing testimony for a hearing all confront the same question in different forms. Each group asks why programs that support farm income sit inside the same law as programs that support household food budgets, and each group inherits the harder question of whether the two halves belong together at all. The phrasing changes with the audience. A student asks how the pairing began and whether the pairing followed any logic at the time of origin. A staffer asks whether the pairing can survive a difficult count of votes in a chamber where neither side commands a majority alone. A journalist asks who gains and who loses if the pairing breaks apart. An advocate asks which half carries the other through the legislative process and which half absorbs the greater risk in a separation. Underneath the varied phrasing sits one comparison, and the comparison returns in every reauthorization cycle because the underlying tension receives a temporary settlement in each enactment rather than a permanent resolution.

The recurrence follows from the structure of the vehicle itself. A farm bill authorizes its major programs for a limited period, roughly five years under the pattern established across the decades before the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, and expiration forces Congress to revisit the entire package on a recurring schedule. Each revisit reopens every settled bargain inside the package, including the bargain between commodity support and nutrition assistance. Budget baselines drawn for each new cycle also restate the relative size of the two halves under fresh economic conditions, and a restated share invites a fresh argument. The question acquired an empirical edge in the 2013 cycle, when the House of Representatives passed a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill as separate measures and neither separated bill became law, while the recombined conference report of January 2014 passed both chambers and became the Agricultural Act of 2014. That sequence converted an abstract design question into a tested proposition, and the test result entered every later discussion of separation as evidence that advocates on both sides had to answer.

### What the Commodity Side Contains

The commodity side of the farm bill consists of the commodity titles proper, which carry price and revenue support for producers of covered farm commodities, together with the crop insurance title, which carries federally subsidized insurance against yield and revenue losses. Price support operates through statutory reference prices. When market prices fall below a reference price fixed in the statute, payments to enrolled producers rise automatically under the formula, and when market prices run above the reference price, payments shrink toward zero without any further action by Congress. Revenue support follows a parallel logic keyed to revenue rather than price alone, so a shortfall in the combination of price and yield can trigger assistance even in a year when price in isolation appears adequate. Marketing loans give producers a further floor by allowing a commodity to serve as collateral for a loan at a statutory rate, with repayment terms that adjust when market prices fall below the loan rate, so the mechanism cushions a price collapse at the moment a harvest reaches the market. Disaster assistance addresses losses that fall outside the price and revenue formulas, covering events such as livestock losses and orchard damage under standing authorities that the farm bill renews. Each element shares one characteristic. Eligibility and payment size turn on production, acreage, price, and yield, variables rooted in the farm economy rather than in household need.

Crop insurance occupies a separate title and a separate administrative channel, and the separation inside the commodity side matters for the larger comparison. Under the crop insurance title as it stood in the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, the federal government subsidized the premiums producers paid for insurance policies and shared in the risk those policies carried, so a producer paid only part of the actuarial cost of coverage. Insured values rose and fell with the value of the crop, which meant federal outlays on the insurance title moved with commodity prices even in years when no indemnity pattern changed. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline, a projection for extension of the 2014 act across fiscal 2019 to 2028, placed crop insurance outlays at 78.0 billion dollars, about 9 percent of the projected total, and commodity program outlays at 61.1 billion dollars, about 7 percent of the projected total, against projected total outlays of 867 billion dollars across the same period. Together, the two commodity components accounted for roughly one dollar in six of projected outlays in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, a substantial commitment concentrated on a comparatively narrow population of producers and landowners.

### What the Nutrition Side Contains

The nutrition title is dominated by a single program to a degree that has no parallel on the commodity side. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, known as food stamps before the renaming that took effect on October 1, 2008, accounted for the overwhelming share of nutrition title spending, and the title functioned in budget terms as a vehicle built around that one program. The program provided food assistance to eligible low income households through benefits administered by the Food and Nutrition Service within the Department of Agriculture, and participation expanded or contracted with economic conditions and household circumstances rather than with any change in the statute. Around that center, the nutrition title carried a set of far smaller programs with distinct delivery channels. The Emergency Food Assistance Program moved food through a network of food banks and local distribution organizations. The Commodity Supplemental Food Program supplied food packages to defined categories of recipients. The Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations delivered food assistance within tribal communities. The Senior Farmers Market Nutrition Program connected older recipients with fresh produce sold at farmers markets. None of those programs approached the central program in scale, and each survived inside the title, sharing the title authorization and the reauthorization schedule.

The scale difference between the two sides was stark in the same projection that measured the commodity components. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline, covering fiscal 2019 to 2028 as a projection for extension of the 2014 act, placed nutrition title outlays at 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of projected total outlays of 867 billion dollars, with conservation at 59.7 billion dollars, about 7 percent, and the remaining eight titles together at about 1 percent. A reader of that baseline could describe the farm bill accurately as a nutrition law with agricultural titles attached, and a reader of the legislative history could describe the same statute with equal accuracy as an agricultural law that had carried food assistance since 1973. Both descriptions captured part of the record, and the friction between the two descriptions powered the separation debate. The nutrition title also held a distinctive budget character. The program at its center was an appropriated entitlement, authorized by the farm bill and funded through annual appropriations, and mandatory in character, so benefit obligations followed eligibility under the statute rather than a fixed pot of discretionary money divided among applicants.

### Jurisdiction Came Before Combination

The pairing began with a jurisdictional decision that preceded any combined farm bill by nine years. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, made food stamps a permanent program, placed administration in the Department of Agriculture, and lodged legislative jurisdiction in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. That assignment carried consequences that unfolded across the following decades. Committee members and committee staff developed working knowledge of food assistance inside committees organized around agricultural constituencies, and the program acquired the agriculture committees as its institutional home before any farm bill contained a nutrition title. A full account of [the Food Stamp Act of 1964 and the origins of SNAP](/2019/09/01/food-stamp-act-1964-snap-history/) shows a program built inside the agricultural committee structure from the first day of permanent authorization, rather than a program transferred into that structure at a later date. Jurisdiction therefore explains the location of nutrition assistance in the statute book without any reference to vote trading. The committees that wrote farm legislation already held the pen on food stamps, and the Department of Agriculture already ran the program, so the administrative and legislative geography of food assistance was agricultural geography long before the two subjects shared a bill.

Sequence matters because sequence separates inheritance from design. An arrangement inherited without deliberation might rest on nothing stronger than habit, and habit supplies a weak defense when a challenger proposes a tidier structure. The record of the 1964 act showed something different from drift. Congress in 1964 chose to make a pilot era program permanent, chose the Department of Agriculture as the administering department, and chose the agriculture committees as the committees of jurisdiction, and each choice was a legislative act open to amendment at the time. Later Congresses retained the assignment through successive reauthorizations, and retention across decades of opportunities to move the program amounted to a repeated decision rather than a single forgotten one. The jurisdictional foundation was thus deliberate at the point of origin and ratified by repetition, and any account of the combined farm bill has to grant that foundation before reaching the separate question of why food stamp reauthorization entered the farm bill vehicle itself in 1973.

### A Deliberate Combination, Not an Accident

The combination of the two subjects in one bill was a distinct and later step. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, was the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program, according to the Congressional Research Service, and the incorporation reflected a deliberate committee agreement. From 1973 onward, the farm bill served as the recurring vehicle for both commodity support and food assistance, and the pattern held through the recombined Agricultural Act of 2014 and the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. The broader record traced in [the history and structure of the farm bill](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/) supports the same reading, because a vehicle renewed on a recurring cycle across nearly five decades, with the combined form restored in 2014 after a separation attempt inside the House, bears the marks of maintenance by choice rather than survival by neglect. Legislators who disliked the combination had repeated occasions to end the arrangement, and the arrangement persisted through changes in party control of both chambers and of the presidency.

The political logic that sustained the agreement rested on an exchange of votes often described as a logroll. Urban members supplied votes for farm support, and rural members supplied votes for nutrition assistance, and neither constituency commanded a floor majority alone in either chamber. A farm only coalition faced defeat or a narrow and unstable margin because production agriculture was concentrated in a minority of congressional districts, and a nutrition only coalition faced a parallel problem because the case for food assistance spending competed against many other claims on the floor. Combination pooled the two minorities into a majority. Each side accepted provisions the side would not have chosen in isolation as the price of passage for provisions the side regarded as essential, and the exchange repeated in every cycle because the underlying arithmetic changed little from one reauthorization to the next. The logroll was therefore not a decorative feature of the arrangement. The logroll was the operating mechanism of the arrangement, and evaluations of the combined farm bill that ignore the vote exchange evaluate a statute detached from the condition that made enactment possible.

### Four Differences That Organize the Dispute

Money supplies the first difference, and money also supplies the most misread evidence in the dispute. A second presentation of scale, distinct from the April 2018 baseline, came with enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, when the Congressional Budget Office projected 428 billion dollars in outlays over fiscal 2019 to 2023, with nutrition at 76 percent, or 326 billion dollars, and the remaining 24 percent, or 102 billion dollars, falling mostly to crop insurance, commodity programs, and conservation, and the act was scored budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 against extension of prior law. The two presentations measured different periods and answered different questions, and neither licensed the claim that one side of the bill permanently dwarfed the other. Shares moved with conditions. A baseline drawn beside a recession showed a larger nutrition share, and a baseline drawn beside a price collapse showed larger commodity and insurance shares, so the share in any single document was a snapshot of economic conditions at the moment of projection rather than a property of the legislative vehicle.

Constituency supplies the second difference. The commodity titles answered to producers, landowners, lenders, insurers, and the rural communities organized around farm production, a constituency defined by occupation and geography and represented with particular intensity in districts where agriculture drove the local economy. The nutrition title answered to recipient households spread across urban, suburban, and rural districts alike, together with the retailers, state agencies, and community organizations that carried benefits to the point of use, a constituency defined by income and household circumstance rather than by occupation. The two constituencies overlapped in rural counties where farm households and recipient households lived in the same communities, and the overlap complicated any clean geographic story about the bill. The governing fact remained the arithmetic recorded in the combination itself. Each constituency, acting alone, lacked a reliable floor majority, and each constituency gained enactment for its priorities through association with the other, so constituency in the farm bill was a question of coalition construction rather than a question of shared identity between the two groups.

Volatility supplies the third difference, and the contrast runs in opposite directions on the two sides of the bill. Commodity side spending was price driven. Payments under the commodity title rose automatically when market prices fell below statutory reference prices and shrank when prices were high, and the scale of that movement appeared in fiscal 1998 to 2001, when Congress added about 22.5 billion dollars in emergency market loss payments on top of about 20.7 billion dollars in scheduled contract payments during the price collapse that followed the 1996 act, according to the Congressional Research Service. Crop insurance outlays moved with prices as well, because insured values rose and fell with the value of the crop. Nutrition side spending was countercyclical and participation driven. Average monthly participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program stood at 26.3 million in fiscal 2007, peaked at 47.6 million in fiscal 2013 in the aftermath of the recession of 2007 to 2009, and fell to about 40 million by fiscal 2018, according to figures from the Department of Agriculture and the Congressional Budget Office, and program spending peaked in fiscal 2013 at about 80 billion dollars. Participation moved without any change in the statute, driven by joblessness and household income, so one side of the bill expanded when farm prices collapsed and the other side expanded when the wider economy contracted, and the two expansions answered to different triggers on different clocks.

Jurisdiction supplies the fourth difference, and jurisdiction frames the institutional argument that outlasts any single budget cycle. Both title groups were written by the House and Senate Agriculture Committees, while administration sat elsewhere inside one department, with the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program administered by the Food and Nutrition Service, commodity programs administered by the Farm Service Agency, and crop insurance administered by the Risk Management Agency. The defense of the arrangement held that nutrition benefited from the durable bipartisan coalition of the farm bill and from the roughly five year reauthorization cycle, and that the agriculture committees developed food program expertise across decades of continuous responsibility. The criticism of the arrangement held that members chosen for agricultural constituencies wrote the largest food assistance program in the federal government, and that oversight of nutrition competed with commodity politics inside a single committee. Both positions described the same institutional facts and drew opposite conclusions from the shared description, which is why the jurisdictional difference resisted settlement by evidence alone and returned with the money, constituency, and volatility differences at the opening of every reauthorization debate.

## Money, Spending, and the Meaning of a Dollar

The money axis compares the commodity title and the nutrition title by following projected outlays through a single legislative vehicle that carries both subjects at once. A farm bill total compresses different kinds of spending, driven by different forces, paid to different recipients, under different rules, into one number. The nutrition title sends resources toward household food purchases. The commodity title sends resources toward producers when prices or revenues fall short of statutory benchmarks. Crop insurance sends resources through a risk structure built on premiums, subsidies, and indemnities. Each flow answers a different policy problem and reacts to a different trigger. The money axis therefore requires two disciplines. The first discipline is presentational: every total belongs beside its source, its period, and the law or extension that the source scored. The second discipline is mechanical: every category deserves description at the level of payment, recipient, and trigger, because a dollar that reaches a household as a food benefit and a dollar that reaches a producer as a price linked payment perform different work even though both dollars pass through the same statute.

The first verified presentation is the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for extension of the 2014 act across fiscal 2019 to 2028, a projection of spending under an assumed continuation of prior law rather than an enacted statute. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, total projected outlays came to 867 billion dollars. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, the nutrition title accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of that total. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, crop insurance accounted for 78.0 billion dollars, about 9 percent of that total. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, commodity programs accounted for 61.1 billion dollars, about 7 percent of that total. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, conservation accounted for 59.7 billion dollars, about 7 percent of that total. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, the other eight titles together accounted for about 1 percent of that total. Read on its own terms, the baseline answers a precise question about distribution among titles under an extension of the 2014 act, and any use of the baseline for a different question changes the meaning of the answer.

The second verified presentation belongs to a different source moment and a different legislative object. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 measured the act that became law, centered on fiscal 2019 to 2023. In the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, total projected outlays came to 428 billion dollars. In the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, nutrition accounted for 76 percent of the total, or 326 billion dollars. In the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, the remaining 24 percent, or 102 billion dollars, flowed mostly to crop insurance, commodity programs, and conservation. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act also scored the act as budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 against extension of prior law, a judgment that compares the enacted statute with the baseline path rather than a judgment that the act spent nothing. The April 2018 baseline scored an extension that Congress did not enact in that exact form, while the enactment projection scored the statute that Congress passed in December 2018. Merging the totals or quoting a number from one presentation beside a number from the other produces a hybrid that no analyst at the Congressional Budget Office ever published.

### Why do two official money totals describe the same vehicle so differently?

The two totals answer different questions over different periods. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline projected an extension of prior law across fiscal 2019 to 2028, and the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act scored enacted law across fiscal 2019 to 2023. Different windows produce different totals.

The period difference alone explains a large part of the numerical distance between 867 billion dollars and 428 billion dollars. A projection that accumulates outlays across fiscal 2019 to 2028 collects twice the calendar span of a projection that accumulates outlays across fiscal 2019 to 2023, and programs with mandatory character generate outlays in every year of either window. The measuring instrument changed. One instrument assumed continuation of the 2014 act, and the other instrument measured the legal text of the 2018 act after passage. Baseline construction adds another layer of difference, because a baseline encodes economic assumptions, participation expectations, price expectations, and program rules at the moment of construction, and the April 2018 baseline froze those assumptions at April 2018. The baseline supplies the path against which change gets measured, and the enactment projection supplies the estimated cost of the change and of the law that resulted. Each document deserves belief within its stated purpose, and neither document licenses a reader to strip a figure away from that purpose and carry the figure into a debate about a different period.

The near similarity of the two headline nutrition figures tempts a shortcut, and the shortcut deserves resistance. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, nutrition held about 77 percent, and in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, nutrition held 76 percent. The numerator for nutrition in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 was 663.8 billion dollars, while the numerator for nutrition in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023 was 326 billion dollars. The denominator in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 was 867 billion dollars, while the denominator in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023 was 428 billion dollars. Two ratios can sit close together while the underlying totals stand far apart.

A share quoted without source and year, unlike the nutrition share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 or the nutrition share in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, carries no stable meaning, because the figure depends on choices that the quotation hides. Source determines whether the number describes a baseline, an enactment score, an appropriations outcome, or an administrative tally, and each source answers to different rules. Period determines which economic conditions, which participation levels, and which price environments enter the calculation. The discipline demanded by the fact record is unglamorous and absolute. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, nutrition accounted for about 77 percent, and in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, nutrition accounted for 76 percent, and any shortened version of either statement misreports both presentations.

Rhetorical use of the figure runs in two directions, and both directions draw strength from detachment. In one direction, floor opponents of the combined vehicle and advocates of a separate nutrition statute pointed to the nutrition share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 and in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023 and described the farm bill as a welfare bill with farm programs attached. That description converts a spending distribution into an identity claim. The answer, grounded in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 and in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, is that a distribution of projected outlays describes where projected dollars go under stated assumptions, and the description carries no judgment about the legitimacy, design, or legislative home of any title.

In the opposite direction, defenders of a farm only vehicle and opponents of the combined structure pointed to the commodity share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 and to the crop insurance share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 and described nutrition as an intruder that dwarfed the agricultural core of the bill. That description also converts a spending distribution into an identity claim, with the polarity reversed. The commodity share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 was about 7 percent, and the crop insurance share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 was about 9 percent, and neither figure in that baseline and period measures acres covered, producers enrolled, counties affected, or statutory pages devoted to farm programs. Once source and period return to the sentence, the label loses force, because the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 reports projected outlays under an extension scenario, and a projection of outlays cannot expel a title that statute placed inside the vehicle.

Mechanism supplies the corrective that a detached figure withholds. Nutrition dollars flow as household benefits through state administration, with eligibility rules and benefit formulas translating federal funds into purchasing capacity for food at the household level. The recipient unit is the household, and the trigger is need measured under program rules, expressed through income, household composition, and related eligibility factors as the statute and administration define them. Commodity dollars flow through a different channel entirely. The recipient unit is the producer or the farm operation tied to eligible production history, the trigger is price or revenue measured against statutory reference levels, and the payment calculation attaches to base acres rather than to a household budget. A season of high market prices can shrink commodity outlays without any amendment to the statute, and a season of collapsing prices can expand commodity outlays through the same unchanged text. The companion discussion of [farm subsidy policy and its effects](/2021/01/15/farm-subsidy-policy-impact/) traces that payment logic through program history, and the money axis depends on the same distinction between a benefit keyed to household need and a payment keyed to market shortfall.

Insurance dollars follow a third channel that resembles neither household benefits nor direct commodity payments. Under the crop insurance title, federal dollars flow as premium subsidies that reduce the cost a producer pays for coverage and as indemnities that compensate insured losses under policy terms. Insured values move with the value of the crop, so insurance outlays respond to price movements through a channel distinct from the reference price channel of the commodity title. Aggregation hides that distinction. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, crop insurance accounted for about 9 percent, and in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, commodity programs accounted for about 7 percent, and those two categories in that baseline and period sit close together in projected dollars while operating through sharply different mechanisms, because premium subsidy economics and reference price economics answer different questions about risk, income, and market signals.

Volatility deepens the mechanical difference and explains why any single window produces a partial portrait. On the commodity side, payments rise automatically when market prices fall below statutory reference prices and shrink when prices run high, a design that concentrates outlays in adverse market years. During fiscal 1998 to 2001, across the price collapse that followed the 1996 act, Congress added about 22.5 billion dollars in emergency market loss payments on top of about 20.7 billion dollars in scheduled contract payments, according to the Congressional Research Service record verified for the series. On the nutrition side, movement follows participation and economic conditions rather than commodity prices. Average monthly participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program stood at 26.3 million in fiscal 2007, peaked at 47.6 million in fiscal 2013 in the aftermath of the recession of 2007 to 2009, and fell to about 40 million by fiscal 2018, according to Department of Agriculture and Congressional Budget Office figures. Spending on the program peaked in fiscal 2013 at about 80 billion dollars, according to the same Department of Agriculture and Congressional Budget Office record. Each share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, and each share in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, is a snapshot of conditions under a named source and period rather than a permanent property of the vehicle.

Dollar share also fails as a measure of importance beyond spending, and recognition of that limit belongs at the center of the money axis, because each share in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 measures a flow of projected dollars and nothing else. A title can govern eligibility for millions of acres, shape planting decisions across entire regions, set conservation obligations on enrolled land, or define the terms under which credit reaches rural borrowers, while consuming a modest portion of projected outlays in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028. Regulatory importance travels through rules, prohibitions, conditions, and authorities, and none of those instruments register in an outlay table except through indirect administrative cost. Acres affected travel through enrollment, eligibility, and land characteristics, and an acre under a conservation contract or a crop insurance policy carries policy significance regardless of the payment total assigned to that acre in a given year. Transfer volume and legislative complexity move independently.

Budget neutrality adds a final qualification that detached rhetoric routinely omits. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act scored the act as budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 against extension of prior law, and neutrality of that kind describes a comparison between two projected paths across the same decade. Neutrality does not describe the level of spending under either path, because a statute can spend hundreds of billions of dollars and remain neutral against a baseline that projected equivalent spending, and the score measures difference rather than magnitude. One misreading treats neutrality as evidence that the 2018 act imposed no fiscal cost, even though the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023 placed projected outlays at 428 billion dollars for that five fiscal year span alone. The opposite misreading treats the 428 billion dollars in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023 as evidence of expansion, even though the neutrality judgment for fiscal 2019 to 2028 recorded no net change against the extension path.

The money axis therefore establishes scale, mechanism, and volatility, and the axis withholds any automatic verdict on separation. Nutrition dominated projected outlays in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, and nutrition dominated projected outlays in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023, and dominance in projected outlays in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028 or in the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act for fiscal 2019 to 2023 describes fiscal weight rather than legislative fitness. The contribution of the money axis is narrower and more valuable than a verdict, because the axis forces every participant to name a source, name a period, describe a mechanism, and acknowledge volatility before drawing a conclusion, and arguments that depend on a detached figure, a merged total, or a confusion between magnitude and difference fail at the first axis.

## The Constituency Arithmetic Behind the Coalition

The constituency axis begins with a map rather than with a budget table. Nutrition benefits under the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program reached households in every congressional district in the United States, because eligibility followed household income and household circumstances rather than the location of a farm, a processing plant, or a particular crop. A district drawn around a dense city center contained participating households, and so did a district drawn around suburbs, small towns, and farmland. Participation was heaviest in urban and suburban districts, where population density placed large numbers of eligible households inside a single set of district lines, and where a single member of the House of Representatives could represent hundreds of thousands of residents among whom nutrition assistance formed part of the household economy. The benefit itself moved through ordinary retail channels in those districts, through grocery stores and other authorized retailers, so its presence in a district did not depend on any local production decision. That geographic spread gave the nutrition title a constituency that touched every seat in the House and every state in the Senate, even though the intensity of that presence varied sharply from place to place and from district to district.

Commodity and crop insurance benefits followed a different map, one drawn by production rather than by population. Payments under the commodity title and indemnities and premium support under the crop insurance title concentrated in a smaller number of rural districts where production agriculture ranked as a major employer and where farms, input suppliers, equipment dealers, grain handlers, lenders, and processors formed a connected local economy. In those districts, a change in a reference price, a payment limit, or an insurance rule registered as a change in the income of identifiable operations and in the business volume of the firms that served those operations. Outside those districts, the same provisions attracted far less direct attention, because fewer constituents received a commodity payment or held a crop insurance policy. The contrast was therefore not between a program with a constituency and a program without one. The contrast was between a benefit distributed across every district at varying intensity and a benefit concentrated in fewer districts at high intensity, and that contrast in distribution shaped every vote that followed on the combined vehicle.

### Why does neither constituency win on its own?

Neither side reaches a majority because the districts where commodity and insurance benefits concentrate are too few to pass a bill alone, and the districts where nutrition participation is heaviest, though far more numerous, do not by themselves supply enough votes in either chamber to enact a farm bill.

That arithmetic held in both chambers, though it operated through different structures. In the House, where seats follow population, the rural districts in which production agriculture dominated the local economy formed a clear minority of the 435 seats. Members from those districts could anchor a farm bill, supply its committee leadership, and write its commodity and insurance provisions with detailed knowledge of the programs involved, but the anchor did not amount to a floor majority. A bill carrying only commodity and insurance provisions needed votes from members whose districts contained few or no direct recipients of those provisions, and those members had to find a reason in the bill to support it. In the Senate, where each state held two seats regardless of population, agricultural states carried proportionally greater weight, and farm interests found a more favorable hearing than population alone would have predicted. Even in the Senate, however, the states in which production agriculture dominated formed a minority of the chamber, and a majority required support from senators representing states where nutrition participation, retail food sales, and urban and suburban constituencies carried greater weight than commodity payments.

The nutrition constituency faced the mirror image of the same problem. Its geographic reach was total, extending into every district and every state, but reach and intensity are different properties, and a benefit present in a district was not automatically the benefit that determined its member's vote on an agriculture bill. In many suburban districts, participation was substantial in absolute numbers yet formed a smaller share of the district's population and economy than commodity receipts formed in a production district. Members from such districts could support nutrition assistance as part of a larger measure without treating the nutrition title as the sole test of the vote. Assembling a floor majority for nutrition provisions standing alone, severed from the rest of the farm bill and from the committee structure that carried them, required holding together members whose districts differed widely in participation rates, in retail structure, and in the place of agriculture in local employment. The fact sheet record for the combined vehicle states the underlying condition in plain terms: urban members supplied votes for farm support, rural members supplied votes for nutrition assistance, and neither constituency commanded a floor majority alone.

The exchange that answered the arithmetic has a conventional name in legislative practice, the logroll, and in the farm bill the logroll operated as a mechanism with identifiable parts rather than as a vague spirit of cooperation. The first part was jurisdictional. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, placed the permanent food stamp program under the Department of Agriculture and within the legislative jurisdiction of the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. Jurisdiction came before combination in a single bill, and it meant that the members who wrote commodity programs also held responsibility for the food program from the program's permanent beginning. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, then became the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program, and the Congressional Research Service characterized that step as a deliberate committee agreement rather than an accident of drafting. From 1973 forward, the two constituencies negotiated inside one committee room, under one pair of committees, over one legislative vehicle, and the structure itself forced the exchange into the open at every reauthorization.

Inside committee markup, the mechanism took a concrete form. Both titles moved together through the same markup, amendment by amendment, under the direction of a chair and a ranking member who had to report a bill capable of passing the full chamber. A member who sought a change in a commodity provision, a conservation practice, or an insurance rule negotiated in a room where nutrition provisions sat on the same table and where the votes required to report the bill included members whose first concern was the nutrition title. Conversely, a member who sought a change in nutrition eligibility, benefit calculation, or program administration negotiated in a room where farm district members held seats, seniority, and votes on the same reported bill. The markup instead priced disagreement inside a single transaction. Concessions on one title purchased movement on the other, objections on one title carried consequences for the other, and a coalition sufficient to report the bill from committee had to satisfy enough members from both constituencies to survive the floor vote that followed.

On the floor, the same mechanism widened from a committee coalition into a chamber coalition. Floor managers assembled support across the two constituencies by presenting the bill as a single measure whose parts depended on each other, so that a member voting for the bill could point to the title that mattered most in the district while accepting provisions directed elsewhere. Urban and suburban members supplied votes that farm district members could not supply for themselves, and farm district members supplied votes, committee expertise, and organizational support that nutrition provisions could not generate on their own inside the Agriculture Committees. The resulting majorities were typically bipartisan in composition, drawn from both parties and from regions with different economic profiles, and that bipartisan character was a product of the district arithmetic rather than a decoration added after the votes were counted. A bill that needed rural and urban votes in the same tally could not be written as a purely regional measure, and a bill that needed production districts and high participation districts in the same tally could not survive as a purely partisan measure in most cycles.

The Senate illustrated the geographic logic with particular clarity, because the Senate's structure rewarded breadth across states. A combined bill could draw support from senators representing major production states and from senators representing states where nutrition participation and food retailing carried the greater weight, and the two groups together produced margins that neither group produced separately. On June 10, 2013, the Senate passed the combined bill S. 954 by 66 to 27, a margin of 39 votes in a chamber where the farm state bloc alone fell well short of that total. In the 2018 cycle, after conference negotiations between the two chambers, the Senate approved the conference report in December 2018 by 87 to 13, a margin of 74 votes that represented one of the widest recorded for a farm bill conference report in the record covered by Article 261. When the titles moved together, Senate support extended across regions and across party lines to a degree that a single title measure did not reproduce, and the breadth of the producing states plus the breadth of the participating states together exceeded the breadth of either set standing alone.

The stability of the exchange rested on a reciprocal exposure that participants on both sides understood without any formal enforcement device. Each side's program functioned as the other side's hostage and as the other side's guarantee at the same time. If farm district members defected from nutrition provisions in numbers large enough to defeat or strip the nutrition title, the urban and suburban votes that carried commodity and insurance provisions lost their reason to stay in the coalition, and the farm titles faced the floor exposed to the minority arithmetic that had made the exchange necessary in the first place. If nutrition supporters defected from commodity and insurance provisions in numbers large enough to defeat them, the farm district members who supplied committee leadership, floor management, and votes for the nutrition title inside an agriculture vehicle lost their reason to carry a bill whose benefits flowed overwhelmingly outside their districts. Defection therefore carried a price denominated in the defector's own title, and that price distinguished the farm bill logroll from a casual alliance of convenience. The bargain policed itself through the structure of the vehicle, because an attack on one half of the bill was, in practical effect, an attack on the prospects of the other half.

The roughly five year reauthorization cycle renewed the bargain on a schedule that both constituencies could plan around. Farm bills expired, and expiration forced committees to write a successor measure, reopened every title to amendment, and returned both constituencies to the same negotiating table under deadline pressure. A bargain struck in one cycle did not bind a later Congress in its details, and each reauthorization renegotiated reference prices, insurance terms, nutrition rules, and scores of other provisions against changed economic conditions and changed chamber majorities. What the cycle preserved was not any particular set of terms but the occasion itself, the recurring moment at which each side needed the other's votes again. Participants who contemplated defection in one cycle faced the knowledge that the same coalition would be needed again within roughly five years, and that a reputation for breaking the exchange in one reauthorization would follow a member, a committee, or an organization into the next. The cycle thus converted a one time trade into a repeated relationship, and repeated relationships sustain cooperation through expectations about future rounds as well as through the terms of each round as it arrived.

Against the stabilizing logic stands an asymmetry that cut in the opposite direction, and an accurate account of the constituency axis has to hold both facts at once. Nutrition beneficiaries numbered in the tens of millions across the cycles covered in the fact sheet, with average monthly participation recorded at 26.3 million in fiscal 2007, at a peak of 47.6 million in fiscal 2013, and at about 40 million by fiscal 2018. Those beneficiaries were numerous, spread across every district, and individually connected to the program through a monthly benefit whose size depended on household circumstances. Farm beneficiaries were fewer in number, concentrated in the production districts described above, and connected to the commodity and insurance titles through the operations of identifiable farms and firms. Numerical weight therefore favored the nutrition constituency in any simple count of recipients, while concentration favored the farm constituency in any measure of how directly a title's provisions registered in a particular district's economy. The logroll persisted not because the two constituencies were symmetrical but because each possessed a resource the other lacked, geographic breadth on one side and geographic concentration joined to committee position on the other.

Organization followed the same asymmetry and gave it practical force. Farm beneficiaries acted through producer groups organized by commodity and by region, through general farm organizations with chapters in production areas, and through the associations that represented insurers, lenders, and other firms tied to production agriculture. Such organizations maintained staff, tracked committee action provision by provision, and communicated with members from production districts on a continuing basis across the years between farm bills as well as during a reauthorization. The nutrition side acted through anti-hunger organizations that represented the interests of participating households, through organizations of retailers authorized to accept benefits, and through state and local groups connected to program administration and outreach. Food retailers occupied a distinctive position in that structure, because grocers and other authorized stores had a direct commercial interest in benefit redemption in their stores, in urban neighborhoods, in suburbs, and in rural towns alike, which gave the nutrition constituency an organized business presence in districts where participating households themselves had no single representative organization. Describing that structure requires no loaded characterization of either side. One constituency was numerous and diffuse, with organization supplied substantially by advocacy groups and retailers, and the other was smaller and concentrated, with organization supplied substantially by producer groups built around the commodity economy of specific districts.

At the level of the individual member, the constituency axis resolved into a series of local calculations that aggregated into the national tallies. A member from a production district weighed commodity receipts, insurance participation, and the employment of firms tied to agriculture against the nutrition participation present in the same district, because even the most agricultural district contained households receiving nutrition benefits and retailers redeeming them. A member from an urban district weighed participation figures, retailer volume, and the work of anti-hunger organizations against the smaller farm presence inside the district lines, which in metropolitan districts could approach zero yet never removed the member's vote from the tally that farm titles needed. The combined vehicle gave each of those members a single vote that could be explained at home through the title with the greatest local weight, and separate vehicles would have forced each member to cast distinct votes in which the locally weaker title stood exposed without the protection of the locally stronger one. That exposure, repeated across hundreds of districts, is the constituency mechanism in its most concrete form, and it operated in committee rooms, in whip counts, and in conference negotiations alike through the reauthorizations completed by early 2021.

## The Volatility Axis: Commodity Title and Nutrition Title on Different Cycles

The volatility axis separates the commodity title and the nutrition title more cleanly than any difference in clientele or committee history. Both groups of programs spend more when conditions deteriorate and less when conditions improve, and in that limited sense both groups operate as countercyclical support. The resemblance ends at the variable that drives the movement. Commodity title costs respond to market prices for covered crops. Nutrition title costs respond to household participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. A price decline can expand commodity outlays during a period of strong employment and stable household income. A rise in unemployment can expand nutrition outlays during a period of high crop prices and minimal commodity payments. The two cycles overlap at times, and a severe general downturn can move both cycles in the same direction, but neither cycle controls the other and neither cycle provides a reliable forecast for the other.

That independence matters for interpretation of farm bill spending. A combined total for a single fiscal year blends two separate responses to two separate sets of conditions, and the blend conceals the reason spending changed. If market prices fell, the commodity share of the combined total can rise even though Congress made no decision to favor farm support over food assistance. If unemployment rose, the nutrition share can rise even though Congress made no decision to favor food assistance over farm support. The statute in each case supplies standing rules, and conditions supply the trigger. Understanding the farm bill therefore requires attention to the trigger in each title, to the lag between a change in conditions and a change in outlays, and to the way a snapshot taken beside one set of conditions can distort any comparison drawn across titles. The money axis in the wider comparison insists on a named source and a named period for every share for exactly that reason.

On the commodity side, the mechanism is written directly into payment formulas. Payments under the commodity title rise automatically when market prices fall below statutory reference prices, and payments shrink when market prices remain above those reference prices. No new appropriation decision and no new eligibility campaign produces the increase. The formula itself converts a lower price into a larger federal obligation, because the gap between the market return and the statutory floor widens as the market return declines. The reverse conversion operates with equal automaticity. A strong price season compresses the gap, payments contract, and federal outlays fall, even though the reference prices, the eligible commodities, and the enrollment of producers in the program have undergone no change at all. Price, rather than headcount, supplies the primary movement, and price can move sharply within a single crop year.

Crop insurance reinforces the price driven character of the farm side, although crop insurance sits in a separate title from the commodity title in the structure of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018. Insured values rise and fall with the value of the crop, so premium support and indemnity exposure carry a market component even in years when commodity payments remain contained. Yields and weather also affect insurance outcomes, so price does not explain every insurance fluctuation. The central point for the comparison holds nonetheless. Farm side outlays as a group carry a direct exposure to commodity markets that nutrition outlays do not carry, and that exposure can produce large swings without any parallel movement in household need for food assistance.

### What moves nutrition title costs when the statute does not change?

Nutrition title costs move with participation. When household incomes fall and unemployment rises, more households meet eligibility rules that Congress left unchanged, enrollment climbs, and benefit outlays climb with enrollment. When incomes recover, participation recedes and outlays recede, even though the statutory benefit formula and eligibility thresholds remain exactly as enacted.

Participation driven movement operates through household circumstances rather than through a market price formula. A household enters the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program after income, household composition, and other eligibility factors place the household within the rules Congress enacted. Job loss, reduced hours, reduced earnings, and the exhaustion of household savings can each move a household toward eligibility, and a large increase in unemployment moves many households in that direction at the same time. The aggregate result resembles an automatic stabilizer in function, because spending expands during a downturn without a new statute, but the channel differs from the commodity channel in an important respect. Commodity payments respond to a single observable market signal for a covered crop. Nutrition participation responds to millions of separate household determinations, each tied to income and circumstances at the household level, administered through state agencies under federal rules.

The household channel also produces a different relationship between need and enrollment. Eligibility alone does not generate a benefit payment. A household must apply, establish eligibility, and remain enrolled, and administrative practice, awareness of the program, and household decisions about seeking assistance all mediate the connection between economic distress and recorded participation. During a sharp downturn, participation can continue climbing after the initial shock has passed as eligible households enter in sequence rather than in a single wave. During a recovery, exit from the program follows reemployment, restored hours, and rising earnings, and those gains arrive unevenly across regions, industries, and household types. Participation therefore traces the lived recovery of household income rather than the formal dating of a recession, and the trace can extend well beyond the quarter in which aggregate output turned upward.

The commodity swing received a stark demonstration in fiscal 1998 to 2001, during the price collapse that followed the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996. The act of 1996 had moved farm support toward scheduled contract payments that were designed to decline over time and to remain detached from current market prices, on the expectation that market returns would sustain farm income without price contingent support. Market prices instead fell heavily, farm income came under severe pressure, and the scheduled structure proved unable to absorb the decline within its fixed amounts. Congress responded outside the scheduled structure. According to Congressional Research Service figures, Congress added about 22.5 billion dollars in emergency market loss payments on top of about 20.7 billion dollars in scheduled contract payments across fiscal 1998 to 2001. The combined scale of the response showed that a price collapse could more than double the federal commitment contemplated by the scheduled payments alone, and the need for repeated emergency action showed that the volatility had not disappeared merely because the statute had declined to price it.

The emergency episode also clarified the political form that commodity volatility takes when a statute excludes price response. Volatility does not vanish. Volatility reappears as pressure for ad hoc legislation, assembled under crisis conditions, debated as an exception, and enacted after producers have already absorbed a portion of the loss. Each emergency bill requires a fresh majority and a fresh negotiation over amounts and distribution. Producers face uncertainty not only about market prices but also about whether Congress will act and how any assistance will be allocated. Budget projections built on the scheduled payments understated the commitment that conditions could force, because the projections treated the fixed schedule as the full policy. The fiscal 1998 to 2001 experience therefore stands as evidence about design as well as about scale. A farm support system operating in volatile commodity markets will pay for price collapses either inside the statute or outside the statute.

The price triggered design that succeeded the emergency approach internalized the same swing inside the statute. Under that design, reference prices and payment formulas stand ready before any collapse occurs, and outlays expand by operation of the formula when market prices fall below the statutory triggers. The expansion requires no emergency bill, no crisis negotiation over whether assistance will arrive, and no retroactive reconstruction of eligibility after losses have accumulated. Budget baselines for such a design must present commodity costs as an estimate conditioned on price expectations, because actual outlays depend on prices that no forecaster can know at enactment. A baseline built on favorable price assumptions will understate later outlays if prices collapse, and a baseline built on depressed price assumptions will overstate later outlays if prices recover. The statute acknowledges that farm support costs will swing, assigns the swing to a defined trigger, and confines congressional discretion to the periodic task of setting reference prices and program terms during reauthorization rather than to repeated emergency improvisation between reauthorizations.

The nutrition swing unfolded on a larger population scale and on a slower timetable. According to figures from the Department of Agriculture and the Congressional Budget Office, Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program average monthly participation stood at 26.3 million in fiscal 2007, before the full force of the recession of 2007 to 2009 reached household budgets. Participation then climbed through the downturn and through the early recovery, and average monthly participation peaked at 47.6 million in fiscal 2013. Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program spending peaked in fiscal 2013 at about 80 billion dollars. Participation fell to about 40 million by fiscal 2018, as household incomes recovered and enrollment receded from the fiscal 2013 peak. No change in the basic statutory structure explained the rise from 26.3 million in fiscal 2007 to 47.6 million in fiscal 2013, and no change in the basic statutory structure explained the decline to about 40 million by fiscal 2018. Economic conditions moved the caseload, and the caseload moved the spending.

The timing of the nutrition peak deserves particular attention, because the timing exposes the lag built into participation driven spending. The recession of 2007 to 2009 had formally ended years before average monthly participation reached 47.6 million in fiscal 2013. A reader who expected nutrition spending to crest with the recession itself would misread the fiscal 2013 peak as evidence of a statutory expansion or of administrative failure, when the figures instead recorded the slow recovery of household income after a severe downturn. Eligibility follows household income, and household income recovery trails aggregate recovery. Workers return to employment in sequence, hours and earnings rebuild gradually, and households that entered the program during the downturn leave only after earnings rise enough to end eligibility. Households with the weakest attachment to employment recover last, and those households remain on the rolls longest. The participation peak in fiscal 2013 and the spending peak in fiscal 2013 at about 80 billion dollars therefore measured the depth and duration of household distress after the recession of 2007 to 2009, rather than the preferences of the Congress that happened to sit in fiscal 2013.

A comparison of the two lags sharpens the contrast between the title groups. Commodity payments respond to prices with a lag governed by marketing years, price measurement, and payment schedules, but the underlying signal is a market price that moves continuously and that can reverse direction within a single season. A price recovery can shut off the payment trigger with comparative speed. Nutrition participation responds to household income with a lag governed by job finding, earnings growth, application processing, and periodic eligibility redetermination, and the underlying signal moves at the pace of household recovery. A labor market recovery can take years to lift the lowest income households clear of eligibility, and participation declines across that entire interval rather than at a single trigger point. The commodity cycle can therefore produce a sharp spike and a sharp retreat, while the nutrition cycle tends to produce a long climb and a long descent. Budget observers who apply the rhythm of one title to the other will misjudge both.

The analytical payoff of the volatility axis concerns the spending share between the titles, and the payoff is cautionary. Because the drivers differ, the share moves with conditions rather than with congressional decisions. A Congress can leave every reference price, every eligibility rule, and every benefit formula untouched across a period, and the recorded share will nonetheless shift as prices and participation move in different directions or at different speeds. A rising nutrition share across such a period records a change in the relative weight of household distress and market conditions, not a legislative transfer of resources from farmers to food assistance recipients. A rising commodity share records the operation of price triggers during a collapse, not a legislative demotion of nutrition. Any argument that treats a change in share as a revealed congressional preference without first accounting for prices and participation commits an elementary error, because the share is an outcome of two formulas reacting to two environments, and the environments supply most of the movement between reauthorizations.

Baseline selection can manufacture apparently dramatic conclusions from that elementary error. A baseline drawn beside a recession inflates the nutrition share, because participation remains elevated from the downturn while commodity payments may remain modest if prices hold firm. A baseline drawn beside a price collapse inflates the farm share, because commodity payments and insurance outlays surge while participation may remain near ordinary levels if employment holds firm. Each baseline accurately reports the period the baseline covers, and each baseline misleads as a portrait of the permanent character of the farm bill. The defect lies not in the arithmetic but in the generalization. For that reason, the presentation of any share requires the discipline applied elsewhere in the comparison: the share must travel with the name of the source that produced the estimate and with the years the estimate covers, so that a reader can place the snapshot beside the conditions that produced the snapshot and can refuse to extend the snapshot beyond those conditions.

For legislators, the volatility axis reframes the question of control. Congress controls the triggers, the reference prices, the eligibility rules, the benefit formulas, and the duration of the authorization. Congress does not control the prices or the unemployment that activate those provisions between reauthorizations. A legislature dissatisfied with spending growth in a title must therefore distinguish growth produced by a trigger operating as designed from growth produced by a design defect, and the distinction requires examination of conditions during the growth period. Commodity spending that rose because prices fell below reference prices set by Congress itself represents the statute performing an insurance function. Nutrition spending that rose because participation climbed from 26.3 million in fiscal 2007 to 47.6 million in fiscal 2013 represents eligibility rules reaching households whose incomes had fallen into the eligible range. Evaluation begins only after the driver has been identified and measured against the conditions that activated the driver.

Combination places two differently timed cycles inside one authorization, so reauthorization debates inherit whichever crisis phase happens to coincide with the expiration calendar. Separation would allow each cycle to receive debate on a timetable suited to the relevant driver, with commodity terms examined against price conditions and nutrition terms examined against household conditions. Whether that buffering counts as stability or as evasion depends on the verdict reached on enactability and coalition evidence, the ground reserved for the decision sections of the comparison. The volatility evidence alone establishes the narrower point with full force. The commodity title and the nutrition title do not merely serve different constituencies. The two titles breathe on different cycles, answer to different signals, and swell and recede on different timetables, and any structure chosen for the farm bill must govern that difference rather than wish the difference away.

## Jurisdiction and the Committee Arrangement

The jurisdiction axis concerns authorship rather than spending, eligibility, or benefit design. Under the arrangement that governed farm bill writing through the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, the House Agriculture Committee and the Senate Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry Committee wrote both the commodity titles and the nutrition title. One authorizing committee in each chamber therefore drafted the provisions that set reference prices and payment conditions for supported commodities and the provisions that set eligibility and benefit rules for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. The arrangement assigns the largest food assistance program in the country to committees whose membership, staffing patterns, and electoral foundations rest heavily on agricultural constituencies. Committee rosters have included members from urban and suburban districts, but producers, commodity organizations, and rural governments form the constituency that follows committee work most closely. The defense and the criticism begin from the same structural fact and draw opposite conclusions about its consequences.

The sequence matters because jurisdiction came first and combination came second. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, made food stamps a permanent program, placed administration in the Department of Agriculture, and placed legislative jurisdiction in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. Food assistance thus had an institutional home, an administering department, and an authorizing committee before any decision linked its reauthorization to commodity programs. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, then became the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program. The combination of 1973 therefore joined two programs that already shared a department and a committee, in a deliberate committee agreement. Any proposal to undo the pairing would have to undo both layers, the committee assignment settled in 1964 and the combined vehicle adopted nine years later.

### Why does an agriculture committee write the nutrition title?

Jurisdiction followed the department. The Food Stamp Act of 1964 placed administration in the Department of Agriculture, and legislative jurisdiction followed administration to the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. When the programs combined in 1973, nutrition entered the farm bill under committees that already held authority over food stamps.

The deeper explanation involves administrative logic and legislative logic. Food stamps in the form authorized in 1964 grew out of surplus commodity distribution and related food assistance efforts that the Department of Agriculture had administered, so placement in that department followed an existing administrative line. Legislative jurisdiction in Congress generally tracks executive administration, because authorizing committees oversee the agencies that run the programs under their authority and draft the statutes those agencies implement. Assignment to the agriculture committees gave each chamber one committee responsible for both the statute and the department that carried out the statute. The 1973 combination reinforced that alignment, allowing one committee in each chamber to report a measure covering commodity support, conservation, credit, and food assistance, and allowing floor managers to assemble votes for the combined measure rather than for each part separately. Jurisdiction, administration, and coalition building thus pointed in the same direction.

Defenders of the arrangement, including committee leaders across successive reauthorization cycles, present the pairing as a source of durability for nutrition assistance rather than as a constraint on the program. In the defense account, the farm bill coalition gives nutrition a vehicle that commands votes from members who would not support a freestanding food assistance bill and from members who would not support a freestanding farm bill. Rural members supply votes for nutrition assistance and urban members supply votes for farm support, and neither constituency commands a floor majority alone. The combined vote totals in successful cycles form part of the defense evidence. The conference report for the Agricultural Act of 2014 passed the House by 251 to 166 and the Senate by 68 to 32 after recombination, and the conference report in the 2018 cycle passed the House by 369 to 47 and the Senate by 87 to 13. Defenders read margins of that size as evidence that the combined vehicle converts narrow, contested support for each half into broad support for the whole.

Defenders of the arrangement also emphasize regularity. In the defense account, a program housed in the farm bill gains a reauthorization rhythm tied to the expiration of farm programs, and that rhythm forces Congress to revisit nutrition law on a predictable cycle rather than leaving the program without updated authorization for long stretches. The farm bill has moved through reauthorization roughly every five years across the half century after 1973, and the defense holds that nutrition law benefits from attachment to a measure that farm constituencies, commodity organizations, conservation groups, rural lenders, and food assistance advocates all expect Congress to complete. A freestanding nutrition bill, in this account, would compete for floor time without a deadline created by expiring farm programs, and a missed cycle would leave eligibility and benefit rules frozen under older law while economic conditions changed. The defense treats schedule as a substantive protection, because authorization lapses and short extensions produce uncertainty for state agencies and for households that depend on predictable rules.

Expertise forms the third strand of the defense. Committee leaders and other defenders present the agriculture committees as holders of genuine food program expertise accumulated across decades of hearings, oversight, staff work, and successive reauthorizations. In this account, expertise resides in institutional memory as much as in individual members. Committee staff who have worked through multiple farm bills learn the interaction between benefit calculation and household income, between state administration options and federal program integrity rules, and between employment and training waivers and labor market conditions. The defense points to regular testimony from the Food and Nutrition Service, from state administrators, from retailers that accept benefits, and from organizations that address hunger and food access. Oversight conducted cycle after cycle, in this account, builds a record that a committee taking the program without that history would need years to match. Committee fit, in the defense account, is learned competence rather than a claim that farm policy and nutrition policy are the same subject.

Critics of the arrangement, including members who have proposed moving nutrition to the committees that handle other social programs, present the same history as evidence of misalignment rather than fit. In the criticism account, jurisdiction settled in 1964 for administrative reasons tied to surplus commodities has governed a program whose scale, purpose, and population changed profoundly across the following decades. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program serves tens of millions of participants in peak years and raises questions about wages, housing costs, health, disability, child well being, and work requirements that critics associate more closely with the committees that handle income security, health, education, and labor policy. The criticism holds that a member selected by voters chiefly for positions on farm support, trade, conservation, or rural credit brings a different set of priorities and a different base of expertise to nutrition decisions than a member whose committee work centers on social programs. The objection in this account is structural rather than personal, directed at the assignment itself and at the incentives and opportunity costs the assignment creates.

Time and attention form a second strand of the criticism. In the criticism account, oversight of nutrition competes for committee time with commodity politics inside one committee that must also handle farm support, crop insurance, conservation, forestry, agricultural trade, rural development, credit, research, and energy provisions in the same reauthorization cycle. Commodity disputes with concentrated producer stakes and immediate price consequences, in this account, tend to command attention during farm bill writing, while nutrition oversight receives attention in bursts tied to controversy rather than through sustained examination. The criticism also holds that the competition shapes which nutrition questions reach the agenda. Provisions that affect the coalition arithmetic of the combined bill receive close attention because floor passage depends on them, while administrative or benefit questions with no bearing on the vote count receive less. A committee organized around social programs, in this account, would set a calendar around the full range of nutrition outcomes rather than around the subset of nutrition issues that intersect with farm bill passage.

The trading objection forms the third strand of the criticism and reaches the coalition logic that defenders praise. In the criticism account, the pairing lets farm interests trade nutrition votes for support, and nutrition votes become currency in negotiations whose central disputes concern commodity programs. Benefit levels, eligibility restrictions, work requirements, and state options can become bargaining material in a negotiation that also covers reference prices, insurance subsidies, and conservation funding, so that neither title receives a clean test of support on its own merits. The separated House votes of 2013 supply material for both readings. The House passed a farm only bill, H.R. 2642, on July 11, 2013, by 216 to 208 and a nutrition only bill, H.R. 3102, on September 19, 2013, by 217 to 210, and neither measure became law. Defenders read those margins and the later recombination as evidence that separation weakens both halves. Critics read the same episode as evidence about the particular bills, the proposed reduction of roughly 40 billion dollars over ten years in the nutrition measure as scored at the time, and the conditions of that cycle, rather than as a permanent verdict on separate consideration.

Beneath the authorizing committees sits an administrative layer that mirrors the combined structure inside one department. The Department of Agriculture houses both sides of the farm bill through distinct agencies with distinct missions. The Food and Nutrition Service administers the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and other federal food assistance programs, issuing guidance to state agencies and reviewing state administration. The Farm Service Agency administers commodity programs, farm loans, and related producer services through state and county offices. The Risk Management Agency administers the federal crop insurance program, setting policies and standards for the private insurers that deliver coverage to producers. One secretary therefore oversees nutrition assistance, commodity support, and crop insurance. The administrative arrangement reinforces the legislative arrangement, because the agriculture committees oversee the department that contains all three agencies, and questions that cross agency lines land within one oversight relationship. Readers tracing the legal structure that governs that administration can follow [the framework for SNAP administration law](/2019/12/01/snap-administration-law/) as a companion to the jurisdictional account given here.

The budget character of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program adds a second set of committees to the picture. The program is an appropriated entitlement. The farm bill authorizes the program, sets eligibility and benefit rules in statute, and establishes the legal right of qualifying households to receive benefits, and annual appropriations provide the funding through which benefits are paid. The program remains mandatory in character, because spending follows from the authorizing law and from participation by eligible households rather than from a fixed discretionary allocation that administrators divide among applicants, yet funding flows through the annual appropriations process. That dual character gives the agriculture appropriations subcommittees in the House and the Senate a recurring piece of the program alongside the authorizing committees. Appropriations bills fund benefit payments and federal administrative costs, carry provisions that affect program operation within the appropriations year, and provide the venue for funding decisions when participation rises during a recession or falls during an expansion.

Analysis of a change in jurisdiction requires care, because a shift in the authorizing committee alone would move only part of the institutional structure, and the remaining parts would move only if Congress also changed them. If jurisdiction over nutrition moved to the committees that handle other social programs, the authorizing work would move first. Hearings and markup on eligibility, benefit adequacy, work requirements, and program integrity would occur in the receiving committees, and conference negotiations would pair members drawn from those committees rather than from the agriculture committees. The farm bill reported by the agriculture committees would then cover commodity, conservation, credit, insurance, and other agricultural titles without a nutrition title, and the coalition arithmetic of that bill would change because the measure would no longer carry the votes that nutrition attracts. A separate nutrition authorization would need its own floor strategy and deadline discipline, and the roughly five year rhythm described by defenders would hold only if the receiving committees sustained a comparable rhythm by practice or by statutory design.

If jurisdiction moved, appropriations alignment would present a distinct question that an authorizing change alone would not settle. Funding for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program flows through the agriculture appropriations subcommittees because the program sits in the Department of Agriculture and because appropriations subcommittee jurisdictions have developed around departmental accounts. A transfer of authorizing jurisdiction to committees outside agriculture would leave a mismatch unless the appropriations subcommittees also redrew their boundaries. In that conditional case, appropriators who fund farm programs would retain annual authority over a nutrition program authorized elsewhere, and the receiving authorizing committees would write eligibility and benefit law without a corresponding appropriations subcommittee aligned to the same subject. Alternatively, if appropriations jurisdiction also moved, the receiving subcommittees would gain a large mandatory account whose spending moves with participation and economic conditions, and the agriculture appropriations bill would shrink to farm, conservation, rural, and food safety accounts with a different political balance. Either version would multiply the points at which a nutrition provision could stall between authorization and funding.

Agency home would present the third institutional question, and the conditional analysis again separates what a committee change would do from what only an executive reorganization would do. A change in congressional jurisdiction by itself would not move the Food and Nutrition Service out of the Department of Agriculture, because agency placement rests on statute and executive organization rather than on House and Senate committee rules alone. If only committee jurisdiction moved, the receiving committees would oversee an agency housed in a department whose other agencies remained under agriculture committee oversight, and oversight would cross departmental lines as a routine matter. If agency placement also moved to a department aligned with other social programs, the transfer would reach state administration agreements, retailer authorization systems, data and reporting structures, and the personnel and budget accounts that support the program, and the transition would require statutory amendment beyond a change in committee rules. A proposal that also names a new appropriations alignment and a new agency home promises a far larger institutional reconstruction, with transition costs and coordination losses that defenders of the existing arrangement cite and with coherence gains that critics of the existing arrangement cite.

The jurisdictional question therefore resists a verdict based on committee labels alone. The arrangement settled in 1964 and combined in 1973 produced a durable legislative vehicle, one department housing both sides of the program structure, and a coalition that repeatedly carried reauthorizations through both chambers. The same arrangement assigned the largest food assistance program to committees formed around agricultural constituencies, placed nutrition oversight in competition with commodity business for finite committee attention, and made nutrition votes part of the exchange that carries farm programs. Defenders of the arrangement, including committee leaders across cycles, weigh durability, schedule, and accumulated expertise most heavily. Critics of the arrangement, including members who have proposed transferring nutrition jurisdiction, weigh subject alignment, oversight attention, and independence of judgment most heavily. The administrative and appropriations layers show why the argument concerns an institutional system rather than a single committee assignment.

## The Split as an Experiment in Enactability

Arguments about commodity titles and nutrition titles had long turned on logic, jurisdiction, and coalition. Logic could show that the two groups of programs rested on different foundations. Coalition analysis could explain the logroll, under which urban members supplied votes for farm support and rural members supplied votes for nutrition assistance, because neither constituency commanded a floor majority alone. None of those arguments established what would happen if the combined vehicle were taken apart and each half had to move through the legislative process on its own strength. The sequence in 2013 and early 2014 supplied that missing evidence. A combined bill failed in the House of Representatives. A combined bill passed the Senate. The House then passed a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill. Neither separated bill became law. The House recombined the two bills for conference, a recombined conference report passed both chambers, and the Agricultural Act of 2014 was signed with the titles together.

The Senate passed the combined bill S. 954 on June 10, 2013, by 66 to 27, a margin of 39 votes. The combined form could therefore command a substantial Senate majority in that Congress, and the Senate had placed itself on record for joined legislation before the House completed its own attempt. The House reached the opposite result ten days later. The combined House bill H.R. 1947 failed on June 20, 2013, by 195 to 234, a margin of 39 votes against passage. A bill could attract a strong majority in one chamber and fail decisively in the other, and neither result alone predicted a statute. The House failure also prevented any simple claim that the combined form guaranteed success. Combination had sustained farm bill coalitions across earlier reauthorizations, and the Senate vote showed that the coalition could be assembled there. In the House at that moment, the combined bill fell well short. The experiment began with a genuine failure of the established vehicle.

Separation was therefore tested after the combined House bill had been defeated, not against a combined bill that had already succeeded in that chamber. The House passed the farm only bill H.R. 2642 on July 11, 2013, by 216 to 208. The margin was eight votes. No Democrat voted in favor, and twelve Republicans voted against. The bill did not pass by reconstructing the broad cross party coalition associated with combined farm bills. Support came from one party alone, while a portion of that party also supplied opposition. An eight vote margin in a chamber that had rejected the combined version represented a real change in immediate House arithmetic. Separation had allowed a farm bill to clear the chamber that had rejected the combined bill three weeks earlier. Any fair account must grant that result. The farm only approach was not incapable of passing the House, and the recorded tally proves House passability under the conditions attached to that particular bill.

The farm only bill carried a substantive provision that complicates both its passage and its failure to become law. H.R. 2642 also carried a repeal of the permanent law of 1938 and 1949. Major farm organizations opposed that repeal, including the American Farm Bureau Federation. Permanent law formed part of the background structure against which farm bill reauthorizations were written, so repeal was not a procedural detail attached to an otherwise unchanged farm bill. Opposition to H.R. 2642 could therefore reflect opposition to repeal rather than opposition to farm programs, opposition to separation, or opposition to both. Support likewise combined several judgments in one vote. A member could favor farm programs and accept repeal, favor separation and tolerate repeal, or favor repeal strongly enough to support the package despite reservations about other elements. The tally of 216 to 208 measured the whole loaded package.

The nutrition half followed more than two months later and by an even narrower margin. The House passed the nutrition only bill H.R. 3102 on September 19, 2013, by 217 to 210, a margin of seven votes. The bill proposed reductions of roughly 40 billion dollars over ten years in nutrition assistance, as scored at the time. The bill did not ask the House to vote on separation as a clean institutional proposition. The bill asked the House to separate nutrition assistance from the farm bill and to reduce nutrition assistance by roughly 40 billion dollars over ten years in the same act. Members opposed to separation could vote against the bill, and members opposed to reductions of that scale could also vote against the bill. Both forms of opposition joined the same negative tally. Presidential opposition added a further barrier, because the president's advisers recommended vetoes of both separated bills. Such a recommendation did not itself defeat a bill, and neither bill reached the stage where a veto could be issued. The recommendation nevertheless signaled that enactment would likely require a fundamental change in the legislation or a coalition large enough to overcome a veto. House margins of eight votes and seven votes bore no resemblance to veto proof majorities.

### Recombination and the Measurement

The House reversed the separation before conference. On September 28, 2013, House Resolution 361 combined the two House passed bills into H.R. 2642 so the House could enter conference with the Senate. Recombination occurred nine days after the nutrition only bill passed, so the completed pair of separated bills existed together for little more than a week before the House joined the components again. House Resolution 361 did not enact either half. The resolution changed the posture of negotiation. Instead of sending the Senate a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill as the basis for separate statutes, the House placed the components back inside a single vehicle. Conference then produced the result that the separated bills had not produced. The conference report was filed on January 27, 2014. The House approved the report on January 29, 2014, by 251 to 166. The Senate approved the report on February 4, 2014, by 68 to 32. The Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, was signed on February 7, 2014, with the titles recombined. The farm only bill did not become a farm only statute, and the nutrition only bill did not become a nutrition only statute. The legislation that completed every stage was a combined farm bill.

The House margin for the conference report gives the experiment its sharpest numerical contrast. The farm only bill passed by 216 to 208, a margin of eight votes. The nutrition only bill passed by 217 to 210, a margin of seven votes. The recombined conference report passed the same chamber by 251 to 166, a margin of 85 votes. The affirmative vote rose to 251, and the margin expanded to a scale entirely different from the party line margins that carried each half. That expansion is consistent with the logic of the logroll. Members who could not support farm programs without nutrition assistance, or nutrition assistance without the farm coalition, could support a package containing both. The conference report also contained negotiated contents that differed from the separated bills, so the margin cannot be attributed to recombination alone with mathematical certainty. The broad pattern nevertheless stands. Each half alone drew a bare House majority. The halves together drew a House majority larger by 85 votes than the opposing total.

The distinction between passability and enactability is the analytical center of the experiment. House passability asks whether a bill can secure a majority in the House of Representatives. By that test, both separated bills succeeded, with 216 votes for the farm only bill and 217 votes for the nutrition only bill. Enactability asks a larger question. Enactability asks whether a bill can secure passage in both chambers in an agreed form, complete conference where conference is required, obtain a presidential signature or attract enough support to overcome a veto, and become law. By that test, both separated bills failed. Neither became law. The experiment did not show that separation was impossible in the House. The experiment showed that House passage by seven or eight votes on party lines was insufficient, under the conditions of 2013, to carry either half through the full lawmaking process. A claim that the halves could not pass a chamber is contradicted by two roll calls. A claim that the halves demonstrated a workable alternative route to legislation is contradicted by the absence of a separated statute at the end of the process.

A margin of seven or eight votes can be decisive for passage and fragile for enactment at the same time. Such a margin leaves little room for losses during negotiation, amendment, conference bargaining, or changes required to obtain Senate agreement and presidential approval. A bill beginning with a bare majority in one chamber often must change to move through later stages, and every change risks subtracting votes from the original coalition without guaranteeing replacement votes. The Senate results reinforce the point. The Senate passed the combined S. 954 by 66 to 27 and later approved the combined conference report by 68 to 32, with more than 60 votes in favor on each occasion. The Senate supplied no corresponding passage vote for a farm only statute or a nutrition only statute in the sequence. Separation demonstrated support in the House, by narrow margins and on party lines, while combination demonstrated support in the Senate, by broad margins at the opening and closing stages. Enactability required a form capable of uniting those institutional realities. Recombination supplied that form. Separation, as attempted in 2013, did not.

### The Counter-Reading at Full Strength

The counter-reading begins with a proposition that no honest use of the episode can avoid. One episode under particular conditions is evidence, not proof. The sequence occurred in one Congress, under one alignment of House control, Senate control, and presidential position, during one farm bill cycle, after one combined House bill failure, and with two separated bills carrying particular substantive provisions. A single sequence cannot eliminate every alternative explanation or predict the behavior of a different Congress confronting different bills. The margins are real, and the final outcome is real, but inference from a single case carries uncertainty that repeated cases would reduce. Treating the episode as proof that separation could never work would claim more than the evidence can bear. The episode can support a strong judgment about the only sustained attempt that occurred. The episode cannot logically foreclose a differently constructed attempt under different political conditions.

The substantive loading of the farm only bill gives the counter-reading specific force. H.R. 2642 was not a neutral farm bill stripped of nutrition language and otherwise identical to the farm portion of a combined bill. The bill carried repeal of the permanent law of 1938 and 1949, a provision opposed by major farm organizations, including the American Farm Bureau Federation. That opposition came from within the agricultural constituency that a farm only strategy was supposed to benefit. A farm only bill opposed by major farm organizations over a central structural provision cannot serve as a pure test of whether farm programs alone could command durable support. The bill tested farm programs, separation, and permanent law repeal as one package. A different farm only bill retaining permanent law might have attracted some opponents of H.R. 2642 and reduced opposition from farm organizations. No such bill passed the House in the sequence, so the size of that possible change cannot be measured from a roll call. The possibility is sufficient to prevent the farm vote from standing as a test of separation in the abstract.

The substantive loading of the nutrition only bill was at least as consequential. H.R. 3102 proposed reductions of roughly 40 billion dollars over ten years in nutrition assistance, as scored at the time. That scale of reduction was a major policy choice, not a technical consequence of placing nutrition assistance in a separate bill. Separation by itself did not dictate a reduction of roughly 40 billion dollars, a smaller reduction, no reduction, or any other particular spending path. The House chose to combine the structural decision with a deep reduction, and the vote measured both choices together. Opposition from members who favored separation in principle but rejected reductions of that scale appeared in the same negative column as opposition from members who rejected separation under any terms. Support from members who favored the reductions appeared in the same affirmative column as support from members primarily committed to a separate nutrition vehicle. The tally of 217 to 210 cannot separate those motives. A nutrition only bill built around a different fiscal choice could have produced a different coalition, perhaps broader in one direction and narrower in another.

Institutional context supplies another major element of the counter-reading. The Senate passed a combined bill by 66 to 27 on June 10, 2013, before the House passed either separated half. The Senate commitment to the combined form was recorded in advance and remained decisive when House Resolution 361 recombined the bills for conference on September 28, 2013. The president's advisers also recommended vetoes of both separated bills. The separated bills therefore faced a Senate predisposed toward combination and a president predisposed toward vetoing separation as presented. A differently designed separation might not face the same combination of obstacles. Separation negotiated in advance with the Senate, embodied in bills that both chambers had agreed to consider, and paired with a president willing to sign the resulting statutes would test a different institutional proposition. The 2013 attempt proceeded in the opposite posture. The House moved toward separation after its combined bill failed, while the Senate had already endorsed combination and the executive branch opposed the separated bills. The failure establishes that enactability failed under hostile institutional conditions. The failure does not establish that separation would fail under cooperative institutional conditions designed to give the approach a genuine path.

A future Congress with different alignments could run the experiment differently for reasons beyond procedure. Different partisan control of the House, the Senate, and the presidency could change the starting assumptions. Different relations between farm organizations and congressional leaders could produce a farm only bill without permanent law repeal. Different fiscal politics could produce a nutrition only bill without reductions on the scale proposed in H.R. 3102. Different committee leadership could negotiate the two halves as a coordinated settlement before floor votes, or could sequence the bills so that neither constituency was asked to rely on a later promise. Each variation would alter the bargain presented to members. Political coalitions are not fixed properties of policy areas. Coalitions respond to bill contents, institutional commitments, electoral incentives, and expectations about whether a proposal can become law. The 2013 result captured responses generated by one set of contents and expectations. A future attempt with different contents and expectations could generate different responses, and the counter-reading insists that this possibility receive full weight.

The counter-reading also cautions against treating the 85 vote House margin for the conference report as a pure measure of recombination. The conference report passed by 251 to 166 on January 29, 2014, after months of negotiation following recombination on September 28, 2013. The conference text was a negotiated settlement, not a mechanical joining of H.R. 2642 as passed on July 11, 2013 and H.R. 3102 as passed on September 19, 2013. Bargaining can change provisions, redistribute gains and losses, remove features that cost votes, and add assurances that gain votes. The larger margin reflected recombination and negotiation together. The combined form may have enabled the bargain by placing all constituencies inside one negotiation, and that enabling function is relevant to enactability. The precise contribution of structure cannot be separated from the precise contribution of negotiated substance with the evidence available from tallies alone.

The counter-reading does not make the episode disappear. A hypothetical cleaner separation remained hypothetical in the record through February 15, 2021, while the 2013 attempt produced actual votes and an actual statute through the competing route. Possibilities about a farm only bill without repeal, a nutrition only bill with a different fiscal path, a Senate committed to two vehicles, or a president prepared to sign separated bills identify conditions that did not govern the historical attempt. Those possibilities limit the inference, but those possibilities do not create an alternative historical result. The separated bills that passed the House attracted 216 and 217 votes. The recombined conference report attracted 251 House votes and 68 Senate votes. The Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, was signed on February 7, 2014, with the titles recombined. Any case for a new attempt would need a coalition broader than a bare party line House majority, because the historical attempt showed how little protection such a majority provided at later stages.

What the episode establishes after the counter-reading has received full weight can be stated with restraint and precision. The attempt in 2013 remains the only sustained attempt by a chamber to legislate the halves apart, and the attempt reached passage votes in the House for both a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill before recombination. The outcome remains the strongest evidence available on the question of whether commodity titles and nutrition titles can be separated into freestanding legislation. That evidence shows that the halves could pass one chamber by seven or eight votes on party lines and could not become law in that form, while the recombined text passed the same House by 85 votes and completed enactment as Public Law 113-79. The evidence does not prove that every possible separation must fail. The evidence establishes that the one real attempt failed at enactability, and that any claim for separation must answer that failure rather than rely on the undoubted fact that each half once passed the House.

## The Verdict on Separation and the Combined Vehicle

On programmatic coherence and on committee fit, separation is the stronger design, and the comparison admits no softer formulation of that finding. A commodity title and a nutrition title answer to different problems and succeed or fail by different measures. Commodity programs support farm income against price movements, with payments that rise automatically when market prices fall below statutory reference prices and shrink when prices are high. Nutrition assistance supports household food purchasing against want, with participation that expands in a recession and contracts in a recovery, without any change in the statute. A legislature organized strictly by subject would assign each problem to the committee fitted to that problem, would hear commodity evidence from witnesses concerned with prices, credit, and land, and would hear nutrition evidence from witnesses concerned with poverty and benefit adequacy. Oversight would follow the same division, and accountability would attach to a single subject rather than to a bargain between subjects. The defense of the combined arrangement, as defenders framed the defense, rested on coalition durability and accumulated expertise rather than on subject coherence. The criticism, as critics framed the criticism, rested on the mismatch between agricultural constituencies and food assistance oversight. On the ground the critics chose, the critics held the better argument. Separation aligns jurisdiction with subject and debate with evidence. No honest reading of the four axes reverses that design judgment, and the verdict on design merit favors separation without hedging.

Design merit, however, is only one part of the question, and the record of the one controlled test points in the opposite direction. The test arrived in 2013, when the House attempted a combined bill, a farm only bill, and a nutrition only bill, while the Senate passed a combined bill. The Senate passed S. 954 on June 10, 2013, by 66 to 27. The combined House bill, H.R. 1947, failed on June 20, 2013, by 195 to 234. The House then passed a farm only bill, H.R. 2642, on July 11, 2013, by 216 to 208, with no Democrat voting in favor and twelve Republicans voting against. The House passed a nutrition only bill, H.R. 3102, on September 19, 2013, by 217 to 210. Each half therefore cleared one chamber by a margin of seven or eight votes on a substantially partisan division, and neither half proceeded to enactment. On September 28, 2013, House Resolution 361 combined the two House passed bills into H.R. 2642 for conference with the Senate. The conference report was filed on January 27, 2014. The House approved the report on January 29, 2014, by 251 to 166, and the Senate approved the report on February 4, 2014, by 68 to 32. The Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, was signed on February 7, 2014, with the titles recombined. The recombined report passed the House by 85 votes, more than ten times either separated margin. Neither half commanded a majority in a form that could become law.

The counter-reading of that episode deserves statement at full strength, because the verdict depends on surviving the strongest alternative explanation. The year 2013 supplied one instance under particular conditions, and one instance cannot prove a structural law. The separated measures were substantively loaded rather than neutral. The nutrition only bill proposed reductions of roughly 40 billion dollars over ten years in nutrition assistance, as scored at the time, a scale of reduction that guaranteed opposition beyond the question of structure. The farm only bill carried a repeal of the permanent law of 1938 and 1949, a provision that farm organizations including the American Farm Bureau Federation opposed, so the farm half also fused a structural question with a substantive provocation. The Senate stood committed to the combined form, having passed a combined bill before the House acted on either half, and the president's advisers recommended a veto of both separated bills, so neither half faced a neutral path through the remaining stages. A different Senate, a differently drafted pair of bills, or a presidency neutral on structure could have produced a different result. The correct characterization of the episode is strong evidence rather than proof. That qualification disciplines the inference without dissolving the inference, because every confound operated through the same channel, namely the absence of a coalition broad enough to carry a separated bill through both chambers and a presidential signature.

The deciding factor is enactability, and enactability decides the comparison because a structure that produces no statute delivers neither coherence nor support to anyone. A perfectly fitted committee system attached to a bill that fails delivers no commodity payments and no nutrition benefits. A poorly fitted committee system attached to a bill that passes delivers both, under terms a conference can adjust. Enactability is the condition on which every other value depends, since coherence, constituency representation, volatility management, and jurisdictional fit exist only inside an enacted law. The choice, exactly as the record frames the choice, lies between a more logical structure that may produce no legislation and an illogical structure that has produced a reauthorization roughly every five years for half a century. Faced with that choice, the comparison resolves for the combined vehicle. The illogical structure is the working one. The 2018 cycle corroborates the resolution. In that cycle the combined bill suffered an initial House failure of 198 to 213 on May 18, 2018, and the conference report then passed the House by 369 to 47 and the Senate by 87 to 13 in December 2018. A combined bill could fail, recover, and pass by overwhelming margins within a single cycle. No separated bill in the 2013 test recovered at all. The logroll joining urban votes for farm support with rural votes for nutrition assistance, a bargain given form when the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, first incorporated reauthorization of the food stamp program into a farm bill, supplied the margin that design logic alone never supplied.

### Guidance for a Student Approaching the Combined Vehicle

For a student, the sound sequence begins with the combined vehicle itself, because the coalition is the fact that explains every other feature of the structure. A student who begins with either title in isolation encounters a self-contained system, reference prices and payment limitations on one side and eligibility and benefit calculation on the other, and neither isolated reading explains why the two systems share a statute, a committee, and a reauthorization calendar. The coalition explains the sharing. Urban members supply votes for farm support and rural members supply votes for nutrition assistance because neither constituency commands a floor majority alone, and that exchange accounts for the jurisdiction that the Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, placed in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees, for the deliberate committee agreement of 1973, and for the survival of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program inside the vehicle after the program received that name effective October 1, 2008, under the act of 2008. The money follows the coalition, since a vehicle holding two constituencies funds both. The volatility follows the coalition, since one renewal calendar must absorb a price driven title and a participation driven title within one baseline. The jurisdiction follows the coalition, since the committees that broker the exchange retain the subjects exchanged. A structured route through the statutes, the committee history, and the vote records appears in [the study guide for United States legislation](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/). Read in sequence, the failed combined vote, the two narrow separated passes, the recombination by House Resolution 361, and the wide conference margin form a single argument about how the vehicle works.

### Guidance for a Staffer Reading Baselines and Counting Districts

For a staffer, the operative skill is the disciplined reading of baselines with their years attached, paired with a district level count of effects on both titles. A baseline is a projection bound to a source and a period, and a share quoted apart from that source and period carries no usable information. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline, a projection for extension of the 2014 act across fiscal 2019 to 2028, projected total outlays of 867 billion dollars, with the nutrition title at 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent, crop insurance at 78.0 billion dollars, about 9 percent, commodity programs at 61.1 billion dollars, about 7 percent, and conservation at 59.7 billion dollars, about 7 percent. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 presented a different window, 428 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2023, with nutrition at 76 percent, or 326 billion dollars, and the remaining 24 percent, or 102 billion dollars, mostly in crop insurance, commodity programs, and conservation. A staffer who merges those two presentations manufactures an error, and the second projection described a scored act that was budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 against extension of prior law. The district count demands the same discipline toward the local. Every district contains households receiving nutrition assistance, and a large share of districts contain farm operations, conservation enrollments, or crop insurance participation, in proportions that vary sharply. A vote count built only on commodity effects in a rural district or only on nutrition effects in an urban district repeats the error that the 2013 separation embodied in legislative form. The combined vehicle rewards a count that prices both titles in every district, because the coalition rests on members who can identify a stake for constituents on one side of the vehicle and a reason for tolerance on the other side.

### Guidance for a Researcher Citing the Separation Episode

For a researcher, the open questions are the counterfactuals that the 2013 episode cannot answer, and recognition of those limits disciplines every citation of the episode. The record establishes what happened in one Congress under stated conditions. The record does not establish what a neutral separation would have produced, because no neutral separation reached a vote. A separation drafted without a roughly 40 billion dollar reduction in nutrition assistance, as scored at the time, and without a repeal of the permanent law of 1938 and 1949 might have attracted a different coalition or failed for reasons invisible in the actual roll calls. The record also does not establish what a different Senate would have done, because the Senate of the 113th Congress committed to the combined form on June 10, 2013, before the House completed either separated bill, and a Senate open to conference on separated vehicles would have changed the calculation in the House. Presidential position supplies a third unanswered counterfactual, since veto recommendations hung over both halves and a neutral presidency would have altered the whip counts behind margins of seven and eight votes. Careful citation practice follows. A citation of 2013 as evidence that separation failed under loaded conditions, against a committed Senate, and under veto threat states a defensible claim. A citation of 2013 as proof that separation must fail under all conditions overstates the evidence. The stronger scholarly use treats the vote sequence as a measurement of coalition breadth. The separated bills found breadths of seven and eight votes in one chamber. The conference report found a breadth of 85 votes in the same chamber.

### Guidance for a Journalist Reporting Shares and Votes

For a journalist, the governing rule is that no share appears in print without its source and its year, and any share quoted without those attachments counts as unverified. The rule sounds narrow and operates broadly, because shares shift with the conditions beside which a baseline was drawn. A baseline drawn beside a recession shows a larger nutrition share, because participation rises with need. A baseline drawn beside a price collapse shows larger commodity and insurance shares, because payments rise with the gap between market prices and reference prices. Average monthly participation in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program stood at 26.3 million in fiscal 2007, peaked at 47.6 million in fiscal 2013 in the aftermath of the recession of 2007 to 2009, and fell to about 40 million by fiscal 2018, on United States Department of Agriculture and Congressional Budget Office figures, with program spending peaking in fiscal 2013 at about 80 billion dollars. A report that printed a nutrition share from a recession era baseline beside a commodity share from a price collapse baseline would mislead readers while printing two individually accurate numbers. Source and year prevent that error at negligible cost. The same discipline applies to vote reporting. A report on separation that prints the separated margins of 216 to 208 and 217 to 210 without the conference margin of 251 to 166 in the House and 68 to 32 in the Senate omits the comparison that gives the separated margins meaning. The defense of combined jurisdiction belongs in print as a defense held by named defenders, and criticism as a criticism held by named critics, because unattributed characterization substitutes editorial judgment for reporting.

### A Closing Assessment Across Money, Constituency, Volatility, and Jurisdiction

The four axes, gathered one final time, explain both halves of the verdict. On money, the combined vehicle funds a dominant nutrition title beside far smaller commodity, insurance, and conservation titles, on the figures of the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, and that imbalance tempts an observer toward separation until the imbalance receives a coalition explanation. The large title holds votes that the small titles need, and the small titles hold votes that the large title needs. On constituency, neither the farm constituency nor the nutrition constituency commands a floor majority alone, a fact demonstrated negatively by the separated margins of 2013 and positively by the conference margins of 2014 and 2018, and the vehicle endures because the exchange renews at each reauthorization. On volatility, the two sides move on opposite rhythms, commodity payments expanding when prices fall and nutrition participation expanding when employment falls, under one renewal calendar. The emergency record on the commodity side, about 22.5 billion dollars in emergency market loss payments across fiscal 1998 to 2001 on top of about 20.7 billion dollars in scheduled contract payments, on Congressional Research Service figures, and the participation peak on the nutrition side in fiscal 2013 measure two kinds of exposure that no single title design absorbs alone. On jurisdiction, the House and Senate Agriculture Committees write both title groups, the Food and Nutrition Service administers nutrition assistance within the Department of Agriculture while the Farm Service Agency administers commodity programs and the Risk Management Agency administers crop insurance, and the arrangement concentrates expertise and competition for oversight inside one committee system and one department. Each axis, scored on design merit, points toward separation. The comparison nevertheless resolves on enactability rather than on design merit, because enactability is the axis on which a statute exists at all. Half a century of reauthorization cycles, running from the deliberate combination of 1973 through the recombination of 2014 and the enactment of 2018, tested the arrangement against recessions, price collapses, partisan realignments, and one direct attempt at separation, and the arrangement produced a law in every cycle.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between the commodity title and nutrition spending?

The commodity title and the nutrition title differ in recipient, trigger, and mechanism. Commodity provisions support producers of covered commodities when market prices or revenues fall below levels fixed in statute, and payments attach to production history and base acres. Crop insurance, carried in a separate title, subsidizes premiums and shares risk on policies whose insured values move with the value of the crop. Nutrition provisions, dominated by the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, support eligible low income households, and benefits follow household income and composition under rules administered by the Food and Nutrition Service. One side answers a market shortfall on a farm operation, and the other side answers household need for food, and eligibility on one side proves nothing about eligibility on the other side.

### Q: What share of spending is nutrition versus the commodity title?

In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, nutrition accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of projected total outlays of 867 billion dollars, commodity programs accounted for 61.1 billion dollars, about 7 percent, and crop insurance accounted for 78.0 billion dollars, about 9 percent. In the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 for fiscal 2019 to 2023, nutrition accounted for 76 percent, or 326 billion dollars, of projected total outlays of 428 billion dollars. Measured shares depend on source and period, and the two verified presentations must stay separate. A share quoted without a named source and a named period carries no stable meaning, because each presentation scored a different object across a different span of years and under different assumptions.

### Q: When were the commodity title and food aid first combined?

Jurisdiction arrived before combination. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, made food stamps a permanent program, placed administration in the Department of Agriculture, and placed legislative jurisdiction in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. Combination in a single bill followed nine years later. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, became the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program, according to the Congressional Research Service, and that incorporation reflected a deliberate committee agreement. From that enactment onward, the farm bill served as the recurring vehicle for both commodity support and food assistance, and later reauthorizations retained the combined form through the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018.

### Q: What happened when the House split the commodity title from nutrition?

The House first defeated a combined bill and then passed each half apart, and neither half became law. The combined House bill, H.R. 1947, failed on June 20, 2013, by 195 to 234, after the Senate passed a combined bill, S. 954, on June 10, 2013, by 66 to 27. The House passed a farm only bill, H.R. 2642, on July 11, 2013, by 216 to 208, and a nutrition only bill, H.R. 3102, on September 19, 2013, by 217 to 210. On September 28, 2013, House Resolution 361 combined the two House bills into H.R. 2642 for conference. The conference report passed the House by 251 to 166 and the Senate by 68 to 32, and the Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, was signed on February 7, 2014, with the titles recombined.

### Q: Should the commodity title be separated from nutrition?

The verdict separates design merit from enactability. On coherence and committee fit, separation is the stronger design, because each program group would sit with the committee fitted to the subject and would receive debate on evidence tied to that subject. On the record of enactment, the combined vehicle is the working structure, because neither half commanded a majority in a form that could become law during the one sustained test in 2013, and the recombined conference report passed by wide margins. The deciding factor is enactability. A structure that produces no statute delivers neither commodity support nor nutrition benefits, and the counter reading, that 2013 was one loaded instance rather than proof, travels with the verdict at full strength.

### Q: Does the commodity title need urban votes?

Yes, under the arithmetic that has governed the combined vehicle. Commodity and crop insurance benefits concentrate in rural districts where production agriculture is a major employer, and those districts form a minority of House seats and a minority of Senate states. A farm only bill therefore needs votes from members whose districts contain few direct recipients of commodity payments or crop insurance policies. Urban and suburban members supply those votes in exchange for rural votes for nutrition assistance, in the exchange described as the logroll. Neither constituency commands a floor majority alone, and combination pools the two minorities into a majority. The Senate passage of the combined S. 954 by 66 to 27 on June 10, 2013, illustrates the breadth that pooling produced in that cycle.

### Q: Why does commodity title spending swing so much?

Commodity spending swings because payments are price driven by formula. When market prices fall below statutory reference prices, payments to enrolled producers rise automatically, and when market prices run above those reference prices, payments shrink toward zero without further action by Congress. Crop insurance outlays move with prices as well, because insured values rise and fall with the value of the crop. The scale of a swing appears in the emergency record. Across fiscal 1998 to 2001, during the price collapse that followed the act of 1996, Congress added about 22.5 billion dollars in emergency market loss payments on top of about 20.7 billion dollars in scheduled contract payments, according to the Congressional Research Service. A design tied to market prices inherits market volatility.

### Q: Which should a student study first, the commodity title or nutrition?

A student gains most by beginning with the combined vehicle and the coalition that explains the vehicle. The coalition accounts for the shared statute, the shared committees, and the shared reauthorization calendar, and a reading that begins with either title in isolation leaves that sharing unexplained. Jurisdiction came first, under the Food Stamp Act of 1964, and combination followed under the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973. Money, constituency, volatility, and jurisdiction then fall into place as four axes of one comparison. The 2013 split sequence and the 2014 recombination supply the empirical test, and the verdict on separation, resolved on enactability rather than on design merit, closes the sequence in the order that the evidence accumulated.

### Q: Has the nutrition share of the farm bill always been this large?

No. A farm bill spending share is a snapshot of conditions in a named source and period, rather than a permanent property of the vehicle, and the verified record supplies two distinct presentations. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, nutrition accounted for about 77 percent of projected total outlays. In the Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 for fiscal 2019 to 2023, nutrition accounted for 76 percent of projected total outlays. Participation growth after the recession of 2007 to 2009 raised the nutrition share in presentations drawn beside that period, because average monthly participation rose from 26.3 million in fiscal 2007 to a peak of 47.6 million in fiscal 2013, on Department of Agriculture and Congressional Budget Office figures, without any change in the statute that explained the rise.

### Q: Which committee would write nutrition legislation if the titles were separated?

Committee jurisdiction is set by the rules of each chamber, and a separation statute alone would move nothing automatically. The House and the Senate would have to reassign jurisdiction by rule change or by the terms of legislation, and the receiving committees would take hearings, markup, and conference duties only after such a reassignment. Separation proposals have named the committees that handle other social programs as the candidates, because those committees write law on income security and related household programs. The agriculture committees would retain the commodity, conservation, credit, and insurance titles unless a separate decision changed that assignment as well. Appropriations subcommittee alignment and agency placement in the Department of Agriculture present distinct questions, and neither follows automatically from a change in authorizing jurisdiction.

### Q: What is the logroll that holds the farm bill together?

The logroll is an exchange of votes between two constituencies that lack floor majorities alone. Urban members supply votes for farm support, and rural members supply votes for nutrition assistance, and each side accepts provisions chosen for the other side as the price of passage for provisions regarded as essential at home. Combination pools the two minorities into a majority in committee markup and on the floor of each chamber. The exchange renews at each reauthorization, roughly every five years under the pattern of the farm bill, because expiration returns both constituencies to the same negotiating table under deadline pressure. Defection carries a price denominated in the defector's own title, since an attack on one half endangers the votes that carry the other half.

### Q: Did the Senate pass separated farm and nutrition bills during the split episode?

No. The Senate acted on the combined form at both ends of the sequence and passed no separated bill. On June 10, 2013, the Senate passed the combined bill S. 954 by 66 to 27. The House then passed a farm only bill by 216 to 208 and a nutrition only bill by 217 to 210, and neither measure received a Senate passage vote as a freestanding statute. After House Resolution 361 recombined the two House bills for conference on September 28, 2013, the Senate approved the combined conference report on February 4, 2014, by 68 to 32. The Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, followed on February 7, 2014. Senate support in the record therefore belongs to combined legislation, at a margin of 39 votes at the opening stage and a margin of 36 votes at the closing stage.

### Q: How does a recession change the farm bill spending shares?

A recession changes the shares through participation, without any amendment to the statute. When unemployment rises and household incomes fall, more households meet eligibility rules that Congress left unchanged, enrollment in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program climbs, and benefit outlays climb with enrollment. Average monthly participation rose from 26.3 million in fiscal 2007 to a peak of 47.6 million in fiscal 2013, in the aftermath of the recession of 2007 to 2009, on Department of Agriculture and Congressional Budget Office figures, and program spending peaked in fiscal 2013 at about 80 billion dollars. As the nutrition numerator grows, the nutrition share of any combined total rises and the commodity share is compressed, even in a period when commodity formulas and reference prices remain exactly as enacted.

### Q: How does a collapse in crop prices change the farm bill spending shares?

A collapse in crop prices changes the shares through the commodity formulas, in mirror image of a recession. When market prices fall below statutory reference prices, payments under the commodity title rise automatically, because the gap between the market return and the statutory floor widens. Crop insurance values move with the value of the crop, so insurance outlays and indemnity exposure shift with the same price movement. The emergency record measures the possible scale. Across fiscal 1998 to 2001, Congress added about 22.5 billion dollars in emergency market loss payments on top of about 20.7 billion dollars in scheduled contract payments, according to the Congressional Research Service. As farm side outlays grow, the farm share of a combined total rises and the nutrition share falls in relative terms, without any legislative transfer between the titles.

### Q: Would the nutrition program leave the Department of Agriculture if the titles were separated?

Not necessarily, because three choices that debates often merge are separable. Authorization decides which statute carries a program and which provisions govern eligibility and benefits. Jurisdiction decides which committees in the House and the Senate write and oversee that law. Agency home decides which department and which agency administer the program, and for nutrition assistance that home has been the Food and Nutrition Service inside the Department of Agriculture. Separation debates concern the first two choices in the main. A change in committee jurisdiction alone would leave the Food and Nutrition Service in place, because agency placement rests on statute and executive organization. A transfer of agency home would require separate statutory action and a transition covering state administration agreements, retailer authorization systems, and reporting structures.

### Q: Why do rural members vote for nutrition spending?

Rural members vote for nutrition spending in the main because nutrition votes are the price of commodity votes under the logroll. Production districts form a minority in each chamber, so commodity and insurance provisions cannot reach a floor majority on farm district votes alone, and urban and suburban votes arrive in exchange for rural support of the nutrition title. District level need reinforces the exchange. Nutrition benefits reach households in every congressional district, including rural districts where recipient households, grocery retailers, and community organizations form part of the local economy, so a rural member voting for the combined bill can point to a benefit present at home as well as to the farm provisions that the exchange carries through the chamber.

### Q: How did the conference process recombine the two House bills?

Recombination happened by a House rule before conference opened. On September 28, 2013, House Resolution 361 merged the two House passed bills, the farm only bill passed on July 11, 2013, and the nutrition only bill passed on September 19, 2013, into H.R. 2642, so that the House could enter conference with the Senate on a single combined vehicle rather than on two freestanding measures. The Senate had passed a combined bill, S. 954, on June 10, 2013, and conference negotiated from the combined posture that the resolution restored. The conference report was filed on January 27, 2014, passed the House on January 29, 2014, by 251 to 166, and passed the Senate on February 4, 2014, by 68 to 32. The resulting Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, was signed on February 7, 2014.

### Q: What did the farm only bill propose to do with permanent law?

The farm only bill proposed a structural change to the foundation of farm law. H.R. 2642, passed by the House on July 11, 2013, by 216 to 208, carried a repeal of the permanent law of 1938 and 1949, the standing statutes that remained in place behind each temporary farm bill and to which commodity policy would revert in the absence of a successor enactment. Repeal would have made the commodity title of the bill itself the standing law, removing the backdrop that gave each reauthorization a default and a deadline. Major farm organizations, including the American Farm Bureau Federation, opposed the bill on that ground, so opposition to the farm only bill included opposition from inside the agricultural constituency that a farm only strategy was designed to benefit.

### Q: Which title has the more predictable costs, commodity or nutrition?

Neither title has fixed costs, and predictability differs by driver rather than by title size. Commodity costs hinge on market prices, rising automatically when prices fall below statutory reference prices and shrinking when prices run high, with crop insurance values moving with the value of the crop. Nutrition costs hinge on the wider economy, rising when unemployment and household need raise participation and falling when incomes recover, without any change in the statute. Baselines state both sides as projections built on assumptions about prices, participation, and economic conditions, and a projection records the assumptions of the source and period that produced the projection. That conditional character is the reason a share or a total has meaning only with a source and a year attached.

### Q: What should a reader check before trusting a farm bill spending share?

A careful reader checks four items before trusting any farm bill spending share. The first item is the source, since a budget office baseline, an enactment score, an appropriations outcome, and an administrative tally answer different questions under different rules. The second item is the status of the figure, in particular whether the source presented a baseline projection for an extension of prior law or a score of an enacted statute. The third item is the period covered, because a ten year window and a five year window accumulate different totals from the same programs. The fourth item is whether the figure is projected or actual, since a projection rests on assumptions about prices and participation that later conditions can displace. A share that survives those four checks can be quoted with the source and period attached.
