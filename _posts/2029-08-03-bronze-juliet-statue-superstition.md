The bronze Juliet who stands in the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta in Verona is the most handled work of sculpture in the Romeo and Juliet afterlife, and the handling has left evidence no archive could have commissioned. Her right breast shines the colour of new coinage against the dark patina of the rest of the figure, because for half a century visitors have touched that one place, in the belief that the touch brings luck in love. The first figure placed in the courtyard, Nereo Costantini's original of 1972, was touched until the metal cracked at the breast and fractured at the arm, and in 2014 the city withdrew the original into museum keeping and set a replica in its place. The replica has been touched in its turn, and within ten years the same small wound opened in the same place. This article examines the statue and its superstition as a single object of study: the sculptor and the commission, the ritual and its undocumented birth, the physics of the wear, the retirement and the copy, the statue's sisters in Munich, its cousins among the rubbed bronzes of Europe, and the objections the rite has drawn. The verdict argued throughout is that the bronze is the one object in Verona the myth made by use rather than by decree. The house was identified by an antiquarian's pun and composed by a museum director; the balcony was assembled from tomb marble by a civic workshop; the letters are answered by an organized club. The superstition attached to the statue has no author, no founding document, no office, and no script. It was made by hands, it is recorded by hands, and its physical trace, a breast polished bright and then worn through, is the courtyard's only honest archive: the single exhibit at the site whose every claim about itself is true.

The distinction matters because the Casa di Giulietta is a site where nearly everything else arrives with paperwork. The identification of the Via Cappello house with the Capulets rests on the Cappello family's documented tenure and on Antonio Avena's restoration campaign of 1939 and 1940, which gave the courtyard its famous balcony; the full audit of that composition belongs to the companion examination of [the Casa di Giulietta and the balcony that its record cannot support](/2029/06/22/casa-di-giulietta-balcony-myth/). The statue entered that composed courtyard three decades later, in 1972, into a shrine whose liturgy was already forming, and it altered the liturgy's centre of gravity. A balcony can only be looked at. A letter can be written and left. A bronze body at ground level, at the scale of a body, can be touched, and touch is the one devotional act that leaves a cumulative, measurable, and finally destructive record of itself. The statue's biography is therefore readable in a way the balcony's is not: not in municipal files, though those exist, but in the metal. The shine is a census. The crack is a finding. The hole is a verdict delivered by the material on the ritual that made the material famous.

## The play ordered statues first: the golden vow of Act 5

The courtyard figure fulfils, in bronze and at a discount, a commission the play itself issues in its final scene. Standing over the bodies in the Capulet vault, the two fathers end their feud with a competitive act of commemoration. Montague speaks first: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That whiles Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3). Capulet answers in kind: "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie: / Poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3). The vow is precise in its economics. The statues are to be gold, they are to be without rival in the city, and they are payment, the fathers' settlement for children the fathers' quarrel consumed. Verona in the play gets its statues as an act of public penance, decreed from above by the two men with the authority and the money to decree them.

The city of Verona, five centuries later, reversed every term of that settlement while keeping its shape. The figure in the courtyard is bronze rather than gold, though the touch of hands has gilded the one part of it the ritual uses. It represents Juliet alone, without the matching Romeo Capulet promised, an asymmetry the whole tourist geography of the city repeats, as the survey of [Romeo's house and Verona's tourist geography](/2029/07/20/romeos-house-verona-tourism/) records in the unvisited palazzo that bears his name. And it was not raised by fathers in penance. It was placed by a service club and a municipality as furnishings for a courtyard, and it acquired its meaning afterwards, from below, by accumulation. The play's statues were to be the feud's final artefact, decreed and then, in the fiction, presumably admired at a distance proper to gold. The courtyard's statue became an artefact of the opposite process: unplanned, unpriced, and handled until it broke.

The comparison is not a decorative parallel. It identifies what kind of object the bronze is. The golden statues of the closing scene belong to the play's world of fathers, decrees, and public settlement, the world whose failures the tragedy has spent five acts itemizing: the Prince's edicts, Capulet's arrangements, the Friar's scheme, each an instrument of authority producing the opposite of its intention. A statue tradition generated by that world would be founded by charter and maintained by custodians. The courtyard ritual was founded by nobody and maintained by everybody, and its history, told in full below, is the history of an authority-free practice growing on a municipal object until the object failed under it. When the city finally intervened, in 2014, it intervened as a custodian of last resort, replacing its own property, and even then it could not intervene in the practice, only in the practice's victim. The replica was installed, and the hands resumed. Verona built the statue the play ordered, and then discovered that the play's method, decree, had nothing to do with what the statue became.

There is one more term of the vow worth weighing. Montague promises that no figure "at such rate be set" in Verona, at such a price, and the tragedy's own word for the children's value is set by the Prince's audit of the catastrophe, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3). The courtyard bronze gives that accounting a material parody. The figure did acquire a rate, a measurable cost, though the city's accountants rather than the father's grief supplied it: a foundry bill for the copy, a conservation file for the original, a service-life estimate for the replacement. The statue's worth is computed in the budgets of its maintenance, and its fame is computed in the depth of its wear. No figure in Verona has been set at such a rate, though the rate turned out to be denominated in millimetres of lost bronze.

## The maker: Nereo Costantini and a posthumous placement

The figure is the work of Nereo Costantini, a Veronese sculptor born at Nogara in 1905 and dead at Verona on 5 August 1969. The bronze was realized in 1969, the year of his death, which makes the courtyard statue a posthumous presence from the start: Costantini never saw it in its place, never saw a visitor touch it, and left no recorded statement about what the figure was for. That silence is a fact of the record, and the record's silence extends across the whole question of intention. No letter, interview, or programme note from the sculptor prescribes a ritual, blesses a superstition, or so much as mentions the courtyard crowd. Whatever the statue means in use, it does not mean it by its maker's direction.

The figure itself stands 2.65 metres, somewhat above life size, a girl in a long flowing gown in the Renaissance taste the shrine preferred, her hair dressed up from her neck. The pose matters to everything that followed, and it deserves exact description. One of the figure's hands is raised to her own breast; the other gathers the folds of her skirt. The raised hand, as the house article in this series records, rests at the breast as if in the act of feeling a heartbeat or stilling an emotion, a conventional gesture of modest agitation in the sculptural vocabulary Costantini inherited. The gesture was composed for the eye. It was not composed for the hand of a stranger. Yet the geometry of the placement made the gesture an instruction: a figure at ground level in a small courtyard, her own hand marking the spot, at the height of a reaching arm. Visitors did not choose the breast at random from the figure's anatomy. The sculptor's composition pointed to it, the first guidebooks repeated it, and the polish made the pointing permanent. The superstition's target was selected by the statue before the superstition existed.

The placement is documented with unusual firmness for an object of this kind, though with one division the honest account must carry. The statue was installed in the courtyard in 1972, at the initiative of the Lions Club of Verona, the service organization that had championed the sculptor's memory; the city's official tourism portal dates the placement to that year, and the same autumn, October and November 1972, the Museo di Castelvecchio mounted a commemorative exhibition of Costantini's work under the joint auspices of the Comune and the Lions Club. The bronze thus arrived in the courtyard inside a civic act of mourning for its maker, three years after his death. A minority dating attaches the placement to 1973, the year the house opened as a furnished museum; the division is natural, since the statue and the museum belong to the same phase of the shrine's completion, and the record carried in this series dates the placement to 1972 on the portal's authority while noting the variant. What does not divide is the sequence: made in 1969, placed in 1972, or in 1973 at the outside, posthumous in either case.

The Lions Club framing deserves a paragraph of its own, because it fixes the statue's original social meaning before the ritual rewrote it. A service club's gift of a commemorative figure, installed alongside a memorial exhibition, belongs to the civic culture of postwar Italy: local pride, a local artist honoured in his own city, a famous story given a face. Nothing in that framing anticipates a luck rite. The club did not endow a superstition; it endowed a likeness. The courtyard received the object as décor with a pedigree, the shrine's altarpiece in a space whose other furnishings, the balcony above and the letter-laden passage behind, were already teaching visitors to behave as pilgrims. Into a room arranged for devotion, the club set a body. The body's career from that moment belongs not to the club, which fades from the statue's record almost at once, but to the crowd.

### Did the sculptor design the statue to be touched?

No evidence in the documented record suggests that Costantini intended any ritual use. The bronze was realized in 1969, the year of his death, and placed in 1972; no statement by the sculptor prescribes touching, mentions luck, or addresses the courtyard crowd at all.

The raised hand at the figure's breast is a conventional gesture of the sculptural vocabulary he inherited, composed for the eye, and the ritual that later fixed on that spot was the crowd's reading of the composition, not the maker's instruction.

The point is worth pressing because the alternative account, the designed shrine, flatters every custodian who has managed the site since. If the ritual had been intended, the damage would be a plan fulfilled and the replacement a scheduled renewal. The record supports neither comfort. The sculptor died before the placement; the placing body was a service club honouring his memory; the city's own descriptions of the figure speak of legend in the passive voice, a belief that exists, without an author to cite. The statue's meaning was crowdsourced before the word existed, and the crowd that sourced it was anonymous, international, and unled. That anonymity is the superstition's defining archival property, and it separates the bronze from every other object in the courtyard, each of which has a maker, a date, and a file.

## The pilgrim's hand: the play's own theology of touching

The ritual's script, if an authorless practice can be said to have one, was written by the play. In the ball scene of Act 1, Romeo approaches Juliet with a conceit drawn from the cult of saints, the one devotional system in his world where touching a represented body was an authorized act. "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5). Juliet, cast by his metaphor as the saint in the shrine, corrects the theology while accepting the touch: "For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5). The sonnet the two of them build across the exchange is a negotiation about the licence of contact, and its conclusion is the tragedy's most quoted piece of stagecraft: "Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake" (1.5), says the statue that does not move, and Romeo answers, "Then move not, while my prayer's effect I take" (1.5). The play imagines its heroine as an image in a shrine, petitioned by a pilgrim, granting by stillness.

The courtyard ritual is that scene running without its dialogue. The visitor casts himself or herself in the pilgrim's part, approaches the image, and performs the contact the sonnet negotiated, with the play's own economy of petition and grant simplified into a single clause: the touch brings luck in love. Even the division of the statue's body follows the sonnet's logic. In the exchange, the hands meet palm to palm, the licensed contact, while the kiss waits on permission. At Verona the licensed contact migrated from the image's hand to its breast, a substitution the play would call profanation and the crowd calls tradition, but the structure, a pilgrim, an image, a petition about love, a grant expected from stillness, is the sonnet's structure entire. Shakespeare's audience knew the real-world practice behind the metaphor: pilgrims touched the reliquaries and images of saints, wore the touch-sites bright, and carried the licence of that contact into every church in Europe. The bronze in the courtyard is a Protestant playwright's imaginary saint, materialized by a Catholic city's sculptor, petitioned by the citizens of everywhere. The superstition is unscripted in the archival sense. In the literary sense it was scripted in 1595.

The second touch-text of the play sharpens the point. Watching Juliet at her window, Romeo wishes himself a glove: "See how she leans her cheek upon her hand. / O that I were a glove upon that hand, / That I might touch that cheek" (2.2). The wish is for mediated contact, his hand inside the instrument that touches her. The courtyard inverts the wish. There the visitor's bare hand takes the contact directly, and the mediation runs the other way: the bronze is the glove, the dead metal interposed between the living hand and the fictional girl, touchable precisely because she is not there. A relic mediates a saint who existed; the bronze mediates a heroine who did not, and the mediation works identically, which is why the custodians' insistence on the heroine's fictionality, printed in the museum's own materials, has never thinned the queue. The rite does not require a historical Juliet. It requires a surface, a story, and a permission, and the play supplies the second and third while the sculptor supplied the first.

### Why the right breast and not the left?

The choice of spot was overdetermined by the figure itself and then fixed by repetition. The statue's own raised hand rests at her breast, marking the place in the composition; the figure stands at ground level in a confined courtyard, so the breast lies at the height of a visitor's reaching hand in the photograph pose the courtyard encourages.

Once early touching polished the spot visibly brighter than the surrounding metal, the shine itself became the instruction, showing each arrival exactly where the rite is performed. No document records a deliberation, and the record supports the sequence, composition, then contact, then polish, then prescription, rather than any founding choice.

The hand-to-hand contact of the sonnet also left a residue in the courtyard's wider liturgy, for the statue is not the only thing visitors touch. The passage walls take the written deposits, the balcony takes the gaze, and the figure takes the hand, and the distribution of acts among the site's objects follows the body's own grammar: sight for the architecture, writing for the walls, touch for the body. Of the three, only touch consumes its object. A gaze costs the balcony nothing; a note costs the wall a cleaning; a hand costs the bronze a fraction of its substance, repeated past counting. The ritual's tragedy, in the strict sense of a flaw producing a fall, is that its chosen medium of devotion is abrasion.

## The older grammar: saints' feet and the archaeology of the touch

The Verona rite feels modern because its object is modern, but its grammar is among the oldest in European devotion, and the grammar's type case stands in Rome. In St Peter's Basilica, the seated bronze of the apostle, a work of the late thirteenth century attributed to Arnolfo di Cambio, extends its right foot beyond the pedestal, and the foot is worn to a smooth, featureless stump, its toes effaced by the touches and kisses of pilgrims across seven centuries. The basilica's custodians have never needed to explain the foot. The wear is the explanation, legible to any visitor: here is a body that the faithful are licensed to touch, and the licence, exercised past counting, has consumed the bronze's detail at exactly the licensed point. The parallel with the courtyard is structural and complete. A represented body, a designated contact point, a petition carried in the hand, and a cumulative record written into the metal by the petitions themselves: the Verona figure's biography is the Vatican foot's biography compressed from centuries into decades by the acceleration of modern travel.

The wider archaeology of the practice fills in the grammar's rules. Pilgrims at the great shrines touched what they were permitted to touch and wore it in proportion to the permission: the tombs of saints kissed through their grilles, the steps of holy staircases hollowed by knees, the thresholds of sanctuaries grooved by feet. The contact was never vandalism in the actors' own account. It was the devotional act itself, the moment the petition left the realm of words and entered the body, and the wear it caused was read for centuries as evidence of the shrine's power rather than of the crowd's destructiveness, proof that the traffic between the living and the holy was heavy at that gate. A shrine whose image showed no wear was a shrine whose petitions had gone elsewhere. The custodians of such places managed the consumption as Verona manages it, by expectation and, when the object was fabric, by renewal: vestments, coverings, and hangings at the great cult sites were replaced as devotion consumed them, and the replacement was part of the cult's ordinary economy rather than a scandal within it.

Shakespeare's audience stood inside that economy and needed no gloss for the ball scene's conceit. When Romeo casts Juliet as the shrine and his hand as the pilgrim, the metaphor's force depends on a congregation that knew images were touched, that the touching was licensed by the image's holiness, and that the licence had limits the sonnet then negotiates line by line. What the Reformation had interrupted in England, the practice itself, survived in the language as the sonnet's living furniture, and the play exported the grammar to every audience that followed. The courtyard at Verona is where the grammar came home to material form: a Catholic city, a bronze image, a heroine petitioned in the posture of a saint, and a crowd performing the act the play had described as metaphor. The rite's defenders sometimes call it a tradition of the place. The archaeology corrects the claim's scale. It is a tradition of the species of act, practised wherever represented bodies are set within reach of wishes, and Verona's distinction is not the act but the archive: nowhere else has the grammar consumed a modern figure fast enough for the consumption to be documented year by year, in newspapers, by the custodians themselves.

One contrast inside the archaeology sharpens the statue's case. The Vatican foot belongs to a body the tradition holds to be a saint's likeness, anchored in a tomb the basilica claims to keep; the touch there is a transaction with a presence. The Verona figure anchors nothing, represents a person who never lived, and stands over no grave, the heroine's supposed tomb being a separate exhibit across the city with an empty sarcophagus of its own. The petitions at Verona are therefore addressed to the story alone, unmediated by relic, bone, or burial, and the archaeology's lesson is that the mediation was never the mechanism. The mechanism is the represented body at hand height and the wish in the visitor. Presence is the optional extra the history of the practice keeps discarding, at Verona most completely of all.

## An invented tradition with no inventor: Hobsbawm, Bronner, and MacCannell

Three accounts of how such a rite comes to exist compete for the statue, and the article's central adjudication is among them. The first is Eric Hobsbawm's. In the introduction to The Invention of Tradition (1983), the cultural historian defined the invented tradition as a set of practices, normally governed by accepted rules and of a ritual or symbolic nature, which seek to inculcate values by repetition and imply continuity with a suitable historic past. The Casa di Giulietta is a Hobsbawmian site almost to the letter: an identification implied by a pun, a restoration that composed the past it claimed to recover, a civic brand built on the composition. A reader armed with Hobsbawm will file the breast-touching under the same heading without pausing: one more ritual manufactured by the shrine to thicken its atmosphere, its continuity with the past implied by the word tradition and false in the same way.

The filing fails on the statue's evidence, and the failure is instructive. Hobsbawm's invented traditions have inventors, identifiable agencies that formalize the rite and propagate it for their own purposes; the formalization is the point of the analysis. For the touching custom, no agency can be produced. The Lions Club placed a likeness and vanished from the record. The municipality's materials describe the belief in the passive voice and have never prescribed it; the city's interventions in the courtyard, fines for notes stuck to walls, the rationing of entry, run against the crowd's practices more often than with them. The guidebook industry amplified the custom, but amplification is not invention, and the earliest strata of the record show the practice already formed and being reported, not launched. Hobsbawm supplies the right category for the house and the wrong one for the hand.

The second account is the folklorist's. Simon J. Bronner, in Campus Traditions (2012), compiled the modern record of statue-touching as folk practice: the noses, feet, and hands of campus bronzes across America, John Harvard's foot at Harvard, Abraham Lincoln's nose at the University of Illinois, rubbed bright by generations of students seeking luck in examinations and love. Bronner's catalogue establishes the crucial fact about the gesture: it is generic. Given a bronze body at hand height and a community with wishes, the touching custom arises without instruction, attaches to whatever part the composition or the joke designates, and justifies itself afterwards with a luck formula fitted to the site's story. No ministry of culture is required. On Bronner's evidence, the Verona rite is a local instance of a worldwide folk reflex, its love-luck formula drawn from the heroine's story the way the Harvard formula is drawn from the examination calendar. The sculptor supplied the protuberance; folklore did the rest.

The third account is Dean MacCannell's. In The Tourist (1976), the theorist of tourist ritual described sight sacralization as a staged process: a sight is named, framed and elevated, enshrined, mechanically reproduced, and finally socially reproduced, as groups name themselves and their places after it. The Casa exhibits the process whole, and the companion study of the city's geography applies MacCannell's stages to the house's career. The statue's ritual, on this reading, is the mechanism by which sacralization completes itself in the visitor's body: the marker system, guidebooks, portals, the shine itself, instructs the pilgrim where the sacred point lies, and the touch is the pilgrim's ratification of the marking. MacCannell explains the rite's transmission better than Bronner, whose folklore arises but does not obviously travel, and better than Hobsbawm, whose inventors cannot be found. What neither MacCannell nor Bronner quite states, and what the statue's material record forces into the open, is that this rite writes its archive into its object. Sacralization at Verona did not stop at framing and naming. It proceeded to erosion.

The adjudication follows from the three accounts' respective failures and successes against the physical evidence. Hobsbawm is right about the site and wrong about the statue: the house is an invented tradition with inventors, and the superstition is an invented tradition without one, and the difference is the article's whole subject. Bronner is right about the gesture's origin, a folk reflex documented across continents and centuries of bronze, and his catalogue acquits the Veronese crowd of mere gullibility: the visitors are performing a recognizable human practice, not a municipal trick played upon them. MacCannell is right about the rite's propagation, since the marker system of guidebook, portal, and polished metal demonstrably instructs each new cohort. The verdict that accommodates all three is the one the dispatch of the evidence compels: the superstition is unscripted at its origin, scripted only in its transmission, and unique among the courtyard's phenomena in owing its existence to the visitors it serves. Verona decreed a house, a balcony, a tomb, and a letter office. It never decreed a touch. The touch is the myth's one democratic artefact, and its archive, the wear, is accordingly the only archive at the site that nobody curated.

### Was the luck custom invented by Verona's tourist office?

No document supports an official invention, and the circumstantial record points the other way. The body that placed the statue in 1972 was the Lions Club of Verona, a service organization honouring the sculptor's memory, and the club prescribed no ritual.

The municipality's own descriptions report the belief as an existing fact of the courtyard rather than a practice the city instituted, and the city's documented interventions at the site have regulated the crowd's devotions, fining the notes on the walls and rationing entry, rather than authoring them. Guidebooks repeated and spread the custom after it existed. The founding act that Hobsbawm's model requires cannot be produced for the touching rite.

A harder question hides inside that one: if nobody invented the custom, when did it begin? The evidence permits bounds rather than a date. The rite cannot predate the figure it uses, so its terminus is the placement of 1972; the tradition's own claim to antiquity, repeated in traveller accounts that call the belief ancient, collapses against that single fact, since a custom younger than the moon landings wears the word ancient only as a costume. At the other bound, the practice was sufficiently established to be the courtyard's signature act in the guidebook literature of the following decades, described as the thing visitors do rather than as a novelty. The polish itself is the intermediate evidence: bright metal is cumulative, and by the time journalists began remarking the shine as a phenomenon, the rite had been running long enough to re-surface bronze. The honest chronology is therefore a bracket, 1972 to the guidebook record, inside which an unknown number of unknown hands converted a commemorative likeness into a luck instrument, and the conversion left no minute, no memoir, and no name. Folklore of this kind is usually reconstructed from texts. This instance can be reconstructed from a wound.

## The physics of devotion: patina, sweat, and the first wound

Bronze keeps what it is given. A cast figure outdoors acquires a patina, the dark skin of oxidation that protects the metal and records its exposure, and touching interrupts that record in a specific way. The hand removes the patina mechanically, grain by grain, and deposits in its place the chemistry of skin: moisture, salts, and the mild acidity of sweat, which attacks the exposed surface and keeps it bright. The result, visible in every photograph of the courtyard figure, is a two-tone object: the dark, weathered bronze of the untouched passages and the gold-bright metal of the touched ones. The shine that visitors read as the statue's beauty, and guidebooks describe as her golden breast, is not a finish applied by the foundry. It is the colour of bronze with its protective skin worn off, the metal's nakedness, and it is beautiful in the way a scar is legible. The statue's most famous feature is its damage at an early stage.

The damage proceeded in a documented sequence, and the sequence converts the superstition from anecdote into case history. The shine came first, remarked as charm. Then the thinning: constant contact, tens of thousands of hands a year across four decades, does not merely clean the surface but removes it, and the removal concentrates where the contact concentrates, at the point of the breast the ritual specifies. The Italian reporting of the statue's later crisis gives the mechanism in the custodians' own terms, the acidity of the sweat of hands combined with continuous physical pressure on the material. By the time the alarm was raised, the wear at the breast had passed from polish to perforation in places: the metal was worn through. A second failure ran in parallel. The figure's raised right arm, the one visitors leaned on and hung from while posing for photographs, developed a fracture, and the Juliet Club's president, Giulio Tamassia, put the behaviour on record when the alarm was first raised in 2013: people climb up onto the base of the statue and hang onto her. The statue was being dismantled by affection at two points, the breast by the rite and the arm by the photograph.

The custodians' response, when it came, was framed as conservation rather than correction, and the framing is itself evidence. Erika Righetti of Verona city council, announcing the intervention in 2014, attributed the figure's condition to wear and tear from touching and from rain and weather, a formulation that distributes the blame between the crowd and the climate in proportions no conservator believed. Mayor Flavio Tosi supplied the stake: the statue of Juliet, with the Roman arena, was the best known symbol of Verona in the world. The best known symbol of the city was being rubbed away by the devotion that made it the best known symbol, and the city's problem had no solution inside the logic of the shrine, since the rite could not be forbidden without falsifying the shrine's welcome and could not be permitted without consuming the shrine's centrepiece. The solution eventually chosen, substitution, is the shrine's oldest technology: the balcony itself is tomb marble standing in for an architecture that never existed. Verona replaced the statue with its own image and let the rite continue, having calculated, correctly as events proved, that the rite was addressed to the figure and not to the original metal.

### How fast does a bronze wear through under ritual touching?

The courtyard supplies the only calibrated answer the practice has produced, and it divides into two measurements. Costantini's original withstood the rite for forty-two years, from 1972 to 2014, before the breast was worn through and the arm fractured, a span that included the decades before mass tourism reached its later volume.

The replica, installed in 2014 with an expected service life of just under fifty years, showed a perforation at the same point within ten. The acceleration between the two measurements is the material's own graph of the courtyard's intensifying traffic, drawn in millimetres of lost bronze rather than in the admission statistics the museum keeps.

The physics carries one more implication the custodians have been slow to say aloud. A rite whose medium is abrasion cannot be rendered sustainable by any management of the queue, because the damage is not a side effect of the devotion but its substance: every fulfilled petition removes its fraction. The only variables are the thickness of the bronze and the number of the petitioners. A thicker cast buys years; a thinner crowd buys years; neither changes the equation's direction. The statue's file is therefore a countdown in two columns, and the second column, the copy's decade, is the more alarming document, since it was compiled under modern conservation advice with the first column's fate fully known.

## 2014: the retirement, the foundry, and the letters inside

The original was withdrawn from the courtyard in February 2014, after forty-two years of service. The city commissioned a local foundry to produce an exact replica, a mould taken from the original so that the copy could stand in the courtyard while the original passed into museum keeping. The copy was installed in the courtyard by June 2014, when the Veronese press announced the statue's return home in its new body. The original, withdrawn from touch, is kept inside the house museum, where the figure that absorbed the rite's first four decades stands as an exhibit about the rite itself. The division of labour between the two bodies is exact and a little melancholy: the copy receives the devotion, the original keeps the evidence.

The cost of the substitution is a small study in the record's habits, since the figures divide and no published municipal invoice settles them. At the commissioning in 2014, British reporting put the foundry's price at 20,000 euros, with about a week required to make the mould. A decade later, press accounts of the copy's own damage repeatedly priced the replica at 15,000 euros, a figure the British tabloid conversion rendered as 12,800 pounds. The two numbers cannot both be the invoice, and the honest treatment is the one applied here: the casting cost Verona a sum reported between fifteen and twenty thousand euros, modest against the courtyard's earnings and beside the point either way, since the purchase was never of a statue but of time. The wider accountancy of the lovers, the admissions, weddings, and royalties that the legend generates for the city, belongs to the companion audit of [Verona's economy built on two fictional lovers](/2029/07/27/verona-economy-fictional-lovers/); the statue's own line in that ledger is the foundry bill, the conservation file, and the standing expectation of a third cast.

Then the restoration produced the strangest document in the statue's file. When the restorers took the withdrawn original in hand, they found the hollow bronze filled with deposits: hundreds of love letters, notes, and the keys of love locks, pushed into the figure through the cracks that the rite had opened in her breast and arm. The report, carried in February 2015 as the restoration proceeded, described the interior of the statue as completely filled with the pilgrims' paper. The discovery reframes the damage entirely. The cracks were not only wounds. They were an aperture, and the crowd, finding its saint's body breached, had used the breach as a letter box, posting petitions into the figure's interior in the oldest votive grammar there is: the offering inserted into the image. The statue had become a reliquary in spite of itself, its hollow body stuffed with the same confessions the courtyard's walls and the Juliet Club's office receive in their sanctioned channels, the overflow of a devotion that every official receptacle in Verona exists to manage. The full economy of those letters, the volunteers who answer them and the office that sorts them, belongs to the companion study of [the Juliet Club and the letters to Juliet](/2029/06/29/juliet-club-letters-to-juliet/); the statue's share of that economy is the unsanctioned fraction, the letters that bypassed the secretaries and went into the bronze directly.

The episode deserves its place at the centre of the statue's biography because it is the one event in the record that no party to the cult intended and every party illuminates. The sculptor cast a hollow figure, as founders do. The rite cracked it, as abrasion does. The crowd interpreted the crack as an invitation, as crowds at shrines have interpreted apertures in images since pilgrims began pressing offerings into the hands and wounds of saints. And the custodians, opening the figure, found themselves holding a votive deposit of exactly the kind that archaeologists recover from ancient shrines, assembled by accident in four decades inside a twentieth-century bronze. The statue's interior is the courtyard's truest archive in miniature: uncurated, undated, anonymous, and composed entirely of use. The wear on the breast is the archive's index; the letters inside are its documents.

The retirement also settled, by experiment, the question the custodians had debated in the abstract: whether the rite was attached to the original object or to the figure it embodied. Relic cults attach to the object, and the translation of a relic is a crisis. The courtyard registered no crisis. The copy was installed in June 2014 and the touching resumed upon it without a recorded pause in the practice, the queue re-formed, and the polish began its second accumulation on schedule. The experiment's result is the strongest evidence in the statue's file for the folklorist's account over any relic logic: the crowd's devotion is addressed to a role, the heroine in her courtyard, and any faithful body cast for the role will serve. Verona's custodians had, without intending any philosophy, run the control experiment of substitution that tests a cult's object-attachment, and the cult failed the relic test completely. What the visitors venerate is not Costantini's bronze. It is the place where the heroine can be touched, and the bronze is whoever fills the place.

## The foundry's part: mould, cast, and the ethics of the spare

The substitution of 2014 deserves a slower telling, because the foundry's work is where the custodians' philosophy became metal. The sequence is documented in the press of that year. The alarm over the original's condition had been raised in 2013, when the Juliet Club's president described visitors climbing onto the base and hanging from the figure. In February 2014 the original was withdrawn from the courtyard, and the city commissioned a local foundry to make an exact replica. The method was the founder's standard answer to a worn original: a mould taken from the figure itself, a process the council's spokeswoman estimated at about a week, from which the new bronze could be cast with the original's every dimension, including, at the moment of moulding, the original's accumulated wear. By June 2014 the copy stood in the courtyard, and the Veronese press announced the homecoming in the natural vocabulary of the shrine: the statue of Juliet returns home, the copy installed in the courtyard.

A new cast leaves a foundry with an even, founder's surface, and the copy therefore began its service in a condition the courtyard had not seen since 1972: uniformly surfaced, its breast the same tone as its shoulder, the rite's target invisible except to those who already knew the liturgy. The point is worth dwelling on, because it isolates the rite's true carrier. For a season, the figure in the courtyard carried no advertisement of the custom, no shine to instruct the uninstructed, and the custom proceeded anyway, on guidebook instruction and the queue's memory, until the hands re-inscribed the marker on the new metal. The experiment the substitution ran was thus double. It proved the devotion indifferent to the object's originality, and it proved the devotion independent of the polish that supposedly recruits it. The shine is the rite's record and its reinforcer; the foundry's season of even bronze demonstrated that it is not the rite's cause.

The spare is the institution the foundry's work created, and it deserves naming as the shrine's mature form. A cult that consumes its centrepiece on a measurable cycle requires either prohibition, surrender, or supply, and Verona chose supply: the heroine is kept in production. The ethics of the arrangement divide the commentators the statue's file gathers. The conservation purist holds that a replica in the original's place deceives the visitor about the object's nature, whatever the labels disclose. The practice's defenders answer that the courtyard never sold authenticity in the first place, that its balcony is assembled tomb marble and its bedroom holds a film prop, and that the one honest transaction at the site is the rite itself, which the spare keeps possible. This article's verdict sides with the defenders on the fact and with the purists on the disclosure: the spare is legitimate because the rite is the patrimony, and the spare is respectable only while its status is confessed in the open, as the custodians, to their credit, confessed it in 2014 and again in 2024, when the wound in the second body was reported in the same public terms as the wound in the first.

One further entry belongs to the foundry's chapter, and it concerns the original's surface at the moulding. A mould taken from a worn figure copies the wear along with the form, and the replica installed in 2014 therefore carried, in its casting, the smoothed breast and the stressed arm of the body the rite had spent forty years shaping. The copy was never a restoration of Costantini's 1969 surface. It was a portrait of the original as the crowd had left it, which means the crowd is, in a strict founder's sense, among the replica's makers. The second figure's biography began where the first figure's ended, and the perforation that opened in the copy's breast in 2024 opened in metal that the mould had already thinned at the inherited spot. The custodians' fifty-year estimate, examined in its place below, never had a chance against that inheritance: the spare was born pre-worn at the exact point of its future failure.

## The Munich sisters: the custom emigrates

In 1974, two years after the courtyard placement, Verona exported the figure. Munich and Verona had been twin cities since 1960, and for the 150th anniversary of Munich's municipal savings bank, Verona's savings bank presented its partner institution with two casts of Costantini's Juliet. One was placed at Shakespeareplatz in Bogenhausen, across the Isar; the other beside the Old Town Hall at Marienplatz, in the centre of the old town. The gifts were diplomacy of the standard twinning kind, a heroine for a square named after her author, a figure for the town hall of a friendly city. What the diplomacy did not export, because it did not need to, was instructions. The custom travelled in the figure.

Both Munich casts show the Verona pattern: the right breast polished bright against the weathered bronze, worn by the same gesture performed for the same reason. Traveller accounts of the Marienplatz figure describe the shine and its cause in the same terms the Verona guidebooks use, and a local variation has been recorded alongside the imported rite: at Munich, flowers are placed in the figure's hand, a gentler petition laid over the touch, with both methods visible on the figure at once. The Munich evidence is the comparative proof the folklorist's account required. The breast-touching was not sustained by the Casa's atmosphere, its attendants, or its marketing, because it reproduced itself in a Bavarian square and a town hall passage where none of those instruments operated, within a different language, a different tourist economy, and a different relation to the play. The figure carries the rite the way a cutting carries a plant. Given the bronze body and the story, the practice regrows.

The export also clarifies the original's status, in a way the custodians have not emphasized. Verona speaks of the courtyard figure, since 2014, as the copy and the museum figure as the original, a two-body vocabulary. The Munich casts complicate the count: the courtyard object is a copy of a figure that has existed in multiple casts since 1974, and the uniqueness that relic logic requires was diluted by the sculptor's own foundry practice forty years before the substitution. Bronze is an editioned medium; a cast is an instance, not a singleton. The crowd's indifference to the 2014 substitution, which puzzled commentators who expected a relic's following to object, was thus grounded in the medium itself. The visitors had never been touching a unique object. They had been touching the current instance of a figure, and the instance changed in 2014 as the instance had been multiplied in 1974, without the rite noticing either event.

### Could the city simply fence the statue off?

The experiment has been run, in Paris, and its result is the standing warning to every custodian of a touched bronze. In 2004 the authorities at Père Lachaise fenced the effigy of Victor Noir behind barriers with a notice against indecent rubbing.

Protests by the tomb's devotees, led in public by the television presenter Péri Cochin, forced the fence's removal, and the deputy mayor who restored access, Yves Contassot, settled the matter with a formula that conceded the practice while pleading for moderation: the touching could continue, the rubbing should not. Verona's custodians, who have read the same file, chose substitution over prohibition in 2014, preserving the rite's access and replacing its object; the fence was never attempted at the Casa, and the Paris precedent explains why.

## A family of touched bronzes: Victor Noir, the Porcellino, and the Harvard foot

The Verona figure is the most famous living member of a family, and the family's other members establish the rite's pedigree beyond the reach of any marketing office. The patriarch is in Paris. Victor Noir, a journalist shot dead in 1870 by Prince Pierre Bonaparte, was reburied at Père Lachaise in 1891 under a bronze effigy by Jules Dalou that shows him as he fell, top hat beside him, with a realism that extended to a pronounced bulge in his trousers. The effigy's lips, groin, and feet are polished mirror-bright against the green patina of the rest, worn by generations of women performing a rite whose promises divide by body part: a kiss on the lips for a lover, the rubbed groin for fertility and a flourishing intimate life, a flower left in the hat in thanks. No committee invented Noir's cult either; the sculptor supplied a feature, folklore supplied the liturgy, and the cemetery supplied the century. When the authorities fenced the tomb in 2004 against what the notice called indecent rubbing, the devotees protested and the fence came down, an outcome no Verona custodian has needed explained since.

Florence keeps the family's Italian elder. The Porcellino, the bronze boar at the Mercato Nuovo, descendant of Pietro Tacca's seventeenth-century fountain figure, has his snout polished bright by the rite of the return: a hand on the muzzle, a coin placed in the open jaws, and good fortune if the coin falls through the grate below. The boar's cult shows the family's range of promise, since what his touch sells is not love but return, the traveller's wish to come back to the city, and it shows the family's antiquity in Italy, a luck rite on bronze that predates the Casa's composition by centuries in its Florentine lineage. The Harvard foot and the Illinois nose in Bronner's catalogue complete the family's modern register: examination luck, institutional belonging, the same grammar in a secular key. Across the family, the variables are the promise and the body part; the constants are the bronze, the protuberance, the crowd, and the shine.

Set against the family, the Verona figure's distinctions sharpen. She is the only member whose promise is love itself rather than fertility, return, or examination success, the only one who is a character rather than a person, and the only one whose body has been replaced in office while the cult continued uninterrupted. Victor Noir's effigy is the man himself in effigy, buried beneath it; the Porcellino is an animal portrait of no one; the Harvard figure is a sitter misnamed by tradition. Juliet is a fiction twice removed, a sculptor's imagining of a playwright's imagining, and the rite performed on her is addressed, strictly, to nobody. That the most abstract member of the family sustains the most intense cult is the family's deepest datum. The touch does not traffic in presence. It traffics in story, and the story with the least body behind it generates the most hands.

The gender asymmetry of the family also belongs in the record, because the objection section below turns on it. The rubbed parts divide by the figure's sex with uncomfortable consistency: the female figures are touched at the breast, the male figures at the groin or the foot, and the promises divide with them, fertility and love on one side, virility and luck on the other. Bronner's campus catalogue shows the same reflex in miniature. The asymmetry is not Verona's invention and cannot be charged to the Casa's account; it is the folk grammar of the practice wherever bronze bodies stand for wishes. But Verona's figure concentrates the grammar's female case in its purest form, a girl of thirteen in the fiction, petitioned at the breast by a laughing crowd, and the concentration is what drew the critics whom the statue's file must answer.

## The objection: folklore fun or a licensed grope

The critique arrived late in the rite's history and has never been answered by the rite's defenders so much as outlasted. Its fullest statement is Lisa Morrow's account in Hyperallergic, published in May 2021 after a visit in 2017, which records the scene from the balcony with a witness's specificity: the crowd below, men and women of many nations, the men performing the touch for the camera with an arm around the figure's waist, the jovial and unembarrassed atmosphere, the house attendant telling the custom's story with delight. Morrow's verdict is that the rite functions as a licensed transgression, a chance to perform, under folklore's alibi, a handling of a girl's body that the performers' own societies would sanction in any other setting, and that the statue's silence is the point: the figure cannot object, and her fictional age, thirteen in the play, makes the licence uglier the longer it is examined. The piece anchors its argument in a documented scandal: an American surgeon had included in a gynaecology conference presentation a photograph of himself, hand on the statue's breast, grinning for the camera, and the image opened a flood of complaints from female colleagues about harassment in his profession. The courtyard's joke, exported to a professional podium, became evidence in a different proceeding, and Morrow's essay is the record of that collision.

The objection has an Italian register as well. In December 2023, as the copy's wear was becoming news, a headteacher from a school in Tuscany criticized the breast-touching in print as sexist, a judgement the British reporting of the following March carried into the international file; the journalist Enrico Ferro, writing in il Mattino di Padova, confined himself to the conservation conclusion, that the damaged statue must be repaired, while leaving the practice's name to others. The Harvard Crimson's editorial of August 2023, surveying the courtyard for an American student readership, called the custom the questionable tradition and noted the irony of its birth date: the statue was installed, and the rite began, in the very years when the feminist movement in Italy was at flood tide, a coincidence the editorial leaves to resonate. These are named voices with a genuine disagreement between them and the custodians' position, which is the shrug of the passive voice: the legend exists, the visitors come, the figure is replaced when worn.

The adjudication this article owes is twofold, because the objection is two objections. As a description of the gesture's content, Morrow is right, and the family's comparative record supports her more strongly than her single courtyard: the folk grammar of touched bronzes assigns the female breast and the male groin as the luck-bearing parts with a consistency that no theory of innocent fun dissolves, and the Verona rite is that grammar performed on a figure the fiction ages at thirteen. A defence that begins by denying what the hands are doing has already lost. But as an explanation of the rite's persistence, the objection is incomplete in a way the record exposes. The practice survived its own exposure, in print, in the surgeon's scandal, in the headteacher's protest, and in the international coverage of two perforations, without measurable interruption, performed by women as well as men and defended by its performers as a petition about their own love lives rather than a joke at the figure's expense. The rite's centre of feeling, on the evidence of the letters found inside the bronze, is not leering but longing; the same crowd that laughs for the photograph also posts its confessions through the cracks. The verdict that fits the whole file is uncomfortable in both directions: the custom is a genuine folk devotion conducted in a gendered grammar that its critics correctly name, and its abolition by custodial fiat, attempted in Paris and never in Verona, belongs to the class of remedies the practice has already demonstrated it can survive. What the critics changed is not the rite but the record's honesty about it; the statue's file reads differently after 2021, and that change, not the fence that was never built, is the objection's durable achievement.

## The copy's decade: the second hole and the service life of devotion

In March 2024 the statue's file acquired its second wound and its most damning measurement. L'Arena, Verona's newspaper, reported a hole a few millimetres across in the right breast of the courtyard figure, which is to say of the replica installed ten years earlier, with smaller perforations noted at the breast and the right arm in the conservation commentary that followed. The cause assigned was the established one, the acidity of hand sweat under continuous pressure, and the custodians' estimate trailed behind the report: the copy had been expected to serve just under fifty years. It had reached its first perforation in ten. The international coverage of that March, in the British and trade press, asked the question the arithmetic dictates: if the original, cast for a quieter era, lasted forty-two years and the copy ten, what is the service interval of the third figure, and the fourth?

The numbers refuse the comforting reading that the first statue died of old age. The original's forty-two years divide into a long era of moderate traffic and a short era of mass pilgrimage; the copy lived its whole life in the second era and failed in a quarter of the time. The wear rate is a function of the queue, and the queue is the one variable the shrine's success keeps increasing. The custodians' own later decisions acknowledged the pressure from another direction: in December 2025 the municipality ended the courtyard's free entry and made access a paid, pre-booked ticket, a crowd measure the city framed as the monument's defence against its popularity. Whether the metering slows the bronze's consumption is a question the metal will answer in its own time. The equation the file establishes is indifferent to the ticketing regime: at the documented rate, a courtyard Juliet is a consumable with a service life, and the foundry is a standing supplier to a rite.

Conservation has names for this situation, and the names clarify the custodians' real choice. A sacrificial surface is one whose loss is accepted as the price of a practice's continuation; a replica installed to be worn is a sacrificial object entire. Verona has, without adopting the vocabulary, adopted the policy: the original is preserved by withdrawal, the copy is expended by design, and the rite proceeds on the expendable body. The policy is defensible, and this article defends it against the purist alternative, because the alternative demonstrated at Paris is prohibition, and prohibition of a folk rite by fence and notice has a documented record of failure even at a grave. What the policy requires, and what the record shows only intermittently, is candour about its terms: the copy should be presented to the crowd as a copy serving a term, the original's withdrawal explained as the rite's own consequence, and the estimate of the term published with the humility the second hole taught. A shrine that meters its visitors and replaces its centrepiece on a shortening cycle is managing a devotion, and the management is more respectable, not less, when its arithmetic is public.

The options in front of the custodians can be enumerated from the file's own precedents, and the enumeration shows why the present policy persists by elimination. Prohibition has the Paris record against it, a fence removed by protest within the year. Withdrawal without replacement would end the rite by ending its object, an act the 2014 decision already considered and refused when the city chose supply over surrender. Rotation, alternating two casts between courtyard and museum so that each rests while the other serves, would halve each figure's exposure while doubling the foundry's role, and it converts the copy's status from secret to system; nothing in the record says the custodians have adopted it, and the second hole's publicity suggests the single-body regime continues. A thicker recast, cast with additional metal at the breast in anticipation of the wear, is the founder's honest answer, and it concedes in the mould what the rhetoric resists: that the breast will be consumed and the casting should budget for the consumption. A sacrificial surface treatment, renewed as it wears, protects the metal while surrendering the shine that marks the rite, and the shine's loss would test the queue's memory exactly as the foundry's even bronze tested it in 2014, successfully. Every route except prohibition leaves the equation's direction unchanged. The custodians are not choosing between damage and safety. They are choosing the interval, the price, and the candour with which the consumption is announced, and the file's judgement is that the interval is shortening faster than the announcements have admitted.

## The choreography: queue, pose, and photograph

The rite has a fixed form, and the form is legible in every eyewitness account the file contains. The visitor crosses the courtyard, takes a place in the informal queue that forms at the figure's side, and waits for the predecessors to finish. The act itself is brief: a step forward, the hand placed on the right breast, a pause for the photograph, and the place yielded to the next petitioner. Around the act, a small theatre assembles itself. Companions hold the camera; the queue watches; the performed touch is timed to the shutter as much as to any private hope. Morrow's balcony testimony describes the social weather exactly: laughter, cheering among groups of young men, an older quietness in others, women performing the touch with a knowing smirk for the lens. The rite is simultaneously a petition and a performance, and the two registers do not cancel each other. A folk practice that could not survive being photographed would have died with the camera phone; this one was built by the camera as surely as by the wish.

The pose the photograph standardized deserves its own analysis, because the pose is where the rite's two meanings, devotion and display, physically separate. The petitioner's version of the touch is the flat hand laid on the breast, the sonnet's palm-to-palm contact translated to the figure's marked spot, held for the seconds the exposure needs. The performer's version, the one Morrow's essay prosecutes, adds the arm around the figure's waist and the grin, converting the petition into a trophy. Both versions abrade the same square centimetres, and the bronze cannot file them separately. The custodians' statistics of damage are thus a merged account of longing and clowning, and any honest reading of the wear must hold both entries at once, as the letters inside the figure argue for the longing and the conference photograph argues for the clowning. The statue's archive, like most archives, is morally illegible in the aggregate and eloquent only in its particulars.

Weddings bring the choreography its most formal register. Couples married at the Casa, in the ceremonies the house hosts, descend to the courtyard for photographs in which the bronze stands as the marriage's presiding fiction, and the touch, performed in wedding clothes before the assembled families, sheds the queue's irony entirely. The companion examination of the wedding trade at the house treats that commerce in its place; the statue's share is the image, the bride and groom flanking a figure whose breast two generations of strangers have polished with petitions for exactly the state the couple has reached. In the wedding photograph the rite reads as blessing rather than superstition, the crowd's accumulated hope made available, by contact's logic, to the pair who pose as its fulfilment. The bronze is the only wedding guest at the Casa who attends every ceremony, and the photographs make her the marriage's witness in the strict folk sense: the one who was there, touched by everyone, believed in by the pose if not by the doctrine.

The queue itself is the rite's institutional form, the only one it has. No attendant directs it, no ticket times it, no sign instructs it, and yet it assembles daily in the same place with the same etiquette of turn and witness. The municipality's later rationing of courtyard entry altered the queue's supply without altering its form, since a booked crowd queues exactly as a free one did. Students of ritual have a term for such self-organizing repetition, and the statue's file supplies a pure case: a liturgy with rubrics and without a rubricator, maintained by imitation alone. Each arrival learns the rite from the bodies ahead in line, which is why the rite's target has never drifted, why the left breast stays dark while the right breast shines, and why the practice survived the substitution of its object without a day's confusion. The liturgy is stored in the queue, and the queue re-forms every morning.

## The transmission: how a touch travels

A custom without an inventor still requires a distribution network, and the statue's file lets the network be traced through its vectors. The first vector is the guidebook sentence, whose grammar is the passive of folklore: it is said, tradition holds, visitors believe. The sentence performs two services at once, reporting the rite and prescribing it, since a traveller told that visitors touch the breast for luck has been handed both the news and the instruction in a single clause. The national tourism portal, Italia.it, repeats the promise in its own voice, that the gesture brings fortune above all to those seeking love, which gives the rite the nearest thing to an official imprimatur it possesses: not an institution's invention, but an institution's echo. Tour leaders at the courtyard deliver the sentence aloud, at the figure's side, to groups already disposed by the queue's example, and the sentence and the example together recruit each cohort in under a minute.

The second vector is the shine itself, and it is the more powerful of the two. A guidebook sentence can be skipped; a patch of gold-bright metal on a dark figure cannot be unseen, and it poses its question to every arrival: why is that part bright? The answer is the rite, and the asking is the recruitment. MacCannell's marker theory describes the mechanism in its general form, the marker creating the sight by designating its significance, but the statue improves on the theory, since its chief marker is manufactured by the practice it marks. The polish is at once the rite's advertisement, its instruction manual, and its proof of antiquity, the visible evidence that the custom is real because thousands have done it. No other element of the Verona cult feeds on itself so efficiently. The balcony's fame requires the play, the films, and the restoration's story; the breast's fame requires only the breast's brightness, renewed by every hand it attracts.

The third vector is the photograph, and the fourth is its digital circulation. The courtyard pose was standardized in the era of the companion-held camera and industrialized in the era of the front-facing one, and each circulated image carries the rite to audiences who have not yet booked a ticket, pre-instructing them in the gesture and its spot. The statue's fame in the image economy is disproportionate even within the Casa's own: the balcony photographs as architecture, grand and distant, while the figure photographs as an encounter, a person-shaped object at arm's length with a person's hand upon it. The image of the touch is the cult's most reproduced icon, and its reproduction is the rite's missionary arm. When the second hole made news in March 2024, the coverage illustrated itself with the same pose from its archives, so that even the conservation crisis was transmitted to the public in the gesture's own iconography. The file's irony completes itself there: the wound and the advertisement are the same picture.

The fourth vector is the press itself, and the statue's file cannot be understood without it, because the rite's modern history is punctuated by news cycles that functioned as recruitment drives. L'Arena, the city's newspaper, has been the cult's chronicler of record, and its coverage follows a rhythm the custodians can set their calendars by: the alarm of 2013, the withdrawal and substitution of 2014, the discovery of the interior letters in 2015, the second perforation in 2024. Each cycle reported a conservation problem, and each report carried, in its illustrations and its explanatory paragraphs, the complete instruction set of the rite, the figure, the breast, the promise, delivered to a readership far larger than the courtyard's queue on any day it described. The international echoes amplified the pattern: the British coverage of 2014 and 2024 explained the custom to audiences who had never heard of the Casa, in the tones of amused disbelief that travel writing reserves for other people's devotions. Journalism did not invent the superstition, and nothing in this section should be read as the Hobsbawm charge returning by the back door. The press is a vector, not an author. But a vector that explains a rite while deploring its effects is a vector still, and the statue's biography keeps the receipts: the two great accelerations of its fame, and of its wear, follow the two great waves of its coverage, as the copy's decade of failure, lived entirely inside the age of the image feed and the news alert, measured against the original's quieter forty years.

## Three media of devotion: the gaze, the letter, and the hand

The courtyard offers its visitors three devotional media, and the statue's singularity appears only when the three are costed together. The balcony takes the gaze. Looking costs the looked-at nothing; the tomb marble Avena assembled has survived its century of being seen without measurable loss, and the gaze is the only transaction at the site that leaves both parties exactly as it found them. The walls and the letter office take the word. Writing costs the site a cleaning regime and the club a corps of volunteer secretaries, real costs, recurring and budgetable, but the medium itself is gentle: paper accumulates beside the monument rather than consuming it, and the accumulation can be cleared, archived, or answered without the monument's substance entering the account. The statue takes the hand, and the hand is the one medium whose every use is a withdrawal. Touch is devotion denominated in the object's own material, and the courtyard's three media thus form a scale of intimacy priced in the monument's body: sight free, script cheap, contact ruinous.

The scale explains the site's management history better than any theory of the crowd. The custodians have regulated each medium in proportion to its cost. The gaze required nothing until it required crowd control, and received it last, in the ticketing regime of the middle 2020s. The word required policing earliest in its unruly form, the notes stuck to the passage walls with chewing gum drawing fines in 2012, because paper's disorder is visible and its sanction easy. The hand required the gravest intervention, the replacement of the object itself, because its disorder is invisible until it is structural: a shine misread as beauty, a thinning discovered as a hole. The site's guardians met each medium with the instrument its cost dictated, fines for the paper, turnstiles for the gaze, a foundry for the hand, and the sequence of those instruments across the decades is the administrative shadow of the scale the three media form.

The scale also explains the statue's emotional primacy in the cult, the reason the figure and not the balcony stands at the centre of the visitors' accounts. Costly signals carry conviction that free ones cannot. A gaze proves attendance; a letter proves literacy and a stamp; a touch that visibly diminishes a bronze, performed in the knowledge of the queue and the photograph, is the visitor's body entered as evidence in the petition, the hand's brief pressure standing for the whole of a hope that could not be phrased. The petitions found inside the original's hollow body were the letters of visitors for whom the sanctioned media had failed, the word sent by the hand's route, through the wound the hand had made. In the courtyard's economy the statue is the high-denomination instrument, and its consumption is not the cult's accident but the cult's proof of seriousness, the price that certifies the petition was real. A shrine that replaced its centrepiece for any cheaper reason would be confessing a lesser devotion.

## The retired original: the museum's quietest exhibit

The figure inside the house has the strangest afterlife of any object in the cult, because its retirement inverted its nature. In the courtyard, Costantini's bronze was a working image, handled past endurance in the service of a hope. In the museum, the same bronze is a document, handled by nobody, in the service of a record. The transition was accomplished by a doorway: the figure crossed from the courtyard into the house in 2014 and changed ontologies in the passage. Visitors who meet the original indoors meet it under the museum's discipline, at viewing distance, among the house's other composed exhibits, the film costumes and the heroine's bed, and the meeting has a quality the courtyard never offered: the evidence can be inspected. The polished breast indoors is no longer an invitation. It is a datum, the rite's first four decades rendered as a surface, and the cracks that admitted the letters are part of the exhibit's testimony whether or not the labels say so.

The pairing of the two figures across the doorway is the site's most instructive accident. The Casa has always dealt in doubles, the real house and the imagined residence, the medieval stones and the composed facade, and the statue's doubling extends the series with a difference: this double was produced by the cult itself, worn into necessity by the visitors' hands. The copy outdoors certifies the original's importance, since objects are replaced at cost only when their function matters; the original indoors certifies the copy's meaning, since the wear on the retired figure is the biography the new figure is re-enacting at speed. A visitor who sees both has seen the cult's whole mechanism laid out in two bodies, the expendable and the preserved, the role and the record. No curator designed the lesson, because no curator designed the wear. The lesson is the crowd's work, exhibited by the crowd's consequences.

Conservation ethics has a settled discomfort with such arrangements, and the statue's file states it cleanly. The discipline's instinct is to protect the authentic object from consumptive use, which Verona obeyed by withdrawing the original; its second instinct is to distrust the replica, which Verona disobeyed by installing one as the cult's working body. The disobedience is the interesting act. A purist custodianship would have withdrawn the figure entirely and left the courtyard to its balcony, ending the rite by removing its object and falsifying nothing. The city instead judged the rite itself to be part of the patrimony, a living practice with a stronger claim than the bronze that hosted it, and supplied the practice with a renewable host. That judgement, made by a municipal administration in a foundry contract, is the most philosophically committed act in the statue's file: the intangible heritage was ranked above the tangible, the use above the object, by the very authority whose absence at the rite's birth this article has been documenting. The inventor Hobsbawm could not find at the custom's origin appears at its rescue, and what the authority invents, when it finally acts, is not the tradition but its permission to continue.

The original's museum years have one more entry to come, whenever the custodians publish a full condition account, and the file's honesty requires noting the gap. The public record gives the withdrawal, the discovery of the interior deposit, and the figure's indoor keeping; it does not give a conservator's measured survey of the surviving metal, the depth of the thinning at the breast, or the stabilization the fractures received. Those documents exist or will exist in the conservation file, and the statue's biography will not be complete until they are public. A cult whose archive is a wound deserves the wound's measurements on the record, and the custodians who published the second hole's millimetres in 2024 have already accepted the principle. The original's numbers are the last unpublished chapter of the courtyard's honest archive.

## Verdict: the courtyard's only honest archive

The verdict can be stated in its full form. Every other element of the Casa di Giulietta asks to be believed on some authority's word: the house on the antiquarians' identification, the balcony on the restoration's composition, the tomb across town on a tradition of showing, the letters on the club's organized faith. The statue asks to be believed on evidence that any visitor can inspect and no authority composed. The bright breast is the certain record of the hands; the crack is the certain record of their number's excess; the letters inside are the certain record of their hopes; the replica is the certain record of the custodians' surrender to the practice; the second hole is the certain record that the surrender changed nothing. The superstition is false in its promise, since no file of granted petitions exists and the granted could not be filed if it did, and the statue is true in its testimony, since everything the metal shows happened exactly as the metal shows it. A shrine whose relic is a novel's heroine can offer no truer object than that: an archive of the desire that built the shrine, kept in the only medium the desirers were allowed to write in.

The play, as usual, reached the verdict first. The fathers at the tomb promise statues of gold as the feud's settlement, images of the children raised by authority to close the account. Verona's account was closed differently, by a figure of bronze raised by a service club and finished by a crowd, gilded not by the founder's metal but by the petitioners' hands at the one spot their petitions used. The gilding is the difference between the play's world and the statue's. In the play, value descends from the fathers' decree. In the courtyard, value accumulated from below, touch by touch, until the accumulation destroyed its vessel and the vessel had to be renewed. If the series' long examination of the lovers' afterlife has a single object that proves the audience, and not the institutions, is the afterlife's true author, the bronze in the courtyard is that object, and its polished, perforated breast is the proof's signature. The statue is not the shrine's decoration. It is the shrine's confession, written by its visitors in the only script the shrine could not edit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who made the bronze statue of Juliet in Verona?

Nereo Costantini, a sculptor of Verona, made the figure, realizing the bronze in 1969 after a career spent in his native city's artistic life. Costantini was born at Nogara in 1905 and died at Verona on 5 August 1969, the year the statue was completed, which makes the courtyard figure a posthumous work: the sculptor never saw it installed and left no recorded word about its later career. The city and the Lions Club honoured him with a commemorative exhibition at the Museo di Castelvecchio in the autumn of 1972, the season the bronze took its courtyard place.

### Q: When was the bronze Juliet placed in the courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta?

The placement is dated to 1972 by the city's official tourism portal and by the record carried in this series, three years after the sculptor's death. The statue arrived at the initiative of the Lions Club of Verona, the service organization that had championed Costantini's memory, in the same autumn that the Comune and the club mounted his commemorative exhibition at Castelvecchio. A minority dating attaches the placement to 1973, the year the house opened as a furnished museum, and the division is understandable, since figure and museum belong to one phase of the shrine's completion. The sequence itself, made in 1969 and placed by 1972 or 1973, does not divide.

### Q: What are visitors said to gain by touching the statue's right breast?

Luck in love is the promise, in the form the courtyard repeats: the touch of the right breast brings fortune to the unlucky in love and, in the phrasing of the national tourism portal Italia.it, helps the toucher find true love. The formula is fitted to the heroine's story, since the figure petitioned is literature's most famous lover, and the petition is made at the body's seat of feeling. No file of granted petitions exists, and the promise has no guarantor, no office, and no priesthood. What the touch demonstrably delivers is the rite itself, a place in the queue's succession and a fraction of the breast's bronze, removed by the petitioner's own hand.

### Q: When did the touching custom begin?

The custom began at some point after the figure's placement in 1972 and was established enough to be the courtyard's signature act in the guidebook literature of the following decades. Its birth cannot be dated more closely, because no document records it: no founding act, no prescription, no first practitioner's account survives in any file. What the evidence supplies is a bracket. The rite cannot predate the statue it uses, and the bright polish on the breast, which is cumulative, proves a long accumulation before journalists began remarking the shine as a phenomenon. Claims that the belief is ancient collapse against the placement date; the custom is younger than the space age and wears its antiquity as a costume.

### Q: Why is the statue's right breast polished gold while the rest of the figure is dark?

The shine is bare bronze showing through where the protective patina has been worn away. An outdoor bronze darkens as its surface oxidizes into a protective skin, and a touched place never keeps that skin: each hand strips the oxidation mechanically and deposits moisture, salts, and the mild acidity of sweat, which keeps the exposed metal bright. The result is the two-tone figure of the photographs, dark weathered bronze across the untouched passages and gold-bright metal at the breast the rite specifies. The famous golden breast is therefore not a founder's finish. It is the metal's nakedness, the colour of a surface whose protection has been removed by devotion, beautiful and diagnostic at once.

### Q: What damage did the touching do to Costantini's original statue?

Four decades of the rite wore the original's right breast through the metal in places and fractured its raised right arm. The perforation at the breast came from the petition itself, thousands of hands a year applying pressure and sweat's acidity to one small area until polish became thinning and thinning became holes. The arm's fracture came from the photograph, since visitors leaned on the figure and hung from it while posing, a behaviour the Juliet Club's president Giulio Tamassia described on record in 2013, when the alarm was raised: people climbed onto the base and held onto the figure. Rain and weather, cited by the city alongside the touching, completed the exposure's work on a surface already stripped of its patina.

### Q: Why was the original statue removed from the courtyard in 2014?

The original was withdrawn in February 2014 because the rite it hosted had consumed it past the point of safe display. The breast was worn through in places, the right arm was fractured, and the deterioration was structural rather than cosmetic, the product of the acidity of hand sweat under continuous pressure acting on metal for forty-two years. The city's stated reasoning joined the touching to rain and weather, and Mayor Flavio Tosi supplied the stake in the same season, calling the figure, with the Roman arena, the best known symbol of Verona in the world. The best known symbol could not be left to be rubbed out of existence, and it could not be fenced without falsifying the shrine's welcome, so the city chose substitution.

### Q: Where is Costantini's original statue kept?

The original is kept inside the house museum, withdrawn from the courtyard in 2014 and preserved beyond the reach of hands. The figure that absorbed the rite's first four decades stands indoors among the Casa's exhibits, where the museum's discipline replaces the courtyard's: viewing distance instead of contact, a label's terms instead of the queue's. The move inverted the object's nature, from a working image handled in the service of a hope to a document inspected in the service of a record. Its polished breast and its fractures are its testimony in that second career, the material summary of its service, and the pairing with the copy outdoors, the preserved original and the expendable replica divided by a doorway, is the site's most instructive accident.

### Q: What was found inside the original statue during its restoration?

Restorers who took the withdrawn original in hand found the hollow bronze filled with love letters, notes, and the keys of love locks, pushed into the figure through the cracks the rite had opened. The discovery was reported in February 2015 as the restoration proceeded, and the interior was described as completely filled with the pilgrims' paper. The crowd had used the statue's wounds as an aperture, posting petitions into the image's body in the oldest votive grammar, the offering inserted into the saint. The deposit reframes the damage: the cracks were wounds, and they were also a letter box, and the statue had become a reliquary in spite of itself, holding the unsanctioned fraction of the same devotion the Juliet Club's office answers through its channels.

### Q: How much did the replica that replaced the original cost?

The reported figures divide, and no published municipal invoice settles them. At the commissioning in 2014, British reporting put the local foundry's price for the exact replica at 20,000 euros, with about a week needed to take the mould from the original. A decade later, the press coverage of the copy's own damage repeatedly priced the replica at 15,000 euros, a figure also rendered as 12,800 pounds in British tabloid conversion. The honest statement is the range: the casting cost Verona between fifteen and twenty thousand euros. Either figure is modest against the courtyard's earnings, and beside the purchase's real object, which was never a statue but time, a renewed term of years for the rite's host body.

### Q: When did the replica develop a hole of its own?

In March 2024, ten years after its installation, the replica was reported with a hole a few millimetres across in its right breast. L'Arena, Verona's newspaper, carried the finding, and the conservation commentary that followed noted smaller perforations at the breast and the right arm as well. The cause assigned was the established mechanism, the acidity of hand sweat combined with continuous physical pressure on the material. The speed of the failure is the datum that matters: the copy accomplished in a decade the perforation that had taken the original four decades, a difference that measures the intensification of the courtyard's traffic more precisely than any admission statistic, since the wear rate is a function of the queue.

### Q: How long was the replica expected to last?

Just under fifty years was the service life assigned to the replica at its installation, as the custodians' estimate was reported when the figure's damage became news. The expectation presumably rested on the original's precedent, forty-two years of service before withdrawal, adjusted for modern conservation oversight. The first perforation arrived within ten years, which falsified the estimate's premise: the original's span included decades of moderate traffic, while the copy has lived its whole life in the era of mass pilgrimage. A service life computed from the first figure's history was bound to flatter the second figure's prospects, and the March 2024 reports carried the custodians' acknowledgement that the term would be shorter than the fifty years projected.

### Q: Why are there statues of Juliet in Munich as well?

Two casts of Costantini's figure stand in Munich because Verona gave them. The two cities had been twinned since 1960, and in 1974, for the 150th anniversary of Munich's municipal savings bank, Verona's savings bank presented its partner institution with two replicas of the courtyard figure. One was placed at Shakespeareplatz in Bogenhausen, the square named for the heroine's author, and the other beside the Old Town Hall at Marienplatz. The gifts were standard twinning diplomacy, a heroine exported as a token of friendship. What travelled with the figures was more than the diplomacy intended, since both Munich casts developed the Verona pattern, the right breast polished bright by the same gesture performed for the same reason.

### Q: What other famous statues are rubbed for luck?

The best documented is the effigy of Victor Noir at Père Lachaise in Paris, Jules Dalou's bronze of 1891, whose lips, groin, and feet shine against the green patina from generations of fertility and love petitions. Florence keeps the Porcellino, the bronze boar at the Mercato Nuovo, whose snout is polished by the rite of the return, a hand on the muzzle and a coin in the jaws for fortune in coming back to the city. The folklorist Simon J. Bronner's Campus Traditions catalogues the American register in 2012: John Harvard's foot, Abraham Lincoln's nose at the University of Illinois, and a company of campus bronzes rubbed bright for examination luck. The family shares a grammar everywhere: bronze, a protuberance, a wish, and a shine.

### Q: Did Costantini live to see his statue in the courtyard?

He did not. Costantini died at Verona on 5 August 1969, the year the bronze was realized, and the courtyard placement came in 1972, three years after his death. The figure is therefore a posthumous presence from its first day, and the sculptor never saw a visitor touch it, never saw the polish gather, and never commented on the rite that would make his work famous. The commemorative exhibition at Castelvecchio in the autumn of 1972, mounted by the Comune and the Lions Club in the placement's own season, was an act of mourning as much as an installation. Every meaning the statue carries in use was assigned after its maker's death, by hands he never anticipated, which is the foundation of the argument that the cult was made by use rather than by design.

### Q: Which lines at the end of the play does the courtyard statue seem to fulfil?

Montague's vow over the bodies supplies them: "For I will raise her statue in pure gold, / That whiles Verona by that name is known, / There shall no figure at such rate be set / As that of true and faithful Juliet" (5.3). Capulet's answer, promising Romeo's statue "as rich" beside hers, completes the commission the play issues and never shows executed. The courtyard figure fulfils the vow with every term altered: bronze for gold, though the hands have gilded the used part of it; Juliet alone, without the matching Romeo; a service club and a municipality for the penitent fathers; and a crowd's handling for the distance gold would have commanded. The vow's economics survive the translation, since the figure did acquire a measurable rate, in foundry bills and service lives.

### Q: Why do critics object to the breast-touching ritual?

The objection, stated most fully by Lisa Morrow in Hyperallergic in 2021, is that the rite licenses a handling of a girl's body that its performers' own societies would sanction anywhere else, performed as a laughing photo opportunity on a figure the fiction ages at thirteen. Morrow anchored the charge in a documented scandal: an American surgeon had presented a conference photograph of himself, hand on the statue's breast, and the image opened complaints from female colleagues about harassment in his profession. An Italian headteacher criticized the custom in print as sexist in December 2023, and the Harvard Crimson's editorial of the same year called it the questionable tradition. The critics' shared point is that folklore's alibi does not change what the hands are doing.

### Q: Did replacing the original with a copy change the ritual?

The replacement changed nothing in the practice, and the non-event is among the most instructive entries in the statue's file. The copy was installed in June 2014, the queue re-formed at its side, the touching resumed on the new metal, and the polish began its second accumulation without a recorded pause. A relic cult would have registered the substitution as a crisis, since relic devotion attaches to the object itself; the courtyard registered no crisis, which proved the devotion is addressed to the figure and the place rather than to Costantini's bronze. The Munich casts had diluted the object's uniqueness as early as 1974, bronze being an editioned medium, so the visitors had never been touching a singleton. The copy's perforation within ten years closed the experiment: the rite treats its host as a role, and any faithful body cast for the role will serve, and be consumed.
