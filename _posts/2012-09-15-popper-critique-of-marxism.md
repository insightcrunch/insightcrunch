Karl Popper is the critic of Marxism whose name is known to people who have never read a page of him, and the version of his argument that circulates under his name is one he would have rejected as a misstatement of his own case. Ask what Popper showed, in a seminar room, an examination hall, or the comments beneath an article, and the answer arrives in a single sentence: Popper proved that Marxism is unfalsifiable, and therefore a pseudoscience. The sentence is confident, quotable, and wrong in a way that matters, because it reverses the order of Popper's actual argument, discards the part of the argument he himself considered the more damaging, and attributes to him a contempt for Marx that his pages conspicuously do not contain. Correcting the summary is not a courtesy to a dead philosopher. It is the precondition for knowing what the critique actually establishes, what it leaves standing, and where the honest replies to it are to be found.

![Popper's attack on Marxist method, falsifiability and historicism separated and answered in turn - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-48.webp)

The correction this article defends can be stated at the outset, because everything that follows is its elaboration. Popper made two independent arguments, separated by subject, by text, and by logical type. The first is a historical argument about what happened to a theory: Marx's theory, on Popper's account, began as a genuinely scientific construction, staked itself on predictions, was refuted by the events it had forecast wrongly, and was then rescued by followers who reinterpreted it until nothing could count against it. The second is a logical argument about what is possible: no theory, Marxist or otherwise, can predict the future course of history, because that course depends on the future growth of human knowledge, and future knowledge cannot be predicted by any rational method without ceasing to be future knowledge. The first argument is about Marxism's conduct after a failure. The second is about the structure of prediction itself. Merging them, as almost every summary does, weakens both: it makes the first sound like a definitional trick and the second sound like a partisan opinion, when in fact the first is an empirical claim about a tradition's behaviour and the second is a piece of logic that applies to every philosophy of history ever written. This article calls that separation the two-argument correction, and it treats the separation as the price of admission to the whole debate.

## The summary everyone repeats, and why it is wrong

Begin with the summary in its strongest common form, because it deserves to be understood before it is corrected. In that form, Marxism presents itself as a science of society, complete with laws, predictions, and a claim to have discovered the direction of historical development. Popper, the story runs, applied the obvious test: a scientific theory must forbid something, must expose itself to evidence that could prove it wrong, and Marxism forbids nothing, since every apparent failure is absorbed by reinterpretation. Revolution fails to arrive where it was expected, and the theory discovers false consciousness. Prosperity rises where misery was predicted, and the theory discovers that misery is relative. The state refuses to wither, and the theory discovers transitional necessities without a term. A theory compatible with every outcome predicts none of them, and so Marxism belongs not with physics but with astrology: impressive in vocabulary, empty in risk. That is the summary, and generations of students have been examined on it.

Three things are wrong with it, and they are worth separating because they have different weights. The first error is chronological. Popper's charge of unfalsifiability was not his starting point but his conclusion about a later phase: he held that Marx's own theory was falsifiable, that it was in fact falsified, and that unfalsifiability was the condition into which the theory was subsequently repaired by its defenders. A summary that begins with unfalsifiability has silently deleted the refutation, which for Popper was the evidential heart of the matter. The second error is one of scope. The summary treats the falsifiability charge as the whole of Popper's case against Marxist method, when Popper himself regarded his argument against historicism, the doctrine that history can be scientifically predicted, as the deeper and more decisive contribution, the one he laboured over longest and prefaced two books with. A reader who knows only the falsifiability charge has not met the argument Popper most wanted judged. The third error is tonal, and it is not trivial. Popper wrote about Marx with a respect that borders on admiration in places: he credited Marx with sincerity, with a burning protest against the evils of an unrestrained capitalism, with sociological insight he compared favourably to that of his contemporaries, and with having posed problems that social science still works on. The summary's sneering Popper, dismissing Marx as a charlatan, is a figure invented by the summary's transmitters, many of them Popper's own self-described followers. The irony is exact: a thinker whose central accusation against Marxists was that they protected a theory from its evidence has been protected by his admirers from his own text.

Why does the merged summary persist, when the primary sources are neither long nor obscure? Partly because it is teachable in a way the real argument is not. A single criterion, a single verdict, and a single villain make a lecture slide; two arguments of different logical types, an evidential narrative about a tradition, a concession that the original theory was scientific, and a reply literature running through Duhem, Quine, and Lakatos do not. Partly because the merged version is useful to both camps in a sterile debate: it gives opponents of Marxism a verdict of pseudoscience that appears to settle everything, and it gives defenders an easy target, since the caricature can be rebutted by pointing to any prediction Marx ever made. Each side prefers the weak version of the other. And partly because the genuinely strong parts of Popper's case are uncomfortable for the summary's users on both sides. The conventionalist twist, his account of how theories are immunised against evidence, describes a temptation in every intellectual tradition, not only one; and the argument against historicism condemns every doctrine of historical destiny, including several that the summary's transmitters have held in secular or religious forms. The two-argument correction removes the comfort. It leaves a critique that is narrower than the legend, stronger where it applies, and answerable only at the points where it actually presses.

This article therefore proceeds in the order the correction requires. It first reconstructs Popper's own intellectual apparatus, the demarcation criterion and its logic, from his earlier work in the philosophy of science. It then states the first attack in its full sequence: the testable theory, the failed predictions as Popper catalogued them, and the immunising repairs he charged the tradition with performing. It states the second attack separately: the definition of historicism, the argument from the growth of knowledge, and the distinction between laws and trends on which so much of it rests. Between those statements stands the article's findable artifact, the Popper argument ledger, which takes the critique apart into its components and assigns each an honest verdict. Only then does the article turn to the replies, which it deliberately draws from philosophy of science rather than from party literature, because the strongest replies to Popper were made by thinkers with no stake in defending Marx: the Duhem and Quine problem about isolated refutation, Lakatos's methodology of research programmes, the contested status of falsificationism itself, and the scholarly demolition of Popper's Hegel scholarship by Walter Kaufmann, which bears on how far the second volume of his great polemic can be trusted as intellectual history. The article concedes throughout what the evidence requires be conceded. The immunising record Popper pointed to is real, and no honest treatment can pretend otherwise. The question is what follows from it, and that question can only be answered once Popper's argument is the one he actually made.

## Popper before the attack: the philosopher of falsification

Popper arrived at Marxism late in his own development, and the route matters, because the criterion he applied to Marx was forged in a different quarrel. Born in Vienna in 1902, he passed through the intellectual ferment of a city that contained, within a few streets, the Vienna Circle's logical positivism, psychoanalysis in its founding generation, and a mass socialist movement whose theoretical culture was unusually serious. As a young man he was briefly drawn to Marxism itself, an episode he later described as instructive and chastening: what repelled him, on his own account, was not the doctrine's compassion but the willingness of its adherents to explain away the deaths of workers in a failed rising as necessities of a history they claimed to understand. Whether or not the autobiographical episode carries the weight he later placed on it, its shape is the shape of the mature critique: the objection was never primarily to Marx's economics but to a habit of mind that treated a theory as confirmed by everything and endangered by nothing.

The technical core of that objection was worked out in his first book, The Logic of Scientific Discovery, published in German in 1934. Its problem was demarcation: what distinguishes empirical science from other intellectual activities, including metaphysics, which Popper was careful not to call meaningless as the positivists did. His answer turned on an asymmetry in logic. A universal theory, of the form that all bodies attract or all swans are white, cannot be verified by any number of favourable observations, because the next observation is always unexamined; but it can be falsified by a single accepted observation of the forbidden kind. Science, Popper proposed, is the activity that exploits that asymmetry: a theory is scientific insofar as it divides possible observations into those it permits and those it forbids, and the more it forbids, the more it says. Testing is then not the accumulation of confirmations, which are cheap for a vague theory, but the sincere attempt to produce the forbidden observation. A theory that survives severe tests is corroborated, never proven, and remains a conjecture awaiting its next exposure. Everything in the later attack on Marxism is present in embryo in this framework: the suspicion of confirmation, the demand for risk, and the insistence that a theory's virtue lies in what it excludes.

Two features of the early framework need emphasis, because they return in the replies. First, Popper presented falsifiability as a methodological proposal, a rule of the game adopted by convention, not as a discovery about the meaning of words. He was explicit that the criterion could not itself be falsified and was not intended to be; it was a norm for scientific practice, defended by its fruits. That modesty, present from the start and repeated in his later qualifications, is routinely forgotten by those who wield the criterion as though it were a law of logic that theories violate at their peril. Second, Popper knew from the beginning that theories are never tested one bare statement at a time. A prediction is derived from a theory only with the help of auxiliary hypotheses and statements of initial conditions, so a failed prediction shows that something in the whole apparatus is wrong without announcing which part. His answer to that problem was not to deny it but to discipline it: the scientist must decide in advance which statements will be treated as the theory under test, and must not afterwards rescue the theory by blaming the auxiliaries whenever failure threatens. The distinction between a permissible revision, which increases a theory's content and its exposure, and an impermissible rescue, which merely patches the hole the evidence tore, is the entire machinery of the later charge against Marxism. Popper even catalogued the rescuing moves in his early work under the heading of conventionalist stratagems: introducing ad hoc hypotheses that add nothing testable, modifying definitions so the counterexample no longer counts, doubting the reliability of the inconvenient observation, and questioning the experimenter's competence. The charge against the Marxists of The Open Society is that they performed these stratagems on a historical scale.

The two books of the attack were wartime works, and their wartime character explains their temperature. The Poverty of Historicism was composed first in conception, presented to a seminar in Brussels in 1936 on Popper's own telling, published as a series of articles in the journal Economica in 1944 and 1945, and issued as a book in 1957 with a dedication to the victims of the belief in inexorable laws of historical destiny. The Open Society and Its Enemies, written while Popper held a lectureship in New Zealand, far from the European catastrophe it analysed, appeared in 1945 in two volumes, the first on Plato, the second, subtitled The High Tide of Prophecy, on Hegel and Marx. It was the second volume that carried the argument about Marxism's degeneration, together with a reading of Marx's method, his historicism, and his moral theory that remains the most influential hostile account in the language. Popper's later academic home was the London School of Economics, and his later philosophy of science, developed in Conjectures and Refutations and in the long postscript to his first book, refined but never withdrew the wartime verdict on Marxism. What it did, as a later section shows, is qualify the criterion in ways that complicate the verdict's popular form. The texts must be kept apart because they argue differently: The Open Society tells a historical story about a theory and its followers, while The Poverty of Historicism attempts a proof about the limits of prediction as such. Conflating the stories, in either direction, is the original sin of the secondary literature this article is written against.

## Falsifiability: the demarcation criterion stated properly

Before the criterion can be applied to Marx, it must be stated with the precision Popper himself insisted on, because most objections to the application are really objections to a slovenly version of the test. The criterion is not that a scientific theory must be false, nor that it must be simple, nor that scientists must abandon a theory at the first awkward result. It is that a theory must be capable of clashing with experience: there must exist some conceivable observation, describable in advance, which if accepted would count against it. The theory achieves this by being universal and restrictive at once. It speaks about all cases of a kind, and it declares that some arrangements of the world will not be found among them. Newtonian mechanics forbade a perpetual motion machine of the relevant kinds and staked itself on the return of comets and the behaviour of tides; Einstein's theory, Popper's favourite contrast, forbade specific deflections of light and could have been destroyed by the eclipse observations of 1919 had they fallen the other way. The theories he placed on the far side of the line, in the celebrated comparison of his early essays, were those whose adherents could explain every outcome within the theory's own vocabulary: whatever a man did, the psychoanalytic schools of Freud and Adler could exhibit it as confirmation, and whatever history produced, a certain kind of Marxist could exhibit as the working of the laws of development. A theory that explains everything, Popper concluded, explains nothing, because explanation requires exclusion.

### Why did Popper think falsifiability separates science from pseudoscience?

Because a theory earns empirical content by forbidding outcomes: the more it rules out, the more the world can say against it, and the more its survival of sincere tests counts. A doctrine compatible with every possible result takes no risk, so its confirmations are worthless and its explanations merely relabel what happened.

Three corollaries of the criterion matter for the Marx case. The first is that falsifiability is a property of a theory at a time, in a form, and not an eternal essence of a doctrine. A theory can begin falsifiable and be repaired into unfalsifiability, or begin vague and be sharpened into testability, and the interesting historical question is always which direction the repairs ran. This corollary is what makes Popper's treatment of Marx so different from the legend: because the criterion applies to states of a theory, his narrative of a falsifiable beginning and an immunised aftermath is not a concession wrung from him but the natural form his criticism takes. The second corollary is that severity is a matter of degree and of background knowledge. A prediction counts as a severe test when it is improbable relative to what was already believed, which is why the return of a comet was routine corroboration and the bending of starlight was a triumph: one was expected on any account, the other was expected on almost none. Applied to social theory, this corollary cuts in unexpected directions, since a theory of society that predicts what every observer already expects, that the rich defend their advantages and the poor resent theirs, risks little however often events comply. The third corollary is that Popper's demarcation is not a criterion of truth, importance, or even rationality in the wider sense. He insisted repeatedly that metaphysical ideas could be meaningful, fruitful, and historically indispensable to science itself, and that his quarrel was only with ideas that claimed the authority of empirical science while declining its discipline. Much confused polemic dissolves once that is granted: to call a doctrine unfalsifiable is not, in Popper's own usage, to call it nonsense, and several of his defenders and several of his critics have built careers on missing the distinction.

The criterion also carried, from the start, a doctrine about how scientists ought to behave when tests go wrong, and it is this doctrine, rather than the abstract criterion, that does the work against Marxism. Popper did not demand that a theory be surrendered at the first falsification; he knew that theories are born refuted, surrounded by anomalies, and that a dogmatic phase in a theory's defence can be productive. What he demanded was that the defence not cheat: any modification made in response to failure must increase, or at least not decrease, the theory's falsifiable content, and must itself be independently testable rather than tailored to the single embarrassing case. A modification that opens new risks is science continuing; a modification whose sole function is to neutralise the result already obtained is what he called a conventionalist twist, and its repetition converts a research enterprise into a defence mechanism. The standard is a standard of intellectual bookkeeping. It asks of any tradition a simple audit: when the world refused to comply, did you change your mind in a way that could be checked, or did you change the subject in a way that could not? Popper's historical claim was that Marxism, after Marx, failed that audit systematically. Whether he was right is a question about a record, and the record is where the argument must now go.

## The first attack: a scientific theory that failed its tests

The first attack is a narrative, and its force depends on the narrative being granted its opening. Popper opens by praising the theory he is about to convict. Marx, he wrote, had attempted something genuinely scientific: an explanation of social change driven by discoverable mechanisms, from which consequences followed about the future of the capitalist order that were not trivially guaranteed. The theory was not, in its original form, a vapour of dialectical phrases compatible with everything. It said that capitalism would concentrate production into ever larger units and ever fewer hands. It said that the intermediate strata would be ground down toward the two great classes. It said that the working class, organised and impoverished by the process itself, would become the overwhelming majority and the agent of the system's overthrow. It said that revolutions, when they came, would come where capitalism was most developed, because only there had the preconditions matured. And in the strong version Popper attributes to the theory's economic core, it said that the material condition of the workers under capitalism was subject to a downward pressure, an accumulation of misery answering to the accumulation of capital. These are claims with edges. Events could fail to sharpen them, and on Popper's reading, events did.

### Did Popper say Marxism is unfalsifiable?

Not originally. Popper argued Marx's theory was testable, made predictions, and was refuted by events, and that later Marxists rendered it unfalsifiable by adding rescuing hypotheses. He also argued separately that predicting the course of history is logically impossible, since it depends on future knowledge.

The refutations Popper catalogued were of a familiar kind, and their familiarity should not disguise their cumulative weight within his argument. The revolution did not arrive in the advanced industrial countries where the theory's logic placed it, and where revolution did transform a great state, in Russia, the country was among the least developed of the European powers, its industrial proletariat a small minority, its revolution carried through under conditions the theory had not forecast. The working classes of the advanced countries did not sink into uniform misery; real wages rose across the long run, the working day shortened, social insurance arrived, and the trade unions whose growth the theory could explain became, in many countries, pillars of a reformed order rather than gravediggers of the unreformed one. The intermediate strata declined in their old forms and replenished themselves in new ones, salaried, credentialed, and stubbornly unproletarianised. Class conflict itself, which Popper never denied as a phenomenon, refused the theory's expected simplification into a single front: national loyalties repeatedly overrode class loyalties at the moments of maximum strain, a fact the First World War exhibited on a continental scale within Marx's followers' own organisations. Popper's point was never that each of these facts, alone, executed the theory; theories survive failures. His point was that these were not peripheral embarrassments but the theory's central forecasts about the trajectory of the system it claimed to understand, and that a scientific tradition, faced with their failure, would have revised the core or lowered the claim. The Marxist tradition, he charged, did neither. It kept the claim and renovated the insulation.

It is worth pausing on the fairness of the catalogue, because the replies later in this article depend on distinguishing two questions that polemic constantly merges: whether Marx himself committed his theory to these forecasts in these strengths, and whether the tradition that governed parties and states in his name did so. Popper's reading leans heavily on the stronger, more prophetic Marx, the Marx of the Communist Manifesto's confident trajectories and of the more sweeping passages of Capital, and there is a real scholarly question about how far the hedges, conditions, and contrary tendencies Marx himself built into the analysis should soften the forecasts attributed to him. Marx wrote of tendencies operating against counteracting influences, and a tendency statement is a more complicated logical animal than a dated prediction, as a later section examines. But Popper's target, he would have replied, was not only the library Marx. It was Marxism as an operative doctrine, the theory in the hands of movements that did stake authority on the forecasts, did herald each event as their vindication, and did respond to disconfirmation by reinterpretation rather than revision. A critic is entitled to judge a doctrine partly by the intellectual conduct of the tradition that carries it, just as a theory of medicine is judged partly by what its practitioners do at bedsides. The first attack, properly located, is therefore an attack on a tradition's evidential conduct over half a century, resting on a claim about the founder: that the founder's theory was better than the conduct, because it had risked itself and lost, which is the most honourable failure science affords.

There is a further element in the first attack that summaries omit because it complicates the politics usually assigned to Popper. He did not treat the failure of the predictions as a triumph of the existing order, nor the persistence of class conflict as a discovery of his own. He wrote that Marx's analysis of capitalism's workings, its concentration, its crises, its transformation of social life, contained insights of permanent value, and that the theory's refutation concerned its prophetic superstructure rather than its entire analytical content. He likewise held that capitalism unrestrained had produced evils Marx described accurately, and his own political proposals in The Open Society, piecemeal social engineering, state intervention against exploitation, and protection of the economically weak, were closer to the social democracy of his century than to any doctrine of laissez faire. The first attack is therefore not a defence of capitalism against its critic. It is a charge of intellectual dishonesty against the critic's heirs: that they inherited a refuted prophecy and, rather than learning from its failure what Marx himself might have learned, taught generations to call the failure a confirmation. Every element of that charge depends on the sequence being kept straight: science first, refutation second, immunisation third. Delete the first two steps, as the legend does, and the third becomes an empty insult. Restore them, and the third becomes the most serious accusation one intellectual tradition can bring against another.

## The conventionalist twist: how a refuted theory was made irrefutable

The immunisation charge has a technical name in Popper's vocabulary, and the name matters because it locates the charge inside his general philosophy rather than in anti-Marxist animus. In The Logic of Scientific Discovery he had described the conventionalist twist as the manoeuvre by which a theory endangered by evidence is rescued by reinterpretation: the theory, or the evidence, or both, are redescribed until the contradiction disappears. The Open Society's verdict on later Marxism applies the phrase to a whole tradition. Followers of Marx, Popper wrote, reinterpreted both the theory and the evidence in order to make them agree, and in doing so they rescued the theory from refutation at the price of rescuing it from science: a theory saved that way can no longer be refuted by any imaginable event, because the reinterpretation is always available in advance. He compared the procedure to the pattern he had observed in the psychoanalytic schools of his Vienna youth, where every human action, and its opposite, could be exhibited as the theory's confirmation, and he drew the moral that a doctrine which has acquired that facility has exchanged explanation for incantation.

### What is a conventionalist twist, in Popper's sense?

A rescue performed on a theory in trouble: when evidence contradicts a prediction, the follower reinterprets the theory, the evidence, or both, until the contradiction dissolves. Because the move is always available, the theory becomes compatible with every outcome and forfeits the falsifiability that made it scientific in the first place.

The particular twists Popper catalogued repay attention, because their pattern, rather than any single instance, is his evidence. Where revolution failed to materialise in the advanced countries, the theory acquired the auxiliary doctrine of the labour aristocracy: a stratum of workers bribed, by the superprofits of imperialism, out of their revolutionary vocation. The addition explained the failure, but it explained it after the fact, and it sat awkwardly with the original claim that misery and organisation would do the work unaided. Where the workers' material condition improved, the doctrine of misery was quietly reinterpreted: misery became relative rather than absolute, a gap rather than a floor, a reading more defensible in itself but adopted, Popper charged, not because the evidence had suggested it independently but because the original reading had been falsified and the word had to be kept. Where the state declined to wither after revolution and instead swelled, transitional explanations multiplied without the transition acquiring a testable term. Where classes refused to simplify, new categories were elaborated that preserved the schema by subdividing its failures. The philosophical point, in each case, is not that the auxiliary ideas were worthless; some of them, the analysis of imperialism above all, became substantial research enterprises in their own right. The point is about direction of fit. A modification earns its keep in Popper's bookkeeping when it predicts something the original theory did not, exposing itself to fresh failure. A modification that merely reclassifies the failure already suffered, and predicts nothing that could embarrass it in turn, is a twist, and a theory maintained by twists alone has ceased to compete with the world.

Honesty requires recording, at full strength, that the record Popper pointed to was not invented. The twentieth century's Marxist orthodoxies did produce a documented literature of reinterpretation in which events were routinely received as confirmations of positions adopted before the events occurred; the dialectical vocabulary of a claim and its opposite made the reception structurally easy, since a development and its negation could both be exhibited as moments of the predicted process; and the political structure of the movements concerned, in which theoretical deviation carried organisational and sometimes mortal penalties, punished exactly the conduct Popper's method demanded, the public admission that the theory had been wrong. A defender of the tradition who answers Popper should begin by conceding this terrain, because contesting it costs credibility needed elsewhere. The concession does not end the argument, for two reasons the replies will develop. The first is that immunisation is a temptation of every research tradition, including the physical sciences Popper admired, and the audit he demanded has to be applied comparatively rather than to one tradition selected in advance. The second is that a charge about a tradition's conduct does not by itself convict the founder's concepts, several of which survived the century in forms, class analysis, crisis theory, the study of ideology, that continued to take evidential risks inside other disciplines. But those are defences of a different shape from a denial, and they begin from the concession. Popper found a real disease. The disputes are over its distribution, its fatality, and whether his prescribed medicine would kill healthier patients than the one he treated.

There is also a logical subtlety in the immunisation charge that both sides habitually miss, and that the argument ledger below records as the charge's deepest vulnerability. Strictly, a theory is never rendered unfalsifiable by any finite series of rescues; it is rendered unfalsifiable only relative to the ingenuity and loyalty of its defenders, since a sufficiently determined defender of any theory whatever, including celestial mechanics, can always invent a twist. Popper knew this, and it is why his charge is ultimately sociological and moral rather than purely logical: it accuses a community of adopting, as its settled practice, the stratagems his method forbids. That relocation of the charge matters enormously for the reply. A logical charge would admit only a logical answer. A charge about communal practice admits evidence about practice: about the occasions when Marxist scholars did revise under pressure, abandon forecasts, dissolve categories, and rebuild, and about the comparable occasions in rival traditions. The first attack, at its strongest, is therefore a historical and ethical indictment of specific orthodoxies, written in the vocabulary of a philosophy of science. Judged as a universal logical verdict on every idea Marx ever had, it overreaches its own machinery. Judged as an indictment, it stands on a record its targets supplied.

## The Popper argument ledger

The article's central artifact takes the critique apart, because most disputes about Popper are disputes between people defending different components under one name. The Popper argument ledger sets out each component of the attack in the form this article has been reconstructing: what Popper actually claimed, where he claimed it, the strongest reply the subsequent literature produced, and an honest verdict of stands, partially stands, or fails. The verdicts are this article's, argued for in the sections before and after the table; the table's function is to prevent the verdicts from being smuggled past the reader as atmosphere. A reader who finishes the article disagreeing with two verdicts and accepting five has used the artifact as intended, which is more than can be said for any summary that arrives with a single verdict pre-attached to Popper's name.

Read the ledger as a balance sheet and the legend's single verdict becomes impossible to write. The components that stand most firmly are the ones the legend mentions least: the concession that Marx began as a scientist, the indictment of the orthodoxies' conduct, and the logical argument against historical prophecy. The components that stand least firmly are the ones the legend relies on: demarcation as a mechanical test that sorts doctrines once and for all, and the intellectual history, running through Hegel, on which the polemical framing rests. This inversion is the article's recurring finding, and it explains why the debate has been so durable in its unproductive form. Each side defends the components it is strong on and attacks the components it is strong against, and because both sides call the whole bundle by one name, Popper, the exchange never has to specify where it is actually joined. The sections that follow take the second attack, the ledger's strongest rows, with the same care the first attack received, and then give the replies their full hearing, row by row where the table has promised one.

## The second attack: historicism and the poverty of prophecy

The Poverty of Historicism argues a different case from The Open Society, against a different target, with a different kind of weapon, and the failure to register the differences is responsible for more confusion than any other in this literature. The target is not Marxism alone and not even primarily. Historicism, as Popper defines it, is an approach to the social sciences that takes historical prediction to be their central aim and assumes that this aim is attainable by discovering the rhythms, patterns, laws, or trends underlying the evolution of history. The definition is deliberately ecumenical. It covers Marx's laws of motion and the succession of modes of production, but it equally covers racial theories of history, cyclical theories of civilisation, theological readings of destiny, and every doctrine that presents the future as the unfolding of a discoverable script. Marxism enters the book as the most developed, most intellectually serious specimen of the genus, the historicism that had most nearly achieved the form of a science, and Popper is explicit that he chose it for that reason: refuting the strongest version of an error is the only refutation worth publishing.

### What did Popper mean by historicism?

He meant the doctrine that the social sciences should predict the course of history by uncovering laws of historical development, as astronomy predicts eclipses. The term names a method, not a respect for history: Marxism was his chief specimen because it was the most systematic historicism ever constructed.

The book's architecture divides the doctrine into the teachings Popper calls anti-naturalistic and pro-naturalistic, according to whether historicism despairs of applying the methods of physics to society or insists on imitating them. Against the anti-naturalistic side he argues that the social sciences can perfectly well use experiments in thought, construct models, and test hypotheses piecemeal, and that the supposed uniqueness of historical events is shared, in degree, by events in every science. Against the pro-naturalistic side, the side that claims for sociology a Newtonian role toward history, he argues that the imitations are misunderstandings of physics itself: that physical prediction works by applying laws to specified initial conditions, producing conditional forecasts, whereas the historicist wants unconditional prophecy, the announcement of what will happen regardless, dressed in the prestige of laws that do not deliver it. At the centre of the book, however, stands an argument that does not depend on these skirmishes, and Popper plainly regarded it as the load-bearing wall. It is the argument from the growth of knowledge, and it claims to be not a criticism of any particular theory but a proof about the logical situation of every possible prophet of history. It deserves a section of its own.

Before that argument is stated, the moral temperature of the second attack needs registering, because it explains why Popper thought the stakes were more than academic. The dedication of The Poverty of Historicism commemorates the victims, of every creed and nation, of the belief in inexorable laws of historical destiny, and the preface argues that the belief is not an innocent intellectual error: a doctrine that teaches that history is on one's side, that opponents are merely obstacles to a guaranteed outcome, and that violence is the midwife of a birth already decreed, dissolves the restraints that fallibilism imposes on political conduct. If the future is known, the dissenter is not a fellow citizen with a different judgement but a relic obstructing a law, and the moral argument against crushing him must be made on grounds the doctrine has already annexed. Popper's alternative ethic, argued across both books, is the ethic of the open society: hold theories as conjectures, hold institutions as revisable, and locate responsibility in persons who can be argued with rather than in processes that cannot. Critics have treated this moral superstructure as separable from the logic, and formally it is: the knowledge argument would be valid or invalid whatever its author's politics. But no account of why a philosopher spent a war writing against a method, rather than against a party, is complete without it. The second attack is a piece of logic with a funeral dedication, and both facts belong to its accurate description.

## The knowledge argument: why the future of knowledge cannot be forecast

The argument can be stated in five steps, and Popper presented it with unusual economy in his preface. The course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge; that premise, he observed, must be granted by everyone who takes ideas, technologies, and sciences to matter in history at all, and materialists who ground consciousness in production grant it in their own idiom whenever they treat invention as a force. It is impossible to predict, by rational or scientific methods, the future growth of knowledge; for if we could state, in advance and in detail, what we shall come to know, we would already possess that knowledge, and it would not be future. Therefore it is impossible to predict the future course of human history insofar as that course depends on knowledge not yet grown. Scientific prediction of society's long trajectory, the historicist's defining ambition, is therefore unattainable in principle, not merely difficult in practice. The conclusion is deliberately narrow and the narrowness is its strength: it does not deny that tomorrow's weather of opinion can be guessed, or that trends can be extrapolated for a while, or that conditional forecasts of the if-this-then-that form are the daily bread of policy. It denies one specific thing, the prophecy of history's course on the basis of its laws, which happens to be the thing the historicist, and the Marx of the strongest prophetic passages, claimed to deliver.

### Why can the growth of knowledge not be predicted in advance?

Because a complete prediction of a future discovery would itself be the discovery, made early: to state in detail what will be known is already to know it, so the knowledge would not lie in the future at all. Partial guesses escape the argument; specified future knowledge does not.

The argument's elegance has attracted objections, and the serious ones deserve their shape. The first objection distinguishes between predicting the content of future knowledge and predicting its occurrence or consequences: a futurologist cannot describe the physics of a later century but may reasonably forecast that computing power will grow or that some cure will be found, and such forecasts, the objection runs, are the kind that matter for history. Popper's rejoinder is built into the conclusion's narrowness: such forecasts are conditional, rough, and short range, and they are exactly what his theory of prediction permits; what they cannot support is the historicist's claim to know the destination of the whole process, because the destination depends on contents, on which inventions arrive and what they make possible, and contents are precisely what cannot be foreknown. The second objection observes that the argument proves too much about individual psychology: no one can predict their own future beliefs either, yet biography remains possible. The rejoinder is again a matter of scope: biography is retrospective explanation, and explanation after the fact is the activity Popper's philosophy not only permits but celebrates; it is prophecy, the claim to explain before the fact with scientific authority, that the argument indicts. The third objection, the most philosophically interesting, asks whether the argument applies to itself: if the future course of thought is unpredictable, is the prediction that historicism will fail itself a piece of historicism? It is not, because it is not a prediction about history's course derived from a law of history; it is a logical remark about the form any such derivation would have to take, closer to a proof of impossibility in mathematics than to a forecast. Impossibility proofs do not prophesy. They close a road, and closing roads is compatible with having no map of where the traffic will go instead.

The bearing on Marx must be stated with care, because it is here that the two-argument correction does its most valuable work. The knowledge argument, if sound, does not show that Marx's analysis of capitalism was wrong, that class is an illusion, that crises are imaginary, or that exploitation is a myth. It shows that one specific ambition attributed to the theory cannot be fulfilled by any theory: the ambition to derive the future succession of social orders with the authority of a natural law. A Marxism that renounced that ambition, retaining the mechanisms, the explanations, and the critique while treating the future as open and its own forecasts as conditional, would not be touched by the second attack at all, though it would remain exposed to the first attack wherever it had in fact immunised itself, and to ordinary evidential judgement everywhere else. A Marxism that insists on the ambition, on history's destination as a deliverable of science, is touched at its centre, because the argument does not depend on any fact about capitalism that could turn out otherwise. This is why Popper considered the second argument his strongest, and why the defenders of Marx who engage only the falsifiability charge have left his best case unanswered on the field. It is also why so many later Marxists, from the analytical school to the historians, quietly performed the renunciation the argument demands, relocating the theory's centre of gravity from prophecy to explanation, and describing the relocation as a return to Marx rather than a retreat from him. Whether that description is honest is a question for the history of the tradition. The logical point stands either way, and the ledger records it as standing within its scope.

## Trends, laws, and the piecemeal alternative

Beneath the knowledge argument sits a distinction that Popper regarded as the source of nearly all historicist confusion, and it is the distinction on which the Marxist reply most plausibly fastens. A law, in the usage of the sciences, is a universal statement: it speaks of all cases, everywhere and at all times, and it supports prediction only in combination with initial conditions that must be supplied from outside the law. A trend is an observed drift in some quantity over some period: the growth of population, the concentration of firms, the rising share of services in employment. Trends are real, and describing them is honourable empirical work, but a trend statement is not a law, because it smuggles its period and its conditions inside itself. The trend continues while the conditions that produced it persist, and it reverses, bends, or dissolves when they change, without any law having been broken. The historicist error, on Popper's diagnosis, is to observe a trend, invest it with the grammar of a law, and then issue the unconditional prophecy that the law form seems to license but the trend content cannot support. Population rises, and the prophet announces a necessary future of multitudes; production concentrates, and the prophet announces a necessary future of monopoly and collapse. In each case the empirical kernel may be sound and the prophetic husk is not, and the tragedy of the Marxist tradition, for Popper, was to have welded the two together so that defending the kernel seemed to require defending the husk.

The distinction matters to the fairness of the whole critique, because Marx's own theoretical vocabulary sits awkwardly across it. When Capital speaks of the laws of motion of capitalist society, and then expounds them as tendencies operating in the teeth of counteracting influences, the fall of the rate of profit against the forces that cheapen capital, accumulation against the reserve army it creates and drains, the text is doing something more subtle than either a Newtonian law or a bare trend: it is describing mechanisms whose net outcome is an empirical resultant, not a deduction. Popper's reply to that subtlety was characteristic and only partly satisfactory. He held that a tendency formulated so elastically, as a pressure whose direction is asserted while its magnitude and timing are left to counteracting influences, forfeits the falsifiability that a mechanism claim needs, since every outcome, the tendency's appearance or its frustration, is compatible with the formula. The Marxist counter reply, that conditional mechanism claims are the standard currency of the special sciences and that geology and evolutionary biology trade in little else, is among the strongest in the whole literature, and a later section gives it its full weight in the discussion of demarcation. For the ledger's purposes the finding is partial standing: Popper's diagnosis of trend dressing itself as law convicts a great deal of actual historicist writing, including some of Marx's epigones and some passages of Marx, while his dismissal of tendency statements as such convicts more than his own philosophy can comfortably afford, since his favoured sciences harbour statements of the same logical family.

The political corollary of the second attack, developed in both books, is the contrast between utopian and piecemeal social engineering, and no account of Popper on method is complete without it, because it is where method becomes conduct. The utopian engineer, in Popper's typology, works from a blueprint of the whole society, derived from the philosophy of history that certifies the destination; because the blueprint is total, disagreement about it cannot be settled by experiment, only by power, and the engineer's failures are therefore attributed to saboteurs and wreckers rather than to the plan. The piecemeal engineer works on institutions one at a time, against concrete and remediable evils, expects unintended consequences, and builds the feedback mechanisms, free criticism, reversible decisions, elections that can dismiss governments without bloodshed, by which errors are caught. Popper presented the contrast as a methodological one before it was a political one: piecemeal engineering is falsification institutionalised, the social analogue of the severe test, while utopian engineering is immunisation institutionalised, a structure that cannot learn because its theory of history has pre-certified its direction. Readers unsympathetic to Popper's politics have often conceded the institutional point while resisting its genealogy, and readers sympathetic to his politics have sometimes mistaken the corollary for the argument. The ledger's verdict of partial standing reflects both facts: as a warning about the epistemics of total planning it has worn well, and as a proof about Marx, whose own scattered remarks on transitional arrangements fall far short of a blueprint, it is an argument by association that requires the historicist premise to do its work.

## Popper on Marx: the respect inside the attack

No section of this article is more necessary, or more likely to be resisted by readers formed by the legend, than the one that records what Popper actually said in praise of the man he spent two volumes opposing. The record is not a stray courtesy in a preface. It is structural to The Open Society's second volume, which opens its Marx chapters by distinguishing the thinker from the tradition and awards the thinker a seriousness it denies the church. Popper credited Marx with an intellectual sincerity he held to be rare and with a moral passion he shared more than he opposed: the protest against the exploitation and degradation of workers under an unrestrained capitalism was, in Popper's own judgement, justified in its object, and he wrote of Marx's burning indignation at the hypocrisy of a civilisation that preached freedom while practising what he called, following Marx closely here, the veiled slavery of the wage worker. A critic who grants his target's indictment of the target's society has conceded something most polemic never concedes, and Popper granted it in print, repeatedly, while holding the theory of history that grew from the indictment to be false and dangerous.

The intellectual credit runs parallel to the moral credit. Popper treated Marx as the last great thinker of what he called sociologism, the attempt to ground the study of society in laws of its development, and he praised the attempt's ambition even in pronouncing it misconceived. He singled out Marx's analysis of institutions, the insistence that legal and political forms be explained by the economic conditions of their operation rather than by the intentions of their designers, as a permanent acquisition for social science, whatever became of the laws it was meant to serve. He acknowledged the power of the theory of class as an instrument for reading nineteenth century politics, while contesting its elevation into the single key of history. He even recorded, against the interest of his own polemic, that Marx had explicitly warned against transforming his account of Western European development into a universal path fatally imposed on all peoples, a warning contained in Marx's late correspondence that orthodox historicism preferred not to quote. The portrait that emerges from the volume itself is of a critic prosecuting a theory he believes has done catastrophic harm, while insisting, against his own readers' inclinations, that its author was a scientist of genuine stature whose failure was the honourable failure of a falsified conjecture before it was the dishonourable success of an immunised creed. The standard summary reverses every term of that portrait, and one measure of the summary's corruption is that generations of Popper's admirers have cited him for a dismissal he went out of his way not to perform.

### Did Popper respect Marx, on the evidence of his own pages?

Yes, within the limits of an opponent: he praised Marx's sincerity, his indignation at exploitation, and his institutional analysis, called the original theory scientific, and reserved his contempt for the followers who protected it from the refutation he believed it had honourably suffered.

Why insist on the respect at this length? Because it disciplines both camps in the uses they make of him. For the anti-Marxist camp, the respect removes the licence to treat Popper as a certificate that nothing in Marx requires reading: the certificate's issuer read Marx closely, learned from him, and said so, and a dismissal that Popper did not perform cannot borrow his authority. For the Marxist camp, the respect removes the comfort of treating the critique as prejudice: a prosecutor who concedes the accused's sincerity, insight, and scientific standing, and who directs his heaviest fire at the conduct of the accused's defenders, cannot be answered by an examination of his motives, only by an examination of his record claims and his logic. The replies that follow are the strongest the literature affords precisely because they accept those terms. They do not plead that Popper was unfair in spirit; several of them concede that his account of the orthodoxies was fair in fact. They attack instead the philosophy of science he brought to the record, and they attack it from ground he himself had surveyed: the holism of testing, the history of the sciences he admired, and the standards his own criterion would impose on geology, biology, and economics if it were applied without favour.

## The first reply: Duhem, Quine, and the impossibility of isolated refutation

The strongest replies to Popper were not written by Marxists, and that is the first fact about them a student should absorb, because it locates the debate where it belongs: inside the philosophy of science, where Popper's criterion had to survive scrutiny from thinkers whose only commitment was to describing how science actually proceeds. The foundational difficulty had been stated before Popper's attack was published, by the French physicist and historian Pierre Duhem, and generalised after the war by the American philosopher W. V. O. Quine. A physical theory, Duhem argued, never confronts an experiment alone. The prediction that fails is derived from a cluster: the theory under scrutiny, auxiliary hypotheses about the instruments and the environment, mathematical auxiliaries, and statements of initial conditions. When the predicted event does not occur, logic certifies only that something in the cluster is false; it is silent about which member to convict. Quine extended the point to the whole web of belief: any statement can be held true, come what may, if the adjustments elsewhere in the web are drastic enough, and no statement, not even a law of logic, is in principle immune to revision. The moral is not that testing is impossible. It is that refutation is underdetermined by logic and settled, where it is settled, by the judgement of a scientific community about which adjustment best serves the whole enterprise.

Applied to Popper, the Duhem and Quine problem does not refute the criterion so much as relocate it, and the relocation dissolves the clean verdicts of the legend. Popper knew the problem; his entire methodology of conventional decisions about basic statements, and his rules against conventionalist stratagems, were designed to govern exactly the freedom Duhem exposed. The scientist, in Popper's picture, agrees to treat certain observation statements as unproblematic for the purpose of a test and agrees not to rescue theories by moves on the forbidden list. Falsification is thus an achievement of methodological discipline rather than a gift of logic. But that concession, which Popper made openly, transforms the charge against Marxism in a way his popularisers rarely follow. If falsification is a matter of a community's discipline rather than of a theory's logical shape, then unfalsifiability is not a property a doctrine can be convicted of possessing in the abstract; it is a prediction about what its community will do when tests go wrong. The Marxist can then ask, with some justice, for the comparative evidence: which traditions, measured by their conduct under failure, kept the discipline, and which did not? The history of physics, examined without piety, contains its own immunising episodes, its decades in which anomalies were absorbed by ingenuity and the recalcitrant were treated as cranks. The question Popper taught everyone to ask survives the Duhem and Quine correction. The verdict he attached to one tradition does not survive it in its simple form, because the correction makes the verdict an empirical claim about all traditions, to be tried on records he examined for one defendant only.

There is a second consequence, quieter and more damaging to the legend's use of the criterion. If no theory is ever refuted in isolation, then the phrase that a theory was falsified always conceals a decision about blame, and decisions about blame are governed by considerations Popper's formal criterion does not supply: the availability of alternatives, the fertility of the research the theory still organises, the distribution of the failures across its core and its periphery. Working scientists do not abandon theories into a vacuum; they abandon them toward rivals, and a theory with grievous anomalies but no successor may rationally be retained, patched, and worked with for generations, as Newtonian mechanics was retained through known anomalies because nothing else could compute a tide. The Marxist who retains the concepts of class and exploitation while conceding the failure of the prophetic superstructure is, on this understanding, behaving the way scientists behave with every framework theory they possess: discarding the derivations that failed, keeping the machinery that still organises inquiry, and awaiting a successor that has not yet arrived. Popper's bookkeeping would call some of that immunisation, and in the orthodox cases he named, bookkeeping is the right word. But the Duhem and Quine correction establishes that the line between legitimate retention and immunisation is drawn by judgement within a practice, not by a criterion applied from outside it, and that is why the philosophy of science after Duhem and Quine has treated falsification as one virtue among several in theory choice rather than as the border post between science and its others.

## The second reply: Lakatos and the methodology of research programmes

Imre Lakatos, who studied under Popper in London and spent a career amending him, produced the reply that has done most to reorganise this debate, because it accepts Popper's deepest intuition, that science is distinguished by its response to failure, while rejecting the unit at which Popper aimed. Theories, Lakatos argued, are not what science tests one at a time; the real units are research programmes, sequences of theories bound together by a hard core of assumptions the programme's practitioners agree not to abandon, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses they are licensed to revise, and a positive heuristic, a policy for building models and digesting anomalies, that gives the programme its momentum. Newton's programme, Lakatos's parade case, was born drowning in anomalies and survived them for two centuries, not because its practitioners cheated but because its heuristic kept converting failures into problems with solutions, and occasionally into the prediction of facts nobody had looked for, the existence and position of an unseen planet being the immortal example. A programme is appraised, on this scheme, not by whether it has suffered refutations, since all have, but by the character of its shifts: a problem shift is progressive when the new theory in the sequence predicts some novel fact and some of those predictions are corroborated, and degenerating when the sequence merely accommodates, after the fact, whatever the world has already announced. Appraisal is retrospective, comparative, and patient. There is no instant rationality, and no moment at which a bell rings and a programme is declared unscientific.

### How does Lakatos change the question put to Marxism?

He relocates it from logic to history: not whether the doctrine forbids some outcome in the abstract, but whether its successive versions have predicted novel facts or merely absorbed known ones. A programme may degenerate for decades and still be science, and may revive; the verdict becomes comparative and revisable.

Set against the reconstructed Popper, the Lakatosian reply cuts with a fine edge in both directions, and its evenhandedness is its credibility. Against the legend's Popper it is decisive: if research programmes are the unit of appraisal, then the discovery that Marxism has auxiliaries, anomalies, and loyal defenders places it in the universal condition of science rather than outside it, and the question of its standing becomes the slower question of its shifts, a question that cannot be settled by a criterion but only by a history. Against the complacent defender of Marxism, however, Lakatos is the least comfortable ally imaginable, because his test is in one respect harder than Popper's. Popper's immunisation charge could always be parried by pointing to the theory's falsifiable statements; Lakatos asks for the programme's novel facts, the predictions of phenomena unknown or unexpected when the theory framed them, and asks whether the corroborations kept pace with the accommodations. His own application of the scheme to Marxism, in a celebrated radio talk and its published version, was severe: he listed the programme's bold early predictions, their failures, and the sequence of auxiliary constructions, the theory of imperialism, the doctrine of the labour aristocracy, and others, that explained the failures without predicting novel facts in return, and he pronounced the programme degenerating by his own criterion. The verdict is not beyond appeal: historians of the tradition can contest the list, adduce novel content in the programme's later branches, its treatments of crisis, of the state, of ideology, and of capitalist development in the periphery, and argue that Lakatos measured a political church's pronouncements where he should have measured a research tradition's best work. But the form of the question has been permanently improved. After Lakatos, nobody honest can ask whether Marxism is falsifiable and stop there; the askable question is whether its problem shifts have been progressive, and that question has the virtue of being about Marxism's actual career rather than about a slogan's logical shadow. This article's ledger records the demarcation row as partial standing for exactly this reason: the demand for risk survives, the border post does not.

## The third reply: an explanatory theory and the scarcity of dated predictions

A third reply, older in the tradition than the philosophy that frames this article, questions the indictment's first premise: the size of the stake Marx himself placed on prediction. Popper's narrative requires a theory that forecast the future boldly enough to be refuted by it. The defenders answer that Marx's mature work is principally explanatory and analytical, a theory of how capitalism works, of the mechanisms by which value is produced and appropriated, classes formed and reproduced, crises generated from within the system's normal functioning, rather than a timetable of its end. The dated, located forecasts that would give falsification its cleanest grip are, on this account, surprisingly scarce in the texts: the Manifesto's rhetoric apart, Marx repeatedly declined to write recipes for the cook shops of the future, refused to specify the succession in countries unlike the ones he studied, and in his late correspondence explicitly rejected the conversion of his sketch of Western European development into a universal path every nation must tread. Where the theory does commit itself, the commitment is characteristically to tendencies operating against countertendencies, to the direction of pressures, and to conditional judgements about what must happen if specified blockages persist. A theory of that shape is not immune to evidence, since its mechanisms can fail to be found and its claimed pressures can be shown to be artefacts, but it is badly described as a prophecy that events have refuted, and its defenders conclude that Popper convicted the church's catechism in the founder's name.

The reply has genuine force, and it has a cost that its users do not always count. Its force is textual: the serious scholarship of Marx's late years, the drafts on Russia, the ethnological notebooks, the qualifications he attached to his own generalisations, supports the picture of a thinker more tentative about trajectories than the prophet of either the legend or the catechism. Popper's catalogue of refuted predictions draws its items disproportionately from the tradition's operative certainties and from Marx's most prophetic registers, and a fair audit must discount the items in proportion. But the cost is this: a theory defended as principally explanatory must accept judgement by explanatory standards, and those standards are not soft. An explanation competes with rival explanations of the same phenomena and is appraised by its reach, its coherence, its capacity to unify what rivals leave scattered, and its fertility in generating findings its rivals did not expect. On those standards the contest between Marxist explanation and its competitors, in economic history, in the sociology of the state, in the study of ideology and culture, is a live and unsettled one, conducted case by case, and it is not obviously a contest the defence wins by having abandoned prophecy. Worse, the retreat from prediction sits uneasily with the tradition's self understanding for most of its career: movements did not march under the banner of an explanatory framework of contested fertility, and a defence that saves the theory by redescribing what it always was must answer for the century in which it was understood, by friend and foe alike, as a science of history's direction. The honest position, occupied by many of the tradition's own historians, is that Marx's work contains both registers, the analytical and the prophetic, in unresolved tension, and that Popper's attack lands on the prophetic register with full force and on the analytical register hardly at all. That position concedes the ledger's strongest rows and contests its weakest, which is where this article's reconstruction has been pointing throughout.

## The strongest objection: a criterion that would disqualify the established sciences

The reply that presses Popper hardest, and the one this article is required to give at full strength before answering, is not a defence of any Marxist claim. It is an attack on the measuring instrument. If falsifiability, applied as the legend applies it, excludes Marxism from science, then consistency requires applying it where its users prefer not to look, and the results are an embarrassment of exclusions. Evolutionary biology, in its historical claims, reconstructs singular past events, the descent of lineages, the causes of extinctions, from traces, and its central mechanism, selection, has been accused within biology itself of the elasticity Popper charged against dialectics: the survival of the fittest can be redescribed, by the careless, as the fitness of the survivors, and particular evolutionary narratives are notoriously accommodating after the fact. Geology's grand claims concern unrepeatable processes extended over durations no test can reproduce, and its practitioners revise auxiliaries with a freedom any Marxist would envy. Economics, the social science with the strongest claim to quantitative rigour, protects its cores with a protective belt of ceteris paribus clauses so routine that its failed forecasts, which are legion and consequential, have never once induced the profession to surrender its framework, or its critics to deny it the name of science without the denial sounding eccentric. Cosmology reasons about a unique object. Climate science, whose models are clusters of theory, auxiliary, and parameterisation of exactly Duhem's description, cannot be falsified by a cold winter or verified by a hot one. Either the criterion, mechanically applied, disqualifies most of what the modern world calls science, in which case it is too strict to be the test of anything; or it is applied selectively, to one defendant at a time, in which case Marxism's exclusion is not a finding but a choice. That is the objection in its strongest form, and it is a strong form indeed.

The answer available to Popper's side is better than the objection usually allows, and giving it fairly is necessary before the concession this article owes is made. Popper never proposed that the criterion be applied mechanically, by inspectors with checklists, and his own writings on the historical sciences treat evolutionary theory as a fertile metaphysical research programme whose influence on science he honoured, a judgement that concedes the criterion's non mechanical character from within his own corpus. His actual demand was always about conduct at the point of failure: the severe test sincerely attempted, the rescue that increases content rather than merely absorbing shock, the community that rewards the bearer of bad news. Measured by conduct rather than by logical shape, the established sciences he is accused of disqualifying mostly pass his audit in their best practice, because their communities do, eventually and noisily, execute theories, demote frameworks, and teach their own refuted ancestors as history. The objection's parade of anomalies therefore does not quite reach him: he can grant every anomaly and ask his question again, about what the community did next. But the concession the objection extracts is real, and this article makes it without varnish. Insofar as the defence of Marxism against Popper relies on the objection, it defends Marxism's scientific status by lowering the bar rather than by clearing it: it shows that the border is unpoliced for everyone, not that this particular traveller's papers are in order. A tradition that has to argue that geology also cheats has not thereby shown that it does not. The objection destroys the legend's verdict, the clean exclusion, and it leaves standing Popper's harder, smaller question, the audit of conduct, on which the Marxist orthodoxies' record remains, as conceded earlier, a genuine liability. That is the equilibrium the evidence supports: the criterion fails as a wall and survives as a question, and the question is uncomfortable for everyone who hears it, which is the mark of a good one.

## The fourth reply: philosophy of science after Popper

The generation of philosophers who inherited Popper's problem did not leave his criterion where they found it, and their amendments are the context in which any verdict on the criterion must now be delivered. Thomas Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions, published in 1962, described normal science as puzzle solving within a paradigm that practitioners do not test but presuppose, and described the abandonment of paradigms as a gestalt-like conversion under accumulated strain rather than a falsification by a crucial instance; the picture was drawn from the physical sciences themselves, and its moral for the demarcation debate was that the conduct Popper prescribed is not the conduct even of his exemplary sciences, most of the time. Paul Feyerabend pressed further, arguing that every methodological rule, including Popper's, has been fruitfully violated in the episodes the sciences themselves celebrate, and that a single rule enforced on all inquiry would have strangled several of its triumphs in their cradles. Meanwhile the logical difficulties accumulated around the criterion's own formulation: falsifiability classifies statements, but probability statements, existential statements, and the dispositional vocabulary of real theories fit the classification awkwardly; a theory hedged with probabilities forbids nothing strictly, yet probabilistic physics is nobody's pseudoscience. Popper answered many of these objections across a long career, refining the notions of severity, corroboration, and verisimilitude, and conceding, in his later statements of the position, that the criterion was a proposal for a convention, that falsifications themselves are conjectural and revisable, and that a dogmatic defence of a theory can be, for a time, a rational part of its development. The picture that emerges from the whole literature is neither the legend's guillotine nor its complete repeal. Falsification survives as a discipline, a preference, a way of holding theories at arm's length and making them earn their keep, and it has lost, perhaps permanently, the office of border guard between science and non science. The consequence for the Marx case is exact: a verdict of exclusion, delivered in the name of contemporary philosophy of science, cites an authority that philosophy has itself retired from that office, while a demand for risk, for content, for conduct under failure, cites an authority that remains in good standing and that the Marxist tradition must answer on the evidence of its record, like every other tradition.

## The Hegel problem: Kaufmann's rebuttal and the reliability of the second volume

The reply that does the most damage to Popper's own book, as distinct from his criterion, concerns not Marx but Hegel, and it matters here because the second volume's architecture runs through Hegel: the dialectic Marx inherited, the historicism Popper traced to its source, and the intellectual genealogy on which the volume's authority as history depends. Popper's chapter on Hegel, the longest act of demolition in the book, presented Hegel as a servile apologist for the Prussian state, a mystifier whose dialectic was a device for evading contradiction, and a fountainhead of the tribal nationalism Popper saw consummated in the century of the book's writing. In 1959 the philosopher Walter Kaufmann, of Princeton, published a long examination of the chapter under the title of the Hegel myth and its method, and its findings were of the kind that do not heal. Kaufmann showed that key quotations in Popper's account were wrenched from contexts that reversed their bearing, that some were drawn not from Hegel's works but from hostile secondary compendia, that translations were tendentious where the argument needed them to be, and that positions Popper ascribed to Hegel in damning form were positions Hegel had explicitly argued against. The essay did not undertake to make Hegel a liberal or a lucid stylist; Kaufmann's own Hegel was difficult, speculative, and often uncongenial. It undertook the narrower task of showing that Popper's Hegel was, in significant part, a construction, and that the construction's method, selective quotation in the service of a verdict reached in advance, was the method Popper's own philosophy exists to forbid.

### Why does the Kaufmann rebuttal matter beyond Hegel scholarship?

Because the second volume's authority rests on its readings, and the Hegel reading was shown to fail the evidential standards Popper demanded of others. The failure does not touch the logic of the knowledge argument, which stands or falls alone, but it reprices the volume as intellectual history.

The consequences must be allocated carefully, because both camps in this dispute have overdrawn them. The rebuttal does not refute the poverty of historicism argument, which is a piece of logic owing nothing to any reading of Hegel; it does not refute the falsifiability criterion, which was framed a decade before the Hegel chapter; and it does not even refute the possibility that Hegel's philosophy encouraged the historicist habits Popper deplored, a claim that could be re-established by honest scholarship. What the rebuttal damages is the trustworthiness of The Open Society as a work of intellectual history at exactly the point where the volume asks to be trusted most: its portrait of a lineage, Plato to Hegel to Marx, in which each thinker is read as a station in the career of an error. A reader who has watched the Hegel station constructed from doctored evidence is entitled to reexamine the Marx station with new suspicion, and scholars who have done so have found the Marx chapters markedly better, closer to the texts, fairer in quotation, while remaining a prosecutor's brief rather than a judge's summing up. Popper's own response to the criticism, across later editions and replies, was partial: corrections were absorbed at points, the general portrait was defended, and the exchange cooled into one of the century's unfinished scholarly quarrels. The ledger's verdict on the Hegel component is therefore fails, the only outright failure among the major components, and it is recorded there not as a debating trophy for the other side but as a demonstration of this article's method: a critique is strongest exactly where it submits to the discipline it preaches, and Popper's critique, at its Hegelian foundation, did not. The two-argument correction protects the logic from the lapse. The lapse is real, and it belongs in any honest account of what the most cited critique in the subject is worth.

## Popper's later qualifications, and what they change

Popper lived for half a century after the two books of the attack, and he did not spend those decades merely repeating them. The qualifications he entered across that long afterlife are the reply literature's most underused exhibit, because several of them, taken together, move his own position measurably toward the middle ground this article's ledger occupies, and a verdict on Popper that ignores his final position judges a man by his wartime draft. The first qualification concerns the criterion's status. From The Logic of Scientific Discovery onward, and with increasing emphasis in the later restatements, Popper insisted that falsifiability was a methodological proposal, a rule he recommended for adoption, not a truth about nature or meaning that a theory could be convicted of violating; the consequence, which he accepted, is that a community declining the rule is not irrational by definition but differently committed, and must be argued with rather than excommunicated. The second qualification concerns falsification itself. In his later philosophy he stressed that the basic statements by which theories are tested are themselves accepted by convention and decision, that observations are theory laden, and that a falsification is therefore a fallible, revisable event in a community's life, not a lightning strike from the world. The distance between that doctrine and the Duhem and Quine problem he had resisted is not large, and his critics noticed the convergence even where he did not advertise it. The third qualification concerns Marx directly. In the later restatements of the case, including the essay in which he revisited his intellectual formation and the lectures of his London decades, Popper continued to hold that Marxism in its orthodox forms had degenerated into immunisation, but he repeated, and in places sharpened, the concessions the legend deletes: that Marx's theory in its original form had been falsifiable and scientific in intent, that its failure was the failure of a bold conjecture, and that his complaint had always been addressed to the rescue operation and to the prophetic claim rather than to the analytical work. He also, in his essay on dialectic, allowed that the pattern the dialecticians were gesturing at, the fruitful tension of opposed tendencies in development, could be redescribed without mystification as trial and error, while maintaining that the dialectical licence to welcome contradiction was a standing invitation to the immunising habit. A fourth qualification, less often noticed, touches the political moral itself: in later reflections on the open society he acknowledged that piecemeal reform, his own prescription, operates within traditions and institutions it cannot redesign from nothing, and that the rationalist attitude he preached was itself a moral decision, adopted on faith in argument rather than derived from proof. The concession completes the portrait of a thinker whose final positions were everywhere more fallibilist than his slogans, about his enemies' doctrines and about his own.

Taken together, the qualifications change the standard verdict in a precise way: they convert Popper from the author of a border into the author of an audit, and they deprive both the legend's worshippers and its refuters of their favourite text. The worshipper who cites Popper for the proposition that Marxism is, by logical inspection, a pseudoscience is citing the young polemicist against the old philosopher's own account of what his criterion was. The refuter who demolishes the mechanical criterion and declares the critique answered is demolishing a position its author had already vacated, while leaving unanswered the audit question the author never withdrew: what did the tradition do when its forecasts failed, and did its revisions add content or only insulation? A student who learns to ask after the qualifications, to ask which Popper is being quoted, the Popper of the wartime volumes, of the London refinements, or of the legend that neither would fully recognise, has acquired the single most useful habit this topic teaches, since the same triage applies to Marx, to Hegel, and to every figure in the series this article belongs to, whose name is used as a verdict by people who have read a summary of a summary. The texts are the court of appeal. Popper, to his credit and his cost, left enough of them, early and late, damaging and generous, to convict the legend out of his own mouth.

## The eclipse and the anecdote: how Popper taught himself the criterion

The criterion did not descend on Popper from pure logic, and his favourite way of teaching it was autobiographical, an origin story he retold in the essay that became the opening of Conjectures and Refutations. The setting is Vienna in 1919, in the aftermath of collapse, when four theories competed for the allegiance of a young intellectual: Einstein's relativity, fresh from its eclipse confirmation, and the three doctrines Popper grouped as the historicist and psychological creeds of his city, Marx's theory of history, Freud's psychoanalysis, and Adler's individual psychology, with which Popper had briefly worked. The contrast that struck him was not between the theories' contents but between their adherents' tempers. The followers of Marx, Freud, and Adler, he recalled, saw confirmations everywhere: the world was full of verifications of their theories, every newspaper and every consulting room supplied fresh instances, and a case that seemed to resist the theory could always, with a little interpretative effort, be exhibited as its triumph. Einstein's theory, by contrast, had staked itself on a precise and improbable consequence, the deflection of starlight by the sun's mass in an amount the theory specified, and had the eclipse expeditions of 1919 found the undeflected positions, the theory would have been, in its author's own judgement, untenable. One doctrine risked its life on a number; the others risked nothing on anything. Popper's conclusion, drawn at the age of seventeen and elaborated for sixty years, was that the difference between science and its imitators lies not in the accumulation of instances but in the exposure to a verdict.

### What did the eclipse expedition show Popper that Vienna's consulting rooms did not?

That a theory can be staked on a single specified result: had the starlight not bent by the predicted amount, Einstein's construction would have fallen. The doctrines Popper had worked among faced no such moment, since every result, and its opposite, could be absorbed as confirmation.

The anecdote's illustrative cases repay scrutiny, because they contain the immunisation charge in miniature and show its evidential texture. Popper described presenting Adler with a case that seemed to contradict the theory of inferiority feelings, and Adler, without examining the child in question, explained the case within the theory's terms at once, on the strength of his thousandfold experience; when Popper remarked that this new case had just enlarged the thousandfold experience by one, the circularity stood exposed: the experience counted as evidence because the theory certified it, and the theory stood confirmed by the experience it had certified. The Marxist instances Popper collected in the same years had the same anatomy. A prediction of the theory's was compared with events and found to fit, or found not to fit and then shown, by a reinterpretation of the terms, to have fit after all; the working class rising was the theory confirmed, and the working class declining to rise was the theory confirmed at a deeper level, false consciousness being the name of the depth. It is important, for fairness, to record that the anecdote is Popper's own narrative of his formation, polished over decades of retelling, and that historians treat such conversion stories as literature as well as evidence. But the structural observation it dramatises does not depend on the accuracy of a teenager's diary. Any reader of the literature of any total doctrine can replicate the observation from their own shelf: the interpretative apparatus that renders a theory compatible with its own apparent failures is visible wherever one looks for it, and the looking is Popper's permanent bequest to his readers, including the readers he would least have wished to arm, since the apparatus is found in the apologetics of market dogma and national destiny as readily as in the texts he prosecuted. He aimed the instrument at three doctrines of his youth. The instrument outlived the aiming, and it points wherever it is pointed, which is why a series devoted to Marx must handle it with respect and without favour.

## Demarcation among its neighbours: verification, meaning, and the road not taken

To see what is distinctive in Popper's answer to the Marx case, it helps to set it beside the answers on offer in his Vienna, because the legend's Popper, the hanging judge of doctrines, resembles the Vienna Circle's positivists far more than he resembles himself. The positivists proposed verification as the criterion of meaning itself: a statement that could not, in principle, be verified by experience was not false but nonsense, a pseudo statement, and on that basis they proposed to clear philosophy's ground of metaphysics, theology, and ethics at a stroke. Popper rejected the proposal at every point. Verification, he argued, is logically unattainable for the universal statements science consists of, since no finite run of instances verifies a law about all instances; meaning and testability are different properties, and conflating them destroys science together with metaphysics; and a criterion of meaning is itself unverifiable, so the proposal devours its own authority. His falsifiability was designed as a criterion not of meaning but of demarcation between the empirical sciences and everything else, metaphysics included and honoured, and it was offered, as his later qualifications insist, as a proposal about method rather than a discovery about language. The distance from the positivists matters for the Marx verdict in a way polemic on both sides obscures. A positivist verdict on Marxism would have been that its central statements are cognitively meaningless, noises shaped like propositions. Popper's verdict was never that. It was that a specific tradition had forfeited, by its conduct, a status its founder's work had earned, a verdict that presupposes the statements mean enough to be tested, to fail, and to be rescued. The Marxist who answers Popper as though he were a positivist is fighting the wrong Viennese, and the anti-Marxist who quotes Popper as though he had declared Marxism meaningless is quoting a verdict its author declined, on philosophical grounds he published at length, ever to deliver.

There is a second neighbour worth distinguishing, because it supplies the reply literature with its most quietly effective weapon. The verificationists themselves, facing the problem that their criterion excluded the universal statements of science, retreated to weaker notions, confirmability in degree, testability in principle, the gradual accumulation of favourable instances, and Popper spent a parallel career arguing that confirmation in degree is a snare: that a theory's probability, in the sense of its likelihood of being true, rises as its content falls, so that the safest statements are the emptiest, and that science should prefer the improbable, contentful, severely tested conjecture to the probable truism. Set beside that doctrine, the immunised Marxism of the orthodoxies appears in an even colder light than the falsifiability charge alone provides: each conventionalist twist, by widening the theory's compatibility, thins its content, so that the rescued doctrine approaches the condition of a statement that says almost nothing and is therefore almost certainly safe. Popper's philosophy thus convicts the rescue operation twice over, once for withdrawing from falsification and once for purchasing safety at the price of content, and the second conviction has no analogue in the positivist armoury. It also, however, supplies the defence with its measure of how far a legitimate revision may go: a revision that increases content, that says more than the theory said before and risks more in consequence, is not a twist but a growth, and the history of Marxist thought contains revisions of exactly that character alongside the twists, in its theories of the state, of ideology, and of capitalist development outside Europe, which committed the tradition to claims its founder had not made and evidence could have shamed. The audit Popper demands is therefore not a ceremony with a foreknown outcome. It is a case by case examination of a tradition's revisions, some of which pass his test with honours. The orthodoxies he named fail it. The philosophy that frames the test, properly reconstructed, is more discriminating than either its legend or its targets have usually allowed.

## Bernstein's heresy: the tradition's own falsificationist

The most instructive figure in the whole debate is one Popper's narrative touches only obliquely, and his story belongs in any complete account, because he demonstrates from inside the tradition exactly what conduct Popper's method demanded, and exactly what the tradition's institutions did to the man who practised it. Eduard Bernstein was no outsider: a leading theorist of German social democracy, an intimate of Engels, a custodian of the doctrine's texts. In the later eighteen nineties, writing from London exile and publishing his conclusions in the book that became known as Evolutionary Socialism, Bernstein did the thing the first attack accuses the tradition of never doing. He compared the theory's central forecasts with the evidence and reported, in print, that they were failing. The middle classes were not disappearing; they were changing their composition and growing. The condition of the workers was not sinking toward uniform misery; real wages and organisation were rising. Crises were not obviously deepening toward a final collapse; credit, cartels, and the world market were modifying the cycle the theory had projected. Capital was concentrating in some branches and dispersing, through joint stock ownership, in others. Bernstein drew the methodological moral explicitly: a theory that prided itself on being scientific had to submit to its own evidence, revise its expectations, and rebuild its politics on the revised ground, which in his case meant a democratic and gradualist socialism pursued through the institutions that existed. He performed, from within Marxism and before Popper was born, the precise operation Popper later codified: he treated the theory's forecasts as falsifiable, accepted the falsification, and paid the price of revision in the open.

The tradition's response to Bernstein is the best single piece of evidence in Popper's entire case, and it was assembled by the tradition itself. Bernstein was not answered primarily by counter evidence, though able critics, Rosa Luxemburg above all, engaged his arguments with brilliance. He was answered by anathema: the revisionist became a heretic, his name a term of abuse in the movement's vocabulary for a century, his methodological point, quite apart from his politics, treated as apostasy rather than as science's ordinary metabolism. Luxemburg's great reply, for all its power, defended the collapse forecast Bernstein had questioned, and the parties of orthodoxy closed ranks around the forecast rather than around the audit. A Popperian reading of the episode writes itself: here was the crucial experiment the tradition conducted on itself, in which the member who practised falsification was excommunicated and the members who practised immunisation kept the pulpits, and the selection pressure thus established guaranteed the conduct the Open Society later indicted. A fair reading must add what Popper's narrative underplays: that Bernstein's revision also carried a political wager, about the democratisation of the Wilhelmine and later the Weimar order, that events treated at least as harshly as they treated the orthodox forecasts, and that his critics' suspicion that the revision of method was the servant of a revision of aims was not paranoia. But the methodological residue is inexpugnable. When the most Popperian act in the tradition's history was performed by a Marxist, the tradition's immune system identified it as the disease. No exhibit in the ledger weighs more, and it weighs, notice, in Popper's favour on the conduct question while weighing against the legend on the doctrine question: the theory, it turns out, contained the resources for its own correction, in a thinker formed entirely by its texts. What failed was not the ideas' capacity to be revised but the institutions' willingness to survive the revision. That distinction, between a doctrine and the church that guards it, is one Popper sometimes honoured and sometimes trampled, and Bernstein's ghost is the standing reminder of why it must be kept.

## Popper on Marx's method and moral theory: the wider indictment

The second volume's case against Marx is wider than the two arguments this article has separated, and the wider charges deserve their place, both because they influenced the volume's reception and because several of them have had a second life in later scholarship. Three deserve examination. The first is the charge of holism and essentialism: that Marx's method treats society as an organic whole whose essence, the mode of production, unfolds through history, and that this essentialism, the belief that concepts like capitalism or the state have inner natures to be unveiled rather than problems to be analysed, is the root error of which the prophecies are the fruit. Popper's alternative, methodological individualism, holds that social wholes are to be explained by the actions, interactions, and unintended consequences of individuals situated in institutions, and that any method beginning from the whole's essence will end by immunising itself, since essences can be redefined when instances misbehave. The charge has genuine bite against the Hegelian register of Marx's writing, and less against the analytical register, where Marx's own practice, in the French histories above all, is a tissue of situated actors, accidents, and unintended outcomes that a methodological individualist can read with pleasure; the tension between the two registers runs through this article's argument, and it is fairest to say that Popper convicted the essence while the practice kept escaping him.

The second wider charge concerns moral theory, and it is the one Popper's admirers quote least, because it disturbs the political moral usually drawn from the book. Popper argued that Marx, having unmasked the moral codes of his society as ideology, the legal and ethical superstructure of an economic base, was left without a moral standpoint of his own from which to condemn the system he anatomised, and that he filled the vacancy with history itself: the future's verdict replaced the conscience's, and the morality of the movement became what Popper called a historicist moral theory, in which the coming order is right because it is coming. The charge is subtle and it cuts at the tradition's rhetoric, in which historical inevitability was routinely preached as moral warrant, and it explains, better than any other part of the volume, the specific tone of Popper's alarm: a movement that has outsourced its conscience to the future has disabled the only instrument by which it could correct itself in the present. Defenders reply that the unmasking of ideology is a diagnosis, not a prohibition, that Marx's pages burn with a moral passion no reader has ever missed, and that inferring the absence of a normative standard from the presence of a sociology of norms is a philosopher's error. The exchange remains genuinely open, and it belongs in this article because it shows the critique operating at its best level, as an argument about the logic of a tradition's self understanding, rather than at the level of the legend's border post.

The third wider charge is the one with the longest afterlife: Popper's attack on what he called the conspiracy theory of society, the doctrine that social phenomena are to be explained as the intended results of the designs of powerful persons or classes. Marxism, on his account, flirted with this error whenever it read events as the bourgeoisie's machinations, and the true task of social science, he countered, is the explanation of unintended consequences: how institutions arise that nobody designed, how the pursuit of one end produces its opposite, how markets crash and parties ossify against the wills of everyone involved. The irony here is rich enough to deserve a pause, because unintended consequence is the very engine of Marx's own best analysis: the capitalist who cheapens labour and thereby undermines demand, the competition that forces each owner to accumulate on pain of ruin, the system whose laws operate behind the backs of its agents, are Popperian themes in Marxist dress, and Popper himself acknowledged the theory of unintended consequences to be the legitimate core of Marx's institutional analysis. The charge therefore lands, once again, not on the analytical practice but on the rhetorical habit of the tradition, its conspiratorial shorthand in agitation; and the pattern of the whole volume stands revealed in miniature. Again and again, the closer Popper reads, the more his target narrows to the tradition's operative habits and its prophetic superstructure, and the more the founder's analytical work escapes the indictment it was summoned to answer. A critique that repeatedly acquits the accused while convicting the church is an unusual document, and the legend that flattened it into a verdict against the accused has the matter, as this article has argued throughout, precisely backwards.

## The internal response: analytical Marxism under Popperian pressure

One measure of a critique's force is what it does to the thinking of those who accept its target's importance, and by that measure the most interesting chapter in Popper's afterlife was written by the Marxists who took him seriously. The analytical Marxism that emerged in the later twentieth century, associated with G. A. Cohen's defence of historical materialism, John Roemer's reconstruction of exploitation, and Jon Elster's relentless methodological audits, was formed in an intellectual climate Popper had made: its founding gesture was the demand that Marxist claims be stated clearly enough to be wrong, that mechanisms be specified where the tradition had gestured at dialectics, and that functional explanations, the tradition's characteristic form, in which institutions are explained by their beneficial consequences for a class or a system, be required to show their workings or be withdrawn. Cohen's celebrated defence of the primacy of the productive forces was, in form, a Popperian event: an attempt to state the theory's core as a set of claims definite enough to argue about, and Elster's critique of Cohen, demanding the microfoundations that functional explanation omits, was Popper's methodological individualism operating inside the tradition's own seminar rooms. Whatever one thinks of the school's conclusions, its existence refutes the cruder version of the immunisation charge as a timeless truth about Marxist thought: here were Marxists who treated unfalsifiability as a reproach, clarity as a duty, and the founder's claims as conjectures answerable to evidence and logic, and who said, in so many words, that the tradition's survival depended on accepting the discipline its critics had demanded.

The analytical episode also clarifies what answering Popper, as opposed to denouncing him, actually costs, and the cost explains why the episode remained a minority report within the wider tradition. Clarity is expensive. A functional explanation dismantled into mechanisms may survive as social science and expire as inspiration; a theory of history restated as a set of conditional claims about technology and class loses the prophetic authority that filled halls; and a movement's intellectuals who submit its doctrines to the audit may find, as Bernstein found, that the audit's findings are unwelcome to the movement's institutions in exact proportion to their honesty. The analytical Marxists paid the price in influence within the movements and gained a hearing in the universities, a trade the tradition has made repeatedly since Popper's volumes appeared, and one that maps precisely onto the ledger's distinction between the doctrine and the church. For the doctrine, the Popperian pressure proved survivable and even fertile, because doctrines are only ideas, and ideas can be clarified. For the church, the pressure remained indigestible, because churches are institutions, and institutions experience falsification as a threat to the loyalty they exist to organise. The two-argument correction generalises the lesson: Popper's historical argument about immunisation is best read as a claim about institutions guarding doctrines, and his logical argument about prophecy is best read as a claim about one doctrine's ambitions, and the tradition that learned to separate its ideas from its institutions is the tradition that learned, at last, to answer him in kind. Students meeting the topic through examination syllabuses, where Popper appears as a named evaluation and little more, are rarely told any of this; the fuller account is available in the series' treatment of [analytical Marxism and dialectics](/2012/10/01/analytical-marxism-and-dialectics/), which takes up the school's handling of the dialectical inheritance directly.

## Severity in practice: how a test earns its verdict

Popper's philosophy is often summarised by its slogan and rarely by its machinery, yet the machinery determines what the critique of Marxism can and cannot claim, and a section on the machinery is owed to any reader who wants to operate the audit rather than merely cite it. A test, in Popper's developed account, is severe when the result it produced was improbable in the light of background knowledge without the theory under examination: when the theory told the investigator to look for something the rest of what was known said should not be there, and it was there. Corroboration is the record of such survivals, and it is strictly a report about the past performance of a theory under sincere attempts to break it; it confers no probability of truth, since the next severe test is always pending and since, on Popper's logic, the most probable statements are the emptiest ones. The acceptance of the basic statements that record a test's outcome is itself a social act: the community of investigators agrees, by a decision that can be revisited, to treat a described observation as settled for the purpose in hand, knowing that every observation is soaked in the theories of the instruments and the observers. Falsification, therefore, is in Popper's own mature account a methodological achievement stacked on conventions, which is precisely why his rules against the conventionalist stratagems carry the moral weight they do: the conventions are the load bearing walls of the whole structure, and a community that adopts the stratagems has not broken a law of logic but has resigned from the institution that makes verdicts possible. A reader who grasps this has grasped why the critique of Marxism takes the form of a narrative about conduct rather than a demonstration from definitions, and why Popper's own later qualifications, far from being a retreat, were the explicit statement of what his machinery had always implied.

Applied with this machinery in view, several familiar episodes in the Marx debate change their aspect. Consider the tradition's handling of the rising organic composition of capital and the associated tendency of the rate of profit to fall. Stated as a mechanism with counteracting influences, the claim directs attention to measurable quantities, the ratio of constant to variable capital, the movement of profit rates across long periods, the cheapening of the elements of constant capital that the theory itself lists among the countertendencies; and investigators inside and outside the tradition have spent decades over national accounts and firm data attempting exactly the severe tests the machinery prescribes, with results that remain contested in the way genuine empirical results are contested, by counter measurement and rival derivation rather than by anathema. Whatever the eventual verdict on the tendency, the episode shows a Marxist claim behaving, in the hands of researchers, the way Popper said scientific claims should behave: staked, measured, and argued over on evidence. Consider, by contrast, the handling of the forecast of revolution in the advanced West by the parties that had staked their authority on it: there the machinery of testing was never engaged, the counteracting influences were never enumerated in advance, and the verdict of events was never accepted as a verdict, because the statements in question functioned in those institutions not as conjectures but as charters. The difference between the two episodes is not a difference between two doctrines but between two institutional settings for the same doctrine, one of which permitted the audit and one of which could not survive it. Severity, in other words, is not a property a text possesses on the page; it is a property of the relation between a text and the community that holds it, and Popper's philosophy, fully stated, knew this. The legend that converted his audit into a property of doctrines was cruder than its author, and a series that has followed his argument this closely is entitled to end the machinery's exposition by returning the compliment his enemies rarely pay him: the mature Popper is a subtler thinker than the Popper of the slogan, in the Marx case above all.

## Popper as a reader of Marx: the scholarly audit of the prosecutor

A final theatre of the debate concerns not Popper's philosophy but his scholarship, the question of whether the Marx of the second volume is the Marx of the texts, and it belongs in this article because the volume's authority, in classrooms and common rooms, rests as much on its air of close reading as on its logic. The audit of the prosecutor has produced a mixed report, more favourable than the Hegel audit and less favourable than the legend's admirers suppose. On the credit side, Popper's reading of Marx's economic theory was serious by the standards of hostile criticism in his generation: he engaged the labour theory of value, the theory of accumulation, and the account of crises as arguments rather than as incantations, he distinguished the theory's analytical claims from its prophetic ones with a care few critics matched, and his summary of the theory's sociological core, its institutionalism and its doctrine of unintended consequences, has been quoted with respect by scholars who reject his conclusions. On the debit side, the volume's Marx is assembled with a prosecutor's selectivity: the prophetic passages are quoted at length and the qualifications at a discount; the late Marx, with his cautions about universal paths and his openness on Russia, appears, but briefly, and in a subordinate clause of the argument; and the dialectical method is treated throughout in its most vulnerable form, as a licence to affirm contradictions, rather than in the stronger forms its serious exponents, then and since, have given it. The scholarly consensus that has slowly formed is that the second volume is a great polemic containing a good, partial, dated reading of Marx, of the kind that remains indispensable as an adversary and unusable as a textbook. That verdict has a Popperian neatness: the volume is itself a bold conjecture about its subject, severely tested by two generations of scholarship, corroborated in its structure of accusation, and falsified in a number of its exhibits, beginning with Hegel.

There is a reciprocal point, seldom made, about what the scholarly audit reveals of the tradition Popper prosecuted. The defence of Marx against his prosecutor was conducted, in the decades after the volume appeared, largely by the methods the volume recommended: by returning to the texts, establishing contexts, correcting quotations, and distinguishing what the founder wrote from what the church attributed to him. The great editorial labours on Marx's manuscripts and drafts, the publication of the Grundrisse and of the unexpurgated correspondence, the reconstruction of the late Marx from the notebooks, armed the defence with exhibits the prosecutor had not seen, and the defence's strongest modern form, that Marx's prophetic register is one register among several and not the theory's essence, is a conclusion of archival scholarship rather than of dialectical agility. In this sense the debate's history vindicates a method neither camp owned: the patient, evidence governed correction of inherited portraits, which is falsification's homelier cousin, the footnote. Popper's Hegel chapter failed that discipline and was corrected by it; his Marx chapters submitted to it better and were refined by it; and the tradition's self understanding was improved by being forced through it. A student who wants the wider map of the objections to the theory, beyond the one prosecutor this article owns, will find it in the series' survey of the [critiques of Marxism in full](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/), and the specific objections to the philosophy of history that Popper's volume fed are gathered in the treatment of [the case against historical materialism](/2010/04/15/case-against-historical-materialism/), to which his charges about prophecy and immunisation supplied much of their modern form.

## Popper's positive programme: situational logic and the explanation of institutions

Popper was not only a prosecutor, and the constructive half of his social philosophy is the standard against which his reading of Marx should finally be measured, because it reveals both the genuine kinship he refused to acknowledge and the genuine difference he was right to insist on. His positive proposal for social science was the method of situational analysis: reconstruct the situation in which agents found themselves, their aims, their information, the institutional constraints on their choices, and the logic that made their actions adequate to the situation as they saw it, and then derive the outcome, including the vast unintended surplus no agent chose. The method, which he also called the zero method or rational reconstruction, begins by assuming agents act appropriately to their situations and then corrects the model where behaviour departs from the assumption, and it assigns institutions the central explanatory role: institutions are the frozen logic of past situations, the channels that make some actions easy, some costly, and some unthinkable. Against this programme Popper set what he called historicism and psychologism, the two errors of explaining social outcomes by destiny or by human nature, and he presented Marx as the thinker who had come closest to the institutional method before betraying it to prophecy: Marx's analysis of how the factory system, the market, and the law of property structured the choices of capitalist and worker alike was, in Popper's judgement, situational analysis of genius, conducted in the service of a philosophy of history the method itself refutes, since a situational analyst knows that outcomes depend on situations, and situations containing new knowledge cannot be inherited from a law.

The kinship matters because it explains the peculiar temperature of the second volume, which is not the temperature of contempt but of exasperated recognition: Popper prosecuted in Marx a version of his own method wedded to everything his philosophy opposed. The difference matters equally, and it can be stated as a clean disagreement about where explanation terminates. For the situational analyst, explanation terminates in institutions and the logic of situations, which are themselves to be explained by further situational analysis, in an open regress that never reaches a foundation. For the Marx of the analytical register, explanation terminates, at least provisionally, in the structure of production relations, which are held to condition the situations and their logics systematically rather than accidentally. Popper's methodological individualism denies that the conditioning can be given that direction without essentialism; the Marxist tradition answers that the situations Popper reconstructs are not given by nature but are produced and reproduced by the very relations the analysis treats as background, so that terminating explanation in situations merely declines to ask where situations come from. The dispute is real, it is still alive in the philosophy of social science, and it is a far better dispute than the legend's shouting match about pseudoscience, because both positions are stated in it clearly enough to lose. A reader who reaches this point of the article has the materials to conduct that dispute properly: the demarcation machinery from the early sections, the audit of the tradition's conduct from the middle ones, and now the constructive programmes whose collision is the debate's actual content. That the collision is so rarely staged, in a literature content to trade verdicts, is the last count in this article's indictment of the summary, which protects both camps from the argument each would find hardest.

## Verisimilitude: what a refuted theory leaves behind

One further piece of Popper's mature philosophy completes the account, because it governs what follows, on his own principles, from the verdict his first attack delivered. A falsified theory, in Popper's logic, is not thereby worthless, and the later philosophy laboured to say why. Science, he held, aims at truth, and its progress consists not in accumulating certainties but in replacing theories with successors of greater truthlikeness, greater verisimilitude: a false theory can contain more truth than its predecessor, can capture structures its successor must preserve, and can remain, after its execution, the best map of its domain that inquiry possesses until the successor arrives. Newton's mechanics is the standing example, falsified at the margins by relativity and quantum theory and still the framework in which bridges are built and tides are timed. The doctrine changes the emotional register of falsification entirely: to call a theory refuted is not to call it rubbish, and Popper, who spent decades formalising verisimilitude against technical objections, would have been obliged by his own philosophy to treat a refuted Marx exactly as he treated a refuted Newton, as a conjecture of great content whose truthlike parts a successor theory must explain and retain. The point is worth pressing because it disciplines the triumphalism the legend licenses. If Marx's prophetic claims were falsified, the falsification, on Popper's own terms, leaves standing everything in the theory that was truthlike: the mechanisms that were found where the theory said to look, the phenomena it unified that rivals left scattered, the institutions it taught social science to interrogate. A successor social science, on the Popperian picture, would not begin by forgetting Marx but by explaining why a theory with so much truth in it failed where it failed, which is, not coincidentally, the research programme the best Marx scholarship of the last half century has actually pursued, under other flags.

The doctrine also cuts the other way, and fairness requires the cut to be shown. Verisimilitude is merciless toward the immunised version of a theory, because a theory repaired into compatibility with everything has, by Popper's content doctrine, surrendered the very property in which truthlikeness consists: it says too little to be close to the truth about anything in particular, since closeness to truth is measured by the truths a theory asserts and the risks it runs in asserting them. The rescued doctrine of the orthodoxies is thus, on the mature Popper's scales, worse off than the refuted doctrine of the founder: the founder's theory, having risked much and lost, retains the dignity and the content of a great false theory, while the church's version, having risked nothing, possesses neither truth nor the instructive shape of error. There could be no more complete inversion of the legend, which teaches students to pity Marx and admire the certainty of his defenders: on the philosophy of the critic himself, the pity and the admiration change places. It is fitting that the article's last substantive section should record this inversion, because it is the two-argument correction carried to its conclusion. Popper's legacy to the study of Marx is not a verdict to be quoted but a set of instruments to be used: the separation of arguments, the audit of conduct under failure, the suspicion of every rescue, including the rescues performed on Popper. Used honestly, the instruments convict the orthodoxies, acquit much of the founder, retire the slogan, and leave the argument where serious argument belongs, in the open.

## The Oedipus effect: predictions that change what they predict

Buried in The Poverty of Historicism is a subsidiary argument that has outgrown its parent in influence, and no complete account of Popper on method can omit it, because it identifies a difficulty for social prediction that would remain even if the knowledge argument were waived. Popper called it the Oedipus effect, after the prophecy whose utterance set in train the events that fulfilled it: in social life, a prediction is itself a social fact, entering the situation it describes and altering the behaviour it purports merely to forecast. A forecast of a bank's failure, believed, causes the run that fails the bank; a forecast of a party's victory, believed, swells or depresses its vote; a forecast of revolution, believed by a government, provokes the repression that either strangles the revolution or manufactures its grievances. The social scientist therefore cannot stand outside the system in the astronomer's clean position, not because society is too complex, though it is, but because the forecast is one of the system's moving parts. Popper drew the moral that social predictions are interventions and must be judged partly as actions, with the prophet bearing a responsibility the astronomer escapes, and he used the point against the historicists' favourite rhetorical position, that of the neutral scientist merely reporting history's intentions: a reported intention of history, broadcast to history's participants, is a move in the game it claims to umpire.

The effect's bearing on the Marx case is double edged in a way that rewards attention. Against the tradition, it explains a pattern Popper's other arguments leave mysterious: why the prophecies so persistently failed in the act of being believed. A theory that announces the inevitable radicalisation of the working class arms its opponents with the announcement, and the reforms that falsified the misery forecast, social insurance, labour law, the shortening of the working day, were enacted in part by statesmen who had read the prophecy and preferred to falsify it cheaply rather than fulfil it dearly; Bismarck's social legislation, designed explicitly to undercut a rising socialist movement, is the type case of a prediction engineering its own refutation. For the tradition, the same effect supplies a partial defence its advocates have not always known they possessed: a prophecy that fails because it was heeded has not been falsified in the astronomer's sense, since the initial conditions the prophecy assumed included its own absence from the scene, and the theory's causal story, that the announcement of danger moved the system, may be the true part of the whole transaction. Popper, to his credit, saw the complication and refused to let it dissolve his main argument: the Oedipus effect, he held, limits prediction further rather than rescuing prophecy, since a prophet who must calculate the effects of his own prophecy on the prophesied system faces a regress no method terminates. But the honest bookkeeping of this article records the effect as the point where the second attack's clean logic meets the mess of its subject matter, and where both sides can learn something the slogan never taught: predictions about society are wagers placed inside the casino, and the house is listening.

## The defence's best exhibits: Marx against the universal path

The fairness of Popper's refutation narrative turns, in the end, on a small set of texts in which Marx addressed the scope of his own claims, and the defence's handling of them is the strongest purely scholarly move in the entire reply literature, because the exhibits are the founder's own and their dates are late. The first is the letter of 1877 to the Russian journal that had discussed Capital, in which Marx protested, with unusual explicitness, against the conversion of his historical sketch of capitalism's genesis in Western Europe into a historico philosophical theory of the general path every people is fated to tread. The protest is not ambiguous, and it is not early: it comes a decade after Capital's first volume, from the theorist in his maturity, contemplating the use of his name by interpreters who needed a universal script. The second exhibit is the correspondence of 1881 with Vera Zasulich, who had asked whether Russia's peasant commune was doomed by the theory's logic to dissolve before capitalism, and Marx's reply, drafted repeatedly and with visible care, declined the doom: the commune's fate, he held, would be settled by the historical environment in which it stood, and the analysis of Capital supplied no fatality either way for a country whose development had not run the Western course. The third exhibit is the joint preface Marx and Engels attached to the Russian edition of the Communist Manifesto in 1882, which entertained, conditionally and in print, the possibility that Russia might pass to a communal order without traversing the full capitalist sequence, if a Russian revolution became the signal for revolution in the West. Taken together, the exhibits show the founder, in his last decade, resisting precisely the ambition the second attack convicts: the ambition to convert an analysis of one civilisation's trajectory into a law of all trajectories.

The prosecutor's answer to the exhibits is known, and it is not nothing. Popper's target, he and his defenders reply, was the Marxism that governed the world, the doctrine of the parties and the textbooks, and that doctrine did preach the universal path, did quote the Manifesto's certainties and not the letters' cautions, and did treat the exhibits, when it noticed them at all, as occasional pieces beneath the system's dignity. A critic of a living doctrine is entitled to its operative form, and the operative Marxism of the century between the exhibits and the critique was a historicism of exactly the kind The Poverty of Historicism describes. The defence's counter is that entitlement has limits: a volume that names the founder in its indictment, and that generations of readers have taken as a verdict on the founder, owes the founder his whole record, and a reading that builds the refutation narrative on the strong prophetic claims while footnoting the late disclaimers has selected its evidence in the manner the prosecutor elsewhere forbids. The dispute cannot be closed by logic, since it concerns the ethics of representation, but its terms can be fixed, and this article fixes them as the ledger does: the refutation narrative stands against the tradition's operative doctrine, partially stands against the founder's published system, and weakens in exact proportion as the founder's late qualifications are admitted to the record. Students who cite the exhibits should cite them whole, and prosecutors who answer them should answer them, not the use their own side made of ignoring them. In a debate this saturated with summary, the documents remain the only participants who never exaggerate.

## The ethics of criticism: the strongest form of the other side

Popper's philosophy contains one more instrument, rarely listed among his doctrines, which this article has been using throughout and which deserves its name before the verdicts are gathered. In his later writings on the conduct of discussion, above all in the essay on the myth of the framework, Popper argued that fruitful criticism requires a deliberate act of reconstruction: the critic must state the opposing position in the strongest form it can bear, stronger, where necessary, than its own defenders have managed, because a criticism that demolishes only the weak form of an argument has demolished nothing that anyone needed. He paired the rule with a confession he proposed as the motto of rational discussion, that he might be wrong and his opponent right, and that by an effort the two might get nearer to the truth between them. The rule and the confession are not ornaments on the philosophy; they are the falsificationist discipline applied to persons, the refusal of the conventionalist twist in its social form, where the twist consists of quoting the opponent's weakest sentence and calling the quotation a refutation. Measured by that discipline, the second volume's record is mixed in the pattern this article has established: the Marx chapters largely honour it, reconstructing the theory's analytical power before prosecuting its prophecy, while the Hegel chapter violates it in the manner Kaufmann documented, and the legend violates it wholesale, in both directions, by every camp that has used Popper's name.

The rule also supplies the fairest available test for the replies this article has marshalled, and applying it to them is the last act of evenhandedness the subject requires. The Duhem and Quine reply honours the rule, since it grants the immunisation phenomenon and contests only its logic. The Lakatos reply honours it in an even stronger form, by accepting Popper's demand for growth of knowledge and rebuilding the unit of appraisal until the demand can be fairly applied. The explanatory defence, that Marx predicted little and explained much, honours the rule in its scholarly version, where the late texts are produced and the prophetic register conceded, and violates it in its polemical version, where the tradition's century of prophecy is treated as a misunderstanding nobody important ever held. The demarcation objection, that the criterion would exile half the sciences, honours the rule when it is pressed, as here, to its concession, that it lowers a bar rather than clearing it. A debate conducted under Popper's own ethics, by both sides, converges on roughly the position this article's ledger records, and the convergence is itself evidence that the ledger is not a compromise manufactured for comfort: it is where the strongest forms of the arguments, allowed to meet, actually finish. That is the deepest reason the two-argument correction matters beyond this topic. Summaries spare their users the meeting, and the meeting is the only place verdicts worth having are reached. The series to which this article belongs has staked its method on the meeting, for the founder's texts above all; extending the courtesy to the founder's greatest prosecutor is not generosity. It is consistency, and consistency, in the long run of an argument, is the only courtesy that counts.

## Which of Popper's arguments still stand

The verdict can now be gathered without another summary of the kind this article exists to correct, because the ledger has done the gathering in advance and the sections have argued each row. The concession about Marx's original scientific standing stands, and it convicts the legend rather than the theory's defenders. The refutation narrative stands in part: the tradition's operative forecasts did fail in the directions Popper catalogued, while the attribution of every item at full strength to Marx's own texts requires the discounts the scholarship has since applied. The immunisation charge stands against the orthodoxies it named, as a documented pattern of conduct, and does not stand as a logical property of the doctrine as such, since immunisation is a practice and not an essence, and practices can be reformed, as parts of the tradition demonstrably reformed theirs under the pressure of the century's events. The demarcation criterion stands as a disciplinary demand and has fallen as a border post, a fall its own author's later qualifications ratified. The historicism argument, the ledger's least quoted row, stands within its scope almost entire: no reply in the literature has restored the possibility of predicting the growth of knowledge, and the wholesale migration of serious Marxist scholarship from prophecy to explanation is the tribute the argument collected without ever being answered in form. The Hegel scholarship fails, and its failure instructs. The political moral, piecemeal against utopian engineering, stands as a warning whose force each generation retests against its own planners. What does not stand, when the inventory is complete, is the sentence with which this article began, the single line about unfalsifiability and pseudoscience, which misstates the sequence, omits the stronger argument, and borrows an authority its supposed author spent fifty years qualifying. Nor does the mirror image stand, the defensive slogan that Popper has been refuted and may be set aside: the philosophy of science that amended his criterion preserved his central demand in every successor scheme, and the tradition's own best scholars have conducted their work, for two generations, under the discipline of clarity and exposure that he, more than anyone, taught the century to require. Between the two slogans lies the actual state of the question, narrower than either and more interesting than both. The two-argument correction is offered as its replacement: not a slogan but a reading instruction, separating what Popper argued historically from what he argued logically, so that each can be given the verdict, and only the verdict, that it earned.

## For students and exam candidates

Popper is the evaluation reference most likely to be rewarded on any examination question about Marxism in philosophy, politics, or sociology, and he is also the reference most likely to be squandered, because the summary version examiners have read a thousand times signals that the candidate knows the legend rather than the argument. The discriminating moves are the ones this article has built its structure on. Separate the two arguments and say that you are separating them: the falsifiability charge, which is a historical claim about a theory that was refuted and then immunised, and the historicism charge, which is a logical claim about the impossibility of predicting a history that depends on future knowledge. Quote the concession: Popper held Marx's original theory to be scientific, and a candidate who writes that sentence has already distinguished the script from the cohort's. Then evaluate with the philosophy of science rather than with adjectives: Duhem and Quine on isolated refutation, Lakatos on programmes and problem shifts, and the concession that these replies lower the bar for everyone rather than clearing it for Marx alone. Deploy the knowledge argument where questions touch prediction, determinism, or the laws of history, and the conventionalist twist where they touch the tradition's record; the treatment of [economic determinism in the debate over Marx's method](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/) supplies the adjacent material. Close with the verdict the evidence supports: the charges stand against the orthodoxies and the prophecy, and fail as a mechanical test of the doctrine.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This topic teaches a transferable skill better than almost any other in the syllabus, because the pathology it exhibits, a strong argument flattened into a false slogan by its own supporters, is the standard fate of every difficult thinker the students will ever meet. A productive seminar begins by writing the legend's sentence on the board and asking the class to find it in Popper: the exercise fails, instructively, and the search through the second volume's actual sequence, concession, refutation, immunisation, teaches more about reading than a term of exhortation. The ledger in this article is designed for classroom use: assign each row to a pair of students, one to defend Popper's claim from the texts and one to press the strongest reply, with the verdict to be negotiated rather than announced. The Bernstein episode makes an ideal second session, since it relocates the falsification question inside the tradition and breaks the seminar's tribal alignment before it forms. Teachers should insist throughout on the distinction between the two arguments, and should mark down, visibly, any answer that grades a doctrine by a mechanical test while excusing the grader's own tradition from the audit. ## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The researcher's first discipline in this field is bibliographic, because the conflation of Popper's texts is the original error from which the others propagate. The falsifiability narrative, with the conventionalist twist and the verdict on the followers, belongs to the second volume of The Open Society and Its Enemies of 1945; the argument against historicism, with the knowledge proof and the laws against trends distinction, belongs to The Poverty of Historicism, published as articles in Economica in 1944 and 1945 and as a book in 1957; the autobiographical anecdote of the eclipse and the consulting rooms belongs to the later Conjectures and Refutations. Citations that attribute the knowledge argument to the falsifiability book, or the immunisation charge to the historicism book, should be treated as markers of secondhand acquaintance. Historians of the debate will want Kaufmann's 1959 rebuttal on Hegel beside the volume it audits, and Lakatos's application of the research programme scheme to Marxism beside Popper's original charge, since the pair mark the two moments when the debate's terms were reset. Journalists, finally, are offered the accurate one sentence version, for use where the legend's sentence would otherwise go: Popper argued that Marx's theory began as science, failed its forecasts, and was rescued into irrefutability by its followers, and separately that no one can predict a history shaped by knowledge that does not yet exist. The wider question of the theory's scientific standing, beyond the one prosecutor, is taken up in the series' examination of [whether Marxism is a science](/2019/10/01/is-marxism-a-science/), and the study notes companion for this series, [the Marxism study notes at VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), collects the textual anchors for both arguments in one place.

## The two-argument correction as a reading discipline

The article ends where it began, with the correction, because the correction is finally not about Popper at all. Every figure in this series arrives at the reader through the same machinery that mangled him: a system too large for a summary, a summary too useful to be checked, and a verdict too convenient to be surrendered. Marx himself suffered the machinery first and worst, at the hands of friends before enemies, and the parallel is not decorative. A tradition that immunised its founder's forecasts against the century also summarised its prosecutor's argument into a shape the prosecutor disowned, and the two failures are one failure, the preference for the usable version over the true one. The discipline this article has tried to practise, on the critic exactly as the series practises it on the founder, is the refusal of that preference: arguments separated, sequences restored, concessions recorded, verdicts allocated row by row, and the texts treated as the court of appeal over every summary, including this one. Popper's name for the underlying virtue was intellectual honesty, and he located it, rightly, not in holding correct opinions but in the readiness to state what would change one's mind. By that standard his two arguments, corrected and separated, remain the most useful hostile instruments the study of Marx possesses, not because they end the argument, but because, read properly, they are the ones that teach the argument how to continue.

A last word is owed to the reader who came to this article for a verdict to carry away, since the article has deliberately refused to supply one in the portable form. The refusal is the verdict's content. What Popper established, when his case is separated into its components and each is given its earned weight, is a set of findings no subsequent literature has dislodged: that the Marxist orthodoxies of the twentieth century maintained their doctrine by the reinterpretive methods his philosophy forbids; that the ambition to predict the course of history founders on the unpredictability of the knowledge that shapes it; and that a theory's scientific honour is measured not by the loyalty of its defenders but by the risks its statements continue to run. What he did not establish is equally definite: he did not show that Marx's theory was born a pseudoscience, since his own narrative begins by certifying its scientific birth; he did not show that its analytical machinery was worthless, since his own philosophy of verisimilitude assigns falsified theories their truthlike residue; and he did not show that his criterion could police the borders of science, since his own later work withdrew it from that office. A reader who holds both halves of that inventory at once possesses something better than a slogan for either camp: a map of where the argument about Marxism's method actually stands, drawn by the unusual method of reading its most famous prosecutor as carefully as he demanded, and occasionally failed, to be read. The map is this article's deliverable, and the two-argument correction is the compass printed in its corner.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was Popper's criticism of Marxism?

Popper's criticism had two independent parts. He argued, in The Open Society and Its Enemies, that Marx's theory was originally scientific and falsifiable, that its central predictions failed, and that later followers rescued it by reinterpretation until it became unfalsifiable. He argued separately, in The Poverty of Historicism, that predicting the course of history is logically impossible for any theory, because history depends on the future growth of knowledge, which cannot itself be predicted. The two arguments differ in kind, one historical and one logical, and most summaries damage both by merging them into a single charge about pseudoscience.

### Q: Did Popper say Marxism is unfalsifiable?

Not as a starting point, and not about Marx's own theory. Popper's sequence ran the other way: the theory began falsifiable, staked itself on predictions about revolution, immiseration, and the simplification of classes, and was in his judgement refuted by events. Unfalsifiability entered as the end of the story, the condition produced when followers reinterpreted theory and evidence to absorb every failure, a manoeuvre he called a conventionalist twist. The popular summary deletes the first two steps and presents his conclusion about the tradition's later state as though it were a definition of the doctrine from the outset, which reverses his argument.

### Q: What is historicism according to Popper?

Historicism, in Popper's usage, is the approach to social science that takes historical prediction as its main aim and holds the aim attainable by discovering the laws, rhythms, or trends of historical development. It is a doctrine about method, not a synonym for studying history, and Popper applied it far beyond Marxism, to racial, cyclical, and theological philosophies of destiny alike. Marxism was his chief specimen because it was the most systematic and intellectually formidable historicism available. His book against the doctrine argues that its central ambition confuses trends with laws and demands an unconditional prophecy that neither physics nor logic can supply.

### Q: Did Popper respect Marx?

Within the limits of a determined opponent, yes, and the evidence is in his own pages. He praised Marx's sincerity and his burning indignation at the exploitation practised by an unrestrained capitalism, credited his analysis of institutions and of unintended consequences as a permanent acquisition for social science, and insisted that Marx's theory in its original form had been a genuine scientific attempt, bold enough to be refuted. His contempt was reserved for the followers who, in his account, protected the theory from the consequences of its failure. The legend's Popper, sneering at a charlatan, is a figure his text does not contain.

### Q: What is a conventionalist twist?

A conventionalist twist is Popper's name for the rescue of a theory in evidential trouble by reinterpretation rather than revision. When a prediction fails, the follower modifies an auxiliary assumption, redefines a term, or redescribes the evidence, so that the contradiction dissolves without the theory risking anything new. Popper had catalogued such stratagems in his philosophy of science before applying the idea to Marxism, whose orthodoxies he charged with performing the twist systematically: misery redefined as relative, delay redefined as a stage, failure redefined as confirmation at a deeper level. A theory maintained by twists forfeits falsifiability, since the rescue is always available in advance.

### Q: How do Marxists reply to Popper?

The strongest replies borrow their weapons from philosophy of science rather than from party literature. The Duhem and Quine problem holds that no theory is refuted in isolation, since predictions depend on auxiliaries, so the blame for a failure is always partly a judgement. Lakatos recasts the question: appraise research programmes by whether their revisions predict novel facts or merely absorb old ones. Defenders add that Marx made few dated predictions and that the theory is principally explanatory, and they note that falsificationism itself has been heavily amended by Kuhn and others. Honest versions of the reply concede the orthodoxies' immunising record while contesting the mechanical verdict.

### Q: Is falsifiability a good test for social theory?

It is a good discipline and a poor border post. As a discipline, the demand that a theory state what would count against it, and that its defenders not rescue it by contentless reinterpretation, exposes real evasions in social theory as elsewhere, and no serious tradition can afford to refuse it. As a border post, mechanically applied, it excludes too much: probabilistic claims, historical reconstructions in geology and evolutionary biology, and most of economics would fail alongside Marxism, and Popper himself presented the criterion as a methodological proposal rather than a law of reason. The defensible use is comparative and behavioural: audit what a tradition does when its forecasts fail.

### Q: Was Popper fair to Hegel?

On the evidence assembled by Walter Kaufmann in his rebuttal of 1959, substantially not. Kaufmann showed that Popper's Hegel chapter relied on quotations removed from contexts that altered their bearing, on hostile secondary sources in place of Hegel's texts, and on attributions of positions Hegel had argued against. The finding does not touch Popper's logical argument against historicism, which owes nothing to Hegel scholarship, and scholars judge his Marx chapters closer to their texts than his Hegel chapters. But the episode permanently repriced The Open Society as intellectual history and stands as the clearest case of the critic failing the evidential discipline he demanded of others.

### Q: Does Lakatos offer a better test than Popper?

For most purposes in this debate, yes, because Lakatos appraises the unit that actually exists in history. Instead of asking whether a single theory forbids some outcome, his methodology of research programmes asks whether a sequence of theories, held together by a hard core and a heuristic, has shifted progressively, predicting novel facts that were then corroborated, or degeneratingly, absorbing known facts after the event. The test is slower, comparative, and revisable, which matches how sciences actually keep and discard frameworks. Its discomfort for Marxism's defenders is that Lakatos applied it himself and returned a severe verdict on the programme's problem shifts, so adopting his test means arguing about the record rather than dismissing the standard.

### Q: Which of Popper's arguments still stand?

The ledger's verdicts are uneven, which is the finding. The immunisation charge stands against the orthodoxies it named, as documented conduct. The argument against historicism stands within its scope: no reply has restored the possibility of predicting the growth of knowledge, and serious Marxist scholarship's migration from prophecy to explanation concedes its force in practice. The demarcation criterion stands as a disciplinary demand and has fallen as a mechanical border between science and non science, a fall Popper's own later qualifications ratified. The refutation narrative stands in part, subject to discounts for Marx's hedges and tendencies. The Hegel scholarship fails outright.

### Q: How does Popper's falsifiability charge differ from his historicism charge?

They differ in subject, in logical type, and in what could answer them. The falsifiability charge is a historical claim about one tradition: that Marxism began as a testable theory, was refuted, and was rescued into irrefutability by its followers, and it can be answered by evidence about that tradition's conduct, its revisions, and its founders' texts. The historicism charge is a logical claim about every possible theory of history: that the course of history cannot be predicted because it depends on unpredictable future knowledge, and it can be answered only by breaking the proof, not by producing a better record. Merging them makes the first look definitional and the second look partisan; both impressions are artefacts of the merger.

### Q: What should a student separate in the claim that Marxism was refuted and then immunised?

Three separations earn the marks. Separate the stages: an originally falsifiable theory, a claimed refutation by events, and a subsequent rescue operation are distinct claims requiring distinct evidence, and a strong answer tests each rather than swallowing the sequence whole. Separate the accused: Marx's texts, with their tendencies and qualifications, are one defendant, and the orthodox traditions that governed parties and states are another, and Popper's evidence is much stronger against the second. Separate the standards: the refutation claim is judged by the Duhem and Quine problem about isolated testing, and the immunisation claim by the comparative audit of what traditions do after failure, in which Marxism has company.

### Q: Why does the poverty of historicism target laws of history rather than class theory?

Because its argument operates at the level of any claimed law of historical development, whatever its content. The knowledge proof shows that the future course of history cannot be derived in advance, since that course is shaped by discoveries whose content cannot be known before they are made; the laws against trends distinction shows that observed drifts cannot be promoted into unconditional prophecies. Neither step mentions class, exploitation, or the value theory, and a class analysis that renounced historical prophecy entirely, retaining its mechanisms and explanations while treating the future as open, would not be touched by the book at all. Popper aimed at the prophetic ambition Marxism shared with every philosophy of destiny, not at its sociology.

### Q: What do defenders concede when they answer Popper on prediction?

More than the polemical versions of the reply admit. They concede that the orthodox traditions did repeatedly reinterpret the theory to absorb failed forecasts, since contesting that record costs the credibility the rest of the reply needs. They concede, usually in practice rather than in words, that the strong prophetic claims, revolution in the advanced countries on a maturing schedule and history's destination as a deliverable of science, cannot be sustained in their original form, which is why so much serious defence relocates the theory's centre to explanation. And they concede that the surviving defence lowers the bar for everyone, showing that the criterion also embarrasses geology, evolutionary history, and economics, rather than showing that Marxism clears a bar the others pass.

### Q: How does Popper's own later qualification change the standard verdict?

Popper spent fifty years qualifying the instruments of his wartime attack, and the qualifications retire the legend's version of him. He insisted that falsifiability was a methodological proposal adopted by convention, not a truth a theory could be convicted of violating; he stressed that falsifications themselves rest on accepted basic statements and remain fallible and revisable; and he repeated, and sometimes sharpened, the concession that Marx's original theory had been scientific and falsifiable, his complaint being addressed to the rescue operation and the prophetic claim. A verdict citing Popper must therefore cite the later Popper too, and his final position supports an audit of a tradition's conduct rather than an excommunication of a doctrine by logical inspection.
