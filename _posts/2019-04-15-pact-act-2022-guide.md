When a veteran develops a serious disease years after leaving a war zone, and the service record shows the place of service but no measurement of the smoke, chemicals, or radiation encountered there, who bears the cost of the missing evidence? The PACT Act answered that question for a large class of toxic exposure claims. Rather than leaving each claimant to reconstruct an unrecorded exposure and persuade a physician to connect that exposure to a later disease, the statute assigned whole categories of service a legal presumption of exposure and whole lists of diseases a legal presumption of connection. The operative question was never whether toxic substances were present in the theaters of war. The operative question was one of proof, and the statute answered the proof question in favor of the claimant inside defined boundaries of place, period, and diagnosis.

![Toxic exposure presumptions and the PACT Act burden shift - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-10.webp)

The formal identity of the statute fixes its place in the law. The Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022 was enacted as Public Law 117-168, signed by President Joseph Biden on August 10, 2022, after passage through Senate bill 3373. The long name honored Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson, an Ohio Army National Guard soldier whose lung cancer and death gave the legislative campaign a named case, and the short name condensed the promise of the statute into a single word. The act amended title 38 of the United States Code across benefits, health care, research, and departmental administration, and a separate title created a federal court remedy for the water contamination at Camp Lejeune in North Carolina. Scale alone did not define the enactment. Definition did, because nearly every major provision turned on categories written into the statutory text.

The presumption provisions formed the center. For veterans of the Gulf War and post September 11 eras, the act presumed exposure to airborne hazards for service in listed locations and made twenty three diseases presumptive, spanning respiratory illnesses and cancers of the brain, lungs, kidneys, pancreas, and other organs. For veterans exposed to herbicide agents, the act widened the map of covered service beyond Vietnam to Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Guam, American Samoa, and Johnston Atoll, and added hypertension and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance to the disease list. For radiation exposed veterans, the act added the cleanup of Enewetak Atoll and the response efforts at Palomares in Spain and Thule in Greenland to the activities that qualified. Beside the new burn pit structure, the act strengthened the older Gulf War framework for qualifying chronic disabilities that resisted diagnosis.

Benefits were only half of the design. The act doubled the enhanced health care enrollment period for combat veterans from five years to ten years, opened a one year special enrollment window for a defined class of combat veterans whose earlier period had expired, and required toxic exposure screening for veterans enrolled in department health care. Research titles directed studies of mortality, cancer, and the health effects of exposure, and a new fund in the Treasury, the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund, carried the financing structure that produced the sharpest dispute of the final debate. That dispute concerned budget classification rather than eligibility, and the scale of the cost estimates followed from the classification choice as much as from the benefits themselves.

The claim that organizes the whole statute can be stated plainly. A presumption is a burden shift. In an ordinary claim, the claimant bears the burden on every element, and a failure of evidence on the connection between service and disease ends the claim. Under a presumption, the connection is supplied by law once qualifying service and a listed diagnosis are shown, and the government must produce affirmative evidence of the kind the statute describes to defeat the connection. The substance of the statute is therefore proof rather than money. Payment rates, rating percentages, and the claims machinery all predated the act and continued under the act. What changed was the allocation of evidentiary risk in cases where war had made individual proof impossible to assemble honestly.

The boundaries matter as much as the expansion. A presumption reaches only the locations, periods, and conditions that the text names, and a claimant outside those lines received no shortcut from the act. A presumption also decides connection alone. Rating, effective date, and payment remained separate questions under separate rules, and the department still had to find qualifying service and a diagnosed disability in the individual record. The account that follows traces the exposure problem, the equipoise standard that the Agent Orange framework built, each exposure category in turn, the enrollment and departmental provisions, the two rounds of passage in the summer of 2022, and the budget argument that nearly stopped the statute days before enactment.

## The Exposure Problem the Statute Answered

The Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, Public Law 117-168, signed on August 10, 2022, grew out of a mismatch between how modern American wars produced illness and how the veterans benefits system demanded proof of illness. The wars fought after the attacks of September 11, 2001 sent hundreds of thousands of service members to expeditionary bases in Iraq, Afghanistan, and neighboring countries, where waste had to be destroyed in places with little or no municipal sanitation, few incinerators, and supply lines built for combat rather than for environmental management. The solution adopted across the theater was the open-air burn pit, a shallow excavation or bermed area in which refuse was piled, soaked with jet fuel, and set alight. That choice solved an immediate logistics problem. It also placed a slow, diffuse, and poorly documented exposure at the center of thousands of later disability claims, and it forced Congress to confront a question that the Vietnam generation had already pressed for decades: what happens when a veteran is sick, the exposure was real, and the evidence needed to connect the two was never collected in the first place.

Burn pits were not a marginal practice confined to a handful of remote outposts. The Government Accountability Office reported in October 2010 that United States Central Command estimated 251 active burn pits in Afghanistan and 22 in Iraq in August 2010, and the same report observed that virtually every United States military installation in both countries had used burn pits. The Department of Defense estimate cited by the Government Accountability Office put solid waste generation at about ten pounds per soldier each day, a figure that explains both the appeal and the scale of open burning. A base housing hundreds of troops generated tons of refuse each week, and a major installation housing tens of thousands generated far more. Burning was expedient, required no specialized plant, and kept waste from accumulating around living quarters in a combat zone. For those operational reasons, pits operated at small patrol bases and at the largest installations in the theater alike, and the practice continued for years before comprehensive Central Command guidance on open pit burning arrived in 2009.

The largest pits were industrial in scale. The burn pit at Joint Base Balad in Iraq covered nearly ten acres, operated around the clock, and consumed more than 200 tons of waste in a day. Into pits of that kind went the full waste stream of a deployed force: food scraps and packaging, plastic bottles and other plastics, tires and vehicle parts, batteries, electronics and computer components, chemicals and paint, medical waste that included dressings and amputated limbs, animal carcasses, human waste, and worn out equipment of almost every description. Department of Defense guidance restricted the burning of items known to produce harmful emissions, including plastic, but the Government Accountability Office found in 2010 that operators at the four Iraqi bases visited for that review were not complying fully with the guidance, and Defense officials acknowledged that operators had routinely burned prohibited items from the start of each conflict. The fuel used to ignite and sustain the fires, JP-8 jet fuel, added a further layer of combustion products to a plume that already mixed the emissions of every material in the pile.

Exposure under those conditions bore little resemblance to an industrial accident with a known date, a known chemical, and a defined group of workers. Smoke from a pit drifted with the wind across runways, work areas, dining facilities, and sleeping quarters, and it did so day and night for the length of a deployment. A service member assigned to a large base breathed pit smoke while working, eating, exercising, and sleeping, whether or not the assigned duties involved the pit itself. Dosage depended on wind direction, distance from the pit, the materials burning on a particular day, and the length of the tour, none of which appeared in an individual service record as a measured quantity. The Department of Defense estimated that nearly 3.5 million troops from the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan may have had exposure sufficient to raise health concerns, a figure reported by Air Force Times in March 2022, and that estimate captured the essential difficulty. The exposed population was enormous, the exposure itself was a mixture rather than a single substance, and the record keeping of the period documented units, locations, and duties far better than it documented smoke.

The Agent Orange experience in Vietnam supplied the template for that difficulty, and legislators, veterans organizations, and Department of Veterans Affairs officials returned to the comparison repeatedly during the debates of 2021 and 2022. Representative Elaine Luria of Virginia, chairing a House Veterans Affairs subcommittee hearing on burn pit claims, recorded the phrase that veterans groups had made familiar by describing burn pits as the Agent Orange of the post-September 11 generation. The comparison rested on structure rather than on chemistry. In each case a substance spread across a war zone in enormous quantity entered the bodies of service members as a routine incident of deployment. In each case the illnesses attributed to the exposure emerged years or decades after homecoming. In each case the government asked individual veterans, claim by claim, to supply proof that a system built for battlefield records had never generated. And in each case the argument over benefits lasted far longer than the exposure itself.

Operation Ranch Hand, the Air Force herbicide program in Vietnam, sprayed an estimated 18 million to 20 million gallons of herbicides, including approximately 11 million to 12 million gallons of Agent Orange, over about 12,000 square miles of southern Vietnam between 1961 and 1971, according to a Congressional Research Service report on Agent Orange assistance to Vietnam. The spraying meant to strip jungle canopy that concealed opposing forces and to destroy crops that fed them. Agent Orange, a mixture of the herbicides 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T, carried a manufacturing contaminant, the dioxin known as TCDD, which the Environmental Protection Agency classified as a developmental toxicant and a probable human carcinogen. Aircraft sprayed the defoliant over forests and farmland, ground crews handled and loaded it, and troops on patrol moved through sprayed terrain, drank local water, and ate local food. As with burn pit smoke a generation later, exposure was an ambient condition of service in a place, not a discrete event stamped into a personnel file.

For Vietnam veterans, the consequences of that design became clear only across the 1970s and 1980s, as veterans developed cancers, chloracne, and other conditions that veterans attributed to herbicide exposure and sought disability compensation from the Department of Veterans Affairs. The claims system treated each application as an individual proof problem. A veteran had to establish service in Vietnam, actual exposure to herbicides, a diagnosed disease, and a medical connection between the exposure and the disease. Spraying records documented missions flown over coordinates on particular dates; the records did not document which individual soldiers stood beneath a given spray run, handled a leaking drum, or camped in a defoliated area weeks later. Dioxin left no signature in a service file, and a physician examining a veteran in 1985 could rarely state, from the medicine alone, whether a lymphoma had originated in herbicide exposure in 1969, in civilian exposures after discharge, or in causes that medicine could not identify. Denials accumulated across two decades, and the political resolution, when it arrived, changed the structure of proof rather than the underlying science. The Agent Orange Act of 1991, Public Law 102-4, established a presumption of service connection for diseases associated with herbicide exposure in veterans who served in the Republic of Vietnam between January 9, 1962 and May 7, 1975, and the statute directed a scientific review process, conducted through the Institute of Medicine, for adding diseases as evidence of association developed. Congress, in effect, decided that place and period of service would stand in for individual exposure proof, and that a listed diagnosis would stand in for an individual medical link.

### Why could veterans not simply prove their illnesses came from service?

Because a claim required proof of exposure during service and a medical opinion linking that exposure to a diagnosed disease, and decades later neither element could be documented reliably: service records rarely recorded diffuse exposures, and medicine could seldom attribute an individual cancer or respiratory disease to one wartime source.

The ordinary route to disability compensation, known as direct service connection, rested on three elements that the Department of Veterans Affairs required in claim after claim. A claimant needed a current diagnosed disability. A claimant needed an in-service event, injury, illness, or exposure. And a claimant needed a medical nexus, a reasoned medical opinion connecting the diagnosed condition to the in-service event. The first element was usually the easiest, since a physician could diagnose a cancer, a respiratory disease, or an autoimmune disorder from examinations and tests conducted long after discharge. The second and third elements carried the weight of the system, and both depended on evidence that wartime institutions had weak reasons to create and weaker systems for keeping.

Exposure proof failed first at the level of records. Armies record what armies need for operations: unit assignments, orders, duty rosters, medical encounters, and disciplinary and personnel actions. No comparable apparatus measured the air that a particular soldier breathed on a particular night at Balad, Bagram, or any of the hundreds of smaller installations with pits, and the Government Accountability Office reported in 2010 that Central Command did not routinely collect data even on the number of burn pits in use, reconstructing a theater-wide count only by querying individual base commanders. If the operating command lacked a reliable census of the pits themselves, an individual veteran stood little chance of producing an official record placing a specific body downwind of a specific fire on a specific date. Personnel rotated between bases, lived at varying distances from pits, and worked shifts that changed across a tour. A discharge document established the fact and the broad location of deployment. A discharge document said almost nothing about smoke.

Medical proof failed at a deeper level, because the nexus question asked medicine to perform a task that epidemiology performs only at the level of populations. A population study can compare disease rates between a large exposed group and a large unexposed group and can estimate whether exposure raised the risk of a condition across thousands of people. A treating physician confronted with one patient faces a different problem. Lung cancer arises in people who never deployed, respiratory disease has causes ranging from smoking to infection to occupational dust, and a rare cancer in a young veteran may be consistent with toxic exposure without bearing any marker that separates an exposure-caused case from a case of unknown origin. Latency compounded the problem. Diseases attributed to burn pits and to Agent Orange alike often appeared ten, twenty, or thirty years after exposure, across an interval filled with civilian jobs, other exposures, aging, and medical histories that gave a skeptical examiner alternative explanations and gave an honest examiner genuine uncertainty. The legal standard asked whether a connection was at least as likely as not, and for a large class of claims the available evidence could not carry a reasoned opinion across even that threshold, because the exposure half of the equation had dissolved into unrecorded air.

The results appeared in the adjudication statistics that lawmakers cited throughout the legislative fight. Laurine Carson, a Department of Veterans Affairs official, testified that between 2007 and 2020 the department approved burn pit related disability claims for 2,828 veterans out of 12,582 claims, a denial rate of about 78 percent. Department officials maintained during those years that the scientific evidence linking burn pit exposure to specific diseases remained insufficient to support broader presumptions, and department witnesses also warned that expanded eligibility would bring large patient volumes and large costs. Veterans organizations, joined by lawmakers of both parties on the House and Senate Veterans Affairs Committees, answered that the insufficiency of the evidence was itself a product of the exposure conditions and the record keeping failures described in the Government Accountability Office reports of 2010, 2016, and 2018, and that a proof standard demanding documentation the government had never created transferred the cost of that failure onto sick veterans. That disagreement, evidence against equity, defined the policy question that Congress took up in 2021.

Congress had taken narrower steps before the comprehensive bill. Legislation enacted in 2013 required the Department of Veterans Affairs to establish a registry for individuals who may have been exposed to toxic airborne chemicals and fumes from open burn pits, and the department established the Airborne Hazards and Open Burn Pit Registry in 2014. A registry gathered self-reported health information for research and monitoring, granted no benefits, created no presumption, and decided no claim, and the registry provisions receive fuller treatment in the burn pit account below. The gap between documentation and entitlement therefore persisted after 2014, and veterans organizations including Burn Pits 360, founded by retired Army Captain LeRoy Torres and Rosie Torres, pressed for legislation that would move from recording exposure to presuming its consequences. Across the 117th Congress, members introduced numerous standalone measures addressed to pieces of the problem, including bills on burn pit exposure recognition, on presumptive benefits for war fighters exposed to burn pits and other toxins, and on Agent Orange exposure equity, and the House and Senate Veterans Affairs Committees began combining provisions from multiple standalone bills into comprehensive packages during 2021.

The Senate track produced the Comprehensive and Overdue Support for Troops of War Act of 2021, the COST of War Act, Senate bill 3003, which Senator Jon Tester of Montana, chairman of the Senate Veterans Affairs Committee, advanced and which the committee reported on October 19, 2021. The House track produced the bill that ultimately supplied the enacted text and title. On June 17, 2021, Representative Mark Takano of California, chairman of the House Committee on Veterans Affairs, introduced the Honoring our PACT Act of 2021 as House bill 3967, and the committee held a markup on June 24, 2021. The House passed an amended version on March 3, 2022 by a vote of 256 to 174. In the Senate, Tester and the ranking Republican member, Senator Jerry Moran of Kansas, pursued a staged approach during the same period, and on February 1, 2022 the two senators introduced the Health Care for Burn Pit Veterans Act, Senate bill 3541, a narrower measure extending health care enrollment periods for combat veterans, which the Senate passed by unanimous consent on February 16, 2022. The comprehensive approach prevailed over the staged approach as 2022 advanced. The floor sequence that followed, from House passage in March 2022 through the Senate votes of June and July 2022 and final passage in August 2022, receives full treatment in the passage account below.

The name placed at the head of the enacted statute belonged to a soldier whose case embodied each element of the proof problem. Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson, a native of Pickerington, Ohio, enlisted in the Ohio Army National Guard in 2003, according to an Ohio National Guard history published by the Defense Visual Information Distribution Service. Robinson deployed to Kosovo for a year in 2004 and later deployed to Iraq, serving with the 285th Area Support Medical Company, and Senator Rob Portman of Ohio recorded on the Senate floor on June 16, 2022 that Robinson twice earned recognition as Ohio Army National Guard noncommissioned officer of the year, in 2012 and 2013. After the Iraq deployment, doctors diagnosed Robinson with an autoimmune disorder and with late stage lung cancer, conditions that a Senate Veterans Affairs Committee summary described as associated with burn pit exposure and that Robinson attributed to the smoke of the pits near which he had served. Robinson died in May 2020 at the age of 39, leaving a wife and a daughter, three years after the lung cancer diagnosis reported by the Akron Beacon Journal at the signing of the act. The Senate Veterans Affairs Committee attached the Robinson name to the comprehensive bill, and the long title enacted on August 10, 2022 read the Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022. In that title, Congress answered the exposure problem with a statement of attribution that individual claims had so often failed to sustain: a named soldier, a named exposure, and a statutory promise that proof would no longer rest entirely on records that the wars had never produced.

## Presumptions and the Equipoise Standard

A disability claim ordinarily turns on proof. A claimant presents medical records, service records, and opinion evidence, and an adjudicator decides whether a disability shown after service connects to an event, injury, or disease during service. That middle element, the connection, carries a technical name in veterans law. It is called a nexus, and it is often the hardest element in the claim. A nexus asks for a reasoned medical judgment about origin, sometimes about an exposure that occurred decades before symptoms appeared, at a dose nobody measured, in a place where no monitoring equipment stood. For toxic exposure claims, the nexus question can be unanswerable in the form the claims system asks it, because the evidence needed to answer it was never created at the time of service and cannot be reconstructed afterward with confidence.

A presumption exists to take that unanswerable question out of the individual case. A presumptive condition is a condition that Congress, or the Secretary of Veterans Affairs acting under authority Congress granted, has placed on a list tied to a defined category of service. Placement on the list represents a judgment made once, at the level of the category, that the association between that category of service and that condition is strong enough to justify connection without case by case proof of causation. When a claimant belongs to the covered category and has the listed condition, the law treats the condition as service connected. The adjudicator checks membership in the category and the presence of the condition, and the connection follows from the rule. A contested evidentiary question that would otherwise be fought claim by claim becomes a categorical rule that applies the same way to every claimant inside its boundaries.

The shift matters because the party bearing the burden of proof usually determines the outcome when evidence is thin. Under an ordinary nexus requirement, thin evidence means denial, because the claimant carries the responsibility of supporting the claim with evidence sufficient to establish the link. Under a presumption, thin individual evidence no longer decides the case, because the link has been established in advance by the legislative or rulemaking process that created the list. A presumption is a burden shift, and the substance of this statute is not money but proof. The payment rates and rating schedule in the Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, Public Law 117-168, signed on August 10, 2022, operated through structures that already existed. What the presumption provisions of that statute changed was the allocation of evidentiary risk between the claimant and the government. Where the science could not deliver certainty and the service record could not deliver measurements, the statute assigned the cost of that uncertainty to the government rather than to the individual who had served in the covered place during the covered period.

### What does a presumption change in a disability claim?

A presumption changes who must prove the medical link between service and illness. Once a condition is listed as presumptive, a claimant with qualifying service and a current diagnosis no longer has to produce evidence connecting the two. The government treats that connection as established, subject to the statutory limits and any affirmative contrary evidence.

Inside an actual claim, the change is concrete rather than abstract. Without a presumption, a claimant with a disease that emerged long after discharge often needed a medical opinion explaining why service caused or contributed to the disease, and that opinion had to rest on something more than the bare fact that the claimant served and later became ill. With a presumption, the medical opinion battle over origin disappears for the listed condition. The reasoning in [the disability compensation framework](/2019/05/15/va-disability-compensation-law/) runs through service, exposure, and diagnosis as defined elements, and the presumption supplies the connective finding that the claimant would otherwise have to litigate through evidence. The dispute, if one remains, moves to the boundaries of the category, to whether the service fits the statutory description and whether the diagnosed condition matches the listed one.

Two distinct presumptions often work together in toxic exposure law. The first is a presumption of exposure. For defined service in a defined place and period, the law presumes that the service member encountered the toxic substance associated with that place and period, unless affirmative evidence establishes that no exposure occurred. The second is a presumption of service connection. For a condition on the applicable list, the law presumes that the condition resulted from the presumed exposure, so the claimant does not have to prove medical causation either. The two presumptions stack. Qualifying service triggers presumed exposure, presumed exposure triggers presumed connection for listed conditions, and the chain from a personnel record to a compensable disability is complete without any witness testifying about a particular day or a particular spraying mission.

The categorical character of the device is both its strength and its acknowledged cost. A categorical rule pays no attention to differences in individual exposure intensity, and it accepts that some compensated conditions would have developed in the absence of service, because diseases such as hypertension and many cancers also arise from aging and heredity. Legislators choosing a presumption accept a measure of overinclusion as the price of avoiding the opposite error, the denial of meritorious claims because proof of individual causation cannot be assembled decades later. That trade was the stated logic of the framework the 2022 statute inherited, a framework built for herbicide exposure in Vietnam and then extended, in the 2022 enactment, to other exposures and other eras of service.

The inherited framework came from the Agent Orange Act of 1991, Public Law 102-4, enacted on February 6, 1991, and codified in relevant part at section 1116 of title 38, United States Code. The 1991 statute supplied both a list and a procedure. The list named conditions Congress itself treated as presumptively connected to herbicide exposure for veterans who served in the Republic of Vietnam, including chloracne, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and soft tissue sarcomas subject to stated exceptions. The procedure governed every later addition, and the procedure became the durable contribution of the 1991 statute, because it specified how new scientific evidence would be gathered, who would evaluate that evidence, what threshold the evidence had to meet, and what the Secretary had to do when the threshold was met.

The threshold was the equipoise standard, and its wording deserves precision because the entire mechanism turned on it. The 1991 statute directed the Secretary to prescribe regulations providing that a presumption of service connection is warranted whenever the Secretary determined, on the basis of sound medical and scientific evidence, that a positive association existed between exposure of humans to an herbicide agent and the occurrence of a disease in humans. The statute then defined a positive association in a way that broke with ordinary expectations about proof. A positive association existed when the credible evidence for the association was equal to or outweighed the credible evidence against the association. Equality was enough. A tie in the credible scientific evidence did not produce inaction or a demand for stronger studies. A tie produced a presumption, because the Secretary's duty to issue the regulation followed from the finding of a positive association rather than from a finding of proven causation.

That definition carried two consequences. The first concerned the kind of scientific conclusion the process required. The National Academies committees that evaluated herbicide evidence worked in categories of association, grading the evidence for each disease as sufficient to indicate an association, limited or suggestive of an association, inadequate or insufficient to determine whether an association existed, or limited or suggestive of no association. The second consequence concerned the direction of scientific doubt. Scientific equipoise, a balanced state of the credible evidence, resolved in the veteran's favor. That doubt moved to the government, which had to grant the presumption and pay the resulting claims unless the evidentiary balance changed under the statutory process.

The statute also assigned the scientific work to an institution outside the Department of Veterans Affairs. It directed the Secretary to seek an agreement with the National Academy of Sciences, under which the Academy would review the scientific and medical information on the health effects of herbicide exposure, including exposure to dioxin, a contaminant of the herbicide component known as 2,4,5-T. The Institute of Medicine of the National Academies convened the committees that performed the work, beginning with the comprehensive report titled Veterans and Agent Orange: Health Effects of Herbicides Used in Vietnam, published in 1994, and continuing through biennial updates that reviewed evidence published after the preceding report. The structure separated the scientific assessment from the benefits decision. A committee of scientists graded the association evidence, and the Secretary then applied the statutory equipoise standard to that assessment together with other sound medical and scientific information. If the determination favored a presumption, the Secretary issued regulations adding the disease. If the determination went the other way, the statute required publication of a notice explaining the scientific basis for declining.

The 2010 additions illustrated the mechanism working as designed, from review to determination to regulation. In October 2009, Secretary of Veterans Affairs Eric K. Shinseki determined, based on the Institute of Medicine's 2008 update on Agent Orange, that the evidence supported presumptions of service connection for three additional disease categories. A final regulation published in the Federal Register on August 31, 2010, then added Parkinson's disease and ischemic heart disease to the presumptive list and expanded the existing category of chronic lymphocytic leukemia to include all chronic B cell leukemias, including hairy cell leukemia. No statute passed in 2010 to accomplish that expansion. The Secretary acted through rulemaking, under the authority and the standard the 1991 statute had set, and the Department's announcement of the final regulation explained the effect in burden terms, stating that veterans with a presumed illness did not have to prove an association between the medical problem and military service.

The mechanism also carried an expiration date, and the history of that date explains why the later path to new presumptions ran through Congress. As enacted in 1991, the Secretary's authority and duties under the presumption provisions were set to expire ten years after the first day of the fiscal year in which the National Academy of Sciences transmitted its first report. The first report went to the Department in July 1993, during the fiscal year that began on October 1, 1992, so the original expiration fell on September 30, 2002. Section 201(d) of the Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001, Public Law 107-103, extended the authority codified at sections 1116(b) to 1116(d) of title 38 through September 30, 2015. No further extension followed. The authority stated in section 1116(e) of title 38 therefore expired on September 30, 2015, and the statutory presumption process built around the National Academies reviews and the Secretary's mandatory response lapsed with the start of October 1, 2015.

What exactly lapsed was itself a subject of named disagreement. Department officials and congressional committee staff maintained that the expiration did not remove the Secretary's underlying power to recognize presumptions, because separate provisions of title 38 gave the Secretary general rulemaking authority over benefits. Veterans advocates answered that the general authority was not the point. Bart Stichman, co-director of the National Veterans Legal Services Program, observed in 2017 that after the sunset the Secretary labored under no statutory deadline and was not required to do anything with a National Academies report. The power to act in the abstract survived, while the duty to act on a schedule, under the equipoise standard, with a published explanation for refusal, did not. A process that had compelled decisions became a discretion that permitted delay. Under the 1991 process, inertia favored a decision. After the sunset, inertia favored waiting, and waiting left the list unchanged.

The timing of the sunset sharpened the effect. The Institute of Medicine report issued in March 2016, five months after the authority expired, upgraded the classification of bladder cancer and hypothyroidism from inadequate or insufficient evidence of an association to limited or suggestive evidence of an association, and it retained Parkinsonism in the limited or suggestive category. Under the expired framework, classifications of that kind would have fed directly into the Secretary's equipoise determination and a mandatory regulatory response. Without the framework, the report produced no automatic process. Secretary David Shulkin stated in November 2017 that a decision on the proposed conditions had been made, yet no regulation adding those conditions followed at that time. Reporting by the Army Times on internal documents showed that advisers in the White House and the Office of Management and Budget questioned the decision in light of the expired statute and raised concerns about cost and about the sufficiency of the evidence. Veterans organizations answered that cost should not decide the question.

When the herbicide list finally changed, it changed by legislation, exactly as the structure of the sunset predicted. The William M. Thornberry National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2021, Public Law 116-283, became law on January 1, 2021, and section 9109 of that act amended section 1116 of title 38 directly, adding bladder cancer, hypothyroidism, and Parkinsonism to the statutory list of presumptive conditions for veterans exposed to herbicide agents. Congress performed, by amendment, the act that the Secretary had once performed by regulation under the 1991 authority. A condition supported by the scientific review process reached the list through a defense bill, after votes in both chambers and enactment into law, because the administrative road built in 1991 had closed in 2015. The 2022 statute followed the same legislative pattern at a far larger scale, naming presumptive conditions in the statutory text for defined exposure categories rather than relying on an administrative mechanism to generate them.

None of this made a presumption an automatic award, and the limits of the device deserve the same precision as its operation. A presumption relieves a claimant of proving the nexus and, where an exposure presumption applies, of proving exposure itself. A presumption does not relieve a claimant of proving the remaining elements of the claim, and a claim built on a presumption can fail on any of those elements. The claimant had to be a veteran with service that qualified under the statutory definition, and the service had to fall within the covered location and the covered period that the particular presumption specified. The claimant also had to show a diagnosed disability. A presumption attaches to a named condition, not to symptoms in the abstract, so medical evidence had to establish that the claimant had the listed disease, and for certain herbicide conditions the statute imposed manifestation requirements, including requirements that a disease become manifest to a degree of disability of ten percent or more, and for some conditions within a stated period after service. A diagnosis of a similar but unlisted condition did not satisfy the element.

The government's side of the burden also had a defined, if narrow, place inside the mechanism. Presumptions of service connection in title 38 operated subject to section 1113, which provided that where affirmative evidence established the contrary, including evidence that an intercurrent injury or disease recognized as a cause of the condition had intervened between separation from service and onset, or that the disability resulted from the veteran's own willful misconduct, service connection under the presumption provisions would not be in order. Mere doubt, a competing hypothesis, or the observation that a disease also occurred in unexposed populations did not amount to affirmative evidence against the individual claim. The presumption displaced speculation with a rule, and only affirmative evidence of the kind the statute described could displace the rule in return.

Service connection, once established through a presumption, also had to be separated from the amount of compensation. A presumption answered the question of entitlement to service connection. It did not assign a disability rating. The rating came afterward, through the schedule for rating disabilities, and it depended on the severity of the individual's condition as shown by examinations and medical records, expressed in percentage evaluations that could range from zero to one hundred percent. A service connected condition evaluated at zero percent established the connection while producing no monthly compensation amount by itself. The presumption governed proof of origin, and origin was only the first question the compensation system asked. Payment also depended on the effective date rules tied to the date of claim and to the date the presumption itself took legal effect, so a veteran who met every element years before filing did not automatically receive payment for those years.

The presumption mechanism, traced from the 1991 statute to the 2022 enactment, is therefore best understood as a technology for deciding under uncertainty. It took the honest scientific position, a graded assessment of association resting at equipoise for many diseases, and attached a legal consequence to that position in advance. Equipoise meant the presumption attached. The alternative, visible in the years after the 2015 sunset, was a system in which balanced evidence attached no consequence at all, reports accumulated without mandatory answers, and each addition required the full apparatus of legislation. The 2022 statute answered that alternative by writing presumptions into law for a new generation of exposure claims, restoring in statutory form the allocation of proof that the Agent Orange Act of 1991 had first codified. The money followed the proof. In this area of veterans law, it always had.

## Burn Pits and Airborne Hazards

A burn pit was an open area, often a shallow excavation, in which a deployed force destroyed waste by fire in the open air rather than by incineration in manufactured equipment. The practice accompanied American operations in the Southwest Asia theater during the Persian Gulf War of 1990 and 1991 and then expanded on a far larger scale during the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan that followed the attacks of September 11, 2001. At forward bases that functioned as self contained towns, the pits received nearly all of the refuse that base life generated. Accounts documented by the Department of Veterans Affairs and by congressional oversight bodies described plastics and foam packaging, metal cans, rubber and tires, paints and solvents, petroleum products, batteries, electronic equipment, wood, food waste, medical waste, and human waste entering the pits, with jet fuel used as an accelerant. The best known pit operated at Joint Base Balad in Iraq, a site of about ten acres that burned waste around the clock for years before the pit closed in 2009. Smoke drifted across runways, work areas, and living quarters, and service members at many locations described soot settling on bedding, clothing, and equipment.

The statute treated burn pits as the most visible source within a wider category of airborne hazards. Section 406 of the act, titled as the Presumptive Benefits for War Fighters Exposed to Burn Pits and Other Toxins Act, addressed exposure to substances, chemicals, and airborne hazards as a class. That framing encompassed fine particulate matter from desert dust and from combustion, fumes from burning waste and fuel, and the mixture of pollutants that hung over large bases where generators, vehicles, aircraft, and open fires operated together. A veteran filing a disability claim rarely possessed a personal record of the chemical composition of the air at a particular base on particular dates, and the Department of Defense had not maintained individual exposure measurements for most personnel in most locations. By legislating in terms of airborne hazards tied to places and periods of service, Congress built a rule that an adjudicator could apply from service records alone, without reconstructing the contents of a specific pit or the direction of the wind during a specific deployment.

Before August 2022, that kind of reconstruction was close to what the claims system demanded in most burn pit cases. Outside a small set of regulatory presumptions, a veteran had to establish a connection between a diagnosed disease and an exposure during service, usually through medical evidence and opinion offered years after the exposure had ended. The Department of Veterans Affairs had taken one regulatory step on August 2, 2021, when it began processing claims for asthma, rhinitis, and sinusitis on a presumptive basis for veterans exposed to particulate matter in the Southwest Asia theater beginning August 2, 1990, and in Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, Syria, or Djibouti beginning September 19, 2001. That action, which Secretary of Veterans Affairs Denis McDonough attributed to a departmental review of a National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report and other evidence, carried a significant limit, because the three conditions qualified only if they became manifest within ten years of a qualifying period of service. Diseases with long latency periods fell outside the rule. The scientific record behind broader action was contested for many years. A 2011 report of the Institute of Medicine, later renamed the National Academy of Medicine, found the available evidence inadequate or insufficient to determine associations for most health outcomes examined in relation to burn pit emissions, while Burn Pits 360, founded by Rosie Torres and Le Roy Torres, maintained that the pattern of respiratory disease and rare cancers among Iraq and Afghanistan veterans demonstrated a connection that the claims system refused to recognize. The act resolved that dispute in favor of presumption for a defined set of diseases, by statute rather than by further departmental rulemaking.

### Which conditions did the act make presumptive for burn pit exposure?

The act made twenty three diseases presumptive in the count used by Congress and the Department of Veterans Affairs in 2022, covering asthma diagnosed after service, chronic respiratory and upper airway illnesses, and cancers of the brain, head, neck, lungs and airways, gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, pancreas, and reproductive organs.

The enumeration in the statutory text rewards close reading, because the headline count and the name by name list differ slightly. Section 406 added a new section 1120 to title 38 of the United States Code, and subsection (b) named the diseases in sequence. Because glioblastoma is itself a form of brain cancer, and because the cancer categories included both lymphoma cancer of any type and lymphatic cancer of any type, a reader counting every label in the text arrives at a larger figure than the twenty three used in congressional and departmental summaries.

The respiratory and related diseases specified in section 1120 were asthma that was diagnosed after service, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, constrictive bronchiolitis or obliterative bronchiolitis, emphysema, granulomatous disease, interstitial lung disease, pleuritis, pulmonary fibrosis, and sarcoidosis. The cancers specified in the same subsection were brain cancer, gastrointestinal cancer of any type, glioblastoma, head cancer of any type, kidney cancer, lymphatic cancer of any type, lymphoma cancer of any type, melanoma, neck cancer of any type, pancreatic cancer, reproductive cancer of any type, and respiratory cancer of any type. The repeated phrase of any type carried substantial legal weight. A category such as respiratory cancer of any type or gastrointestinal cancer of any type embraced the full range of malignancies arising in the named system or region, so a claim did not fail because the specific histology of a tumor differed from the cell types most often discussed in burn pit litigation and advocacy.

Section 1120 also contained an open ended final category covering any other disease for which the Secretary of Veterans Affairs determined, through regulations issued under the decision making process that Title II of the act created in subchapter VII of chapter 11 of title 38, that a presumption was warranted based on a positive association with a substance, chemical, or airborne hazard on the list maintained under section 1119. The list in subsection (b) therefore operated as a floor rather than a ceiling, and the department could add further diseases as evidence developed without a new act of Congress for each addition. The presumption itself followed the classic form used elsewhere in veterans law. For the purposes of section 1110 of title 38, and subject to the rebuttal provisions of section 1113, a specified disease becoming manifest in a covered veteran was considered to have been incurred in or aggravated during active military, naval, air, or space service, notwithstanding the absence of any record of the disease during the period of service. Section 1120 imposed no requirement that a listed disease appear within a stated number of years after separation and no requirement that the disease reach a stated degree of disability before the presumption attached, a deliberate contrast with the ten year manifestation window that had limited the departmental rule of August 2021.

The disease presumption rested on a separate presumption of exposure, and the pairing of the two presumptions formed the core of the design. Section 1119 of title 38, as the act restated it, directed the Secretary to presume that a covered veteran was exposed to the substances, chemicals, and airborne hazards identified on a list that the Secretary was required to establish and maintain in collaboration with the Secretary of Defense, unless affirmative evidence established that the covered veteran was not exposed in connection with the service in question. A claimant therefore had to prove qualifying service and a qualifying diagnosis, while the two links that had defeated earlier claims, proof of personal exposure and proof that the exposure caused the disease, were supplied by law. Section 1119 defined an exposure tracking record system to include the Individual Longitudinal Exposure Record maintained by the Department of Defense, and the act required the Secretary to report to the veterans affairs committees of the Senate and the House of Representatives, beginning not later than two years after enactment and not less often than once every two years after that, on additions to and removals from the list of substances, chemicals, and airborne hazards.

The definition of covered veteran in section 1119 divided qualifying service into two eras, each with a separate list of locations. The first era reached service performed on or after August 2, 1990, the date of the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait, by a veteran assigned to a duty station in Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, or the United Arab Emirates, including the airspace above each of those places. The second era reached service performed on or after September 11, 2001, by a veteran assigned to a duty station in Afghanistan, Djibouti, Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, Yemen, or Uzbekistan, including the airspace above each of those places, and the definition added any other country determined relevant by the Secretary of Veterans Affairs. The two lists reflected the geography of two generations of deployment, the first centered on the Arabian Peninsula and the second dispersed from Central Asia to the Horn of Africa and the eastern Mediterranean. Service in a listed place during the stated period triggered the exposure presumption without any inquiry into the distance between a duty station and the nearest pit, the size of the installation, or the duties performed at the location.

Congress paired that breadth with a staggered schedule of applicability dates in section 406. The amendments applied on August 10, 2022, the date of enactment, for claimants for dependency and indemnity compensation and for veterans whom the Secretary determined to be terminally ill, homeless, under extreme financial hardship, more than eighty five years old, or capable of demonstrating other sufficient cause. For all other claimants, a first group of diseases applied on the date of enactment, covering most of the respiratory diseases in section 1120 together with respiratory cancer of any type, brain cancer, and glioblastoma. The statute then set October 1, 2023 for chronic bronchitis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, October 1, 2024 for head cancer of any type, neck cancer of any type, gastrointestinal cancer of any type, reproductive cancer of any type, lymphoma cancer of any type, lymphatic cancer of any type, and pancreatic cancer, and October 1, 2025 for kidney cancer and melanoma.

The burn pit presumptions did not displace the older Gulf War framework, and the act amended that framework in the adjacent section 405. The older framework rested on section 1117 of title 38, first enacted in the Persian Gulf War Veterans Benefits Act of 1994 as Title I of Public Law 103-446, and on section 1118, added by legislation in 1998, with the definition of qualifying chronic disability expanded by the Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001 in Public Law 107-103. Section 1117 authorized compensation for a Persian Gulf veteran with a qualifying chronic disability, a term that covered a chronic disability resulting from an undiagnosed illness, from a medically unexplained chronic multisymptom illness defined by a cluster of signs or symptoms, or from a diagnosed illness that the Secretary determined by regulation warranted a presumption. Federal law named chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, and functional gastrointestinal disorders, including irritable bowel syndrome, as examples of the medically unexplained category, and section 1117 listed signs and symptoms that could manifest such an illness, including fatigue, headache, muscle pain, joint pain, neurological and neuropsychological signs and symptoms, sleep disturbances, and gastrointestinal and cardiovascular signs and symptoms. The logic of that framework differed from the logic of section 1120 in a fundamental respect. Section 1117 compensated clusters of symptoms that resisted diagnosis, on the reasoning that the absence of a diagnosis should not defeat a claim arising from Southwest Asia service, while section 1120 compensated named diagnosed diseases, on the reasoning that the presence of a listed diagnosis should end the dispute over causation.

Section 405 strengthened the older framework at the same time that section 406 built the new one beside it. As amended, section 1117 provided for compensation where a qualifying chronic disability became manifest to any degree at any time, language that removed the structure under which a disability had to appear during service in the Southwest Asia theater or reach a degree of ten percent or more during a presumptive period fixed by regulation, and with that structure the calendar deadline that had bounded Gulf War claims through a succession of regulatory extensions. Section 405 also restated the definition of a Persian Gulf veteran in section 1117 to cover service on active duty in the Southwest Asia theater of operations, Afghanistan, Israel, Egypt, Turkey, Syria, or Jordan during the Persian Gulf War as federal law defined that period. The two frameworks overlapped in geography without merging in substance. A veteran with a listed cancer or a listed respiratory disease followed the section 1120 route, while a veteran with a chronic pattern of unexplained symptoms and no listed diagnosis followed the section 1117 route, and a veteran whose record supported both theories retained both.

Prevention of missed claims formed the purpose of the screening requirement that Title VI of the act imposed under section 603. A screening that identified a possible exposure gave the examining clinician a basis for evaluation and gave the veteran a documented occasion to learn about registry examinations, benefits counseling, and the filing of a claim, and veterans who were eligible for department health care but had not enrolled were to have an opportunity to enroll and receive the screening. Title VI placed that duty alongside the publication under section 602 of a list of department resources for toxic exposed veterans, with an outreach program for such veterans and for caregivers and survivors. The cadence, minimum content, and enrollment setting of the screening receive full treatment in the health care account below.

The research and registry provisions supplied the evidentiary machinery that the presumptive structure still required. Title V of the act began with section 501, which required an interagency Toxic Exposure Research Working Group through which the Department of Veterans Affairs was to collaborate with the Department of Defense and other federal entities to identify collaborative research activities on the health consequences of toxic exposure during military service and to develop a five year strategic plan for that research. Section 502 required the department to analyze clinical data gathered in the provision of hospital care, medical services, and nursing home care to toxic exposed veterans, where the data was likely to be scientifically useful in determining the association between a medical condition and a toxic exposure, and to report on the treatment of veterans for conditions related to toxic exposure. Section 503 required an updated analysis of total mortality and respiratory disease mortality among veterans who served in the locations and periods that defined the exposure presumptions, including service in Kuwait on or after August 2, 1990. Section 504 required an epidemiological study of the health trends of veterans who served after September 11, 2001, and section 505 required a study of the incidence of cancer among veterans. The remaining sections of Title V required a study of the health effects of Manhattan Project waste on certain veterans under section 506, a study of toxic exposure and mental health outcomes under section 507, a study of veterans in the territories of the United States under section 508, a public department website for toxic exposure research under section 509, and a report on the health effects of jet fuels under section 510.

The registry provisions worked from records and participation rather than from clinical data alone. The Airborne Hazards and Open Burn Pit Registry had been authorized by section 201 of the Dignified Burial and Other Veterans Benefits Improvement Act of 2012, Public Law 112-260, and the department launched the registry in 2014 as a voluntary record through which eligible veterans and service members reported deployments, exposures, and health concerns by questionnaire, with an optional medical evaluation available to participants who requested one. Participation in the registry was never a prerequisite to a disability claim, and the act preserved that separation while adding a transparency obligation. Section 808 of the act required the department to make public the number of participants in the registry displayed by state and by congressional district, giving the research titles a population level data source distinct from the clinical data stream that section 502 required the department to mine.

The result was a statutory scheme in which presumption answered the claims of the past and research was assigned the disputes of the future. For a veteran with service in a listed location during a listed period and a diagnosis of a listed disease, sections 1119 and 1120 removed exposure and causation as contested elements and left diagnosis and service as the questions for adjudication. For a veteran with a chronic disability that fit no listed diagnosis, section 1117 as amended by section 405 preserved the undiagnosed illness and chronic multisymptom illness route without a manifestation deadline. For conditions outside both structures, the screening duty in section 603, the registry and tracking systems recognized in sections 1119 and 808, and the studies commanded by Title V created the record on which the Secretary could act through the open ended category of section 1120. The act was named for Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson, an Ohio Army National Guard soldier who died in 2020 at the age of thirty nine from a rare lung cancer that his family attributed to burn pit exposure in Iraq, and the burn pit provisions formed the center of the statute that bore his name when President Joseph R. Biden signed Public Law 117-168 on August 10, 2022.

## Herbicide, Radiation, and Camp Lejeune Expansions

The Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, enacted as Public Law 117-168 and signed on August 10, 2022, treated exposure as a problem of definition as much as a problem of medicine. Title IV redrew the categories in title 38 of the United States Code that decided whose service counted as exposed service. Sections 403 and 404 addressed herbicide exposure. Sections 401 and 402 addressed radiation exposure from a Pacific cleanup and two Cold War nuclear weapons accidents. Section 804, in Title VIII, took a different route for Camp Lejeune in North Carolina, creating a federal court remedy instead of a benefits presumption.

A presumption replaced an individual proof step with a rule of law. Service connection ordinarily required a diagnosed disability, an in service exposure, and a link between the two, and the exposure step often turned on records that were fragmentary or never created. Once a veteran showed service matching the statutory description and a diagnosis matching the statutory disease list, the law supplied the exposure and, for listed diseases, the connection. A single base, a single province, or a closing date measured in days could therefore decide whether a claim began with the presumption or with an evidentiary contest under the narrower definitions that preceded August 10, 2022.

### Did the act help veterans exposed to herbicides outside Vietnam?

Yes. Section 403 extended the herbicide exposure presumption to service in Thailand, Laos, Cambodia at Mimot and Krek, Guam, American Samoa, and Johnston Atoll during defined periods between 1962 and 1980, so qualifying veterans no longer had to prove individual herbicide exposure on a case by case basis.

Section 403, titled in the act as the Veterans Agent Orange Exposure Equity Act of 2022, amended the definition of covered herbicide service in section 1116 of title 38. Before the amendment, the presumption centered on service in the Republic of Vietnam from January 9, 1962 to May 7, 1975, with separate provisions for service in or near the Korean demilitarized zone and for a small set of aircraft, transport, testing, and storage duties. Veterans who had served in the countries and territories that supported the war from outside Vietnam had to establish exposure as a fact in an individual claim. For Thailand, Department of Veterans Affairs guidance required credible evidence of duty at or near a base perimeter, with military police and perimeter security assignments treated as the clearest route to recognition.

Thailand received the broadest new location provision. The statute covered service in Thailand at any United States or Royal Thai base during the period beginning on January 9, 1962 and ending on June 30, 1976. The Congressional Research Service summary of Public Law 117-168 emphasized that coverage applied without regard to where on the base the veteran was located and without regard to what military job specialty the veteran performed. Herbicides had been used to clear vegetation around base perimeters, and personnel who lived and worked inside the perimeter had been required to document excursions to its edge. Presence at a qualifying base during the window became the exposure fact that the law required.

Laos received a shorter and earlier window, covering service during the period beginning on December 1, 1965 and ending on September 30, 1969. American personnel in Laos served in a war that the United States government conducted with limited public acknowledgment during those years, and that character had compounded the proof problem. Section 403 collapsed both contests into one dated category, and service in Laos during the specified period, paired with a listed disease, triggered the same presumption that service in Vietnam triggered.

Cambodia received the narrowest window in the herbicide expansion. The statute covered service in Cambodia at Mimot or Krek in Kampong Cham Province during the period beginning on April 16, 1969 and ending on April 30, 1969. Congress named two places inside one province and a span of fifteen days, rather than a country or a season. A veteran who served elsewhere in Cambodia, or at Mimot or Krek outside that span, received nothing from section 403 and remained dependent on individual proof. The drafting showed Congress legislating from a defined record of where herbicide connected operations had reached, and accepting the hard edges that a place and date definition created.

Guam and American Samoa extended the presumption into the Pacific logistics chain. The statute covered service in Guam or American Samoa, or in the territorial waters of either place, during the period beginning on January 9, 1962 and ending on July 31, 1980. The closing date fell more than five years after May 7, 1975, the closing date for Vietnam service, reflecting island functions as staging, storage, and transit points that did not end when combat operations ended. Section 403 replaced that case by case treatment with a rule, under which service inside the window satisfied the exposure element in the same manner as service in Thailand or Laos.

Johnston Atoll completed the Pacific geography. The statute covered service on Johnston Atoll, or on a ship that called at Johnston Atoll, during the period beginning on January 1, 1972 and ending on September 30, 1977. For naval personnel, exposure could turn on a port call at the remote atoll rather than an assignment ashore. Taken together, the five additions redrew the herbicide map from a single country presumption into an arc that ran from bases in Thailand through Laos and a two place enclave in Cambodia to American Samoa, Guam, and Johnston Atoll.

Congress staged the location expansion by claimant condition rather than by location. Section 403 provided that the amendments took effect on August 10, 2022 and applied on that date for claimants for dependency and indemnity compensation under chapter 13 of title 38 and for veterans whom the Secretary of Veterans Affairs determined were terminally ill, homeless, under extreme financial hardship, more than 85 years old, or capable of demonstrating other sufficient cause. For everyone else, the amendments applied on October 1, 2022. That two step structure was the template that section 404 then modified for the two new diseases.

Section 404, titled in the act as the Fair Care for Vietnam Veterans Act of 2022, changed what the presumption covered rather than where it applied. The section added monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance as subparagraph (L) and hypertension as subparagraph (M) to the disease list in section 1116(a)(2) of title 38. The additions reached every veteran with covered herbicide service, which after section 403 included the new locations as well as Vietnam itself.

The scientific record behind section 404 had been assembled four years before enactment. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine issued Veterans and Agent Orange: Update 11 (2018) in November 2018, and its committee found sufficient evidence of an association between exposure to at least one of the chemicals of interest in the herbicides and each of the two conditions. For hypertension, the report upgraded the classification from the limited or suggestive category used in the 2014 report cycle. For monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, a condition marked by an abnormal monoclonal protein in the blood, the report considered the condition for the first time, and the Veterans of Foreign Wars described the condition in November 2018 as a precursor to multiple myeloma, a cancer that already stood on the presumptive list.

The two conditions then waited on an administrative decision that never came. On November 15, 2018, the Veterans of Foreign Wars, through National Commander B. J. Lawrence, called on Secretary of Veterans Affairs Robert Wilkie to add both conditions to the presumptive list promptly. Secretary Wilkie declined to make that addition during his tenure and stated that the department would wait for the results of departmental studies of Vietnam veterans before deciding. Congress resolved the dispute legislatively in 2022 by writing both conditions into the statute itself.

Congress did not give the two new diseases the same starting date. For monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, section 404(d) applied the amendment on August 10, 2022 for dependency and indemnity compensation claimants and for the priority categories named in section 403, and on October 1, 2022 for everyone else. For hypertension, section 404(d) applied the amendment on August 10, 2022 for that same priority group, and on October 1, 2026 for everyone else, separating the two diagnoses by four years for the general claimant population. Section 404 also provided that retroactive claims for monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, notwithstanding federal court decisions or settlements in effect on the day before enactment, were to be awarded only to the dependency and indemnity compensation claimants described in the priority provision.

The radiation expansions used the same architecture in a different part of title 38. Section 1112(c) presumed service connection for specified diseases in a radiation exposed veteran, a status that depended on participation in a radiation risk activity as defined in section 1112(c)(3)(B). Sections 401 and 402 added three activities to that definition. The additions reached veterans whose exposure came not from weapons testing itself but from its aftermath, cleaning contaminated ground, recovering weapons and debris, and working where plutonium had been scattered by conventional explosions and fire. Yet the groups stood outside the definition, leaving each claimant to prove dose and causation individually decades after the events.

Section 401, titled in the act as the Mark Takai Atomic Veterans Healthcare Parity Act of 2022, added the cleanup of Enewetak Atoll during the period beginning on January 1, 1977 and ending on December 31, 1980. The Department of Veterans Affairs public health summary recorded that the United States conducted 43 nuclear tests at the Enewetak Proving Ground in the Marshall Islands from 1948 to 1958, and that approximately 6,000 veterans participated in the cleanup project, which ran from May 1977 through May 1980 before the atoll was returned to the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands. Residual soil contamination was placed in the Cactus Crater on Runit Island and covered by a concrete containment structure. Participation in that cleanup, rather than proof of a measured individual dose, became the qualifying fact.

The health risk of the cleanup had been described in different terms by the department and by the veterans who performed the work. Department of Veterans Affairs public health materials characterized the contamination that participants encountered as low level and the risk of health problems as low, noting protective clothing, dose measuring devices, radiation checks, and restricted access to contaminated islands. Cleanup veterans described dusty contaminated soil handled with limited protective equipment and pressed Congress for classification as atomic veterans. Section 401 settled the benefits question in statutory form, treating a cleanup participant who developed a disease specified in section 1112(c)(2) as a radiation exposed veteran for presumption purposes.

Section 402, titled in the act as the Palomares or Thule Veterans Act of 2022, used a more specific formula, requiring onsite participation in a defined response effort. For Palomares, Spain, the period began on January 17, 1966 and ended on March 31, 1967, following the collision of a United States Air Force B-52 bomber and a refueling plane that caused the release of four thermonuclear weapons. A Department of Defense narrative summary recorded that the B-52 collided with a KC-135 tanker during a midair refueling operation on January 17, 1966, that the high explosive material in two weapons exploded on impact and released radioactive material, and that approximately 1,400 tons of slightly contaminated soil and vegetation were removed to the United States.

For Thule Air Force Base, Greenland, the period began on January 21, 1968 and ended on September 25, 1968, following the onboard fire and crash of a United States Air Force B-52 bomber that caused the release of four thermonuclear weapons. Fire broke out aboard the bomber on January 21, 1968, and the aircraft crashed and burned on the ice southwest of the runway at Thule. The high explosives in the weapons detonated in the crash and fire, scattering plutonium across the ice and snow, and response personnel recovered weapon debris and removed contaminated ice, snow, and wreckage during the months that followed. Both accident groups had recovered and decontaminated the remains of American nuclear weapons on allied territory, in Spain in 1966 and in Greenland in 1968, while standing outside the radiation exposed veteran definition.

Camp Lejeune stood apart in both subject and remedy. The Camp Lejeune Justice Act of 2022 was enacted as section 804 of Public Law 117-168, in Title VIII, rather than as a freestanding statute, a placement stated in the public law itself. During the early 1980s, volatile organic compounds, including trichloroethylene, perchloroethylene, benzene, and vinyl chloride, were discovered in two on base water supply systems, and the contaminated wells were shut down in February 1985. Section 804 addressed the separate absence of a damages remedy in court for the wider population of Marines, family members, civilian workers, and others who had lived on or worked at the base.

Section 804 created a federal cause of action. An individual who resided, worked, or was otherwise exposed, including exposure in utero, for not less than 30 days to water at Camp Lejeune between August 1, 1953 and December 31, 1987 could bring an action in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of North Carolina for appropriate relief for harm caused by exposure to the water, provided the claim accrued before the date of enactment. The burden of proof rested on the party filing the action, who had to show one or more relationships between the water and the harm, by evidence sufficient to conclude that a causal relationship existed or sufficient to conclude that a causal relationship was at least as likely as not. The section thus preserved a causation showing in each case, rather than supplying the connection by presumption.

Procedure was part of the remedy. The district court for the Eastern District of North Carolina held exclusive jurisdiction and was the exclusive venue. An individual could not bring an action before complying with section 2675 of title 28, which required presentment of the claim to the appropriate federal agency and a final denial in writing, with an agency failure to dispose of the claim within six months treated at the claimant option as a final denial. An action could not be commenced after the later of the date that was two years after the date of enactment, August 10, 2022, or the date that was 180 days after the date on which the claim was denied under section 2675. Any applicable statute of repose or statute of limitations other than that rule was declared inapplicable, a sentence that addressed the decades between exposure, discovery in the early 1980s, and enactment in 2022.

The election and limits provisions defined what a claimant accepted by using the remedy. An individual, or the legal representative of an individual, who brought an action under section 804 for a harm, including a latent disease, could not thereafter bring a tort action against the United States for that harm under any other law, making the section the exclusive remedy for the harm sued upon. An award was to be offset by the amount of any disability award, payment, or benefit provided under a program administered by the Secretary of Veterans Affairs, under Medicare, or under Medicaid, in connection with health care or a disability relating to exposure to the water at Camp Lejeune. Punitive damages could not be awarded. The United States could not assert a claim to immunity that would otherwise have been available under section 2680(a) of title 28, and the section carried an exception for claims arising out of the combatant activities of the armed forces.

Read together, the expansions showed Congress working through exposure disputes by category rather than by individual adjudication. The herbicide amendments enlarged the map and the disease list, the radiation amendments enlarged the list of qualifying missions to include cleanup and accident response work performed between 1966 and 1980, and the Camp Lejeune provision built a court action with agency presentment first, a single designated district court, an equipoise causation standard, a filing window measured from enactment, and an election that closed off a second tort suit for the same harm. Each boundary, from fifteen days at Mimot and Krek in April 1969 to a four year delay for general hypertension claims ending on October 1, 2026, formed part of the compromise that the text enacted on August 10, 2022 recorded.

## Health Care Enrollment and the Department

Enrollment was the doorway through which the rest of the health care title had to pass. A veteran could fall within a category that Congress had made eligible for hospital care, medical services, or nursing home care from the Department of Veterans Affairs and yet receive none of that care without entering the patient enrollment system operated under section 1705 of title 38, United States Code. The Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, Public Law 117-168, signed on August 10, 2022, treated that doorway as a problem of timing as much as a problem of categories. For combat veterans, the statute widened the window in which enrollment on enhanced terms remained open, and for a narrower group whose window had closed years earlier, the statute opened the doorway again for a single year.
The rule that Congress rewrote in 2022 was older than the act itself. Under section 1710(e) of title 38, a veteran who had served on active duty in a theater of combat operations during a period of war after the Persian Gulf War, or in combat against a hostile force during a period of hostilities after November 11, 1998, held a period of enhanced eligibility for department health care measured from discharge or release. By 2022, that period stood at five years. A veteran who left service and gave no thought to department care during those five years could find that the enhanced route had closed, even though illnesses associated with combat service and toxic exposure often developed on a far slower timetable. The mismatch between a five year deadline and diseases with long latency periods sat at the center of the case for change developed through the Health Care for Burn Pit Veterans Act and other predecessor bills folded into the PACT Act during 2021 and 2022.

### How did the act change health care enrollment deadlines for combat veterans?

The act doubled the enhanced enrollment period for combat veterans from five years to ten years after discharge or release, effective October 1, 2022, and it created a separate one-year enrollment period beginning that same date for eligible combat veterans whose earlier period had already expired before that date.

Section 111 carried the change, under the heading of an expansion of the period of eligibility for health care for certain veterans of combat service, and section 111 took effect on October 1, 2022. The amendment struck the five year period in section 1710(e)(3)(A) and inserted a ten year period in its place, so that the enhanced eligibility of a combat veteran described in section 1710(e)(1)(D) ran for a decade after separation. The same amendment moved the starting boundary of the covered class from January 27, 2003 to September 11, 2001. Section 111 also clarified the combat theater category by naming awards that counted as evidence of such service, including the Armed Forces Expeditionary Medal, the Service Specific Expeditionary Medal, the Combat Era Specific Expeditionary Medal, the Campaign Specific Medal, and any other combat theater award established by federal statute or executive order.

The practical meaning of the longer period lay in the character of the eligibility itself. Enhanced eligibility placed a combat veteran inside the enrollment system on the strength of the qualifying service and within the statutory window, without first requiring an adjudicated finding that a particular illness was connected to that service. Care under the combat veteran authority addressed illness generally rather than a single diagnosed condition tied by evidence to a deployment. A ten year window therefore gave a veteran a full decade in which an undiagnosed respiratory problem, a slow emerging malignancy, or a condition that a civilian physician had never thought to connect with burn pit smoke could surface while the door into department care stood open on the most favorable terms the statute offered that class.

The second half of section 111 addressed veterans for whom even a ten year period, applied from the date of discharge, arrived too late. Congress wrote the special enrollment period as a defined statutory class rather than as a general amnesty for lapsed eligibility. The class covered a veteran described in section 1710(e)(1)(D) who had been discharged or released from the active military, naval, air, or space service after September 11, 2001 and before October 1, 2013, and who had not enrolled under that authority before October 1, 2022. For a veteran within that class, the statute substituted a one year period beginning on October 1, 2022. Department guidance described the same window as running from October 1, 2022 to October 1, 2023, and the Congressional Research Service, in a 2023 report, recorded the period as beginning on October 1, 2022 and ending on September 30, 2023, the final full day of that statutory year.

Congress paired the special window with machinery to make sure that the intended veterans learned of the deadline before the deadline passed. Section 111 required the Secretary of Veterans Affairs, not later than December 1, 2022, to submit to the Senate and House Committees on Veterans Affairs a plan to conduct outreach to the veterans in the special enrollment class, notifying those veterans of eligibility under the new provision. Section 111 also required the Secretary, not later than January 30, 2024, to report to the same committees the number of veterans in that class who had enrolled during the one year period. A reopened year of eligibility accomplished little for a veteran who had left service in 2005 or 2009 and had no regular contact with the department, and Congress therefore wrote notification and accounting into the same section.

Once a veteran enrolled, the enrollment had a durability that the deadline language could obscure. The Congressional Research Service summary of the act explained that a veteran who had enrolled remained enrolled, could seek care at any department facility, and did not need to reestablish eligibility for enrollment purposes. The deadlines in section 111 governed entry into the system, not a recurring obligation to prove the right to stay in the system. A combat veteran who entered during the ten year period or during the special one year window held an enrollment that outlasted the window itself.

The distinction between enrollment for health care and eligibility for disability compensation caused persistent confusion in public discussion of the act, and the statute kept the two systems legally separate even where the same service history supported both. Enrollment belonged to the health care system administered under chapter 17 of title 38, with eligibility centered in section 1710 and enrollment administered under section 1705, and enrollment determined access to care furnished by the department. Disability compensation belonged to a different part of title 38, centered in chapter 11, and rested on an adjudicated determination that a veteran held a disability resulting from disease or injury incurred or aggravated in the line of duty. Compensation produced a monthly monetary benefit graduated by rating, and the determination turned on service connection, a concept that the health care enrollment of a combat veteran within the statutory window did not require.

The separation ran in both directions. Enrollment in department health care, standing alone, granted no compensation, established no disability rating, and paid no monthly benefit, and treatment records generated during that care formed only one part of the evidentiary record that a compensation claim might later draw upon. A grant of service connection, by contrast, opened a basis for health care eligibility tied to the service connected condition and, under department guidance issued under the act, a veteran granted service connection for any health condition became eligible for department care, yet the compensation award and the act of enrolling for care remained distinct administrative steps. The Department of Veterans Affairs stated the point plainly in guidance on the special enrollment deadline, noting that many groups of veterans could enroll directly in department health care without first applying for department benefits. A combat veteran therefore faced two clocks that did not substitute for each other, and action on one clock preserved nothing on the other except the practical advantage of care received and records created.

Enrollment also carried a new clinical obligation into the examination room. Section 603 of the act, placed in title VI on improvement of resources and training regarding toxic exposed veterans, required the Secretary to incorporate a screening to help determine potential toxic exposures during active military, naval, air, or space service into the health care screening furnished to veterans enrolled in the patient enrollment system. From the health care side, the screening functioned as an intake and surveillance instrument attached to enrollment rather than as a benefit determination. A screening established no presumption, granted no claim, and rated no disability. The screening instead gave the department a structured record of reported exposure, gave clinicians a prompt to pursue follow up evaluation, and gave the veteran a documented starting point for conversations about care and about any separate claim.

The statute specified the cadence and minimum content of that screening with unusual precision. Section 603 directed the department to begin incorporating the screening not later than ninety days after August 10, 2022, a date that the Congressional Research Service recorded as November 8, 2022, and directed the Secretary to ensure that an enrolled veteran completed the screening not less frequently than once every five years. The questions were to be determined by the Secretary with input from medical professionals, and the screening had to include, at a minimum, a question about potential exposure to an open burn pit and a question regarding toxic exposures commonly associated with service in the Armed Forces. Section 603 further directed the Secretary to consider updates to the content of the screening not less frequently than biennially, and required complementary print materials at each department medical center for veterans without access to the internet.

Title VI surrounded the screening with provisions on the people that stood behind an enrolled veteran. Section 604 required the Secretary to provide education and training to department health care personnel on identifying, treating, and assessing the impact of illnesses related to toxic exposures, including instruction on how to ask veterans for additional information about different toxic exposures. The same section required a training program, provided not less frequently than annually, for processors of claims for disability benefits relating to service connected disabilities based on toxic exposures. Section 602 required the Secretary, not later than one year after enactment and annually thereafter, to publish a list of department resources for toxic exposed veterans and veterans who reported toxic exposure, and for the families, caregivers, and survivors of such veterans, and required the Secretary to develop, with input from the community, an informative outreach program for veterans on illnesses that might be related to toxic exposures, including outreach respecting benefits and support programs.

The remaining provisions turned from the veteran at the enrollment desk to the department that had to absorb, staff, house, and study the enlarged population of eligible veterans. Expanded eligibility enacted on paper would fail in operation if the department lacked clinicians where veterans lived, facilities for an enlarged enrolled population, information systems for the expected claims volume, and a research base capable of distinguishing association from coincidence in exposure science.

Section 104 supplied the planning discipline for the toxic exposed veteran expansion enacted in section 103. Not later than one hundred eighty days after August 10, 2022, the Secretary had to complete an assessment determining the personnel and material resources necessary to implement section 103 and the total number of covered veterans receiving hospital care or medical services from the department, disaggregated by the priority groups in section 1705(a), and had to report the findings, including a specific determination whether the department had the resources necessary for that implementation, to the Senate and House Committees on Veterans Affairs. Section 104 further required information systems to assess implementation, by October 1, 2024, and annual reports beginning by October 1, 2025 and continuing until October 1, 2033. Section 103 itself required an outreach plan not later than one hundred eighty days before each phase in date for a new cohort.

Title IX, devoted to improvement of the workforce of the Department of Veterans Affairs, carried the most direct response to the staffing problem. Section 901 required a national rural recruitment and hiring plan for the Veterans Health Administration, aimed at recruiting health care professionals for rural and highly rural clinics and medical centers, and section 902 authorized the department to buy out the service contract of certain health care professionals who accepted employment at a rural or highly rural facility for at least four years. Section 903 required qualifications and standardized performance metrics for human resources positions, stronger systems for monitoring human resources actions, and a plan for recruitment and retention of human resources employees. Sections 904 through 909 then adjusted pay and incentive tools, through modification of a pay cap, expanded opportunities for housekeeping aides, changes respecting hours, conditions of employment, and pay, waiver of a pay limitation, elimination of a limitation on awards and bonuses, and additional recruitment and retention authority.

Facilities received attention under title VII, the resourcing title. Section 702 authorized the Secretary to carry out thirty one major medical facility leases in fiscal year 2023, predominantly outpatient clinics, together with research facilities, a member services facility, a residential treatment facility, and a community living center, at estimated amounts stated lease by lease in the statute. Section 703 rewrote the approval framework for such leases, requiring that the Senate and House Committees on Veterans Affairs each adopt a resolution approving a lease before funds could be obligated or expended beyond advance planning and design, and section 704 authorized agreements with academic affiliates and other entities to acquire space for providing health care resources to veterans. Section 701 authorized use of amounts in the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund, established by section 805, to continue modernization of the information technology systems of the Veterans Benefits Administration, including claims automation, in support of the increased claims processing expected for newly eligible veterans, with a modernization plan due within one hundred eighty days after enactment.

Research coordination formed the final department facing strand, centered in title V. Section 501 directed the Secretary, not later than one year after enactment and in collaboration with the heads of the other participating entities, to establish a Toxic Exposure Research Working Group drawn from the Department of Veterans Affairs, the Department of Defense, the Department of Health and Human Services, the Environmental Protection Agency, and other federal entities involved in research on the health consequences of toxic exposures during service. The working group was charged with identifying collaborative research activities and resources and with developing a five year strategic plan, with reports to the two veterans affairs committees on establishment, on the activities and plan, and on progress. Companion provisions in title V required analysis of clinical data obtained in furnishing care to toxic exposed veterans, an updated analysis of mortality among veterans who had served in Southwest Asia, a study of health trends among veterans who had served after September 11, 2001, and a study of cancer rates among veterans, while section 509 required a publicly accessible department website serving as a clearinghouse for executive branch toxic exposure research.

Taken together, the enrollment changes and the department facing provisions formed a single legislative design viewed from two sides of the same counter. Section 111 lengthened the period in which a combat veteran could enter department health care on enhanced terms from five years to ten years and restored a year of opportunity to a defined class of veterans with separations after September 11, 2001 and before October 1, 2013. Section 603 made toxic exposure screening a recurring feature of the care that followed enrollment, at intervals of no more than five years. The workforce, leasing, information technology, assessment, and research provisions then attempted to equip the department for wider eligibility, through rural hiring plans and contract buyouts, pay and retention authorities, thirty one authorized leases, claims automation funding, and an interagency structure for exposure research.

## Passage: Two Votes, a Reversal, and a Signing

The passage of the Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022 took five months of floor action in 2022, two passage votes in each chamber, a constitutional correction that forced the bill to begin its final sequence a second time, and a failed Senate vote in late July that placed enactment in doubt six days before the Senate approved the measure by a margin larger than the margin recorded in June. The sequence began in the House, moved to the Senate for amendment and negotiation, returned to the House because a provision in the Senate text raised a question under the Origination Clause of the Constitution, and then returned to the Senate, where the decisive contest occurred not over passage by a simple majority but over whether debate would close at all. The full sequence explains why a bill that passed the Senate by 84 to 14 on June 16, 2022 required a second Senate passage vote on August 2, 2022 before presentation to the President.

Representative Mark Takano of California, chairman of the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs, introduced the Honoring our PACT Act as H.R. 3967 on June 17, 2021. The bill moved through the Committee on Veterans' Affairs, which reported it on February 22, 2022, and the Committee on Armed Services was discharged from further consideration on the same day. A supplemental report followed on March 1, 2022, and the House took up the bill under a rule reported as House Resolution 950. Floor consideration occupied parts of March 2 and March 3, 2022, with debate on a series of amendments offered under the rule, and the vote on the bill itself divided the chamber along narrower lines than the debate had suggested.

On March 3, 2022, the House passed H.R. 3967 by a vote of 256 to 174, recorded as Roll Number 57 in the House. The tally gave the bill a clear majority and sent the measure to the Senate, where the question became the shape of a Senate substitute rather than the survival of the House text in its original form. In the Senate, the Committee on Veterans' Affairs, under Chairman Jon Tester of Montana and Ranking Member Jerry Moran of Kansas, negotiated the version that came to the floor in June 2022. The negotiated measure carried the name of Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson of the Ohio Army National Guard, who had deployed to Kosovo and Iraq and who died in 2020, and the Senate text reorganized and expanded the House bill around health care eligibility, presumptions of service connection, and claims processing for veterans exposed to toxic substances. Because the Senate proceeded by amendment to the House bill, the June vote in the Senate was a vote on H.R. 3967 as amended, and House agreement to the Senate changes remained necessary before the bill could go to the President.

The Senate passed the amended bill on June 16, 2022, by a vote of 84 to 14, recorded as Record Vote Number 230. The margin crossed party lines in both directions and exceeded the sixty votes required to close debate in that chamber, which made the result read, in the days after June 16, as the effective decision of Congress on the substance of the legislation. Statements issued on the day of the vote treated House concurrence in the Senate amendment as the remaining step. The expectation of prompt enactment rested on a procedural assumption that proved wrong within days, because the Senate text contained a provision that House officials identified as a revenue measure, and the Constitution assigns the origin of revenue measures to the House.

Article I, Section 7, Clause 1 of the Constitution provides that all bills for raising revenue shall originate in the House of Representatives, while allowing the Senate to propose or concur with amendments as on other bills. House practice enforces that assignment through the procedure known as the blue slip, under which the House returns a Senate measure, or declines to proceed with it, when the measure contains a revenue provision originating in the Senate. The provision at issue in the PACT Act text concerned tax treatment connected to the recruitment of health care professionals for Department of Veterans Affairs facilities, described in accounts of the correction as a tax benefit intended to draw health professionals to rural facilities. The provision formed a small part of a large bill, and the health care and benefits provisions that made up the bulk of the measure raised no origination question. The remedy required the offending provision to be removed through action that began in the House.

The correction changed the vehicle as well as the text. Rather than proceeding further with H.R. 3967 in its Senate amended form, the House took up S. 3373, a Senate bill introduced on December 9, 2021 by Senator Tim Kaine of Virginia concerning education grants, which the Senate had passed by unanimous consent on February 17, 2022. The House amended S. 3373 by striking its original contents and inserting the text of the PACT Act with the revenue provision removed, so that the corrected measure reached the Senate as a House amendment to a Senate bill. That maneuver satisfied the origination requirement because the operative text, in its corrected form, had originated in the House, and it meant that the Senate faced the measure again in a new posture, as a motion to concur in a House amendment rather than as a bill on initial passage. An attempt to complete the correction quickly in late June 2022 did not produce final action, and the House took up the corrected measure as a floor matter in July.

On July 13, 2022, the House passed the corrected measure, agreeing to the PACT Act text as an amendment to S. 3373, by a vote of 342 to 88, recorded as Roll Number 309. Before the passage vote, the House rejected a motion to commit the bill by a vote of 208 to 219, recorded as Roll Number 308, and then proceeded to final passage on the same day. The July margin in the House exceeded the March margin by a wide amount, with 342 votes in favor against 256 in March, and the action completed everything the Origination Clause correction required from the House. The Senate received the message on House action on July 14, 2022, and the measure lay before the Senate by unanimous consent on July 21, 2022, when Majority Leader Charles E. Schumer of New York offered the motion to concur in the House amendment and filed the procedural motions that structured the debate. A cloture motion on the motion to concur was presented on July 25, 2022, setting the vote that would determine whether the Senate moved to a final decision before the August recess period on the congressional calendar for 2022.

### Why did the bill fail a Senate vote in July after passing in June?

The July vote failed because cloture required sixty votes and received fifty five, after twenty five Republican senators who had supported passage in June voted against ending debate, citing a budget classification provision that treated existing veterans spending as mandatory rather than discretionary spending subject to annual appropriations decisions.

The vote of July 27, 2022 was a vote on cloture, the motion to close debate, rather than a vote on the bill itself, and the distinction governed the arithmetic of the result. Under Senate Rule XXII, cloture required the votes of three fifths of the senators duly chosen and sworn, which in a Senate of one hundred members meant sixty votes. A majority voted to close debate, by 55 to 42, but a majority fell five votes short of the threshold the rule imposed, and Congress.gov recorded the result as cloture not invoked, Record Vote Number 272. Three senators did not vote. Because cloture failed, the motion to concur in the House amendment remained pending without a path to a final vote on that day, and a motion to reconsider the cloture vote was entered, preserving the ability of the majority leader to bring the question forward again. The bill had not been defeated on a passage vote at any point in the sequence; the July setback occurred at the procedural gate that controlled access to a second passage vote.

The composition of the July 27 vote drew the attention that followed. Twenty five Republican senators who had voted for passage of the bill on June 16, 2022 voted against cloture on July 27, 2022, a count stated in statements issued on July 28, 2022 by Senator Kirsten Gillibrand of New York and other supporters of the bill and reported in coverage of the vote by National Public Radio and other outlets on July 29, 2022. Senator Pat Toomey of Pennsylvania, whose stated objection formed the ground offered for the change in position, was not among the twenty five who switched, because Toomey had voted against the bill in June as well and had raised the classification question during the June debate. Two additional Republican senators who had not voted in June recorded votes against cloture in July. Forty one of the forty two votes against cloture came from Republican senators; the remaining vote against was that of Majority Leader Charles Schumer of New York, who switched to the prevailing side as the procedural step that allowed the motion to reconsider to be entered. Every other Democratic senator who voted supported cloture, and the 55 votes in favor combined Democratic votes with the Republican votes that remained in support of advancing the measure.

The stated ground for the July position concerned the classification of spending in the federal budget, a question that a separate treatment of the act's financing provisions examines in full. In statements on the Senate floor and in a statement published on July 27 and July 28, 2022, Senator Toomey objected to a provision of the bill that placed specified veterans health care spending, including spending authorized under prior law, in the mandatory category of federal spending, financed through the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund created by the bill, rather than in the discretionary category, in which Congress sets amounts through annual appropriations acts. Toomey stated that the provision would allow up to 400 billion dollars in spending over ten years to move from the discretionary category to the mandatory category, and he stated that the shift would free an equivalent amount of room under discretionary spending limits for spending on programs unrelated to veterans. Toomey described the provision as a budget gimmick, proposed an amendment that would have kept the classification of the preexisting spending as discretionary, and stated that the amendment would not reduce spending on veterans or limit the expansion of care in the bill. Republican Leader Mitch McConnell of Kentucky stated support for the substance of the bill while opposing the accounting treatment, in remarks reported during the same period.

Supporters of the bill answered the objection on the record during the same days. Senator Tester stated that the Toomey amendment would place limits on the amounts Congress could provide each year for veterans care through the fund, and Secretary of Veterans Affairs Denis McDonough stated in a television interview on July 31, 2022 that holding the spending in the discretionary category carried a risk of rationing care if annual appropriations fell short of need. A further fact framed the statements issued after July 27: the text of the bill that failed the cloture vote on July 27, 2022 was the text that had passed the House on July 13, 2022, its substance and its classification language were the same substance and language that the Senate had approved on June 16, 2022, and the only change made between the June Senate passage and the July House passage was the removal of the revenue provision that the Origination Clause correction had required. The classification provision named in the stated objection had been present in the bill in June, in the same form, when the Senate passed the measure by 84 to 14. Readers weighing the July reversal have that sequence of dated votes and that identity of text as the fixed points of the record.

The response outside the Senate chamber began on the day of the vote and took organized form within twenty four hours. Veterans service organizations that had campaigned for the bill across the 117th Congress, among them the Veterans of Foreign Wars, Disabled American Veterans, the American Legion, Iraq and Afghanistan Veterans of America, the Military Officers Association of America, the Wounded Warrior Project, and Burn Pits 360, issued statements after the July 27 vote calling on the Senate to complete passage without further delay. Family members who had appeared at events for the bill across 2021 and 2022, among them Rosie Torres, a cofounder of Burn Pits 360, and Susan Zeier, the mother in law of Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson, spoke to reporters at the Capitol on July 28, 2022. The comedian and advocate Jon Stewart, who had campaigned for the bill at hearings, press conferences, and media appearances during 2022, addressed a press conference outside the Capitol on July 28, 2022, calling on the Senate to hold a passage vote before the August recess. Stewart appeared at the Capitol again on August 1, 2022, at an event with veterans organizations, and his statements across those days repeated a single demand, that the Senate vote again on the text it had already passed in June.

From July 28, 2022 through August 2, 2022, veterans and family members maintained a continuous presence on the steps of the Capitol in Washington, remaining at the site through periods of heat and rain recorded in coverage of those days, in what participants described as a vigil held until the Senate acted. A core group of roughly forty veterans and supporters camped at the Capitol, according to the National Public Radio account of August 3, 2022, and larger groups gathered for press events at the site on July 28, July 29, and August 1, 2022. Members of Congress and Secretary McDonough visited the gathering during those days, and supporters delivered food and water to the participants. On July 29, 2022, Senator Gillibrand led Senate and House members, veterans organization representatives, Stewart, and former Secretary of Veterans Affairs David Shulkin at a Capitol event pressing for a new vote, and labor and veterans organizations directed letters to the twenty five senators who had changed position, urging passage of the bill itself. Participants stated, in interviews at the site, that the gathering would end when the Senate passed the bill.

Inside the Senate, the days after July 27 produced a negotiated path to a second vote. Majority Leader Schumer announced an agreement under which the Senate would vote on August 2, 2022 on three amendments offered by Republican senators, addressing the financing classification, the use of community care, and related questions, each requiring sixty votes for adoption, and would then vote on concurrence in the House amendment, likewise under an order requiring sixty votes in the affirmative. None of the three amendments, offered by Senator Toomey, Senator Marsha Blackburn of Tennessee, and Senator Rand Paul of Kentucky, achieved the sixty votes required on August 2, 2022; the Blackburn amendment, concerning provision of care under the Veterans Community Care Program, failed by 48 to 47, Record Vote Number 279. The failure of the amendments left the House text intact, and the Senate proceeded on August 2 to concurrence in the same text that had failed to obtain cloture on July 27, with no change in the funding mechanism and no change in any other provision between the two votes.

On the evening of August 2, 2022, the Senate agreed to the House amendment to S. 3373 by a vote of 86 to 11, recorded as Record Vote Number 280. The tally exceeded both the sixty vote threshold set by the order governing the vote and the 84 votes recorded for passage in June, and eleven senators voted in opposition, three fewer than the fourteen recorded in June. Veterans and family members who had maintained the gathering at the Capitol were present in Washington for the vote, and Majority Leader Schumer addressed the group at the Capitol after the result was announced, as did Rosie Torres, who spoke of the veterans who had campaigned for the measure across more than a decade of organized effort. The Senate transmitted its action to the House on August 3, 2022, and the completed measure was presented to the President on August 9, 2022. With the August 2 vote, the bill joined [the longer record of veterans legislation](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/) that stretches from the early republic through the Agent Orange legislation of 1991 and the post 2001 expansions of care, as a statute whose final passage required two complete rounds of bicameral action in a single summer.

President Biden signed the Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022 on August 10, 2022, at a ceremony in the East Room of the White House, and the measure became Public Law 117-168 on that date. Family members of veterans, leaders of veterans service organizations, members of the House and Senate who had managed the bill, and Jon Stewart attended the signing, and Rosie Torres and Le Roy Torres of Burn Pits 360 stood with the President at the ceremony, according to the account published by that organization. In remarks at the signing on August 10, 2022, President Biden connected the act to the exposure of service members to burn pits in Iraq and Afghanistan and to his son, Major Beau Biden of the Delaware Army National Guard, who died of brain cancer in 2015, a connection the President had also drawn in the State of the Union address of March 1, 2022. The signing completed a legislative sequence that had opened with introduction on June 17, 2021, and the enrolled text carried an effective date of August 10, 2022. From that date, the act directed the Department of Veterans Affairs to receive and decide claims under the new presumptions and eligibility rules through [the claims process that would absorb the new filings](/2019/06/15/va-claims-process-law/), the adjudication structure built under Title 38 into which every prior expansion of veterans benefits had also been routed.

## The Budget Classification Argument and What the Statute Did Not Do

The sharpest fight over the Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, signed on August 10, 2022, was not a fight over whether a veteran sickened by burn pit smoke or Agent Orange deserved care. The Senate approved the measure by a vote of 84 to 14 on June 16, 2022. The fight that nearly stopped the statute in late July 2022, days before final passage on August 2, 2022, concerned budget architecture. It asked which column of the federal ledger veterans health spending would occupy, and what other spending might become possible once the column changed.

### Was the objection about the cost of veterans benefits themselves?

No, in Senator Pat Toomey's own framing in July 2022. Toomey said he had no quarrel with the new veterans spending classified as mandatory, and his stated target was the reclassification mechanism and the discretionary room he argued it would create for unrelated programs in later appropriations bills.

That distinction framed everything Toomey said during the last week of July 2022 and everything the bill's managers said in reply. Toomey, a Republican senator from Pennsylvania who had voted against the measure in June 2022 as one of 14 senators in opposition, repeated that his amendment would not reduce or cap veterans spending and would not change the classification of the new spending created by the act itself. His amendment addressed only the treatment of spending that predated the act. The managers, led in the Senate by Jon Tester of Montana, chair of the Senate Committee on Veterans' Affairs, and Jerry Moran of Kansas, the committee's ranking Republican member, answered that the language Toomey targeted had been present through the earlier votes, that it served a deliberate purpose in guaranteeing care, and that his amendment would impose yearly limits with real consequences for veterans seeking treatment. Neither side conceded the other's premise, and the statute was enacted with the classification language intact.

Federal spending falls into two broad categories, and [the budget act that supplies the categories](/2020/01/01/congressional-budget-act-1974-guide/) is the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974. The first category, mandatory spending, also called direct spending, flows from authorizing law itself. When Congress enacts a statute obligating the government to pay benefits to persons who meet stated eligibility criteria, the money follows the eligibility, and no further vote is required each year to release it. Social Security and Medicare have long operated on that basis, as has disability compensation paid by the Department of Veterans Affairs. The amount spent in a given year depends chiefly on how many eligible people claim the benefit and on the rates the statute sets, rather than on a sum fixed in advance by an appropriations committee. Congress can change a mandatory program, but changing it requires a new statute.

The second category, discretionary spending, operates through the annual appropriations process. The President submits a budget request, the appropriations committees divide available funds, and Congress enacts bills that set spending levels for the coming fiscal year. A discretionary program receives only what an appropriations act provides, and its funding can rise, fall, or disappear as majorities change. Across the decades before 2022, most health care delivered by the Department of Veterans Affairs sat on the discretionary side of the line. Congress authorized the department to furnish care to eligible veterans, but clinics, staff, and treatment depended on money voted in annual appropriations acts. Disability compensation, by contrast, sat on the mandatory side. One department therefore administered two kinds of money governed by two logics, one driven by entitlement and one by yearly legislative choice.

That division shaped the politics of veterans health care long before the PACT Act. A discretionary structure gave appropriators yearly leverage over department operations and subjected veterans health funding to the same negotiations and delays that touched every other annually funded function. Advance appropriations, adopted for veterans medical care beginning with legislation enacted in 2009, softened that exposure by providing medical funding a year ahead, but the money remained discretionary. A mandatory structure offered the opposite trade. It promised that money for a defined class of care would not depend on each year's appropriations contest, at the cost of removing a measure of yearly control and committing the Treasury in advance to sums that would vary with demand. The debate of 2022 forced Congress to choose between those logics for a large new class of toxic exposure spending.

The mechanism the act used was a new fund in the Treasury. Section 805 established the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund, to be administered by the Secretary of Veterans Affairs. Section 806 appropriated 500 million dollars to the fund for fiscal year 2022, and the statute authorized appropriations for fiscal year 2023 and each fiscal year after that in such sums as might be necessary to cover the increase in costs, measured against the fiscal year 2021 level, arising from the act. The fund was directed to defined purposes, including delivery of veterans health care associated with exposure to environmental hazards during active military service, expenses incidental to that delivery, administrative costs including information technology and the processing of claims and appeals, and research related to environmental hazards. The decisive feature lay in the budget treatment. Appropriations to the fund were to be treated as direct spending, in the manner of an appropriated entitlement, so sums flowing through the fund counted on the mandatory side of the ledger.

The Congressional Budget Office made that treatment explicit in its cost estimate of June 6, 2022, for the version then before the Senate. The office explained that, at the direction of the Senate Committee on the Budget and in keeping with section 805, it had classified the expenses of the fund as direct spending, although under the law in force before the act those costs were generally classified as discretionary. That decision explains much of the size of the numbers that dominated the July 2022 argument. Readers who want the mechanics of such an estimate can consult [how the Congressional Budget Office scores legislation](/2020/05/15/how-cbo-scores-legislation/), because a score is a set of components rather than a single total. One component measured new obligations created by expanded eligibility and new presumptive conditions. Another measured old spending wearing a new label.

The June 6, 2022, estimate put total direct spending outlays under the bill at 667 billion dollars across the 2022 to 2031 period. Inside that total sat a line that attracted little attention in June 2022 and enormous attention in July 2022. The office estimated that current law discretionary spending reclassified as direct spending would account for 396.6 billion dollars in estimated authorization and 390.3 billion dollars in estimated outlays across the same period. A very large share of the mandatory total therefore did not represent new programs or new benefits. It represented health care and related activity already funded through annual appropriations, recast as direct spending once the increase in costs attributable to the act passed through the new fund. Set against that component, the new spending created by the act was most often cited during the July 2022 debate at 278.5 billion dollars over ten years, a figure drawn from the Congressional Budget Office estimate of June 2022 as reported in congressional debate and press coverage. Senator Toomey used that figure himself, saying after the failed procedural vote of July 27, 2022, that he had no quarrel with the bill creating 278.5 billion dollars in new spending during the next decade classified as mandatory.

Toomey's objection began where that concession ended. In a statement issued during the final debate, Toomey described the act as including a budget gimmick that would allow 400 billion dollars of spending under the law in force before the act to be moved from discretionary to mandatory spending. The rounded figure tracked the reclassification line in the Congressional Budget Office estimate, where the authorization total stood at 396.6 billion dollars. Toomey argued the provision was completely unnecessary to achieve the stated goal of expanding health care and other benefits for veterans, because the new benefits stood on their own mandatory footing and did not depend on reclassifying older spending. From that premise he drew the conclusion that gave his objection its force. Moving roughly 400 billion dollars of existing spending out of the discretionary category over ten years, he argued, would allow an additional 400 billion dollars in future discretionary spending completely unrelated to veterans across the next ten years.

The logic of that conclusion rested on how discretionary totals operate in practice. Discretionary levels in any year are negotiated against totals lawmakers treat as a ceiling, whether imposed by statute, by a budget resolution, or by political agreement. If a large block of veterans health spending no longer counted against those totals, Toomey argued, the same totals could be reached again by appropriating fresh money for entirely different purposes, and total federal spending could rise by the amount of the room created. Speaking on CNN on July 31, 2022, Toomey said the change created room in future budgets for 400 billion dollars of totally unrelated, extraneous spending on other matters, and preventing that result was his object. On the Senate floor on August 2, 2022, he described his amendment as maintaining the policy in force before the act of classifying authorized Department of Veterans Affairs health care spending as discretionary, and he said preventing the change would prevent a budget gimmick designed to grease the skids for up to 400 billion dollars in totally unrelated spending.

Stated at full strength, the Toomey argument did not depend on proving that any particular future appropriation would exploit the room created. It depended on the incentives the classification change would leave behind for a decade of future Congresses. A future appropriations committee would find its discretionary total easier to satisfy because a large stream of veterans health money had moved outside it. Mandatory money, in Toomey's telling, also escaped the discipline of yearly appropriations because it flows under permanent authority. His remedy matched the diagnosis. His amendment would have required amounts for the fund above the fiscal year 2021 baseline to be subject to yearly appropriations caps and would have sunset the fund's special treatment after a decade, leaving the new benefits untouched. The Senate rejected the amendment on August 2, 2022, by a vote that fell short of the 60 votes required for adoption.

The response from the bill's supporters operated on several levels, and the first was chronological. The classification language, supporters noted in late July 2022, had not appeared between the June and July votes. Military Times reported on July 31, 2022, that the discretionary spending language provisions were the same as in June 2022, when the measure passed the Senate easily, and that Toomey's concerns were the same concerns he had raised in June 2022 as one of the 14 senators who objected to final passage then. The bill that returned in July 2022 had undergone a narrow technical correction, after the House removed a tax related provision that had raised an origination problem under the Constitution, and the House passed the corrected measure on July 13, 2022, by a vote of 342 to 88. Tim Borland, national commander of the Veterans of Foreign Wars, observed that 25 senators who had voted for the act in June 2022 changed their vote on the same bill in July 2022.

The second level of the response addressed the purpose of the classification. Senator Tester argued on the Senate floor on August 2, 2022, that the Toomey amendment placed an arbitrary limit on the amount of funding Congress could spend each year in support of veterans, that such limits were called caps, and that caps resulted in the rationing of care, a reading Tester attributed to the Senate Committees on Appropriations and the Budget. In the supporters' account, mandatory treatment was not an accounting trick layered over the real bill. It was the guarantee that made the rest of the bill credible, because presumptive conditions would mean little to a veteran whose treatment depended on a yearly appropriation that might arrive late or fall short of demand. Supporters added that the spending remained veterans health spending either way. The fund could pay only for purposes the statute listed, and reclassification changed the ledger column rather than the destination of the dollars.

The third level of the response questioned the predicted spending spree on its own terms. Appropriations require affirmative enactment, so the room Toomey described could be filled only if a future House, a future Senate, and a future President each agreed to fill it, and supporters treated that chain of hypothetical majorities as a weak ground for delaying benefits that commanded majorities in both chambers in 2022. Senator Moran, who had negotiated the package with Tester and supported final passage, acknowledged in late July 2022 that a problem existed in the way the bill was funded, while stating a willingness to accept whatever solution was necessary to complete the legislation. Tester defended the classification as a protection for care, Moran treated the funding structure as a flaw to be managed, and Toomey treated the same structure as a defect serious enough to justify withholding final passage. The Senate passed the bill without the Toomey amendment on August 2, 2022, by a vote of 86 to 11.

Whatever weight a reader assigns to each side, the statute drew careful boundaries around what it accomplished, and an honest account has to state those boundaries as plainly as the expansions. The first boundary concerns the meaning of a presumption. Under the act, a veteran who served in a listed location during a listed period and who has a listed condition benefits from a legal presumption connecting the condition to the service, removing the need to prove an individual causal link between a particular exposure and a particular illness. A presumption is an evidentiary rule, not an award. A claim could fail because the service record did not place the veteran in a qualifying location during a qualifying period, because the condition claimed was not among the conditions covered, or because the evidence did not establish a disability that existed at the time the claim was decided. Where a claim succeeded, the disability was rated under the schedule of ratings, and the percentage assigned determined monthly compensation.

The second boundary concerns geography and time. The presumptions and expanded health care eligibility attached to service in specified places during specified periods, including defined locations associated with Agent Orange exposure, with airborne hazards and burn pits in the Gulf War and post September 11, 2001, eras, and with other exposures the statute named. Service outside the listed locations, or in a listed location outside the listed period, gained nothing from a presumption written for that location and period, however similar the duties may have been. A veteran in that position retained rights under other law, but the act supplied no shortcut. The same discipline applied to conditions. A disease became presumptive only if it appeared in the statutory list or entered the regulatory list through the review processes the act established.

The third boundary concerns the size of the population the act could reach, a subject on which estimates offered in 2022 varied by source and by definition. The White House fact sheet issued on August 10, 2022, the day of signing, stated that the act would help deliver more timely benefits and services to more than five million veterans across all generations who may have been affected by toxic exposures during service. A narrower official estimate circulated alongside it. The Department of Veterans Affairs, drawing on Department of Defense data, estimated that up to 3.5 million post September 11, 2001, veterans may have been exposed to burn pits, a figure Secretary of Veterans Affairs Denis McDonough used at the signing ceremony on August 10, 2022. The two numbers measured different sets, one counting potential reach across every era the statute touched and the other counting a single era's exposure population, and neither predicted how many individuals would file claims, qualify under the location and period rules, or receive awards after individual adjudication.

The final boundary is structural. The statute widened doors, but it built no mechanism that delivered health care or compensation to a named individual without an individual proceeding. Enrollment in department health care required an application and an eligibility determination. Disability compensation required a claim, a review of service records, medical evidence addressing the existence and severity of the claimed condition, and a decision that assigned or denied service connection and set a rating where service connection was granted. Survivors' benefits followed the same pattern through claims of their own. Even the screening provisions, which required the department to offer toxic exposure screenings to enrolled veterans, produced information for care and for claims rather than benefits in themselves. The PACT Act therefore stands, on its own text, as a statute that shifted burdens of proof, enlarged the categories of service and disease federal law recognized, and changed the budget classification of the money attached to those choices, while leaving the individual claim as the unit through which compensation and treatment had to pass.

## Assessment: A Statute About Proof

Read as a whole, the statute stands or falls on the burden shift at its center. Congress confronted a class of claims in which the decisive evidence, a measured record of individual exposure, had never been created, and in which medical science could grade associations across populations but could seldom certify causation in a single patient. The legislative answer was categorical. Place and period of service would stand in for exposure proof, a listed diagnosis would stand in for the medical link, and scientific equipoise, the balanced state of the credible evidence, would resolve in favor of the claimant rather than in favor of denial. That answer had a lineage. The Agent Orange Act of 1991 built the first version of the mechanism, the lapse of the presumption authority in 2015 showed what happened when the mechanism stopped, and the 2022 enactment restored the allocation of proof in statutory form at a far larger scale. The disease lists, the location lists, and the findings provisions of the enactment all serve that single allocation, and the expansions for herbicide service outside Vietnam and for radiation cleanup and response crews applied the same logic to older wars and older exposures.

The honest limits deserve equal weight. A categorical rule accepts overinclusion by design, because listed diseases also arise from aging, heredity, and civilian exposure, and some compensated conditions would have developed without any service connection at all. The boundaries of place, period, and diagnosis cut the other way, excluding veterans whose service fell outside the lines the text drew, however similar the duties or the exposures may have been. A presumption decided connection and nothing beyond connection. Rating remained a separate inquiry into severity, effective date rules governed payment timing, and a service connected condition rated at zero percent paid nothing by itself. The budget classification fight, settled by enactment rather than by persuasion, left the underlying question of ledger design to future Congresses, and the cost estimates of 2022 measured components of spending under stated assumptions rather than predicting the claims of any individual veteran.

The statute also drew a deliberate line between benefits law and court remedy. Camp Lejeune claimants received a federal cause of action in the Eastern District of North Carolina, with its own exposure duration, filing, and election rules, rather than a presumption inside the benefits system. Toxic exposure screening created a clinical record and a prompt for evaluation, but a screening granted no claim and established no presumption. Research mandates assigned the open scientific questions to studies and registries rather than pretending that enactment had answered those questions. Each device did one kind of work, and the discipline of the statute lay in keeping the devices distinct.

For a reader working through the enactment section by section, the productive order of inquiry follows the structure of a claim. Service comes first, because location and period decide which presumption, if any, applies at all. Diagnosis comes second, because a presumption attaches to a named condition rather than to symptoms in the abstract. Enrollment and screening come third, because health care eligibility and compensation eligibility run on separate tracks that the statute kept apart. A reader tracing those questions across a long statute can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) while matching each provision to the exposure category the provision governs.

The money in the act was large, contested, and real, and the cost estimates will occupy budget analysts long after the passage fight has faded from memory. The durable change, though, sits in the law of evidence. Before August 2022, a veteran with an unrecorded exposure and a listed disease in all but name carried the full weight of a proof problem that the record keeping of wartime had created. After enactment, inside the boundaries the text drew, the law carried that weight instead. That is the sense in which the statute kept its promise. The promise was never that every claim would succeed. The promise was that proof, rather than the absence of records, would decide the claim, and on that question the statute took a side.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the PACT Act cover?

The act covered toxic exposure across several eras of service through a combination of presumptions, health care eligibility, research, and one court remedy. Title IV supplied the benefits center, presuming exposure and service connection for listed diseases tied to airborne hazards and burn pits, extending herbicide presumptions to new locations, adding two herbicide diseases, and adding radiation risk activities at Enewetak, Palomares, and Thule. Title I expanded health care enrollment for combat veterans and for veterans exposed to toxic substances. Title VI required toxic exposure screening, Title V directed research, and section 804 created a federal cause of action for Camp Lejeune water contamination. Section 805 created the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund to carry the financing of the expansion.

### Q: What conditions are presumptive under the PACT Act?

For airborne hazards and burn pit service, section 406 of the act added section 1120 to title 38, naming twenty three diseases in the count used by Congress and the department, including asthma diagnosed after service, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, constrictive bronchiolitis, emphysema, granulomatous disease, interstitial lung disease, pleuritis, pulmonary fibrosis, and sarcoidosis, together with cancers of the brain, gastrointestinal tract, head, kidneys, lymphatic system, neck, pancreas, reproductive organs, and respiratory system, and glioblastoma and melanoma. For herbicide exposure, section 404 added hypertension and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance to the established list in section 1116. The Gulf War provisions separately covered qualifying chronic disabilities, including undiagnosed illnesses and medically unexplained chronic multisymptom illnesses.

### Q: Why did senators reverse their votes on the PACT Act?

The reversal concerned procedure and budget classification rather than the substance of the benefits. On June 16, 2022, the Senate passed the bill by a vote of 84 to 14. After a constitutional defect in a tax related provision forced the House to act again on a corrected vehicle, the Senate voted on July 27, 2022 on cloture, the motion to close debate, which required sixty votes and received fifty five. Twenty five Republican senators who had voted for passage in June voted against cloture in July. The stated ground, pressed by Senator Pat Toomey of Pennsylvania, was a provision treating existing veterans health care spending as mandatory spending through the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund. After six days of protest by veterans on the Capitol steps, the Senate passed the bill on August 2, 2022 by a vote of 86 to 11.

### Q: What was the mandatory spending objection to the PACT Act?

Senator Pat Toomey objected to the budget classification of spending that predated the act. Section 805 created the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund, and appropriations to the fund were treated as direct spending, so veterans health care costs that had been discretionary, funded through annual appropriations, were reclassified as mandatory once the increase in costs attributable to the act passed through the fund. Toomey argued that moving roughly 400 billion dollars of existing spending out of the discretionary category over ten years would free an equivalent amount of room under discretionary totals for spending unrelated to veterans. Toomey stated that the amendment he proposed would not reduce veterans spending and would not change the classification of the new spending the act itself created. The Senate rejected the amendment on August 2, 2022.

### Q: Does the PACT Act cover burn pits?

Yes. Burn pits stood at the center of the airborne hazards provisions. Section 1119 of title 38, as the act restated that section, presumed that a covered veteran was exposed to the substances, chemicals, and airborne hazards on the list the Secretary of Veterans Affairs was required to maintain, unless affirmative evidence established that no exposure occurred. Covered service included duty stations in Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, and the United Arab Emirates on or after August 2, 1990, and in Afghanistan, Djibouti, Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, Yemen, and Uzbekistan on or after September 11, 2001. Section 1120 then presumed service connection for the listed diseases for veterans inside that covered class, so neither personal exposure proof nor a medical causation opinion was required for a listed condition.

### Q: Did the PACT Act extend health care enrollment for combat veterans?

Yes. Section 111 of the act doubled the enhanced enrollment period for combat veterans from five years to ten years after discharge or release, effective October 1, 2022. The same section created a separate one year enrollment period beginning on October 1, 2022 for a defined class of combat veterans whose earlier period had already expired, covering veterans discharged or released after September 11, 2001 and before October 1, 2013 who had not enrolled under that authority before October 1, 2022. Section 111 also required the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to submit an outreach plan for that class and to report the number who enrolled. Enhanced eligibility placed a combat veteran inside the enrollment system on the strength of the combat service itself, without a service connected disability or a means test as a condition of entry.

### Q: How many veterans could the PACT Act reach?

Estimates offered in 2022 varied by source and by definition, and neither figure predicted how many individuals would file claims or receive awards. The White House fact sheet issued on August 10, 2022, the day of signing, stated that the act would help deliver more timely benefits and services to more than five million veterans across all generations who may have been affected by toxic exposures during service. A narrower estimate came from the Department of Veterans Affairs, drawing on Department of Defense data. The department estimated that up to 3.5 million post September 11 veterans may have been exposed to burn pits, a figure Secretary of Veterans Affairs Denis McDonough used at the signing ceremony on August 10, 2022. The first number counted potential reach across every era the statute touched, while the second counted a single era and a single exposure source.

### Q: Did the PACT Act add hypertension to the herbicide list?

Yes. Section 404 of the act, titled as the Fair Care for Vietnam Veterans Act of 2022, added hypertension as subparagraph (M) and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance as subparagraph (L) to the disease list in section 1116(a)(2) of title 38. The scientific record behind the additions came from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report Veterans and Agent Orange: Update 11 (2018), whose committee found sufficient evidence of an association between exposure to at least one of the chemicals of interest in the herbicides and each of the two conditions. Secretary of Veterans Affairs Robert Wilkie declined to add the conditions by regulation, and Congress resolved the dispute by writing both conditions into the statute itself. The additions reached every veteran with covered herbicide service, including the new locations added by section 403.

### Q: What is the full name of the PACT Act?

The full name is the Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act of 2022, enacted as Public Law 117-168 and signed on August 10, 2022. The name honored Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson, a native of Pickerington, Ohio, who enlisted in the Ohio Army National Guard in 2003, deployed to Kosovo in 2004 and later to Iraq with the 285th Area Support Medical Company, and twice earned recognition as Ohio Army National Guard noncommissioned officer of the year, in 2012 and 2013. After the Iraq deployment, doctors diagnosed Robinson with an autoimmune disorder and with late stage lung cancer, conditions that a Senate Veterans Affairs Committee summary described as associated with burn pit exposure. Robinson died in May 2020 at the age of 39, leaving a wife and a daughter.

### Q: When was the PACT Act signed into law?

President Joseph Biden signed the act on August 10, 2022, at a ceremony in the East Room of the White House, and the measure became Public Law 117-168 on that date. The signing closed a sequence that had opened with the introduction of the Honoring our PACT Act as House bill 3967 on June 17, 2021. The House passed that bill on March 3, 2022, the Senate passed an amended version on June 16, 2022, and a constitutional defect in a tax related provision then required the House to act again on a corrected vehicle, Senate bill 3373, which the House passed on July 13, 2022. A Senate cloture vote failed on July 27, 2022, and the Senate agreed to the House amendment on August 2, 2022 by a vote of 86 to 11. The completed measure was presented to the President on August 9, 2022.

### Q: What is the equipoise standard behind presumptive conditions?

The equipoise standard came from the Agent Orange Act of 1991, which directed the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to prescribe a presumption of service connection whenever the Secretary determined, on the basis of sound medical and scientific evidence, that a positive association existed between herbicide exposure and a disease. The statute defined a positive association as a state of the evidence in which the credible evidence for the association was equal to or outweighed the credible evidence against the association. Equality was enough. A tie in the credible scientific evidence produced a presumption rather than inaction, because the duty to issue the regulation followed from the finding of a positive association rather than from a finding of proven causation. Scientific assessment came from committees of the National Academies, which graded the evidence for each disease in categories of association, and the Secretary applied the statutory standard to that assessment.

### Q: Why did the Agent Orange presumption authority lapse in 2015?

The Agent Orange Act of 1991 built a mandatory process around periodic National Academies reviews, under which the Secretary of Veterans Affairs had a duty to respond to the scientific assessment, add diseases that met the equipoise standard by regulation, and publish the scientific basis for any refusal. That authority carried an expiration date. As enacted, the authority was set to expire ten years after the first day of the fiscal year in which the National Academy of Sciences transmitted its first report, a date that fell on September 30, 2002. Section 201(d) of the Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001 extended the authority through September 30, 2015, and no further extension followed, so the process expired on that date. Department officials and congressional staff maintained that general rulemaking authority survived the expiration, while veterans advocates answered that the duty to act on a schedule, under a stated standard, had ended.

### Q: What must a veteran still prove under a presumption?

A presumption removes the need to prove exposure and the medical link between exposure and disease, and a claimant must still prove the remaining elements of the claim. The service record must place the veteran inside the covered location and the covered period that the particular presumption specifies. Medical evidence must establish a diagnosed disability matching a listed condition, because a presumption attaches to a named disease rather than to symptoms in the abstract, and certain herbicide conditions carry manifestation requirements, including a degree of disability of ten percent or more within a stated period for some conditions. The government retained a narrow rebuttal under section 1113 of title 38 where affirmative evidence established the contrary, including an intercurrent cause or willful misconduct. Service connection also remained separate from the disability rating, which depended on severity under the rating schedule.

### Q: Which locations outside Vietnam gained herbicide presumptions?

Section 403 of the act, titled as the Veterans Agent Orange Exposure Equity Act of 2022, extended the herbicide exposure presumption to service in Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Guam, American Samoa, and Johnston Atoll during defined periods between 1962 and 1980. Thailand received the broadest provision, covering service at any United States or Royal Thai base during the statutory period. Laos received a window running from December 1, 1965 to September 30, 1969. Cambodia received the narrowest window, covering service at Mimot or Krek in Kampong Cham Province during the period the statute specified. Guam and American Samoa extended the presumption into the Pacific logistics chain, including territorial waters, and Johnston Atoll covered service on the atoll or on a ship that called at the atoll. Qualifying veterans at those locations no longer had to prove individual herbicide exposure case by case.

### Q: Did the PACT Act cover radiation exposure at Enewetak and Palomares?

Yes. Sections 401 and 402 used the radiation framework in section 1112 of title 38, which presumed service connection for specified diseases in a radiation exposed veteran, a status that depended on participation in a radiation risk activity. Section 401, the Mark Takai Atomic Veterans Healthcare Parity Act of 2022, added the cleanup of Enewetak Atoll during the period beginning on January 1, 1977 and ending on December 31, 1980, a project in which approximately 6,000 veterans participated after 43 nuclear tests at the Enewetak Proving Ground between 1948 and 1958. Section 402, the Palomares or Thule Veterans Act of 2022, added onsite participation in the response at Palomares, Spain, following the collision of a B-52 bomber and a refueling plane on January 17, 1966, and the response at Thule, Greenland, following the crash of a B-52 in January 1968. Participation, rather than a measured individual dose, became the qualifying fact.

### Q: Was the Camp Lejeune Justice Act part of the PACT Act?

Yes. The Camp Lejeune Justice Act of 2022 was enacted as section 804 of Public Law 117-168, in Title VIII, rather than as a freestanding statute. During the early 1980s, volatile organic compounds, including trichloroethylene, perchloroethylene, benzene, and vinyl chloride, were discovered in two on base water supply systems at Camp Lejeune in North Carolina, and the contaminated wells were shut down in February 1985. Section 804 created a federal cause of action for an individual who resided, worked, or was otherwise exposed, including exposure in utero, for not less than 30 days during the period the statute specified. The district court for the Eastern District of North Carolina held exclusive jurisdiction and was the exclusive venue, and election and limits provisions defined what a claimant accepted by using the remedy. The remedy stood apart from the benefits presumptions, addressing the absence of a damages remedy in court.

### Q: Does the PACT Act require toxic exposure screening?

Yes. Section 603 of the act, captioned as the incorporation of toxic exposure screening for veterans, required the Department of Veterans Affairs to incorporate a screening for possible toxic exposures during military, naval, air, or space service into the health care furnished to veterans enrolled in the department health care system. The statute directed the department to begin incorporating the screening not later than ninety days after August 10, 2022, a date the Congressional Research Service recorded as November 8, 2022, and to ensure that an enrolled veteran completed the screening not less frequently than once every five years. The screening had to include, at a minimum, a question about potential exposure to an open burn pit and a question regarding toxic exposures commonly associated with service in the Armed Forces, and the Secretary was required to consider updates to the content not less frequently than biennially. A screening established no presumption and granted no claim.

### Q: What did the Congressional Budget Office estimate the PACT Act would cost?

The Congressional Budget Office estimate of June 6, 2022, for the version then before the Senate, put total direct spending outlays under the bill at 667 billion dollars across the 2022 to 2031 period. A large component of that total was reclassification rather than new spending. The office estimated that current law discretionary spending reclassified as direct spending would account for 396.6 billion dollars in estimated authorization and 390.3 billion dollars in estimated outlays across the same period, because section 805 directed classification of the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund expenses as direct spending. Set against that component, the new mandatory spending created by the act was cited during the July 2022 debate at 278.5 billion dollars over ten years, a figure Senator Toomey himself used while stating that the new spending raised no objection from him.

### Q: What is the Toxic Exposures Fund created by the PACT Act?

Section 805 of the act established the Cost of War Toxic Exposures Fund in the Treasury, to be administered by the Secretary of Veterans Affairs. Section 806 appropriated 500 million dollars to the fund for fiscal year 2022, and the statute authorized appropriations for fiscal year 2023 and each fiscal year after that in such sums as might be necessary to cover the increase in costs, measured against the fiscal year 2021 level, arising from the act. The fund was directed to defined purposes, including delivery of veterans health care associated with exposure to environmental hazards during active military service, expenses incidental to that delivery, administrative costs including information technology and the processing of claims and appeals, and research related to environmental hazards. The decisive feature was budget treatment. Appropriations to the fund were treated as direct spending, in the manner of an appropriated entitlement, so sums flowing through the fund counted on the mandatory side of the ledger.

### Q: Does a presumptive condition guarantee benefits under the PACT Act?

No. A presumption is an evidentiary rule rather than an award. A claim could fail because the service record did not place the veteran in a qualifying location during a qualifying period, because the condition claimed was not among the conditions covered, or because the evidence did not establish a disability that existed at the time the claim was decided. Where a claim succeeded, service connection answered the question of entitlement, and the amount of compensation depended on the disability rating assigned under the schedule for rating disabilities, expressed in percentage evaluations from zero to one hundred percent. A condition evaluated at zero percent established the connection while producing no monthly compensation amount by itself. Payment also depended on effective date rules tied to the date of claim and to the date the presumption took legal effect, so entitlement established years after service did not automatically produce payment for those years.
