## The Question a Song Cue Asks

The most argued-about decision in Baz Luhrmann's 2013 film is not a plot change, a cut character, or a framing device. It is a sound. Understanding how Luhrmann's soundtrack reframes Gatsby means confronting the fact that a movie set in the summer of 1922 plays contemporary hip-hop and pop over its parties, its car rides, and its central romance, and that this was not an accident of production but the organizing interpretive choice of the whole picture. Every other liberty the film takes follows from it.

![How Luhrmann's soundtrack reframes The Great Gatsby through anachronistic music - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-73.webp)

That choice divides viewers more sharply than any other element of the film, and the division tends to be unproductive. One camp calls it a gimmick, a music-video reflex applied to a novel that deserved period fidelity. The other camp calls it bold and stops there, as though boldness were itself an argument. Both responses skip the interesting question, which is whether the anachronism does interpretive work on the book. It does. The claim this article defends is that the 2013 soundtrack is a translation rather than an error: it takes a quality of the novel that period-accurate music can no longer deliver to a modern listener, the quality of shock, and re-creates that shock by other means.

Call this the anachronism-as-argument reading. Its logic is simple and it is testable against the text. Fitzgerald wrote about music that was, in 1922, disreputable, commercially aggressive, racially charged, and thrillingly new. A modern audience hearing period recordings does not experience any of that. Period jazz now reads as elegant, quaint, and safe, the sound of a heritage cocktail bar. Playing it under a Gatsby party communicates the opposite of what the party communicates in the novel. Luhrmann's wager is that the way to preserve Fitzgerald's meaning is to break Fitzgerald's period, and the wager is defensible even where its execution is not.

## What Is Actually on the 2013 Album

Getting the facts right first matters, because a good deal of the argument about this album is conducted by people describing a version of it that does not exist. The soundtrack album released alongside the film was executive produced by Shawn Carter, who records as Jay-Z. Craig Armstrong composed the orchestral score that runs underneath and between the songs, and the two layers are distinct: the score is largely conventional film scoring in a late-Romantic idiom, and the song selections are what generate the controversy.

The song list mixes original recordings made for the film with covers and licensed tracks. Lana Del Rey contributed "Young and Beautiful," which functions as the recurring love theme and appears in multiple arrangements across the picture. Beyoncé and André 3000 recorded a version of "Back to Black," the Amy Winehouse song. Florence and the Machine contributed "Over the Love." Jack White covered "Love Is Blindness," originally by U2. Fergie, Q-Tip, and GoonRock produced "A Little Party Never Killed Nobody," which scores the largest of the party sequences. Jay-Z appears with "100$ Bill" and on "Izzo," and Emeli Sandé recorded a version of "Crazy in Love" with the Bryan Ferry Orchestra. Gotye, The xx, Sia, Nero, and Coco O. of Quadron also appear.

That Bryan Ferry credit deserves attention, because it complicates the simple story people tell about the album. The Bryan Ferry Orchestra performs in a jazz-age idiom, and its contributions include period-style arrangements of modern songs. The film is not, in other words, a wholesale rejection of 1920s music. It is a deliberate collision of two musical eras, sometimes sequential and sometimes simultaneous, in which a contemporary song can be delivered through a 1920s arrangement or a 1920s texture can bleed into a modern beat. The most interesting cues in the film are the hybrids, and they are the strongest evidence that the anachronism was designed rather than defaulted to.

### Who assembled the 2013 soundtrack?

The album was executive produced by Jay-Z, with an orchestral score by Craig Armstrong and additional period-style arrangements by the Bryan Ferry Orchestra. Luhrmann has described the collaboration as a search for music that would do to a modern audience what jazz did to audiences in 1922, which is the film's stated interpretive premise.

## How Luhrmann's Soundtrack Reframes Gatsby

The reframing operates on one principle, and the principle is a translation principle. A translator faced with a pun cannot render it word for word; rendering it word for word destroys it. The translator has to find a different pun that produces a comparable effect in the target language. Luhrmann treats period music as an untranslatable pun. The literal rendering, meaning actual 1922 recordings, preserves the surface and loses the effect. The free rendering, meaning contemporary popular music, sacrifices the surface to preserve the effect.

Whether you accept the translation depends on what you think the effect was. Fitzgerald's party chapter tells you. The orchestra at Gatsby's house is described as no thin five-piece affair, but a whole pitful of oboes and trombones and saxophones and viols and cornets and piccolos, and low and high drums. That is not a description of taste. It is a description of scale and excess, of an ensemble assembled to overwhelm rather than to charm, and the word pitful does the work of turning musicians into a resource poured out by the barrel. The halls are gaudy with primary colours. The sound and the sight are both calibrated to the same register, which is the register of too much.

Now consider what happens when a modern viewer hears a 1922 recording under that scene. The audio artifacts of early recording technology, the narrow frequency range, the surface noise, the mannered vocal delivery, all signal antiquity. The music says museum. The prose says assault. A period-accurate soundtrack is period-accurate and meaning-inaccurate, which is the trap Luhrmann is trying to escape.

Contemporary pop and hip-hop, whatever else they do, restore the register. A hip-hop production designed to sound enormous in a club sounds enormous in a cinema. It has bass a 1922 recording could not physically encode. It signals expense, aggression, and contemporaneity. It is the music of people who have money and want you to know it, which is precisely the social function jazz served in the milieu Fitzgerald was writing about. The anachronism restores the accuracy at the level that matters.

### Why is the 2013 soundtrack deliberately anachronistic?

Because period recordings now read as quaint rather than shocking. Luhrmann's premise is that jazz in 1922 was loud, new, commercially aggressive, and socially disreputable, and that modern hip-hop and pop occupy that position today. Using contemporary music preserves the novel's meaning by sacrificing its period surface.

## The Anachronism Table

The clearest way to test the translation claim is to lay the choices out against the interpretive argument each one makes. This table pairs the film's major musical strategies with the reading of the novel each implies, and with the textual pressure point each is responding to.

| Musical choice | Where it lands in the film | The interpretive argument it makes | Textual pressure point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contemporary hip-hop under the party sequences | Gatsby's West Egg parties | The parties were vulgar, loud, and new, not elegant heritage events | The pitful of instruments, the halls gaudy with primary colours |
| Lana Del Rey's "Young and Beautiful" as a recurring theme | Gatsby and Daisy scenes, reprised across the film | The romance is a fixed idea that repeats rather than develops | Gatsby's dream that must have seemed so close he could hardly fail to grasp it |
| A modern cover of "Back to Black" | Bleak, wealth-adjacent sequences | Self-destruction is the correct emotional key for this world | The moral wreckage the novel keeps in the background |
| Bryan Ferry Orchestra jazz-age arrangements of modern songs | Bridging cues, transitional moments | The two eras rhyme, and the film wants the seam visible | The novel's own sense of a present that is already becoming a period |
| A driving hip-hop cue over the drive into the city | The Valley of Ashes and the road to New York | The road between the eggs and the city is a corridor of aggressive money | Wealth passing through a place where labor is buried |
| A single voice and sparse accompaniment at the close | The final movement | The spectacle collapses into a solitary retrospective voice | Nick's closing meditation, the boats against the current |

The table is not a defense of every cue. It is a demonstration that the cues are legible as arguments. A gimmick, properly speaking, is a choice that produces novelty without producing meaning. Each row above produces meaning, and the meanings are consistent with one another, which is the strongest available evidence that the album was constructed rather than assembled.

## The Novel's Own Music, Read Closely

An honest test of the soundtrack requires knowing what Fitzgerald actually put on the page, and the answer is more interesting than the summary version people carry around. The novel is full of music, and almost none of it is treated with reverence.

The party sequence in chapter three establishes the pattern. The music arrives in Nick's account before the people do: there was music from my neighbour's house through the summer nights. It is ambient before it is attended to, a condition of the air rather than an event. Then the party proper arrives and the music becomes a machine. The orchestra plays yellow cocktail music, a phrase that has done more work in criticism of this novel than almost any other three words, because it fuses a color, a drink, and a sound into a single synaesthetic object. Yellow is the novel's color of money gone slightly wrong. Cocktail names the social function. Music is what is left over. Fitzgerald is not describing a melody. He is describing an atmosphere manufactured for a purpose.

Watch what the music does to time. The lights grow brighter as the earth lurches away from the sun, and the orchestra is playing yellow cocktail music, and the opera of voices pitches a key higher. Nick has stopped narrating events and started narrating a rising system. The verb lurches is violent and astronomical at once, and the human noise is described in an operatic register just as the actual orchestra is described in a cocktail register. The categories have swapped. High art describes the chatter and low commerce describes the band.

Then there is the centerpiece, and it is a joke. The orchestra leader announces Vladmir Tostoff's Jazz History of the World, presented as a work that attracted attention at Carnegie Hall. It is a fictional composition with a grandiose title and a nonexistent composer, and Nick tells us plainly that the nature of Mr. Tostoff's composition eluded me, because he was looking at Gatsby instead. The novel's most prominent named piece of music is unheard, unremembered, and probably ridiculous. Fitzgerald has built a monument to musical pretension and then declined to describe it.

### Does the novel treat jazz as beautiful?

No. Fitzgerald's music is functional and often faintly absurd. The band is a pitful of instruments, the signature piece is a fictional work with a grandiose title that Nick does not even listen to, and the party's music is described as yellow cocktail music, a phrase that makes it a commodity rather than an art.

The domestic music is treated with the same skepticism. When Klipspringer plays for Gatsby and Daisy in chapter five, the pieces are "The Love Nest" and "Ain't We Got Fun," two pieces of commercial sentiment, and the second of them is a song about being broke and cheerful, which lands badly in a mansion. Elsewhere a celebrated tenor had sung in Italian, and a notorious contralto had sung in jazz, a sentence that arranges high culture and low culture into a menu. The dancing is no better: old men pushing young girls backward in eternal graceless circles.

Put all of that together and a striking conclusion emerges. Fitzgerald's attitude toward the music of his own moment is closer to a modern critic's attitude toward stadium pop than to a jazz aficionado's reverence. He hears it as loud, purchased, socially useful, aesthetically dubious, and completely of the moment. That is not a description that a lovingly curated period-jazz soundtrack can honor, because a lovingly curated period-jazz soundtrack is by construction reverent. The reverence is the mistranslation. This is the reading developed at length in [the article on music and jazz culture in The Great Gatsby](/2027/05/10/jazz-music-culture-gatsby/), which traces the motif through every chapter where it appears.
## Where the Album Reads the Novel Correctly

Four specific things the 2013 album gets right about the book are worth naming, because they are the load-bearing part of the case.

The first is scale. Fitzgerald's parties are not tasteful. They are logistical operations. Crates of oranges and lemons arrive and leave as pulp, caterers install canvas, a bar is stocked with cordials so old that most of the guests are too young to identify them. The organizing sensation is surfeit. Contemporary production values, with their compressed loudness and physical bass, reproduce surfeit. A string band cannot. The film's party sequences are frequently criticized for being overwhelming, and that criticism is a compliment misfiled, because the source material is about being overwhelmed. Nick, at his first party, is a man drowning in other people's appetite. [The article on Gatsby's parties as symbol and spectacle](/2025/05/26/gatsby-parties-symbol/) works out how thoroughly the novel builds that drowning into its structure.

The second is repetition. Lana Del Rey's contribution recurs across the film rather than appearing once, and it recurs largely unchanged. This is a genuinely astute reading of the central relationship. Gatsby's love for Daisy is not a developing emotion. It is a fixed object he has been polishing for five years, and the novel is explicit that the object has outgrown its occasion. There must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams, Nick says, and adds that the failure was not her fault but the fault of the colossal vitality of his illusion. A leitmotif that returns identically is the correct musical shape for an emotion that refuses to update. A conventionally developing love theme would have contradicted the book.

The third is the moral key. The film's song selections skew toward damage: addiction, self-destruction, glamour with rot underneath. This is closer to Fitzgerald's tone than a straightforwardly celebratory period soundtrack would be. The novel is not nostalgic about 1922. It was published in 1925, three years after the summer it describes, and its retrospection is already sour. Nick's judgment on the Buchanans, that they were careless people who smashed things and retreated back into their money, is not the verdict of a man having a good time.

The fourth is the seam. Because the film sometimes lets the modern and the period arrangements collide inside a single cue, it makes the act of translation audible instead of hiding it. That is an unusual kind of honesty for a commercial adaptation. The viewer is not being asked to believe that people in 1922 listened to this. The viewer is being told, in the grammar of the sound design, that a substitution is taking place and that they should notice it.

## Where the Album Misreads the Novel

The case against is not that the anachronism is illegitimate. It is that the execution overshoots in a specific and identifiable direction, and the direction is toward pleasure.

The novel's parties are exhausting, faintly sad, and frequently unpleasant. Nick attends them as an outsider who was one of the few guests who had actually been invited, a line that quietly indicts everyone else in the garden. He watches without joining. Guests fight, weep, arrive uninvited, and leave without meeting their host. The parties are populated by people whose names Nick collects in chapter four as a kind of casualty list. Whatever else these evenings are, they are not fun in the uncomplicated sense.

The film's party sequences are fun in the uncomplicated sense. They are constructed with the visual and sonic grammar of a music video, and that grammar has an inbuilt teleology: it wants you to want to be there. The camera swoops, the cuts land on the beat, the fireworks time out to the chorus. The result is that the film's most memorable sequences invite the audience into the very thing the novel is dissecting. Read the film's parties against Nick's account and the mismatch is clear. Nick is estranged. The film's camera is thrilled.

This is not a problem the anachronism created. It is a problem the anachronism accelerated. Period music, whatever its other failures, would not have produced a sequence you want to dance to. The modern production values did, and the film did not build enough counterweight into them. There are moments where the sound design pulls back and lets emptiness in, but they are fewer than the argument requires.

A second and narrower misreading concerns the treatment of Gatsby himself. The film's musical vocabulary codes Gatsby as a figure of contemporary self-invention, an entrepreneur with a great playlist, and that coding softens something the novel keeps hard. Gatsby's money is criminal. Wolfshiem is not a colorful associate, he is a fixer with a documented career, and the novel is careful to attach Gatsby's fortune to bootlegging and to bond fraud in a way that a sympathetic aspirational score tends to blur. [The article on Jay Gatsby as a self-made man reconsidered](/2023/05/15/jay-gatsby-self-made-man/) lays out how much of the character's romance depends on the reader consenting not to look at the source of the cash.

### Does the soundtrack make Gatsby's world too appealing?

Partly, yes. The novel's parties are draining and Nick observes them as an estranged outsider, but the film's music-video grammar makes them exhilarating. The anachronism is defensible in principle; the execution tilts toward pleasure where the novel builds discomfort, which is the strongest fair criticism of the album.

## The Gimmick Charge, Taken Seriously

The word gimmick is doing a lot of unexamined work in the criticism of this film, and it deserves a definition before it is used or dismissed.

A gimmick is a device that draws attention to itself and, once you have noticed it, offers nothing further. It is a hook without a payload. The charge against the soundtrack, stated properly, is therefore this: the modern music grabs you, you register that the music is modern, and then nothing happens, so the whole apparatus is a marketing decision wearing an interpretation as a costume.

That charge has a real basis. The soundtrack was commercially engineered. It carried a major producer's name, it generated singles, and it extended the film's marketing surface into radio and streaming in a way a period compilation could not. Anyone claiming the album was purely an artistic decision is not being straight about how films are financed. Commercial motive was present and was substantial.

But commercial motive does not settle the interpretive question, because the two are not exclusive. The test for whether something is a gimmick is not whether money was involved. It is whether the device generates readings. Apply that test and the album passes. The recurrence of a single love theme produces a reading of Gatsby's fixation. The party production values produce a reading of surfeit. The period-style arrangements of modern songs produce a reading of historical rhyme. Every one of those readings can be checked against the text and defended or attacked on textual grounds, which is exactly what a gimmick cannot support.

There is also a historical point that the gimmick charge tends to miss. Fitzgerald's novel is itself full of commercial popular culture treated seriously as material. "Ain't We Got Fun" was a hit song, not an art object. "The Sheik of Araby" was a novelty tune. Fitzgerald put them in his novel because they were what people actually listened to, and their cheapness is part of the point. A film that used the commercial popular music of its own moment for the same reason is following the novel's method, not violating it.

### Is the 2013 soundtrack just a marketing gimmick?

The commercial motive was real, but a gimmick is a device that produces novelty without meaning, and this album produces meaning. The recurring love theme reads Gatsby's fixation, the party production reads surfeit, and the hybrid arrangements read historical rhyme. Those readings are testable against the text.

## What the Choice Reveals About the Novel

The most valuable thing an adaptation can do for a reader is not to be faithful. It is to reveal something about the source by failing or succeeding in a specific way, and the soundtrack does that unusually well.

It reveals, first, that the novel's music is thematic rather than atmospheric. Most readers file the party music under scene-setting. Once you have watched a film stake its entire interpretation on the music cues, you go back to the text and find that Fitzgerald did the same thing. The music tracks the moral temperature of every scene it appears in. It is loud when the illusion is being maintained and it disappears when the illusion breaks. There is no music in the Plaza Hotel confrontation that matters, and none at the funeral.

It reveals, second, that the novel is already about mediated experience. Gatsby builds a life out of borrowed material: a name, a manner, a set of props, a reading of what a gentleman is that he assembled from outside. A universe of ineffable gaudiness spun itself out in his brain, Fitzgerald writes of the young James Gatz, and the word gaudiness has the same charge as the halls gaudy with primary colours. Gatsby's inner life is constructed from the aesthetic surplus of his period, which is to say from popular culture. A film that scores him with the popular culture of a different period is doing something structurally analogous to what its subject does.

It reveals, third, how much of the novel's power sits in Nick's voice and therefore cannot be scored at all. The film's most acute problem is not the anachronism, it is that music can render spectacle and cannot render irony. Nick's narration is ironic almost continuously. He tells you he is inclined to reserve all judgements and then judges, he calls Gatsby a man who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn and then exempts him, and the gap between statement and behavior is where the book lives. No cue can carry that gap. Every adaptation runs into this wall, and the fuller treatment of why is in [the article on why The Great Gatsby resists adaptation](/2029/12/10/why-gatsby-resists-adaptation/).

## The Pattern Across the Adaptations

Musical strategy has divided the screen versions from the beginning, and the pattern is instructive.

The 1974 film, scored with period material and a restrained approach, is often praised for its accuracy and criticized for its inertia. Its music behaves. It sits under the scenes and confirms the year. The result is a picture in which the parties look like well-catered historical reenactments, and the frequent complaint about that film, that it is beautiful and lifeless, is at least partly a complaint about its sound. Period fidelity produced period distance.

The 2013 film reverses every one of those settings and produces the mirror-image failure mode: enormous energy, unreliable emotional calibration, a picture you feel before you think about. Between them, the two films describe the actual trade-off rather than solving it. Fidelity to the period costs you the period's felt intensity; fidelity to the intensity costs you the period.

That trade-off is the useful takeaway, and it generalizes past music. Every adaptive decision in a Gatsby film sits on the same axis. The full ranking of the screen versions against one another is worked out in [the survey of every Great Gatsby film adaptation](/2029/09/03/gatsby-film-adaptations-ranked/), and the 2013 picture is examined as a whole, beyond its sound, in [the dedicated analysis of the 2013 Baz Luhrmann Gatsby](/2029/09/10/gatsby-2013-luhrmann-film/). For the specific question of how Jay-Z's involvement connects the novel to a longer tradition of American self-invention narratives in popular music, [the comparison of Gatsby with Jay-Z's reading of Gatsby](/2028/07/24/gatsby-vs-jay-z-reading/) takes up the cultural link directly.
## What a Reader Gains From Taking the Album Seriously

Adaptations are usually treated by students as a source of trivia or as a shortcut around reading, and both uses waste them. The productive use is diagnostic. An adaptation is a hypothesis about what a book is for, executed at enormous expense by people who had to make every ambiguity concrete, and the places where the hypothesis strains are the places where the book is doing something a reader may have skimmed past.

The 2013 soundtrack strains at three points, and each one identifies a real feature of the text.

It strains at the question of pleasure. The film cannot make the parties unpleasant and still be a Baz Luhrmann film, so the parties are pleasurable, and the strain sends you back to chapter three to check what Fitzgerald actually did. What he did was build a party out of Nick's discomfort. Nick arrives, cannot find his host, drinks too much, is embarrassed by his own conspicuousness, attaches himself to Jordan out of social desperation, and leaves with the image of a woman weeping and a car wrecked in a ditch. The party sequence is a sequence of small humiliations. A reader who noticed only the glamour has been reading the film's version into the book.

It strains at the question of irony. Music tells an audience how to feel, and Nick's narration is built to prevent settled feeling. He announces that he is inclined to reserve all judgements and then delivers judgements for two hundred pages. He describes Gatsby as a man who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn and then declares him exempt. The prose holds admiration and contempt simultaneously and never resolves them. No cue can do that, so the film has to pick one, and watching it pick shows you how much of the novel's effect depends on refusing to pick.

It strains at the question of the ending. The last page is not a spectacle and cannot be scored as one, which is why the film goes quiet. That quiet reveals the shape of the book: everything before the last chapter is noise generated to hold off a conclusion, and the conclusion arrives when the noise runs out. Gatsby's household is dismissed, the parties end, and Nick reports that he could still hear the music and the laughter, faint and incessant, from his garden, which is a memory rather than an event. The music has already stopped. Only the echo is left.

### What can an adaptation teach a reader of the novel?

An adaptation is a hypothesis about what a book is for, executed at scale by people who had to make every ambiguity concrete. Where the hypothesis strains, the novel is usually doing something subtle. The 2013 film strains at pleasure, at irony, and at the ending, and each strain identifies a real feature of Fitzgerald's design.

## The Album Beyond the Film

One further fact belongs in an honest account: the soundtrack has outlived the discussion of the picture it was made for. The album's individual tracks circulate independently, attached to a general idea of glamour and doomed romance rather than to any specific scene, and a substantial number of people who know several of these songs well have neither seen the film nor read the book.

That circulation is itself a reception phenomenon worth reading. It represents a third-order adaptation: Fitzgerald's novel produced a film, the film produced an album, and the album produced a free-floating aesthetic that now attaches itself to prom photographs, party themes, and social media montages with no narrative attached at all. The novel's imagery has always travelled this way, and [the article on Gatsby-themed parties and the aesthetic](/2030/01/21/gatsby-themed-parties/) follows that migration in detail, but the soundtrack accelerated it considerably.

There is an irony here that Fitzgerald would have recognised. The novel is about a man who assembles an identity out of borrowed cultural material, gets the details slightly wrong, and is admired for the effect anyway. A universe of ineffable gaudiness spun itself out in his brain, Fitzgerald writes of the young James Gatz, describing an inner life built from the aesthetic surplus of a period. A soundtrack that detaches from its source and becomes free-floating glamour is doing to the film what Gatsby did to the idea of a gentleman. The album has become a Gatsby of itself: an assembled surface with an absent original, admired for a feeling it produces rather than for anything it depicts.
## Scene by Scene: Where the Music Has to Choose

The general argument becomes concrete at four moments, each of which forces the sound design into a decision that a reader of the novel can evaluate.

The first is the approach to the Valley of Ashes. In the book this is a descent, and Fitzgerald writes it as a landscape of ash that takes the forms of houses and chimneys and finally of ash-grey men who move dimly and are already crumbling through the powdery air. The passage is one of the few in the novel with no music in it at all. It is silent country between two loud places. A film has to decide whether to honor that silence or to score the transit, and the 2013 picture scores it, using a driving contemporary cue that turns the crossing into a montage of speed. The choice is coherent with the film's overall reading, which treats the road as an artery of money, but it costs the novel's most important structural silence. This is a real loss and it is fair to name it as one.

The second is the reunion in chapter five. Here the novel supplies its own soundtrack, and it is deliberately banal: Klipspringer at the piano playing "The Love Nest" and then "Ain't We Got Fun." The scene's power comes from the mismatch between the enormity of what is happening to Gatsby and the tinniness of the accompaniment. He has waited five years and the room is playing a light commercial number. Any adaptation that replaces this with a swelling romantic cue is deleting Fitzgerald's irony and substituting the emotion the irony was undercutting. The 2013 film handles this scene more carefully than its reputation suggests, but the temptation is visible in the material, and it is the single scene where a period-accurate approach has the strongest claim.

The third is the Plaza Hotel confrontation in chapter seven. This is the novel's structural climax and it is scored, in the book, by an intrusion: a wedding downstairs, the Mendelssohn wedding march coming up through the floor while a marriage is being destroyed in the room above. Fitzgerald wrote a musical irony directly into the scene. An adaptation does not need to invent anything here. It needs only to let the diegetic music do what the text already built. The lesson generalizes: the novel's best musical effects are ironic juxtapositions that are already on the page, and the strongest adaptive move is often restraint.

The fourth is the ending. Nick's closing meditation is the most quoted passage in American fiction and it is a passage of retrospective solitude. Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us, and then the final cadence about boats against the current. Whatever else a film does with its music, this is where the spectacle has to stop. The 2013 picture does pull back here, thinning its texture toward a single voice, and the retreat is the strongest structural argument that the loudness elsewhere was a choice rather than a default. A film that could not stop being loud would have proven the gimmick charge. This one stops.

### How does the film handle music at the ending?

The sound thins toward a single voice and sparse accompaniment for Nick's closing meditation, abandoning the party grammar entirely. That retreat matters interpretively: a film that could not turn the spectacle off would confirm the gimmick charge, and this one turns it off precisely where the novel does.

## The Silence Problem

Something the soundtrack argument tends to obscure is that the novel's most powerful musical decision is to stop. Fitzgerald controls where sound is present with unusual precision, and the absences carry weight.

Consider the shape. Chapters one and two have domestic and urban noise but no orchestras. Chapter three is saturated: the party, the band, the tenor, the contralto, Tostoff, dancing on the canvas. Chapter four moves through Wolfshiem's world and the guest list, and the music recedes to the level of memory. Chapter five brings music back but shrinks it to a single hired man at a piano. Chapters six and seven build toward the confrontation, and by the Plaza the only music is the wedding march bleeding through the floor. Chapters eight and nine, which contain the deaths and the funeral, are silent.

That trajectory is not accidental. Music in this novel is the sound of the illusion being maintained, and it thins as the illusion thins. When Gatsby's household staff are dismissed and the parties end, Nick notes that he could still hear the music and the laughter, faint and incessant, from his garden, which is a memory rather than an event. The last party has already happened. Everything after it is quiet.

A soundtrack can honor that architecture or ignore it, and this is the criterion by which a musical adaptation of this book should actually be judged. Not period accuracy. Not taste. Structural fidelity to where Fitzgerald puts the sound and where he takes it away. Judged on that criterion the 2013 film performs better than its critics allow and worse than its defenders claim: it respects the arc from saturation to silence, and it overfills the early chapters relative to the late ones, which flattens the decline.

## Writing About the Soundtrack in an Essay

A film's music is legitimate essay material only if it is used as evidence for a claim about the novel, and there are three moves that work and one that does not.

The move that does not work is the observation move. Noting that the film uses modern music, that this is anachronistic, and that some critics disliked it produces no argument. It is a fact about the film reported at the length of an argument, and it will read as padding to any examiner who has seen the film.

The first move that works is the translation move. State the interpretive problem, which is that period music now signals the opposite of what it signaled in 1922, then argue that Luhrmann's substitution preserves the effect at the cost of the surface, then test the claim against a passage. The pitful of instruments and the halls gaudy with primary colours are the passages to use, because they establish that Fitzgerald's register was excess rather than elegance.

The second move that works is the structural move. Trace where music appears and disappears across the nine chapters, argue that the distribution tracks the survival of Gatsby's illusion, and then ask whether the adaptation reproduces that distribution. This move is stronger than the first because it uses the film as a testing instrument for a claim about the novel rather than as a subject in its own right, and examiners reward the direction of travel.

The third move that works is the irony move. Argue that the novel's musical effects are almost always ironic juxtapositions, name three of them, which are the yellow cocktail music, the Klipspringer performance, and the wedding march at the Plaza, and then argue that scoring is structurally bad at irony because a cue tells you how to feel and irony requires you to hold two feelings at once. This is the most sophisticated of the three and it turns a question about a soundtrack into a question about the limits of the medium.

In all three cases the essay's center of gravity has to sit in the book. A paragraph about the film that does not end by saying something about Fitzgerald's text has spent its words on trivia. ## Verdict

The 2013 soundtrack is a serious interpretation of The Great Gatsby delivered in an unserious-looking package, and the package has cost it a fair hearing.

The core reasoning is sound. Fitzgerald's music is loud, purchased, commercially current, socially disreputable, and aesthetically dubious, and he treats it that way on the page. Period recordings cannot deliver those qualities to a modern audience because time has made them dignified. The substitution of contemporary popular music restores the qualities and sacrifices the period, and given that a soundtrack cannot preserve both, the trade is defensible on the novel's own terms.

The execution is uneven in one consistent direction. The film's party sequences are more pleasurable than the novel's parties, and that gap is the legitimate complaint. Nick attends these evenings with a growing sense of waste and the film attends them with delight. Where the picture recovers is at the structural level: it does let the music go at the end, which proves the loudness was a decision.

The best way to hold both judgments at once is to treat the album as a strong reading with a weak calibration. It knows what the novel's music is for. It does not always know how much of it the novel wants. A reader who takes that distinction seriously will get more out of both the film and the book than a reader who has already decided that anachronism is either bold or lazy, because the interesting question was never whether the music belonged to 1922. It was whether it belonged to the argument, and on the evidence, it does.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does the 2013 soundtrack reframe the novel?

It reframes the novel by treating Fitzgerald's music as a social signal rather than a period detail. In 1922 the music at a party like Gatsby's was new, loud, commercially aggressive, and socially suspect. Period recordings can no longer transmit any of that to a modern listener, because time has made them sound dignified and remote. By substituting contemporary hip-hop and pop, the film restores the register Fitzgerald actually wrote, which is excess rather than elegance. The novel describes the band as no thin five-piece affair, but a whole pitful of oboes and trombones and saxophones and viols and cornets and piccolos, and low and high drums, and describes the halls as gaudy with primary colours. Those are descriptions of overload. The soundtrack reframes the book by taking the overload seriously and by making the parties feel like an assault on the senses rather than a well-appointed historical reenactment.

### Q: Why is the soundtrack deliberately anachronistic?

Because a literal translation would betray the meaning. Luhrmann's premise is that jazz occupied a specific cultural position in 1922: it was the popular music of the moment, associated with Black American musicians and with venues that respectable people were not supposed to frequent, and it carried a charge of danger and novelty. Playing genuine 1922 recordings under a modern film cannot recover that charge, because those recordings now signal heritage. The anachronism is an attempt to preserve function at the expense of form. The film asks what music today occupies the position jazz occupied then, answers hip-hop and contemporary pop, and uses that instead. Whether the answer is correct is arguable, but the reasoning behind it is a translation problem rather than a lapse in research, and the film is transparent about making the substitution.

### Q: How was Jay-Z involved in the soundtrack?

Shawn Carter, who records as Jay-Z, served as executive producer of the soundtrack album released with the film. In that role he shaped the overall direction of the song selections and the roster of contributing artists, and he appears on the album as a performer as well. His involvement is the most-cited fact about the project and it is also the most-distorted, because it is often described as though he composed the film's score. He did not. Craig Armstrong wrote the orchestral score, and the Bryan Ferry Orchestra supplied jazz-age arrangements, so the album is a layered collaboration rather than a single artist's work. Carter's participation also connects the film to a longer strand of American popular music that treats Gatsby as a figure of self-invention, which is what makes the credit interpretively interesting rather than merely commercial.

### Q: How does modern music convey the 1920s sense of the new?

Through physical impact rather than historical reference. A 1922 recording has a narrow frequency range, audible surface noise, and a performance style that reads today as mannered. It cannot produce bass you feel in your chest, and it cannot produce the sensation of being overwhelmed by volume, because the technology of the period could not encode either. Contemporary production can. When Fitzgerald's guests are surrounded by a pitful of instruments and halls gaudy with primary colours, the intended sensation is saturation, and saturation is exactly what modern production delivers. The anachronism therefore functions as a technical fix as well as a cultural one: it supplies the physiological experience that period recordings are physically incapable of supplying, and that experience is closer to what a 1922 partygoer would have felt standing next to a live band at full volume.

### Q: Is the soundtrack a gimmick?

A gimmick produces novelty without producing meaning, and by that definition the album is not one. Each major musical strategy generates a reading that can be tested against the text. The recurring Lana Del Rey theme, returning largely unchanged across the film, argues that Gatsby's love is a fixed idea rather than a developing emotion, which matches Fitzgerald's line about the colossal vitality of his illusion. The party production values argue for surfeit, which matches the pitful of instruments. The hybrid period arrangements argue that the two eras rhyme. The commercial motive behind the album was real and substantial, but commercial motive and interpretive seriousness are not exclusive, and the test for a gimmick is whether the device supports argument, not whether money was involved.

### Q: How is the anachronism an interpretive bridge?

It works the way a translator's free rendering works. Faced with a pun that cannot survive a literal translation, a translator invents a different pun that produces a comparable effect in the target language. Luhrmann treats 1922 popular music as untranslatable in that sense. Rendering it word for word preserves the surface and destroys the effect, because the effect depended on the music being new and disreputable and it is now neither. Rendering it freely, with contemporary music that is new and disreputable, preserves the effect and destroys the surface. The bridge metaphor is precise: the anachronism carries a specific quality across a hundred years of listening habits, and it is a bridge rather than a demolition because the quality it carries, the shock of the current, is one Fitzgerald put on the page.

### Q: Which songs appear on the Luhrmann soundtrack album?

The album mixes original recordings, covers, and licensed tracks. Lana Del Rey's "Young and Beautiful" functions as the recurring love theme and appears in several arrangements. Beyoncé and André 3000 recorded a version of "Back to Black." Florence and the Machine contributed "Over the Love," Jack White covered "Love Is Blindness," and Fergie, Q-Tip, and GoonRock produced "A Little Party Never Killed Nobody," which scores the largest party sequence. Jay-Z appears as a performer as well as executive producer, and Emeli Sandé recorded "Crazy in Love" with the Bryan Ferry Orchestra. Gotye, The xx, Sia, Nero, and Coco O. also feature. The Bryan Ferry Orchestra credits matter because they show the film mixing period-style arrangements into the modern selections rather than rejecting the 1920s outright.

### Q: What does "Young and Beautiful" do for Daisy and Gatsby?

It supplies the relationship with a leitmotif that returns rather than develops, and that shape is a strong reading of the book. Gatsby's feeling for Daisy is not an evolving attachment; it is a fixed object he has maintained for five years, and Fitzgerald is explicit that the object has outgrown its occasion. There must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams, Nick observes, attributing the shortfall not to her but to the colossal vitality of his illusion. A love theme that develops across a film would contradict that. A love theme that recurs almost identically, in different arrangements but with the same melodic content, is the correct musical shape for an emotion that has stopped updating and is being polished instead.

### Q: Who composed the orchestral score for the 2013 film?

Craig Armstrong composed the orchestral score, which is a separate layer from the song selections and is written in a broadly late-Romantic film-scoring idiom. This distinction gets lost in most discussion of the film, where the whole musical apparatus is attributed to Jay-Z. The score handles the connective tissue: the transitions, the interior moments, the emotional underlining that a pop cue cannot supply. It is also, notably, the layer that carries the ending, where the texture thins toward a single voice and sparse accompaniment as Nick delivers the closing meditation. Understanding that the film has two musical systems rather than one clarifies most arguments about it, because criticism aimed at the anachronism is usually criticism of the song selections and not of Armstrong's work.

### Q: Does the soundtrack contain any actual jazz?

Yes, and this is the most commonly overlooked fact about it. The Bryan Ferry Orchestra performs in a jazz-age idiom and contributes period-style arrangements, including arrangements of contemporary songs delivered through a 1920s texture. The film therefore is not a wholesale replacement of period music with modern music. It is a designed collision, in which the two eras sometimes alternate and sometimes occupy the same cue. Those hybrid moments are the strongest evidence that the anachronism was planned rather than defaulted to, because a filmmaker who simply wanted a modern soundtrack would have had no reason to commission jazz-age arrangements at all. The seam between the two idioms is audible, and making it audible is a deliberate act of honesty about the substitution taking place.

### Q: How does the party music score the spectacle of West Egg?

By supplying scale rather than period colour. Fitzgerald's parties are logistical operations before they are social events: crates of oranges and lemons arrive every Friday and leave every Monday as a pyramid of pulpless halves, eight servants spend a day repairing the damage, and a machine in the kitchen extracts the juice of two hundred oranges in half an hour. The organizing sensation is industrial surplus. Contemporary production values, with compressed loudness and physical bass, reproduce that sensation in a way a string band cannot. The frequent complaint that the film's party sequences are overwhelming is therefore a compliment filed in the wrong place, since being overwhelmed is precisely Nick's experience. The legitimate criticism is not the volume but the pleasure attached to it.

### Q: What did reviewers say about the soundtrack?

Reaction split along a predictable line and stayed there. A substantial body of criticism found the modern music distracting, treated it as evidence of a director prioritizing sensation over the source, and used it as shorthand for the film's broader excesses. A comparably substantial body found it the most defensible thing about the picture, on the grounds that it solved a real problem rather than avoiding it. The album itself performed strongly commercially and generated singles that outlived the discussion of the film. What is worth noticing is that both camps largely argued about whether the anachronism was appropriate rather than about what it meant, which is why the more interesting question, whether the substitution honors the novel's own attitude to music, went mostly unexamined.

### Q: How does the music treat the Valley of Ashes scenes?

It scores them, and that is a defensible choice with a real cost. In the novel the valley is Fitzgerald's one genuinely silent landscape, a place where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and finally of ash-grey men who move dimly and are already crumbling through the powdery air. There is no music in it. It sits between two loud places and the quiet is structural. The film uses a driving contemporary cue for the crossing, turning the transit into a montage of speed and money, which is coherent with its overall reading of the road as an artery of wealth. But it fills a silence Fitzgerald protected, and since the novel's musical architecture depends as much on absence as on presence, this is the clearest instance of the soundtrack overreaching.

### Q: Would a period-accurate soundtrack have worked better?

Not obviously, and the 1974 film is the evidence. That picture used period material and a restrained musical approach, and the most persistent complaint about it is that it is beautiful and inert. Its parties look like well-catered historical reenactments because the music behaves: it sits under the scenes and confirms the year without doing anything else. Period fidelity produced period distance. The 2013 film reverses every setting and produces the mirror-image failure, which is enormous energy with unreliable emotional calibration. Between them the two versions map the actual trade-off rather than solving it. Fidelity to the period costs the period's felt intensity; fidelity to the intensity costs the period. No screen version has yet found a musical strategy that pays neither price.

### Q: How does the soundtrack handle the ending?

It stops. The texture thins toward a single voice and sparse accompaniment as the film moves into Nick's closing meditation on the green light and the orgastic future, and the party grammar is abandoned entirely. This retreat is more important than it looks, because it is the strongest structural argument against the gimmick charge. A film whose loudness was a reflex rather than a decision would not have been able to turn the loudness off, and would have scored the ending with the same energy it gave the parties. This one turns it off at precisely the point where the novel does, since Fitzgerald's final two chapters contain the deaths and the funeral and are essentially silent. The film's musical arc, saturation to silence, tracks the novel's.

### Q: Can I write an essay about the film's music?

Yes, provided the essay's centre of gravity stays in the book. The weak version reports that the film uses modern music, notes that this is anachronistic, and summarises the disagreement, which produces no argument. The strong versions do one of three things. The translation argument states the interpretive problem, defends the substitution as preserving effect at the cost of surface, and tests the claim against the pitful of instruments and the halls gaudy with primary colours. The structural argument traces where music appears and vanishes across the nine chapters, shows the distribution tracking the survival of Gatsby's illusion, and asks whether the film reproduces it. The irony argument holds that Fitzgerald's musical effects are ironic juxtapositions and that scoring is structurally bad at irony, since a cue tells you how to feel.

### Q: Does the soundtrack change how Gatsby's wealth reads?

It softens it, and this is a fair criticism. The film's musical vocabulary codes Gatsby as a figure of contemporary self-invention, an entrepreneur with taste and a great playlist, and the aspirational energy of the cues works against something the novel keeps hard. Gatsby's money is criminal. His fortune runs through Wolfshiem and through bootlegging and bond fraud, and the novel is careful to attach the glamour to the crime rather than to separate them. A score that reads him as an inspirational self-made man performs the same act of consent the reader is repeatedly tempted into, which is agreeing not to look at the source of the cash. The film is not alone in this, but the music makes the softening harder to notice than dialogue would.

### Q: What does the soundtrack get wrong about the book?

Its calibration rather than its concept. The novel's parties are draining, faintly sad, and populated by people Nick catalogues almost as a casualty list; he attends as an estranged observer who was one of the few guests who had actually been invited, a line that quietly indicts the rest of the garden. The film's sequences are exhilarating. The music-video grammar has an inbuilt teleology, which is to make you want to be there, and the novel is engineered to make you want to leave. That gap is the strongest fair complaint. It is not a problem the anachronism created, since the concept is sound, but it is one the anachronism accelerated, because contemporary production values are extremely good at generating pleasure and the film did not build enough counterweight into them.

### Q: Why did Luhrmann pick hip-hop rather than swing?

Because he was matching cultural position rather than sound. Swing is what most people picture when they imagine 1920s music, though it belongs more properly to the following decade, and either way it now signals nostalgia. Luhrmann's question was not what the parties sounded like but what they meant socially: music that was current, commercially dominant, associated with performers outside the white establishment, and regarded by respectable opinion as evidence of decline. Hip-hop occupies a comparable position in the culture the film was made for. The parallel is imperfect, as all such parallels are, but it is a considered parallel rather than a stylistic whim, and it explains why period-style arrangements still appear on the album: the film wanted the rhyme between the eras audible, not the older era erased.
