Famous Romeos are the twentieth century's most instructive failures. The century that rebuilt Shakespearean acting from the ground up, that produced Gielgud and Olivier, Scofield and McKellen, never produced a Romeo the record could call complete. It produced instead a gallery of partial triumphs, each one famous for the half of the part it mastered and quietly forgiven for the half it missed. The lover of Verona is written as two men sharing one name: a lyric poet who invents a private language with Juliet in the orchard, and a street fighter who kills her cousin in the public square an act later. Actors with the voice for the first man kept failing the second; actors with the blood for the second kept slighting the first. The gallery assembled in this article, the InsightCrunch Romeo gallery, sets the century's celebrated performances side by side so that the pattern can be read at a glance, and the verdict argued through the whole survey is simple. Romeo is the role the modern stage never solved, and the reason is structural: the part grades its interpreters on two incompatible examinations, and every famous Romeo of the century passed one and negotiated the other.

![Twentieth-century stage Romeos from Gielgud and Olivier to the century's last Stratford lovers - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-90.webp)

The cliche imagines one Romeo, a generic young lover in tights, sighing under a balcony. The record restores a century of reinventions instead: a Victorian tragedian of forty-four humiliated by the part in 1882, a twenty-year-old lyric prodigy jeered for his legs in 1924, two rivals exchanging the role nightly in 1935 in full view of the London press, a twenty-four-year-old Italianate naturalist in 1960 who made casting directors rethink the age of the lover, a thirty-six-year-old intellectual in 1976 whose Romeo the scholarship files as a near miss, and a twenty-nine-year-old television comic actor closing the century at Stratford in 2000 to reviews that could not agree whether he was the part's future or its warning. Each performance answered the theatre of its own moment. Forbes-Robertson's refinement answered a stage drowning in scenery. Olivier's physicality answered a stage embalmed in elocution. Stride's youth answered a stage of ageing juveniles. Tennant's speed answered a stage of reverence. Read in sequence, the gallery is less a hall of fame than a history of the century's acting written through its most exposing part.

The gallery is built from the record rather than from anecdote, and its sources deserve naming at the outset. The production facts, casts and Stratford succession rest on the Royal Shakespeare Company's own chronology as printed in the RSC Shakespeare Complete Works and on the company's production archive; the judgements rest on the named critics of the day, on Jill Levenson's performance history of the play in her Shakespeare in Performance study and her Oxford edition, and on the theatre historians and biographers who aggregated the press files after the fact. Where the record divides, the survey says so and divides with it, because a gallery of performances that survive only in prose is only as honest as its handling of disagreement. Quotations from the play follow the Arden Third Series text edited by René Weis, cited by act and scene, and are kept short by design: the survey's evidence is the performances, and the lines are quoted where a performance's claim turns on them.

Three facts rise from the table before any argument begins. The first is the age column's disorder: the century's most praised Romeos cluster at the extremes, a boy of twenty and men in their forties, while the written character is a teenager whose beloved is not yet fourteen. The second is the migration of the part's home, from the actor-manager's commercial theatre, through the festival stage at Stratford, into the subsidised company where the role became an examination young actors sat rather than a crown stars wore. The third is the table's silence: no row can be labelled the definitive performance, and the rows nearest the middle of the century, Stride's and McKellen's, are famous as an event and as a warning respectively rather than as interpretations that settled anything. The sections that follow argue each claim in turn.

## The Part They All Faced: What Romeo Demands of an Actor

Every judgement in the gallery rests on the text the actors faced, and the text is unusually ungenerous to its leading man. Romeo enters the play performing love rather than feeling it, sighing for the unseen Rosaline in borrowed conceits that the verse itself exposes as quotation: "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1). Jill Levenson, editing the play for the Oxford Shakespeare and surveying its stage life in her Shakespeare in Performance volume, treats the role's difficulty as a designed feature: the hero begins as a boy imitating passion in Petrarchan oxymorons, and the actor must play the imitation convincingly enough that the audience registers the falseness, yet keep enough credit in reserve for the moment the imitation catches fire. An actor who plays the opening as sincere love has nowhere to go; an actor who plays it as satire has betrayed the boy the audience must grieve for. The gallery's failures divide almost exactly on this first hurdle.

The balcony scene then sets the lyric examination. "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2) is the most famous love speech in the language, and its difficulty is the reverse of obscurity: the lines are so familiar that the actor must restore their strangeness, the spectacle of a boy thinking in cosmology because no smaller vocabulary will hold what he sees. The speech is followed by the shared sonnet logic of the first meeting carried into dialogue, by the dawn parting, and by the marriage scene's compressed ecstasy. An actor without music in his delivery turns this stretch of the play into a recitation the audience endures between fights. The verse-speaking tradition that Gielgud inherited existed because this examination is real, marked in the text, and impossible to fake.

### What makes Romeo harder to play than Mercutio?

Mercutio is harder to cast and easier to land: his great speech is a set piece with a beginning, a swell and a payoff, and a brilliant actor can steal an evening with it while remaining a guest in the tragedy. Romeo must carry the whole arc, from borrowed conceits through passion to homicide and suicide.

Any single brilliant passage in the lover's part leaves the rest of it exposed, which is why the gallery's Mercutios are remembered as triumphs while its Romeos are remembered as attempts.

Act 3 sets the second examination, and it is physical in a sense deeper than swordplay. Romeo, newly married and refusing Tybalt's challenge, watches Mercutio fall and then kills Tybalt in a spasm of remorse and rage, crying "O sweet Juliet, thy beauty hath made me effeminate and in my temper softened valour's steel" (3.1). The speech is the part's hinge: the lover accuses love itself of unmanning him, and avenges the accusation on the nearest body. An actor who has played the first two acts as pure lyricism arrives at this scene with no violence in reserve, and the killing reads as a plot event rather than a character's self-disgust. An actor who has hoarded menace from the start makes the balcony scene a fraud. The part demands that both men be plausible in sequence, and the sequence is the difficulty. The final acts then withdraw every support: banished, misinformed and alone in Mantua, Romeo's last great speeches are addressed to an apothecary, a servant and a corpse, and the actor must sustain the tragedy's temperature in soliloquy, defying the stars in "I defy you, stars!" (5.1) and dying on the quietest line in the play, "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3).

The structural cruelty is compounded by the company Romeo keeps on stage. Mercutio owns the play's most quotable speech, the Queen Mab flight that begins "O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4), and dies on its most quotable curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). Juliet owns the play's steepest growth, from obedience through the potion soliloquy to a death her partner does not even witness awake. The Nurse owns the comedy. Romeo owns the arc, and arcs are the hardest thing to make visible in a single evening. Theatre historians of the role return to this asymmetry with unusual consistency: the part offers no single scene in which a great actor can bank the reputation and coast, and its two summits, the orchard and the street fight, reward opposite gifts. The century's gallery is the record of actors discovering that asymmetry one at a time.

A further cruelty is built into the part's registers. Romeo is the only principal required to alternate, sometimes within a single scene, between the public rhetoric of the feud, all challenge and counter-challenge in the square, and a private lyric idiom invented for two speakers in the dark. Hamlet sustains one mighty register and varies its pressure; Romeo must change instruments. The verse marks the changes for any actor listening closely: the borrowed Petrarchan machinery of the Rosaline scenes gives way at the Capulet feast to the shared sonnet, in which the two speakers complete each other's lines inside a single form, and the sonnet's discipline then dissolves across the orchard scene into the freest love poetry in the canon before Act 3 reimposes the idiom of honour and the sword. An actor whose gift is rhetoric makes the private scenes sound like speeches. An actor whose gift is intimacy makes the public scenes sound like interruptions of a love story that the feud keeps rudely breaking into, which is nearer the truth of the writing and still a distortion, because the feud is not an interruption of the play. It is the play's other half, and Romeo is the only character who must be fluent in both halves at full stretch.

## The Victorian Threshold: Irving's Failure and Forbes-Robertson's Refinement

The twentieth century inherited its Romeo from two Lyceum productions that between them defined the part's false start and its first modern answer. Henry Irving opened his Romeo and Juliet at the Lyceum on 8 March 1882, playing the lover at forty-four opposite Ellen Terry, and the production's arithmetic has never stopped mocking its casting: received with abuse by the critics, counted among the failures of the Irving management, and kept running for a hundred and fifty nights by the spectacle and the fame of its stars. Terry, its most reliable witness, reached the verdict the record sustains: as a whole performance the Romeo failed, structurally, a mismatch of age, instrument and method that no intelligence could argue away, while individual passages of melancholy and tenderness showed what the actor's genius might have done in a part shaped to it. Irving's instrument was built for impersonation of a colder, stranger kind, for Shylock and Mathias and the haunted men of melodrama. Asked for tempestuous young grief, the method produced study where the part needed surrender. The failure matters to the gallery because it was not a failure of talent. It was the actor-manager system exposing itself: the Lyceum's economics required its proprietor in the title roles, and the title role of this play is the one part in the canon that cannot be commanded by seniority.

The contrast inside Irving's own career proves the point with a control experiment of one. The same actor, in the same theatre and under the same system, had made Hamlet the triumph of his management, a performance the age treated as a landmark of psychological impersonation, and would go on to successes in roles of age, guile and haunted fixity that suited his instruments exactly. Hamlet rewards the mature impersonator, because the Prince is written thinking his way around his own performance, and an actor of forty-four can plausibly be a student who has lived in his head for decades. Romeo forbids the impersonator his methods. The character does not think his way around anything; he feels at a speed that leaves thought behind, and the actor who approaches him as a study to be constructed, however brilliantly, has announced the construction in every gesture. Irving's Romeo failed, on Terry's evidence, precisely where his Hamlet triumphed, in the scenes of tempestuous grief where construction must dissolve into surrender. The gallery opens with the lesson because every later row re-teaches it in a new idiom: the part examines the actor's capacity to stop performing expertise, and it fails the century's most expert men first.

Thirteen years later the same stage produced the corrective, and the full account of [Forbes-Robertson and the late Victorian Romeo](/2028/03/17/forbes-robertson-victorian-romeo/) belongs to its own article; the survey needs the outline and the verdict. Johnston Forbes-Robertson opened at the Lyceum in September 1895 under his own management, playing Romeo at forty-two to the Juliet of Mrs Patrick Campbell, with Irving absent in America and the house to make his own. The age was nearly Irving's; everything else was reversed. Where Irving's production had buried the play in scenery, Forbes-Robertson re-centred the part on its speech. His formation had been a painter's before it was an actor's, and his verse delivery carried a visual artist's sense of line: the notices fastened on the beauty and clarity of the speaking, and on a lover whose passion was conducted through the voice rather than against it. The production was still filling the theatre months into the run, advertised as the evening bill at the end of November 1895, and it framed the performance that made his reputation beyond recall, the Hamlet of 1897 in the same house, for which Bernard Shaw's famous superlatives were written. The Romeo came first and made the Hamlet possible; the order is the survey's first lesson in how the part functions as an examination, sat in public, on which larger reputations are then built.

### Why did refinement look like coldness in a Romeo?

Refinement reads as coldness when the verse is so beautifully managed that the boy's disorder disappears into the man's technique. Romeo's speeches are a teenager's improvisations, lurching between conceit and panic, and an actor who polishes them into arias convinces the ear while warning the eye that the feelings have been rehearsed.

The part punishes finish: its passion must seem to outrun its poetry, and Forbes-Robertson's dissenters were, in effect, marking the distance between a perfect instrument and an imperfect boy.

The pairing's ages state the era's convention with unusual clarity. Forbes-Robertson at forty-two played opposite a Juliet of thirty, Mrs Patrick Campbell having been born in February 1865, and no notice in the record treats the arithmetic as remarkable, because the metropolitan stage of the 1890s cast its lovers from its senior ranks as a matter of course and asked technique to supply the adolescence. The gallery's age column shows how slowly that settlement moved: the American Sothern was forty-four in 1904, and the first Romeo in the table under thirty, Gielgud in 1924, was a scandal of youth rather than a promise of reform, jeered for the very inexperience a later age would demand as the part's first qualification. Between Forbes-Robertson's unremarked forty-two and Stride's revolutionary twenty-four stand sixty-five years in which the convention loosened by inches, a few years of actor's age surrendered per decade, and the survey insists on the slowness because it explains the gallery's shape. The lyrical-physical argument could not be settled while the casting question was still open, since a Romeo's style is partly a function of the body the audience is asked to believe in, and the century spent its first two-thirds arguing about instruments before it could hear the argument about methods clearly.

The standing quarrel over the 1895 Romeo, whether the refinement was the part cooled down or the part recovered, is adjudicated in the companion article and can be settled here on the evidence of sequence. Forbes-Robertson's Romeo did not empty the role of passion; it relocated passion's evidence from gesture to utterance, in a decade when the London stage still expected the lover's feelings to be illustrated by his body and his scenery. Shaw's later testimony about the same instrument, written for the Hamlet, describes an actor whose speaking made thought itself theatrical, and that testimony, applied backwards with due caution, explains both the 1895 admirers and the dissenters. The admirers heard the part's poetry treated as action for the first time in their theatre-going lives. The dissenters missed the perspiration. Both were describing the same performance accurately, which is why the quarrel survived: it was the lyrical-physical argument of the whole century, held for the first time in modern dress rehearsal, forty years before Gielgud and Olivier gave it names and faces.

## The American Star Romeos: Sothern, Marlowe and the Partnership Model

Across the Atlantic the part developed under a different economy, and the American rows of the gallery obey a logic of their own. E. H. Sothern and Julia Marlowe opened their Romeo and Juliet at the Knickerbocker Theatre in New York in October 1904, and built the lovers into a marital as well as a theatrical institution, returning to the play across a quarter-century of touring until the partnership itself became the interpretation. Sothern was forty-four at the first opening, the same age as Irving at his failure, and the comparison instructs: where Irving's Romeo was a tragedian alone in the wrong part, Sothern's was half of a double act the public adored as a unit, and the production trimmed the feud, the comedy and the civic frame to keep the lovers at the centre where the partnership's value lay. The American star system did not ask its Romeo to pass the part's double examination. It asked him to be one term of a beloved equation, and within that dispensation Sothern's Romeo, graceful, romantic and subsumed into the partnership, was a complete success of its kind. When Time magazine reviewed the Oliviers' Broadway production in 1940, its benchmark for the aura surrounding a Shakespearean pair was precise: since Sothern and Marlowe, no pair of actors had undertaken the play with more of an aura about them. The partnership model had set the American standard by which even a famous failure would be measured.

The partnership was itself an interpretation, and the survey should not let the American rows pass as mere commerce. A married pair playing the lovers altered the erotic contract of the evening in a way no casting of strangers could imitate: the audience watched a real union rehearse its own mythology, and the balcony scene's risk, the sense of a privacy genuinely shared, was underwritten by the programme's knowledge that the privacy continued after the curtain. Sothern's Romeo, read in this light, was not an incomplete performance of the part but a complete performance of a different thesis about it, that the lover is legible only inside the couple, that Romeo exists as half of a chord. The English gallery, with its sequence of individual assays, mostly assumed the opposite thesis, that the part is a solo examination which the Juliet accompanies. The twentieth century never adjudicated between the two theses, because its theatres were organised to test only one at a time; but the American record's commercial fact, a quarter-century of returns for a Romeo the English standard would have marked as a partial pass, stands as evidence that audiences will forgive the lover almost anything except the absence of a believable beloved opposite him.

The model's second exhibit is a Juliet's production that made its Romeo's reputation by adjacency. Jane Cowl's Broadway Romeo and Juliet of 1923 ran for 157 performances with Dennis King as her Romeo, and Cowl went on to play Juliet 856 times across New York and the road, a career total that made the part her property in American theatrical memory. King's Romeo, praised in its day and preserved in the production's long commercial life, represents the American Romeo at his most functional: a handsome, ardent leading man inside a vehicle engineered for the leading lady, in a theatre culture that had decided the play belonged to its Juliets and its partnerships rather than to its Romeos.

The long run itself deserves a line in the gallery's argument, because repetition is the one examiner the English rows never faced. A Romeo played eight times a week for months, and a Juliet played 856 times across a career, cease to be interpretations in the critic's sense and become feats of sustainability, the verse worn to a groove in which freshness is simulated by craft. The English system, with its short seasons and repertory rotation, protected its Romeos from that examiner and thereby kept their failures interesting; the American system subjected its lovers to it and thereby made their successes a different species of fact. When the survey ranks Gielgud's Romeo above every American row, the ranking should be read with the systems in view: the Londoner was examined on invention, the New Yorkers on endurance, and the part, characteristically, had set both papers without announcing that they were different. The contrast with London is structural. The English gallery is a sequence of individual interpretations, each arguing with the last; the American record of the century's first half is a sequence of occasions, in which the Romeo's task was to complete a famous pair, fill a famous theatre, and carry a famous tour. Olivier's 1940 Broadway collapse, treated in its own section below, is partly explained by the mismatch: he brought the English argument, an interpretation contesting the part, to a stage whose audience had come for an occasion, and the occasion refused to happen.

## Gielgud's Three Romeos: The Making of the Lyrical Standard

John Gielgud played Romeo three times across eleven years, and the three attempts map the formation of the century's lyrical pole better than any single performance could. The first came in May 1924 at the Regent Theatre, where Barry Jackson cast the twenty-year-old opposite Gwen Ffrangcon-Davies. The production was not a success, and the notices found the body before they found the voice. Ivor Brown, the most feared of the younger critics, found the young actor scant of virility and saddled him with the cruellest compliment in the gallery's archive, a Romeo with the most meaningless legs imaginable. Nicholas de Jongh, summarising the wreckage in his Guardian obituary of Gielgud three-quarters of a century later, added the extenuating evidence of orange make-up, a coal-black centre-parted wig and white tights, and recorded that the critics were loud in their scorn. What the scorn missed, or could not yet hear, was the instrument being tuned. Gielgud at twenty was the one genuinely young Romeo in the century's famous line, younger than Stride would be in 1960, and his failure was the failure of youth without technique: the feelings were the right age and the machinery for projecting them did not exist yet.

The formation behind the attempts explains the standard he eventually set. Gielgud was dynastic before he was anything else: the great-nephew of Ellen Terry, who had played Juliet to Irving's Romeo in the gallery's first row, he inherited the Victorian stage's memory in his own family, and his instrument was built accordingly, voice first, the Terry music disciplined by a modern intelligence. The irony of the inheritance is one the survey notes with care. Terry's evidence had convicted Irving's Romeo of a failure of surrender; her great-nephew's Romeo would be convicted, by the next generation's critics, of the opposite failure, a surrender so completely mediated by music that the blood seemed left out of it. Between the two convictions stands the whole education of the English verse stage, and Gielgud's three Romeos are its curriculum.

The second attempt, at the Old Vic in 1929, belongs to the seasons in which Gielgud established himself as the leading Shakespearean of his generation, the Hamlet of 1929 and the Richard II that followed making the reputation the Romeo had only promised. The Old Vic Romeo is the least documented of the three, overshadowed in the record by the Hamlet that shared its season, and the survey treats it accordingly, as the middle term of a formation rather than an exhibit in itself: the lyric instrument, five years stronger, learning the part's architecture in repertory conditions, at a theatre where the audience came for the company and the verse rather than for a star's single assay. By the time Gielgud returned to the part in 1935, as director of his own production at the New Theatre, he had played it in failure and in apprenticeship, and he built the third attempt as a deliberate act of recovery. Levenson's chapter on the production in Shakespeare in Performance is titled "Recovery, 1935", and the word is chosen with editorial precision: Gielgud's text restored what the acting editions had spent two centuries removing, the Prologue's warning, the complete duel sequence, the bawdy exchanges, the double catastrophe in the vault, and the performance gave the restored architecture a Romeo of thirty-one whose verse speaking had become the standard by which the age measured the skill.

The lyrical standard Gielgud set requires definition, because the gallery's whole argument turns on it. His method treated the line as the event: phrasing, tone and rhythm carried the feeling, and the body was asked to illustrate as little as possible, so that Romeo's inner weather registered as changes in the music of the speaking. Critics who later codified the contrast with Olivier, Kenneth Tynan's generation foremost among them, described Gielgud as the musician of the line and Olivier as its athlete, and the shorthand survives because the 1935 production let London hear both in the same speeches on alternate nights. The standard's limitation was visible from the first night of Gielgud's own fame. De Jongh's obituary verdict on the 1935 exchange, that Gielgud's Romeo was all surface and no sensuality while Olivier was a love-possessed Romeo from the fingertips to the heart, states the hostile case in its strongest form, and it cannot be waved away: the lyric method, perfected, could make the orchard scene a concert and leave the street fight, the banishment and the tomb to be got through on technique. The gallery's central question, whether any Romeo joined the music to the blood, is the question Gielgud's three attempts posed and left open.

## 1935: Gielgud and Olivier Alternate Romeo in the Century's Controlled Experiment

The production that anchors the gallery opened at the New Theatre on 16 October 1935 and closed on 28 March 1936, after 186 performances on the National Portrait Gallery's count, with some biographical summaries giving 189 and the discrepancy standing unresolved in the record. Gielgud directed, Peggy Ashcroft played Juliet throughout, Edith Evans played the Nurse, and the two leading men exchanged the roles of Romeo and Mercutio in late November, the first configuration giving Laurence Olivier, at twenty-eight, the lover and Gielgud, at thirty-one, the friend. The full production record, its origins in an abandoned Dickens adaptation and its afterlife in the two men's careers, is set out in the account of [Gielgud and Olivier alternating as Romeo and Mercutio in the 1935 New Theatre production](/2027/10/15/gielgud-olivier-alternate-romeo-1935/). The survey's business is narrower: what the alternation proved about the part, and why its verdict has been disputed ever since by critics who were all looking at the same evidence.

The experiment's design was commercial before it was artistic. Gielgud, who had played Romeo twice and meant to direct, took Mercutio for himself and engaged Olivier, a rising matinee idol without a major Shakespearean success, on the condition that they alternate, so that mixed notices for the younger man's Romeo could not sink the enterprise. The first-night file shows the insurance being tested at once. James Agate of the Sunday Times, the era's most feared and most quoted drama critic, published his notice on 17 October 1935, and it survives, anthologised by Stanley Wells in Shakespeare in the Theatre, as the file's representative document, divided with unusual precision along the production's fault line. Olivier's Romeo won praise for virility and for passion; the verse speaking was deplored in the same columns that celebrated the ardour. The division was not Agate's eccentricity. The aggregated dailies and Sundays, as the biographies reconstruct them, admired the younger man's passion and deplored his speaking of the verse with a consistency that reads like a coordinated verdict, while the Queen Mab speech in Gielgud's delivery was treated as a revelation, a prose-bred flight rebuilt as a spell. One production had given its audience a Romeo whose body believed the part and a Mercutio whose voice enchanted it, and the exchange in late November then submitted the obvious control: the same two men, the same text, the same Juliet, the parts reversed.

The rehearsal record, as the biographies reconstruct it, shows the fault line being dug before the first night. Gielgud the director bullied Olivier about his verse speaking, drilling the younger man toward the music of the lines; Olivier resisted in the name of a different object, the reckless passion of an impulsive sixteen-year-old, pursued with an ardour in the love scenes that Gielgud repeatedly asked him to tone down. Neither man was being difficult for its own sake. Each was defending the half of the part his instrument owned, and the production's genius, half designed and half accidental, was to let the quarrel run in public for five months rather than settle it in the rehearsal room. Ashcroft, at twenty-seven the youngest principal and the only one who played the same role to both configurations, stood at the quarrel's centre as its recording instrument, which is why her later testimony about the first arrangement carries the authority the survey gives it.

The critics who adjudicate the exchange divide into three camps, and the disagreement is the brief's mandatory quarrel in its purest form. Agate's camp, the first-night witnesses, judged the event: Olivier's Romeo was the sensation of the autumn, and the sensation is a fact of theatre history that no later revision can repeal. The theatre historian Jonathan Croall, in his biography Gielgud: A Theatrical Life, performed the second judgement, an analysis of the whole review file across both configurations, and its conclusion cuts against the legend: Olivier was greatly preferred as Mercutio to Romeo, and Gielgud was largely thought the better of the two in both roles. The third camp is testimony rather than criticism. Ashcroft, who played Juliet to both configurations, later held that the first arrangement, Olivier's Romeo against Gielgud's Mercutio, gave the definitive performances of those characters, a judgement from inside the experiment that no aggregation of notices can outrank for authority about what the evenings felt like at their peak.

### Who won the alternation, Gielgud or Olivier?

Each man won the part he was not famous for entering. Olivier's Romeo won the event and lost the file: the aggregated notices preferred Gielgud in both roles, and Olivier's enduring triumph of the production was his Mercutio. Gielgud's Romeo won the long judgement, the more complete performance of the lover.

The alternation's true verdict is that the contest was misconceived, because the production's peak was a configuration, not a competitor. The adjudication the survey adopts follows from separating the three objects the camps were judging. As an event, a first night and a season's talk, Olivier's Romeo won, and Agate's division of praise is the proof. As a performance of the whole part across an evening, Gielgud's Romeo won on the file's aggregate, because the part's lyric examination is longer and more heavily weighted than its physical one, and the examiners marked accordingly. As a theatrical configuration, the first arrangement won on Ashcroft's testimony, because Olivier's ardent Romeo needed Gielgud's Mercutio as its foil and ignition, a dependence the exchange exposed by diminishing both evenings. The alternation therefore did not crown a Romeo. It demonstrated, with a rigour no single production could have managed, that the two halves of the part could be maximised only by two different actors in the same run, and that a season of playgoers would pay for 186 performances to watch the demonstration repeated. That is why 1935 remains the gallery's centre of gravity: it is the one exhibit in which the century's argument about the role was not described but performed, under controlled conditions, with the results published nightly in the press.

The enterprise's commercial frame should not be forgotten amid the aesthetics. The New Theatre production was an unsubsidised commercial venture, mounted in a theatre economy with no state safety net, and its 186 performances were not a by-product of the experiment but its enabling condition: the argument about acting styles sold seats for five months, which is why a management could afford to keep two leading men, a full company and a restored text on the stage while the argument ran. Later institutions could mount comparative experiments as research, protected by subsidy from the verdict of the box office. Gielgud's management ran its experiment as a business, and the business succeeded, a fact that disciplines every romantic account of the production. The verse debate of 1935 was not a coterie quarrel. It was popular entertainment about the most technical question in the actor's craft, and the town paid to watch it settled nightly in two directions at once.

## Olivier's Second Romeo: Broadway 1940 and the Physical Line's Limit

Olivier tested the lesson five years later under conditions of his own choosing, and the result is the gallery's most valuable failure. In May 1940 he staged the tragedy on Broadway himself, directing and playing Romeo at thirty-two opposite Vivien Leigh, in a production mounted with the aura of the most discussed screen pair of the hour behind it. He kept the text almost uncut, a purist's answer to the acting editions, and the production collapsed. Time magazine's notice remains the famous document: a production not merely weak or spotty but calamitous, a Juliet who looked like a poem but had no sense of poetry, a Romeo who made a handsome lover but talked as though he was brushing his teeth, conducting his courtship as though he was D'Artagnan, and a director whose greatest sin was the virtually uncut text itself, its length then ransomed by having the cast race through some lines and swallow others. The verdict's cruelty should not obscure its diagnostic value. The critic was not reporting that Olivier could not act Romeo. The critic was reporting that the physical method, ardour, athleticism, the body believing first, had been asked to carry an evening of a length and verbal density it could not sustain without the lyric equipment the method disdained, and that the actor-director had removed the cuts that might have hidden the mismatch.

### Why did the nearly uncut text sink Olivier's Broadway Romeo?

The uncut text removed the star vehicle's traditional mercy, which was to shorten the play around the lover's strengths. Given the whole architecture, the verse examination ran for hours, and a method built on impulse and physical conviction had to fake the sustained music it lacked, racing some passages and swallowing others.

The failure was of fit, not of talent. Olivier's Romeo had triumphed in extracts of ardour inside Gielgud's shaped production; five years later, in a text nobody had shaped for him, the same instrument stood exposed at full length, and the exposure is what the notices recorded.

The production's circumstances sharpen the lesson. Olivier and Leigh arrived as the most discussed screen pair of the hour, he fresh from the film of Wuthering Heights as the screen's great romantic lover, she the most talked-of cinema actress in the world, and the first-night audience was among the most glittering in Manhattan's history. Time's review conceded the evening its pictorial virtues and nothing else, which is the precise signature of the partnership model's revenge: New York had come for an occasion in the Sothern and Marlowe line, the aura of a famous pair enacting a famous love, and Olivier had instead mounted an argument, an almost uncut text directed as a manifesto about the play. The argument bored the occasion's audience and the occasion ruined the argument's evening, and the Romeo, handsome, ardent and vocally unequipped for the marathon his own integrity had scheduled, paid for both decisions. No row of the gallery teaches more about the part's dependence on its frame.

The survey draws two conclusions from 1940 that govern the rows after it. The first is that Romeo's reputation is unusually hostage to editing: the part as written is longer, wordier and more exposed than the part as traditionally played, and every famous Romeo after Olivier worked inside a text shaped by a director's judgement about where the actor's strength lay. Gielgud's 1935 recovery is the honourable exception that proves the rule's shape, since his Romeo was built by the actor who had restored the text, for his own instrument. The second conclusion concerns the physical line's inheritance. Olivier's Romeo did not found a school of Romeos, as his Mercutio founded expectations for that part and his later Henry V and Othello founded methods for theirs. It founded a permission: the permission to treat the lover's passion as behaviour first and poetry second, to mistrust elocution as a class accent, and to cast the part for heat. Zeffirelli's 1960 production, and John Stride's Romeo inside it, are that permission exercised by a director who had absorbed the lesson of 1940 as well as the sensation of 1935, and who solved the text problem in the opposite way, by cutting roughly a third of the play and letting youth carry the rest.

## The Postwar Settlement: Payne, Johnson and the Young Stratford Romeos

The postwar stage answered the age question before it answered the style question. Peter Brook, twenty-two years old and directing at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre under Sir Barry Jackson's policy of entrusting the festival to youth, opened his Romeo and Juliet at Stratford in April 1947 with a leading man in his late twenties, Laurence Payne, as Romeo, a Juliet of nineteen in Daphne Slater, and Paul Scofield, at twenty-five already the most discussed young actor in the company, as Mercutio. The casting deserves its fame in the gallery for what it refused: after decades of lovers in their forties, the tragedy's three young principals were played by performers whose ages stood within sight of the fiction, and the production transferred later in 1947 to Her Majesty's Theatre in London as a festival novelty that had become a commercial proposition. Payne's Romeo itself survives in the record more faintly than the production around it, a fate the survey notes without embarrassment, since it recurs: Romeo is the part in which an actor can be perfectly adequate inside a famous staging and leave almost no individual trace, the arc's demands consuming the credit that set-piece roles bank. Scofield's Mercutio, by contrast, is the performance from 1947 that entered the general memory, confirming the asymmetry the text builds in. The friend dies owning the evening's most quoted speech and its most quoted curse; the lover lives to the end and owns the blame for the evening's length.

The production around Payne repays a survey paragraph of its own, because its methods set the postwar terms. Brook's staging, designed by Rolf Gérard with music by Roberto Gerhard, stripped the tragedy of the accumulated pictorial tradition and played it as a civic event, a young city killing its young, and he cut the closing reconciliation so that the feud's settlement could not console the audience out of the waste it had watched. The Times notice of 7 April 1947 objected in terms the record preserves, while the Manchester Guardian divided its own notice between praise and the charge of gauntness, and the disagreement is the familiar one in postwar dress: whether the play's poetry is a substance to be served or a varnish to be stripped. For the gallery's purposes the 1947 row establishes that a Romeo could be the least remembered element of a famous production without the production failing, a possibility the star system had never allowed and the company system would make normal.

Glen Byam Shaw's Stratford production of April 1958 brought the settlement to maturity, and its Romeo is the survey's model of the balanced company performance. Richard Johnson, born in July 1927 and therefore thirty at the opening, played Romeo to the Juliet of Dorothy Tutin, whose twenty-eighth birthday fell on the first night, with Edward Woodward, twenty-seven, as Mercutio and designs by Motley. The production's admirers praised its clarity, and the praise, properly understood, was a Romeo verdict: in a staging whose first virtue was that every line landed intelligibly, the lover's speeches were neither sung nor thrown away but thought aloud, and Johnson's Romeo made his name accordingly, the performance that established him as the Memorial Theatre's leading man of the hour and opened the screen career that followed. Set between Brook's youthful experiment and Zeffirelli's naturalism, the 1958 production is the gallery's evidence that the lyric and physical claims could be held in working balance by an actor of no revolutionary method, given a director whose discipline was clarity itself. The balance was not a solution to the part's problem so much as a demonstration that the problem could be managed, season by season, by institutions that cast the role as the head of a company rather than as a visiting star.

The institution arrived three years later. Peter Hall's new Royal Shakespeare Company staged the play in its founding Stratford season of 1961 with Brian Murray, an actor in his early twenties, as Romeo and Tutin returning as Juliet, Ian Bannen as Mercutio, Edith Evans, in her seventies, as the Nurse and Max Adrian as Friar Lawrence. The casting was a manifesto of the ensemble principle: the lover was not the production's biggest name, the Nurse was, and the Romeo's youth, unremarkable in a star vehicle, became programmatic inside a company that intended the classics to be played by the generation they describe. Murray's Romeo left no legend to rival the names around it in the gallery, and the survey reads that absence as the point. Under the company system the part began its migration from crown to examination: a role through which a young actor proved his readiness for the repertoire's heavier assignments, judged by the standard of the verse and the fight in roughly equal measure, with the understanding that the company's next production, not this one, was where reputations would be made.

The 1961 casting carried one further charge that the survey should register, because it joined the gallery's two ends. Evans's Nurse was the last of her four assays of the role, a line that ran from the Old Vic of 1925 and 1926, through the Broadway production of 1934 in which she had stipulated that the Nurse be played uncut, and the New Theatre of 1935, to this founding RSC season thirty-six years later. The young Romeo of the new company therefore played his scenes with the Nurse against the living embodiment of the role's whole modern history, an actress whose first Nurse had predated Gielgud's first Romeo. No programme note announced the symmetry, but the production embodied the institution's founding claim in its cast list: the company's memory would be carried by its elders while its future was examined in its young, and the lover's part sat exactly at the junction, tested by a tradition embodied on the same stage.

## John Stride and the Youth Revolution at the Old Vic in 1960

Between Johnson's settlement and Hall's manifesto, a single production redrew the gallery's map, and its Romeo is the survey's clearest case of a performance famous as an event in casting. Franco Zeffirelli, engaged by Michael Benthall at the Old Vic, opened his production in 1960 with John Stride, twenty-four, as Romeo and Judi Dench, twenty-five, as Juliet, cut the text by roughly a third, dressed the stage in the bustle and heat of an Italian city, and rehearsed his young company toward behaviour rather than declamation. Kenneth Tynan, the era's most feared and followed critic, judged that a miracle had been worked in turning the figures into real people, and singled out the fight scenes for a realism that made the verse party's objections audible as a class position: the guardians of the verse tradition heard poetry sacrificed to bustle, and said so. The two verdicts, as the production's own article in this series concludes, describe different strata of the same evening. The event triumphed; the text was taxed. Stride's Romeo is where the tax and the triumph meet in one body, a lover whose credibility as a teenager in love, quick, physical and unembarrassed, was purchased by accepting losses in the orchard that an older audience had been trained to itemise.

The venue gave the revolution its polemical edge. The Old Vic was the house of the lyric settlement: Lilian Baylis's company had given London Gielgud's Old Vic seasons at the start of the 1930s, Evans had played her first Nurses under its roof in the 1920s, and its stage carried the accumulated authority of the verse tradition in bricks and memory. To mount the anti-elocutionary Romeo there, with a young Italian director and a pair of lovers in their mid-twenties, was to stage the argument on the opposition's ground, and the production's mythology has always included that geography. Revolutions in the theatre are partly addresses: the same staging in a new building would have been an experiment, while at the Old Vic it was a succession crisis, the house's own grandchildren disputing the inheritance in the family hall.

### What did casting a twenty-four-year-old change in 1960?

It changed the evidence. Earlier Romeos had asked the audience to accept a man's representation of youth; Stride's age and Zeffirelli's methods let the audience watch youth itself, with its speed, clumsiness and appetite, and the balcony scene's meaning shifted from a poem about desire to an incident of it.

Once a Romeo of twenty-four had carried a major production, every older casting became a decision that had to be argued rather than a convention that could be assumed.

The verse party's case deserves its fairest statement before the survey moves on, because it was not nostalgia. A third of the play is not an abstraction; it is specific music removed from specific mouths, and the lovers' language is the play's claim that the feeling exceeds the world that crushes it. A production that taxes the text to buy credibility makes a wager: that the audience will grieve more for people it believes in than for voices it is ravished by. Zeffirelli's wager won its evening and founded a casting era, and the survey honours it as the postwar gallery's hinge. But the wager's terms explain why the production made no school of Romeos in Stride's image. Youth cannot be rehearsed into an older actor, and the next young Romeos were cast by directors who had learned the casting lesson without inheriting Zeffirelli's exact balance of bustle and belief, so that the revolution propagated as a policy about ages rather than as a method of playing the part. Stride did not become the century's standard Romeo in the way his production became the century's standard argument for young casting, and his later career never re-centred on the part. The performance's fame is inseparable from Zeffirelli's staging, Dench's Juliet and the cultural moment that wanted Shakespeare rescued from elocution; strip those away and the record preserves a gifted young actor inside a famous mise en scène rather than an interpretation that redefined the role's possibilities from within. The survey's verdict on 1960 is therefore double. As production history, it is the hinge of the postwar gallery, the moment the age convention broke. As Romeo history, it proves the rule that the part's fame attaches to stagings more readily than to performances, because the lover's arc, unlike Mercutio's flight or Juliet's potion speech, cannot be excerpted into a legend. What a Romeo does can only be judged across five acts, and five acts are exactly what memory refuses to keep.

## The RSC Succession: Holm, Dalton and Lesser in the Company Era

The Royal Shakespeare Company's own succession after 1961 shows the examination system at work. The company's chronology for the century lists twelve productions of the play between the founding staging and the Boyd production of 2000, thirteen Romeos in all counting the divided part of 1983, and the gallery's Stratford rows after Murray are drawn from that succession: three Romeos of the 1960s and 1970s whose fame is real but bounded, each illuminating a different settlement between the company and the part, before the survey reaches the examination's most public sitting in 1976. Karolos Koun, the Greek director of the Athens Art Theatre, staged the play at Stratford in 1967 with Ian Holm, in his mid-thirties, as Romeo opposite Estelle Kohler. Holm was the anti-matinee Romeo of the line: compact, watchful, inward, an actor whose intensity ran to stillness rather than display, and his casting inside a continental director's production announced that the company era would treat the lover as an interpretive problem rather than a romantic given. The performance made no bid for the gallery's lyric honours and asked for none of its physical ones; it treated Romeo's catastrophe as a failure of attention in a violent society, and it survives in the succession as evidence of how far the part could be pulled from its traditional centre of glamour without breaking.

Koun's presence in the succession is itself a datum the survey weighs. Hall's company had been founded on the premise that the English classics could be renewed from inside the English tradition, and the invitation to a Greek director with his own Athens Art Theatre behind him, a company built on ensemble realism in a converted space, announced that by 1967 the renewal was expected to come from outside that tradition's manners. Holm's Romeo is unintelligible except as part of that import: an actor of the English new wave, precise, unsentimental and physically unheroic, set inside a Mediterranean staging whose crowds and heat made Verona a place rather than a backdrop. The lover diminished accordingly, from protagonist to citizen, and the diminishment was the interpretation. Later directors would restore Romeo's centrality by star casting or by concept; Koun and Holm remain the succession's standing proof that the part can be de-centred entirely and the play still stand, which is either the role's final humiliation or its deepest compliment, and the survey reads it as the compliment, since only a part of real substance can bear being treated as one strand among many.

Terry Hands's 1973 production restored the glamour on company terms. Timothy Dalton, twenty-seven, played Romeo to Kohler's second Stratford Juliet, the actress having now played the heroine to two different Romeos in the succession, with David Suchet as Tybalt and designs by Farrah that gave the production a stylised, sun-struck Verona. Dalton's Romeo was the leading man's assay the system was designed to produce: handsome, ardent, vocally placed, the performance of an actor visibly en route to larger fame, which duly arrived on screen in the following decade. The survey's interest in the row is diagnostic rather than celebratory. By 1973 the RSC could manufacture a creditable Romeo the way it manufactured creditable Hamlets, by casting a rising leading man at the right age inside a strong design and letting the repertoire do the rest; what it could not manufacture was the individual stamp that had made Gielgud's or Olivier's attempts into arguments the culture kept having. Dalton's Romeo pleased, convinced and vanished into a filmography, and its vanishing is the datum.

Ron Daniels's 1980 production fielded Anton Lesser, twenty-eight, opposite Judy Buxton, in designs by Ralph Koltai. Lesser's Romeo represents the verse technician's settlement carried into the company era: an actor of formidable diction and analytic intelligence, whose lover was built from the text outward, every conceit placed, every transition argued. Between Holm's inwardness, Dalton's glamour and Lesser's technique, the succession of 1967 to 1980 traces the company's quiet conclusion about the part. Romeo had become the repertoire's diagnostic role, the part a management used to learn what kind of actor it had, lyrical, physical or intellectual, before assigning the tragedies in which one gift alone could suffice. The examination metaphor, decorative when applied to Forbes-Robertson, had become institutional fact, and the next Stratford Romeo in the gallery sat the examination in its most public form.

## Ian McKellen's Romeo: Intellect at the Limit in 1976

Trevor Nunn's 1976 Stratford production gave the gallery its most instructive near miss. Ian McKellen, who had acted with Nunn at Cambridge and joined the company that season to play Leontes and Macbeth alongside the lover, played Romeo at thirty-six opposite Francesca Annis, with Michael Pennington as Mercutio and designs by Chris Dyer on a single fixed set. The pairing of roles in one season states the experiment plainly: the same actor, in the same months, played the young lover and the Scottish usurper, and the comparison the audience could not avoid making became the performance's critical frame.

The season's architecture sharpened the test. Nunn's production played on Chris Dyer's single fixed set in the main house while the Macbeth, with Dench as Lady Macbeth, was staged in the company's studio space, and McKellen also played Leontes in The Winter's Tale across the same Stratford months, so that the actor's year described an arc from the lover through the jealous king to the murderer, three studies in obsession at three ages of man. Pennington's Mercutio was judged memorable in a production the company's own archive describes as clear and unfussy, which matters to the Romeo's file: the staging made no alibis. No concept interposed itself between the lovers and the audience, no design argued the evening's thesis for it, and the performance's failure to ignite therefore belongs to the interpretation itself with unusual purity. In the gallery's terms, 1976 is the control experiment for the intellect hypothesis, run under clean conditions by the most intelligent actor available, and its result is the result the survey has reported. McKellen's own account of accepting the part is disarming; the offer to play Romeo to Annis, whom he called the most beautiful woman he had ever worked with, and Macbeth with Judi Dench, was one he found irresistible, the fulfilment of a childhood fantasy of working at Stratford. Nothing in the record suggests vanity or miscasting by indifference. The production was a deliberate test of whether intelligence, the quality McKellen possessed beyond any contemporary, could organise the part.

The scholarship's verdict is that it could not, quite. A Birmingham doctoral study of McKellen's Shakespearean career, Hilary Long's examination of the performances on which his reputation rests, files the Romeo among the limited successes, outside the five performances that built the name, and the production's notices converge on the same reservation from different directions: the erotic charge between the lovers did not ignite as the analysis did. Dominic Cavendish, surveying memorable RSC performances for the Telegraph, described a Romeo tormented by love in a world of unfettered masculinity, a formulation that honours the conception while locating the performance's centre in torment rather than desire. The Stratford article in this series, surveying the company's decades, puts the institutional reading crisply: McKellen at thirty-six carried the company's adult-casting policy to its limit and made the limit itself the interpretation, a Romeo whose tragedy is that his intelligence cannot keep pace with his heart. As an account of what the performance did, that reading is generous and probably just. As an account of what the part needs, it is an admission. Romeo's intelligence is supposed to be outrun; a performance that makes the audience admire the outrunning as a concept has converted the tragedy's wound into its programme note.

### Why is McKellen's Romeo filed as a near miss?

The performance organised everything except the one thing the role cannot survive without, erotic conviction between the lovers. McKellen's Romeo was praised for thought and for torment, and the notices and the later scholarship agree that the love scenes persuaded the intellect more than the senses.

A Romeo may be forgiven weak verse or plain looks; the record forgives neither in the end if the balcony scene leaves the audience analysing instead of believing, and the spectacle of a fine mind inside a boy's catastrophe is analysis framed as performance.

The near miss matters beyond one reputation because it completes the gallery's taxonomy of partial gifts. Gielgud had shown that music alone leaves the blood unaccounted for. Olivier had shown that blood alone leaves the music unaccounted for. McKellen, the most intelligent Romeo of the century, showed that analysis alone leaves desire unaccounted for, the third term of the part's examination and the one no technique supplies on demand. His Macbeth in the same season, played opposite Dench in the Other Place, was the triumph the Romeo was not, and the pairing is the cleanest evidence in the whole survey that Romeo is not simply a smaller tragedy for younger men. Macbeth rewards the actor who understands the character's self-deception and can stage it. Romeo punishes the actor who understands too visibly, because the character's glory is precisely that he does not understand, and the audience must fall for the boy before it is allowed to judge him.

## The Young Pretenders: Day-Lewis, Bean, Rylance, Branagh and Fiennes

The 1980s returned the part to actors in their twenties and scattered it across the decade's new stages, and the gallery's busiest year is 1986, when three Romeos of three different kinds played within months of each other. The decade opened with the strangest exhibit in the table. John Caird's small-scale RSC tour of 1983 shared the role of Romeo between Daniel Day-Lewis and Simon Templeman, an arrangement born of touring practicality that doubled as interpretation: the part, split between two actors, showed its seams, and audiences could watch the lover's construction as construction. Day-Lewis, in his mid-twenties, left the tour for the film work that made his name, and the production continued with its Romeo divided, a standing lesson in how little a given evening depends on a single charisma and how much the legend of a Romeo depends on exactly that. The survey files the experiment as the gallery's limit case in the opposite direction from McKellen: not a great actor failing the whole part, but the whole part deliberately unbundled from any single actor, and surviving.

Michael Bogdanov's modern-dress production at Stratford in 1986, the staging whose scandal was print-deep and whose methods reset the company's sense of the play's audience, cast Sean Bean, twenty-seven, as Romeo opposite Niamh Cusack, with Michael Kitchen as Mercutio and Hugh Quarshie as Tybalt. Bean's Romeo was the street settlement of the physical line: a contemporary Veronese of the production's modern Italy, quick, funny and dangerous in the idiom of the street fight rather than the duelling school, his ardour conducted at the temperature of the world Bogdanov built around him. The performance's fame is the production's fame, which is the 1986 condition generally; but Bean's row earns its place because it demonstrates what Olivier's permission had become at fifty years' distance. The physical Romeo no longer needed to argue against elocution. In a production that treated the play as a contemporary event, physical conviction was simply the house style, and the verse was asked to survive inside it as best it could.

The production around Bean frames the point institutionally. Bogdanov's staging, designed by Chris Dyer, set the feud in a modern Italy of cars, guns and cafe society and was received in some quarters as a scandal, though its own article in this series establishes that the scandal lived in print rather than in any disturbance in the theatre. What the hostile notices were actually registering, beneath the outrage at the concept, was a transfer of the play's centre of gravity from its language to its sociology, and Bean's Romeo was that transfer embodied: a lover whose credibility came from the world he moved in, whose fights convinced because the world was violent in a familiar grammar, and whose love scenes convinced in proportion as the production let behaviour stand where rhetoric had stood. Judged as an interpretation of the text, the performance is thin in the gallery's records; judged as a demonstration of where the part could live in 1986, it is one of the decade's essential rows.

The same year held the fringe's answer and the open air's. Kenneth Branagh, twenty-five, played Romeo at the Lyric Studio in Hammersmith in his own full-scale production opposite Samantha Bond, the success on which the Renaissance Theatre Company was founded the following year and the foundation exhibit of a career built on refusing to wait for institutions. Thirty years later Branagh returned to the play as a director, staging it at the Garrick with Richard Madden as his Romeo, in the production examined in the account of [Kenneth Branagh's 2016 staging](/2027/12/03/kenneth-branagh-2016-staging/); his own summary of the symmetry, given to the Guardian at the time, was that three decades earlier Samantha Bond had played Juliet and he had played Romeo, and that the new production's Paris was Bond's son. The younger Branagh's Romeo belongs to the gallery as the star vehicle reborn at fringe scale: a young actor-manager in Forbes-Robertson's posture, staking a company on the lover's part, and succeeding by energy, charm and organisational will where the verse examination was sat, as it were, in his own hall. Ralph Fiennes, twenty-three, played the third Romeo of 1986 at the Open Air Theatre in Regent's Park, directed by Declan Donnellan opposite Sarah Woodward, at the start of the trajectory that carried him through the RSC to the decade's defining screen work. The park performance is the least documented of the three, and the survey includes it for the pattern it completes: by 1986 the part had become the standard first assay of a generation's leading men, attempted in the same season by the future of the subsidised stage, the fringe and the open air, and remembered in each case as a beginning rather than a summit.

Mark Rylance closed the decade's account at the Swan in 1989, playing Romeo at twenty-nine opposite Georgia Slowe in Terry Hands's production, with the company's smallest Stratford room as the instrument. Rylance's gifts ran to stillness, inwardness and a conversational delivery that treated verse as thought overheard, and the Swan's scale, a few hundred seats wrapped around a thrust stage, converted those gifts into a Romeo of unusual intimacy, the lover's speeches confided rather than proclaimed. The row matters to the argument because it shows the lyric line adapting rather than dying: Gielgud's music, transposed into a minor key and a smaller room, could still fill an evening, provided the production surrendered the grandeur the music once implied. The Swan itself was part of the interpretation. The company's studio-scale house had opened in 1986, built to recover the intimacy of the early modern playhouse within the Stratford complex, and its whole repertory premise was that the classics changed character when the distance between actor and audience collapsed to a few yards. Rylance's Romeo is the premise's best evidence in the gallery: a performance that would have read as underpowered in the main house read at the Swan as confessional, the lover thinking aloud within arm's reach of the front row. Scale, the 1989 row teaches, is a casting decision like age or temperament, and a Romeo unsuited to one room may be definitive in another, a relativism the star era, with its single metropolitan standard, could not have formulated.

Between the shared Romeo of 1983 and the intimate Romeo of 1989, the decade's verdict on the part can be read plainly. The grand manner was finished; what remained was to find the scale at which each new instrument could be believed.

## The Century's Last Romeos: Maloney, Varla, Fearon and Tennant

The 1990s inherited the examination and administered it without illusion. David Leveaux's 1991 Stratford production cast Michael Maloney, in his early thirties, opposite Clare Holman, a romantic Romeo of the old school assayed in a decade fluent in irony, and Adrian Noble's 1995 production gave the part to Zubin Varla, in his mid-twenties, opposite Lucy Whybrow, with Paul Bettany among the production's Parises on his way to a screen career. Neither performance attempted to settle the role, and the survey's point is that nobody expected settlement any longer. The institutional rhythm Hall had founded now produced a Romeo every few seasons as a matter of repertoire, each assayed, reviewed and filed, the part's difficulty absorbed into the company's planning like the difficulty of a mountain in a railway timetable. The decade's context pressed on the part from outside the theatre. The play's currency among the young rose steeply in the century's last years on the strength of its screen versions, and the stage lovers of the 1990s played to houses increasingly full of spectators who had met the story first on film and arrived with a screen Romeo's face in mind. No document in the gallery records a stage actor conceding the comparison, and the survey invents none; but the institutional behaviour is legible without testimony. The RSC's Romeos of the decade were cast young, Varla in his mid-twenties and Fearon at thirty, in productions that treated pace and contemporary legibility as first virtues, as though the company were answering an audience trained elsewhere. The stage Romeo's last decade in the century was thus spent in a competition the gallery's rules exclude from its rows but cannot exclude from its explanations, against lovers who never had to survive Act 5 in one take. That the examination continued to be sat, season after season, by actors who could have taken the easier examination elsewhere, is the profession's own verdict on which of the two the part rewards.

What the decade added to the gallery was a change in who could sit the examination.

Ray Fearon's Romeo, played for the RSC in 1997 under Michael Attenborough's direction, at the Pit in London and the Swan at Stratford, opposite Zoe Waites, was cast as individual casting inside a mixed company, without factional design, and acclaimed on his merits; a later survey of the 2000 production grouped Fearon and Waites with Dench and Stride among the pairings who had captured the lovers' adolescent angst. Two years after the Romeo, Fearon played Othello at the Royal Shakespeare Theatre, the first Black actor to hold that role on the company's main stage since Paul Robeson, a fact he recorded learning only after the event. The sequence is the survey's reason for ending its institutional history here rather than with a verdict on a single performance: the examination that Irving's theatre had reserved for its proprietor, and that the early RSC had administered to its rising leading men, had by the century's end become an examination the whole profession could sit, and Fearon's Romeo, judged in its moment simply as a Romeo, is the proof that the gallery's subject had widened from how the part should be played to who would be allowed to play it.

The century's last Romeo in the gallery sat the examination in its most modern form, as a media event. Michael Boyd's Stratford production opened on 5 July 2000 with David Tennant, twenty-nine, a Scottish actor whose reputation rested on comedy, opposite Alexandra Gilbreath, with Adrian Schiller as Mercutio, in a stark staging between two curving walls that the transfer gossip nicknamed the plague production. The notices divided along the gallery's oldest fault line with an exactness that might have been staged for this survey's benefit. Ian Shuttleworth in the Financial Times heard Tennant shed the adolescent whine of the opening quickly and hit plausible stride as an impassioned but confused young man, while a hostile London notice dismissed the performance as coarse, badly spoken and awkward, the lines hurried as though to finish the play before last orders. The production itself was judged a modest success with glaring weaknesses, its lovers praised and blamed in the same paragraphs, and Tennant's Romeo went on to the Barbican in the new year with the argument unresolved.

Boyd's staging had announced its terms in advance: the director had declared his dislike of the reverential and hermetic attitude he found in the company's traditional handling of the play, and his Verona, in Tom Piper's design of two great curving walls, kept the dead present among the living, the tragedy's ghosts presiding over the scenes that made them. Inside that frame the Romeo's comedy was not an accident of Tennant's casting but a production policy, the early acts played for laughter with a persistence the Barbican notices were still remarking on months later. The policy exposed the oldest nerve in the gallery. A Romeo permitted to be funny, callow and quick is a Romeo whose first act and a half belongs to the world of Mercutio, and the examiners who marked the performance down for coarseness were, whether they knew it or not, defending the lyric settlement of 1935 against the street settlement of 1986 in a new century's dress. The examiners who marked it up heard the same performance as the part's natural gait restored. Both hearings are preserved in the record, and the survey declines to choose between them for the same reason it declined for Stride: they describe strata of one evening, and the strata are the story. A century that had opened with its greatest tragedian abused in the part closed with its newest television star divided over in the part, and the symmetry is the survey's final piece of evidence: the examination had not been passed, the examiners had not agreed on the marking scheme, and the part was exactly where Forbes-Robertson had found it, waiting for the actor who could be both of its men at once.

## Lyrical or Physical? The Verdict on a Century of Famous Romeos

The gallery's evidence can be summed as a set of rankings that refuse to merge. For completeness of the part as written, Gielgud's Romeo of 1935 stands first in the century: the only famous performance that passed the lyric examination at full length, in a text its actor had restored, while keeping enough life in the body to survive the street and the tomb. For consequence, Olivier's Romeo stands first: no other performance in the table changed what the next fifty years of actors believed the part permitted, and it did so partly by failing, on Broadway in 1940, in a way that mapped the physical method's frontier for everyone who followed. For influence on casting, Stride's Romeo stands first: after 1960, youth was evidence and age was an argument that had to be made. For revelation of the part's structure, the divided Romeo of 1983 and the near miss of 1976 stand together, the one showing that the role could survive unbundling, the other showing exactly which of its demands no intelligence can substitute for. The rankings do not merge because the part does not merge, and the survey's verdict follows: the twentieth century produced no definitive Romeo, and the absence is not a scandal of casting but a property of the writing. Shakespeare built the lover as two half-roles riveted together, a poet of the orchard and a killer of the square, and then arranged the plot so that neither half can be cut, faked or delegated. Every famous Romeo in the gallery is a treaty negotiated between the halves, and the treaties are the century's real portrait of itself, its successive beliefs about what a young man in love is made of.

The conditions of the near successes point the same way. The rows that came nearest completeness were shaped around their Romeos: Gielgud directed his own production and restored its text for his own instrument, Forbes-Robertson managed his own theatre, Branagh produced his own fringe staging, and even Olivier's sensation of 1935 happened inside a frame another man's shaping had built for him, as the catastrophe of 1940 proved by removing the frame. The company era's Romeos, by contrast, were cast into concepts designed before they arrived, and their rows are correspondingly thinner as interpretations, however strong as productions. A complete Romeo, if the part ever permits one, will likely come from the older dispensation in modern dress: an actor young enough for the evidence and trained enough for the music, given a production built around the lover rather than around a thesis, and a text cut by somebody who has marked exactly where the actor's two halves divide. The century produced every ingredient of that performance separately. It never once assembled them in the same season.

### Does the role reward youth or experience?

Experience, at a price the record itemises. The performances the file ranks highest came from actors of twenty-eight to thirty-one with the technique to survive the lyric examination, while the two youngest famous Romeos are remembered one for a failure and one for a production that used his age as its argument.

Youth supplies the evidence of desire; experience supplies the music; the part's cruelty is that it marks both papers, and the century's gallery is the record of candidates excelling in one and negotiating the other.

The asymmetry has a technical cause worth stating, because it explains why the Romeo file stays open while other roles close. A benchmark performance is usually founded on scenes: a Juliet's potion soliloquy, a Mercutio's Queen Mab, passages that can be excerpted, remembered and compared across generations until one reading owns them. Romeo's summits are transitions rather than scenes, the turn from imitation to feeling at the feast, the turn from lover to killer in the square, the long diminuendo from Mantua to the tomb, and transitions cannot be excerpted. A Romeo is therefore judged on evidence that dissolves in the remembering, an arc felt across an evening and reconstructed afterwards from impressions, which is why the notices for nearly every row in the gallery praise the passages and argue about the whole. The part is not underrated. It is under-evidenced, by the design of the writing, and fame, which feeds on evidence, attaches elsewhere.

The companion gallery, [the famous Juliets of the twentieth century](/2028/06/02/famous-juliets-twentieth-century/), tells the same century from the other side of the orchard wall, and the comparison sharpens this survey's conclusion. The Juliets of the record include performances the culture agreed to call definitive, benchmarks against which successors are measured and found promising or wanting. The Romeos include no such benchmark, only a sequence of distinguished partial accounts and one great controlled experiment that declined to crown a winner. The asymmetry is worth stating as the gallery's closing fact, because it corrects the cliche with which the survey began. The stage lover of popular imagination, sighing in tights beneath the window, is a Juliet's-eye view of the part. From the Romeo's side of the wall, the century looks different: a hundred years of gifted men discovering, in public and at the height of their powers, that the most famous lover in the language is the one role in the canon that cannot be carried by any single gift, and going back, production after production, to try the next treaty. Researchers assembling the gallery for a seminar or a lecture can lay the twenty-three rows out side by side in the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), and the shape of the argument survives the transfer to slides intact: a column of Juliets remembered as summits, a column of Romeos remembered as attempts, and between them a part that outlasted every instrument built to play it.

The boundary of the gallery deserves a final word, because it is a boundary of medium fully as much as a boundary of date. The screen Romeos of the century, from Leslie Howard in 1936, famously forty-three and borrowed from the stage's own senior ranks, to the young film lovers of the century's last decade, belong to the film articles of this series and are excluded here by design: the stage gallery's subject is what the part does to an actor across five acts in one room, unedited, and the camera's Romeo answers a different and easier examination, in takes. The Howard casting is worth its single sentence in the stage record, because it proves the age scandal was never the stage's private failing: Hollywood in 1936, free to cast any face in the world, repeated Irving's arithmetic with a lover in his forties opposite a Juliet in her thirties, and the film's Romeo survives as a performance of gentle bewilderment that the camera forgives and the stage would have exposed. The screen could protect its Romeos with editing, lighting and retakes; the gallery's men had none of those mercies, which is why their failures are more instructive and their partial successes more honourable. The stage line itself continues past the century's end, into Orlando Bloom's Broadway Romeo of 2013 and beyond, but those performances belong to another hundred years and another gallery. This one closes where it opened, with the examination unpassed and the candidates distinguished: Gielgud for the music, Olivier for the blood, Stride for the youth, McKellen for the warning, and the part itself, undefeated, for everything else.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Romeo gallery, and what does it record about each performance?

The InsightCrunch Romeo gallery is the table at the head of this article, setting the twentieth century's celebrated stage Romeos side by side. For each performance it records the actor, the production and its year, the theatre or company, the Juliet opposite him, the actor's age at the opening where the record fixes it, and the claim on which the performance's fame rests. Its purpose is comparative rather than commemorative: read down the age column and the century's casting scandal appears; read across the claims column and the part's double demand, lyric poet and street fighter in one body, explains why no row can be labelled definitive.

### Q: Why is Forbes-Robertson's Romeo placed at the head of the twentieth-century line?

Forbes-Robertson's Romeo of September 1895 stands at the head of the line because it set the terms the next century argued within. Playing the lover at forty-two under his own management at the Lyceum, opposite Mrs Patrick Campbell, he re-centred the part on its verse after decades in which scenery and spectacle had carried the production, and the notices fastened on the beauty and clarity of the speaking. The performance also functioned as an examination in public: the Romeo came first, and the Hamlet of 1897, for which Shaw's famous superlatives were written, was built on the credit it earned. The century's lyrical tradition starts from his settlement.

### Q: How old was John Gielgud when he first played Romeo in 1924?

John Gielgud was twenty years old when he first played Romeo, at the Regent Theatre in May 1924, in Barry Jackson's production opposite Gwen Ffrangcon-Davies. He remains the youngest of the century's famous Romeos, younger than John Stride in 1960 and Ralph Fiennes in 1986, and the attempt failed on the record: the production was not a success and the notices were scornful. The youth was genuine and the technique was not yet there, which is why the performance matters to the gallery. It is the control case proving that the part cannot be carried by being the right age, any more than it can be carried by seniority.

### Q: What did Ivor Brown mean by calling Gielgud's legs the most meaningless imaginable?

Ivor Brown, reviewing the twenty-year-old Gielgud's first Romeo in 1924, found the actor scant of virility and wrote that he had the most meaningless legs imaginable, a jibe at the physical side of the performance: the white tights, the stance, the absence of any bodily conviction that this was a boy who could fight a duel or scale an orchard wall. Nicholas de Jongh, revisiting the notices in his Guardian obituary of Gielgud, added the mitigations of orange make-up and a coal-black centre-parted wig. The insult survives because it named, at the very start, the weakness the lyric tradition spent the century answering: the voice was already remarkable, and the body had nothing to say.

### Q: What did Peggy Ashcroft later say about the first configuration of the 1935 production?

Peggy Ashcroft, who played Juliet throughout the 1935 New Theatre production, later held that the first configuration, Laurence Olivier's Romeo against John Gielgud's Mercutio, gave the definitive performances of those characters. Her testimony carries a weight no aggregation of notices can match, because she played the balcony scene and the tomb against both arrangements and knew where the production's peak lay from inside it. The judgement complicates the critical file, in which Gielgud was largely preferred in both roles and Olivier preferred as Mercutio to Romeo. Ashcroft's verdict stands in the record as the insider's correction: whatever the marking scheme, the first arrangement was the event.

### Q: Why did Kenneth Tynan treat the 1935 production as the type case of two acting styles?

Kenneth Tynan treated the 1935 alternation as the type case because it was the one production in which the century's two acting methods could be compared under laboratory conditions: one company, one text, one Juliet, and two men exchanging the lover and the friend while the press reported the results nightly. Tynan had not seen it; he was a schoolboy in 1935, and his profiles of the two actors, Gielgud the musician of the line and Olivier its athlete, were built from the production's record, which is itself the point. The file the alternation left behind was so complete that the next generation's most influential critic could found his whole account of Shakespearean acting styles on a production he never attended.

### Q: What did Time magazine call the 1940 Broadway production?

Time magazine called the 1940 Broadway Romeo and Juliet, directed by Laurence Olivier with himself as Romeo and Vivien Leigh as Juliet, not merely weak or spotty but calamitous. The notice has become the production's permanent record: a Juliet who looked like a poem but had no sense of poetry, a Romeo who made a handsome lover but talked as though he was brushing his teeth and conducted his courtship as though he was D'Artagnan, and a director whose greatest sin was the virtually uncut text, its length then ransomed by racing some lines and swallowing others. Olivier's own benchmark in the same notice, that since Sothern and Marlowe no pair had attempted the play with more aura, measures the height from which it fell.

### Q: Why is Paul Scofield's name attached to the 1947 production if he did not play Romeo?

Paul Scofield's name attaches to Peter Brook's 1947 Stratford production because he played Mercutio in it, at twenty-five, and his was the performance the evening is remembered by. The Romeo was Laurence Payne, a leading man in his late twenties, opposite Daphne Slater's Juliet of nineteen. The confusion is common because Scofield became the production's most famous alumnus and because Mercutio, with the Queen Mab speech and the play's most quoted curse, banks a reputation more efficiently than the lover's long arc. The record is worth stating plainly, since it demonstrates the gallery's recurring asymmetry: in production after production, from 1935 to 1947 and beyond, the friend is remembered and the lover is recalled as having been adequate.

### Q: Why does the 1958 Stratford production mark a turning point for the Memorial Theatre company?

The 1958 Stratford production, directed by Glen Byam Shaw and opened on 8 April 1958, marks a turning point because it proved the festival company could hold the play's two demands in balance from its own resources. Richard Johnson, thirty, played Romeo to Dorothy Tutin, whose twenty-eighth birthday fell on the first night, with Edward Woodward as Mercutio and designs by Motley, and the production's praised clarity was at bottom a verdict on the lover: the verse neither sung nor thrown away but thought aloud. Between Brook's youthful experiment of 1947 and Zeffirelli's naturalism of 1960, the 1958 staging showed a resident company managing the part's problem by discipline rather than by manifesto, on the eve of the RSC's foundation.

### Q: What made John Stride's Romeo different from every famous Romeo before him?

John Stride's Romeo, in Franco Zeffirelli's Old Vic production of 1960, was different because the audience was watching youth itself rather than a man's representation of it. Stride was twenty-four and Judi Dench, his Juliet, was twenty-five, in a staging that cut roughly a third of the text and rehearsed toward behaviour rather than declamation, and the balcony scene's meaning shifted from a poem about desire to an incident of it. Earlier famous Romeos, Irving and Forbes-Robertson at forty-four and forty-two, Gielgud and Olivier at thirty-one and twenty-eight, had asked to be believed as teenagers on credit. Stride asked for no credit, and after 1960 every older casting had to argue its case.

### Q: What did Kenneth Tynan claim Zeffirelli had worked in the 1960 production?

Kenneth Tynan judged that Zeffirelli had worked a miracle in the 1960 Old Vic production, turning the figures of the play into real people. The verdict mattered because Tynan was the era's most feared and followed critic and no sentimentalist about classics: his praise certified that the experiment had succeeded as acting and as event before any argument about poetry began. He singled out the fight scenes for their realism, while the guardians of the verse tradition heard poetry sacrificed to bustle and said so in the same weeks. The production's own article in this series adjudicates the quarrel as two true descriptions of different strata of one evening: the event triumphed, and the text was taxed.

### Q: Why did Peter Hall cast a man in his early twenties as the RSC's first Romeo?

Peter Hall cast Brian Murray, an actor in his early twenties, as Romeo in the Royal Shakespeare Company's founding Stratford season of 1961 because the casting was the new institution's manifesto. The company had been founded to play the classics as an ensemble of the generation the plays describe, and the lover's youth, unremarkable inside a star vehicle, became programmatic inside that design, the more so with Dorothy Tutin returning as Juliet, Ian Bannen as Mercutio and Edith Evans, in her seventies, as the Nurse. The Romeo was deliberately not the production's biggest name. Hall was announcing that under the company system the part would be an examination for the repertoire's future, not a crown for its present.

### Q: What distinguished Timothy Dalton's 1973 Romeo within the RSC succession?

Timothy Dalton's Romeo, played at twenty-seven in Terry Hands's 1973 Stratford production opposite Estelle Kohler, stands in the succession as the leading man's assay in its purest form: handsome, ardent and vocally placed, the performance of a rising actor visibly en route to the larger fame that arrived on screen in the following decade. Kohler had by then played Juliet to two different RSC Romeos, Dalton and Ian Holm, which makes the production a useful measuring point in the company's history. What distinguishes the row in the gallery is its completeness and its evaporation in equal measure. The performance pleased, convinced and left no argument behind it, demonstrating that the company system could manufacture a creditable Romeo without producing an interpretation the culture kept debating.

### Q: Why did Daniel Day-Lewis leave the RSC's 1983 Romeo tour?

Daniel Day-Lewis left the RSC's 1983 small-scale tour, in which the role of Romeo was shared between him and Simon Templeman under John Caird's direction, for the film work that made his name. The sharing itself had been born of touring practicality, and the production continued with its Romeo divided after his departure, which is why the exhibit survives in the gallery as more than a curiosity. A part that legend insists must be embodied by one charismatic actor was deliberately unbundled between two, the seams made visible, and the evening survived the operation. The episode stands as the limiting case of the survey's argument: charisma is the gallery's currency, and the 1983 tour is the one row that tried to spend none.

### Q: How did Sean Bean's Romeo embody the design of Bogdanov's 1986 production?

Sean Bean's Romeo embodied Michael Bogdanov's 1986 Stratford production because the performance and the design were the same gesture: a contemporary Verona of modern dress and street temperature, in which the lover's ardour was conducted in the idiom of the street fight rather than the duelling school. Bean, twenty-seven, opposite Niamh Cusack's Juliet, with Michael Kitchen as Mercutio and Hugh Quarshie as Tybalt, did not argue against the verse tradition from outside it; he played inside a production that had already settled the argument in favour of event, and let the verse survive at the temperature the staging set. The row demonstrates what Olivier's physical permission had become at fifty years' distance: no longer a heresy, simply the house style.

### Q: What kind of Romeo did Mark Rylance give the Swan audience in 1989?

Mark Rylance gave the Swan audience of 1989 an intimate Romeo, played at conversational pitch in the company's smallest Stratford room. Rylance, twenty-nine, opposite Georgia Slowe in Terry Hands's production, built the lover from stillness and inwardness, treating the verse as thought overheard rather than music proclaimed, and the Swan's few hundred seats wrapped around a thrust stage converted those qualities into an unusual closeness between the audience and the part. The performance matters to the gallery because it shows the lyric line adapting rather than dying after the physical revolution: Gielgud's faith that the line is the event, transposed into a minor key and a smaller room, could still fill an evening, provided the production surrendered grandeur.

### Q: Did Kenneth Branagh ever play Romeo himself?

Kenneth Branagh played Romeo at twenty-five, at the Lyric Studio in Hammersmith in 1986, in his own full-scale production opposite Samantha Bond, three decades before he directed the play at the Garrick with Richard Madden in the role. The fringe production's success founded his Renaissance Theatre Company the following year, making the performance a foundation exhibit as well as an interpretation: a young actor-manager in Forbes-Robertson's posture, staking a company on the lover's part. Branagh himself marked the symmetry when the 2016 staging came, noting that Samantha Bond had been his Juliet thirty years earlier and that the new production's Paris was her son. The gallery includes his row as the star vehicle reborn at fringe scale.

### Q: How was Ray Fearon's 1997 Romeo cast, and why does the manner of its casting matter?

Ray Fearon's Romeo for the RSC in 1997, directed by Michael Attenborough and played at the Pit and the Swan opposite Zoe Waites, was individual casting inside a mixed company, undertaken without factional design and acclaimed on its merits. The manner matters because it marks the point at which the gallery's question widened from how the part should be played to who would be allowed to play it: the examination that Irving's theatre reserved for its proprietor was now open to the whole profession on equal terms. Two years later Fearon played Othello at the Royal Shakespeare Theatre, the first Black actor in that role on the company's main stage since Paul Robeson, and the Romeo reads in retrospect as the first term of that sequence.

### Q: How old was David Tennant when he played Romeo in 2000, and who directed the production?

David Tennant was twenty-nine when he played Romeo in Michael Boyd's production for the RSC, which opened at Stratford on 5 July 2000, with Alexandra Gilbreath as Juliet and Adrian Schiller as Mercutio. Tennant's reputation at the time rested on comedy, and the casting made the century's last famous Romeo a media event as much as a theatrical one. The notices divided along the gallery's oldest line: the Financial Times heard an impassioned but confused young man once the opening whine was shed, while a hostile London notice found the performance coarse, badly spoken and awkward. The production transferred to the Barbican with the argument unresolved, closing the century's account in the part's traditional condition.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Romeo gallery to argue that the century never settled the role?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch Romeo gallery as comparative evidence, beginning from its age column and its claims column rather than from any single performance. The ages show the part resisting every casting settlement, from Gielgud's failure at twenty to Irving's at forty-four; the claims show fame attaching to half of the role at a time, music in Gielgud, blood in Olivier, youth in Stride, intellect in McKellen. Set against the companion record of the century's Juliets, where the culture agreed on benchmarks, the gallery supports the conclusion that the unsettled Romeo is a property of the writing: the lover is two half-roles riveted together, and a century of distinguished treaties between them is the proof.
