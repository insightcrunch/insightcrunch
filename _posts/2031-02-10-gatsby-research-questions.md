A good question is half the research. The research questions worth asking about Gatsby are not the ones that sound impressive in a seminar; they are the ones that point at evidence, admit more than one defensible answer, and narrow the field of inquiry to something a real investigation can actually settle. Most stalled projects in literary study fail at this first step. The researcher chooses a topic, mistakes the topic for an inquiry, and then discovers months later that there is nothing to argue, because the inquiry was never shaped to produce an argument in the first place. Framing the inquiry well does not guarantee a good project, but framing it badly guarantees a shapeless one.

The difference between a topic and a formulation is the difference between a field and a path through it. "The American Dream in Gatsby" is a field; thousands of pages have been written inside it, and a researcher who starts there will wander. "How does the sequence of revelations about Gatsby's wealth in chapters 4, 6, and 7 shape the reader's judgment of his legitimacy?" is a path; it names the evidence, names the mechanism, and implies a procedure for answering. This article is a working guide to finding such paths: what makes an inquiry researchable, which question areas the novel actually supports, how to tell a topic from an inquiry, and how to revise the weak questions that every researcher drafts first into strong ones that can carry a project.

The promise here is practical rather than inspirational. By the end, a reader should be able to take any vague Gatsby inquiry, run it through a short series of tests, and produce a revision that is specific, evidence-answerable, and genuinely open. The examples are concrete, the before and after pairs are real attempts rather than straw men, and the revision table near the end can be used as a self-check on any inquiry under consideration. A good question is half the research because it decides the other half in advance: the evidence to gather, the method to use, and the shape of the answer.

## Research Questions Worth Asking About Gatsby Begin with the Claim

A good question is half the research, and the claim earns its bluntness by surviving contact with the failures. Watch a researcher spend six months on "the role of women in Gatsby" and the autopsy will always find the same cause of death: the formulation named a theme, not an inquiry, so every reading of every female character felt relevant and none of them felt decisive. The project sprawled because the inquiry had no edges. Now watch the same researcher spend six months on "How does Fitzgerald use Daisy's reported speech, as distinct from her direct dialogue, to control what the reader can know about her intentions?" The second project has edges. It tells the researcher which passages to collect, which linguistic features to count, and what a convincing answer would look like. The first project asks for everything; the second asks for something.

The research questions worth asking about Gatsby share a family resemblance that can be stated plainly. A researchable candidate is specific, evidence-answerable, and genuinely open. Specific means the inquiry names its object tightly enough that a stranger could tell what counts as relevant and what does not. Evidence-answerable means the answer must be reachable through documents, passages, editions, archives, or data that the researcher can actually consult, rather than through speculation or private intuition. Genuinely open means the answer is not already settled by the existing scholarship and cannot be derived from a single authoritative source in an afternoon. An inquiry missing any one of the three is an inquiry that will waste someone's time, and the waste is predictable in advance.

Consider how the three marks work together on a real example. Take the weak formulation "What does the green light symbolize?" It is not specific, since it asks about the whole symbol across the whole novel without naming a mechanism or a passage. It is barely evidence-answerable, since any passage can be cited and no passage can be excluded, which means the evidence cannot discipline the answer. And it is not genuinely open, since every survey of the novel gives a serviceable answer and the scholarship has absorbed the standard readings. Now take the revision: "How does the green light's meaning shift between its appearances in chapter 1, chapter 5, and chapter 9, and what does that movement imply about the novel's treatment of desire?" The revision names three passages, which makes it specific. It is evidence-answerable, because the researcher can collate the three passages, compare their diction and narrative context, and argue from the differences. It is genuinely open, because the movement across the three appearances has no single settled reading; the inquiry admits rival answers that can be adjudicated by closer reading. The revision is not a different topic. It is the same topic with a question inside it.

The claim that a good question is half the research also explains why the other half so often goes wrong. A vague inquiry produces a vague evidence base, because nothing tells the researcher where to stop reading. An inquiry that is already settled produces a summary, because the answer is available before the research begins. A formulation that cannot be answered with evidence produces an essay of opinions, because there is nothing for the evidence to do. In each case the defect in the finished project traces back to the defect in the framing, and the framing is the cheapest part of the project to fix. An hour spent revising the inquiry saves weeks spent revising the argument, because the question is the argument in miniature.

This guide treats inquiry-framing as a craft with teachable moves rather than as a talent that some researchers have and others do not. The moves are concrete: narrowing by naming passages, by naming a mechanism, by naming a comparison, or by naming a boundary the inquiry will respect. Each move turns an unmanageable topic into a manageable inquiry, and the moves can be combined. An inquiry that names both a passage set and a comparison is usually specific enough to start work, and the work itself will refine the inquiry further. The sections that follow make each move explicit, show it applied to Gatsby, and then supply the table of weak-to-strong revisions that puts the whole craft on one page.

### How do you turn a topic into a question?

Attach an interrogative edge to the topic: name the passages, the mechanism, or the comparison the inquiry will examine, turning the topic into something evidence can answer. The test is whether a stranger could tell what you will read, what you will compare, and what would count as an answer.

A topic like "Gatsby and the American Dream" becomes a formulation when you add a verb of inquiry and a boundary, such as "Which passages attach Gatsby's dream to material objects, and what pattern emerges when those passages are read in sequence?" The example shows the edge in action: the verb of inquiry demands an explanation, and the boundary makes the explanation checkable.

The transformation from topic to inquiry is the single most consequential revision a researcher makes, and it repays close attention to mechanism. A topic is a noun phrase: "Narration in Gatsby," "Class in Gatsby," "The Jazz Age in Gatsby." A question is a noun phrase with an interrogative verb and a scope: "How does Nick's retrospective narration in the opening pages position the reader's trust before the story begins?" The interrogative verb, how, why, in what way, to what extent, does the work of demanding an explanation rather than a description. The scope, the opening pages, does the work of making the explanation checkable. Without the verb, the researcher will describe; without the scope, the researcher will describe everything.

There are four reliable narrowing moves, and each deserves a worked example. The first is narrowing by passage: choose the scenes the inquiry will live in. "Time in Gatsby" becomes "How does the clock scene in chapter 5 use stopped and restarted time to stage the novel's larger argument about the past?" The second is narrowing by mechanism: choose the device the inquiry will track. "Fitzgerald's style" becomes "How does Fitzgerald's use of color adjectives in the valley of ashes passages differ from his color vocabulary in the East Egg passages?" The third is narrowing by comparison: choose the two things the inquiry will set against each other. "Gatsby's parties" becomes "What distinguishes the narration of the first party scene in chapter 3 from the narration of the final gathering in chapter 9?" The fourth is narrowing by boundary: state what the formulation will not cover. "The American Dream in Gatsby" becomes "Within the novel's treatment of earned versus inherited wealth, which passages present Gatsby's money as legitimate and which present it as tainted?"

A useful practice task makes the moves concrete. Take three topics, "Daisy's voice," "the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg," and "Nick as narrator," and apply each of the four narrowing moves to each topic, producing twelve candidate questions in about twenty minutes. Most of the twelve will be mediocre, and that is the point: the exercise separates the moves from the inspiration, so the researcher learns that a workable inquiry is manufactured, not awaited. Then apply the three-mark test to each candidate, specific, evidence-answerable, genuinely open, and keep the two or three that survive. The survivors are rarely the cleverest; they are the ones whose evidence is reachable and whose answers are not yet known.

The complication this guide addresses honestly is the confusion of a topic with a question, and the confusion persists because topics feel productive. A topic generates reading lists, and reading lists feel like progress. A researcher can spend a month reading everything about "the Jazz Age in Gatsby" and feel busy while producing nothing arguable. The inquiry, by contrast, generates a much shorter reading list and a much longer evidence log, because the inquiry tells the researcher which documents matter. The honest advice is to distrust the comfort of the topic. If the inquiry feels vast and exciting and slightly vague, it is probably a topic wearing an inquiry's clothes, and the wardrobe change is overdue.

One more distinction protects against a common half-measure. Adding a question mark to a topic does not make it a formulation. "The American Dream in Gatsby?" is a topic with punctuation. "Gender in Gatsby?" is a topic with punctuation. The question mark promises an inquiry that the noun phrase cannot deliver, and the promise will be broken in the second month of the project when the researcher realizes that every passage is relevant and no passage is decisive. The test is not the punctuation; it is whether the sentence names something that evidence can settle. If removing the question mark leaves a noun phrase, the work of framing has not yet begun.

A five-minute autopsy diagnoses any draft inquiry. First, remove the question mark: if a noun phrase remains, it is a topic. Second, run the stranger test: if no passages can be named, it is too broad. Third, run the document test: if no reachable document could settle it, it is unanswerable. Fourth, run the afternoon test: if the scholarship already answers it, it is settled. Fifth, try writing two opposing one-paragraph answers: if only one is writable, the candidate invites no argument. Run the autopsy on "What is the American Dream in Gatsby?" and it fails at step one. Run it on the house-description revision and it passes all five. The autopsy is the whole guide on an index card, and researchers who run it habitually stop drafting topics within a month.

## What Makes a Question Researchable

A researchable inquiry is specific, evidence-answerable, and genuinely open, and each of the three marks can be tested before any research begins. Specificity is tested by the stranger test: hand the inquiry to someone who knows the novel and ask what they would read to answer it. If they can name the passages, the comparison, or the corpus, the formulation is specific enough to start. If they shrug and say "the whole book," the inquiry needs narrowing. The stranger test is brutal and fast, and it should be run on every draft candidate before the inquiry is allowed to organize a project.

Evidence-answerability is tested by the document test. For the inquiry at hand, name the documents that could answer it: which passages, which edition's variants, which archival materials, which periodical reviews, which datasets of textual features. Then ask whether those documents are reachable. An inquiry about Fitzgerald's revisions to the ending is evidence-answerable because the surviving drafts and the published texts can be collated; the 2000 Cambridge edition of Trimalchio, the early version of the novel, makes some of that evidence widely available. A formulation about what Fitzgerald "really intended" by a symbol is not evidence-answerable, because intention leaves no document that settles the matter; the drafts show what he changed, not what he meant. The document test separates questions that research can answer from questions that only speculation can address, and the separation should happen in the first week, not the first year.

Genuine openness is tested by the afternoon test. Spend an afternoon with the standard scholarship on the inquiry. If a settled answer emerges, one that the field accepts and that no new evidence is likely to disturb, the candidate is closed and should be abandoned or reframed. If instead the afternoon surfaces disagreement, partial answers, or silence where an answer should be, the inquiry is open. The afternoon test requires honest reading: the researcher must actually look, not assume the inquiry is fresh because it feels fresh. Many questions that feel original are answered in a footnote of a study the researcher has not yet read, and discovering that in week one is a gift.

The three marks interact, and the interactions produce the most useful diagnostics. An inquiry can be specific and evidence-answerable but closed: "In which chapter does Gatsby first appear by name?" Names a passage set, answerable from the text, but settled in seconds; it is a quiz question, not a research formulation. An inquiry can be specific and open but not evidence-answerable: "What did Fitzgerald privately believe about the reality of the American Dream?" Names an object, admits debate, but no document records the private belief; it invites biography-as-mind-reading. A candidate can be evidence-answerable and open but not specific: "How does language work in Gatsby?" The evidence exists and the matter is debated, but the inquiry names no boundary, so the researcher will drown. The diagnostic value is that each failure suggests its own repair: the closed inquiry needs a why or a how; the unanswerable inquiry needs a document; the vague formulation needs a boundary.

There is a fourth quality worth naming, though it is not a mark of researchability in the strict sense: fertility. A fertile inquiry generates follow-up questions, and the follow-ups are often where the real project lives. "How does the clock scene in chapter 5 use stopped and restarted time?" is researchable; the follow-up, "Does the novel's treatment of time change after that scene, and if so, where is the change visible?" is the dissertation chapter. Fertility cannot be tested in advance with the reliability of the three marks, but it can be sensed: if a candidate immediately suggests the next inquiry, it is probably pointed at a live part of the novel. The three marks decide whether the inquiry can be answered; fertility decides whether the answer will matter.

The most common way an inquiry fails the three-mark test is not dramatic. It fails by being almost right: specific enough to sound precise, open enough to sound debatable, but aimed at evidence that does not quite exist. Consider "How did the first reviewers of the novel understand the green light?" The formulation is specific, it names a corpus, the early reviews; it is open, since the reception of that symbol in the first reviews is not a settled matter; but it is only weakly evidence-answerable, because early reviews may not discuss the green light at all, and the absence of discussion is itself ambiguous evidence. The repair is to widen the corpus slightly or to reframe around what the reviews do discuss: "What symbolic vocabulary do the earliest reviews of the novel use, and how does it compare with the vocabulary of later criticism?" The revised inquiry keeps the historical curiosity and aims it at evidence that exists.

A sample research plan shows how the three marks organize real work. Suppose the candidate is: "How does Fitzgerald's color vocabulary in the valley of ashes passages differ from his color vocabulary in the East Egg passages, and what does the difference do?" Week one: define the passage sets, listing the exact pages in the chosen edition and justifying the boundaries. Week two: extract every color adjective in each set into a simple table, noting the noun each modifies. Weeks three and four: analyze the patterns, asking which colors cluster where, which nouns they attach to, and what work the clustering does in context. Week five: check the pattern against a control, such as the West Egg passages, to see whether the difference is really about the two places or about something else. Week six: draft the argument, letting the table do the evidentiary work. The plan is possible only because the inquiry named its evidence in advance. A vaguer inquiry, "How does Fitzgerald use color?" generates no such plan, because there is no passage set to define and no comparison to run.

A filled-in worksheet shows what the testing stage looks like on paper. Candidate one: "How does the novel's treatment of Gatsby's parties change across chapters 3, 4, and 9?" Stranger test: the stranger names the three party scenes without hesitation, so specificity passes. Document test: the documents are the three scenes in the chosen edition, all reachable, so evidence-answerability passes. Afternoon test: the scholarship discusses the parties at length but rarely tracks the narration's changing distance across exactly these three scenes, so openness passes with a note to verify. Verdict: keep and freeze for the term. Candidate two: "What do the parties symbolize?" Stranger test: the stranger shrugs, since symbolism here could mean anything, so specificity fails at once. The worksheet takes ten minutes per candidate and the verdicts are written in ink. The researcher who tests four candidates on paper in an hour has done more framing work than the researcher who polishes one untested inquiry for a week.

The honest complication deserves restating at the level of practice: confusing a topic with a question is not a beginner's error that experts outgrow. It is a standing temptation, because topics are where the excitement lives and questions are where the discipline lives. Every researcher, at every level, drafts topic-shaped questions first; the difference between a stalled project and a moving one is whether the researcher runs the revision. The three-mark test is the revision made mechanical, and mechanics are what survive the excitement. A good question is half the research, and the test is how the half gets built.

## Question Areas in Textual, Historical, and Theoretical Study

The research questions worth asking about Gatsby fall into three broad areas, and each area has its own characteristic evidence, its own characteristic moves, and its own characteristic failures. Textual questions ask about the novel's language, structure, and narration, and their evidence is the text itself, in one or more editions. Historical questions ask about the world around the novel, its composition, its first reception, its social context, and their evidence is documents outside the novel: drafts, letters, reviews, periodicals, records. Theoretical questions ask what happens when a framework, feminist, Marxist, psychoanalytic, or otherwise, is brought to the novel, and their evidence is the friction between the framework's predictions and the text's resistances. The areas overlap in practice, but an inquiry should know which area it primarily inhabits, because the area decides what counts as evidence and what counts as an answer.

### What does a researchable textual question look like?

It names specific passages and one textual feature, then asks what work that feature does. The evidence is the text itself, the method is close reading across the passage set, and the answer stays open because the passages sustain rival readings.

Consider the example: "How does Nick's use of the second person in the novel's closing pages reposition the reader's relationship to Gatsby's dream?" It names the feature, the second person; names the passage set, the closing pages; and asks about the work the feature does, repositioning the reader. The closing pages sustain rival readings of whom the "you" addresses and to what end, which is what keeps the inquiry open.

The textual area is the most forgiving for a new researcher and the easiest to get wrong by staying too broad. Its characteristic failure is the feature without a passage set: "How does Fitzgerald use imagery?" The repair is always the same, attach the feature to a bounded set of passages and ask about the difference the feature makes there. Consider a worked example in full. Weak: "What is the role of weather in Gatsby?" Strong: "How does the weather in the Plaza Hotel confrontation scene in chapter 7 interact with the scene's dialogue to escalate the conflict, and does the novel use weather this way anywhere else?" The strong version names the scene, names the mechanism, interaction between weather description and dialogue pacing, and adds a control inquiry, the "anywhere else" clause, which forces the researcher to check whether the effect is local or systematic. That control clause is a small device with large consequences: it turns a reading of one scene into a claim about the novel, and it tells the researcher exactly what to read next.

Textual questions also include the editorial and manuscript dimension, where the evidence is not one text but several. "How do the surviving drafts of the novel's ending differ from the published ending in their treatment of Gatsby's dream, and what do the differences suggest about Fitzgerald's late revisions?" is evidence-answerable because the draft materials exist and the Trimalchio edition makes an early version accessible; it is specific because it names the ending and the feature, the treatment of the dream; and it is open because the late revisions admit competing explanations. A researcher pursuing this inquiry learns collation, the careful comparison of variant texts, which is a transferable skill: the same method answers questions about any passage with a draft history. The edition matters here, and the formulation should name it. The Cambridge edition of the novel, edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli and published in 1991, provides the textual apparatus that makes variant-aware questions answerable; an inquiry that depends on wording should always specify which text it is reading.

A realistic practice task for the textual area: choose one recurring word in the novel, "careless," "vast," "orgastic," or another that catches the eye, and build a concordance by hand. List every occurrence with its sentence, note the speaker or narrative context of each, and then draft three questions the pattern suggests. One of the three will usually be researchable, one will be too broad, and one will be closed; the exercise is to sort them using the three-mark test and to say aloud why each verdict holds. The hand-built concordance matters because it forces slowness: the researcher sees the contexts rather than the counts, and the questions that emerge are grounded in the text's actual behavior rather than in a theme's prestige.

### What does a researchable historical question look like?

It names a corpus of documents outside the novel and asks what those documents show about the novel's making, its first readers, or its world. The evidence is the document set, the method is historical or reception analysis, and the answer stays open because the documents rarely speak with one voice.

Consider the example: "How did the novel's earliest reviewers describe Gatsby's wealth, and what does their vocabulary reveal about what the 1925 audience found plausible or implausible in his rise?" It names the corpus, the earliest reviews; names the feature, the vocabulary used for the wealth; and asks what the feature reveals, the audience's sense of plausibility. The early reception of the novel's social vision is not a settled matter, which is what keeps the candidate open.

The historical area's characteristic failure is the context without a corpus: "How does the novel reflect the Jazz Age?" The Jazz Age is a topic, not a document set, and the inquiry as stated lets the researcher cite anything. The repair is to name the documents: "Which specific features of 1920s Long Island social life, as documented in the period's newspapers and social registers, correspond to the novel's depiction of East Egg and West Egg, and where does the novel depart from the record?" The revised inquiry is harder and better: harder because it requires real archival work, better because the departures from the record are where the interpretive payoff lives. A historical inquiry earns its keep when the documents push back against the novel, or the novel pushes back against the documents.

Composition history is the most evidence-rich corner of the historical area. "What do Fitzgerald's revisions between the draft stages reveal about the changing function of Nick Carraway as a narrator?" is answerable because the drafts survive in archives and in published form; it is specific because it names the figure and the function; and it is open because the standard account of Nick's development leaves room for revision. The researcher pursuing this formulation works with manuscript evidence, learns to date undated drafts by internal evidence, and builds an argument from the sequence of changes rather than from any single version. The sequence is the argument: what Fitzgerald changed, and when, shows what problem he was solving.

A second worked example shows the historical area at a different scale. Weak: "Was Prohibition important in the novel?" The inquiry is closed, since the answer is obviously yes, and it names no corpus. Strong: "How do the novel's references to Gatsby's business dealings map onto the documented structure of 1920s bootlegging operations, and where does the novel simplify or romanticize the trade?" The strong version names two corpora, the novel's references and the documented structure of the trade, and asks about the mapping between them, with the departures doing the interpretive work. The researcher reads the business passages alongside historical accounts of the liquor trade and argues from the comparison. The candidate is genuinely open because the degree and purpose of the simplification are debatable, and it is evidence-answerable because both corpora exist.

The honest warning for the historical area is about the archive's silence. Documents do not always answer the inquiry asked of them, and the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A researcher who asks "How did Fitzgerald's contemporaries read the valley of ashes?" and finds no contemporary discussion of that image has not answered the inquiry; the researcher has discovered that the inquiry needs reframing around what contemporaries did discuss. The revision is part of the method. Historical questions should be drafted with a fallback corpus in mind, so that a silent archive redirects the inquiry rather than ending it.

### What does a researchable theoretical question look like?

It names a framework, applies it to a bounded part of the novel, and asks what the application reveals about both the novel and the framework. The evidence is the friction between the framework's predictions and the text's resistances, and the answer stays open because the resistances admit rival interpretations.

Consider the example: "What happens to a Marxist reading of the novel's parties when the reading is forced to account for the servants, the musicians, and the other laboring figures the narration keeps at the edge of the frame?" It names the framework, Marxist; names the bounded site, the parties with attention to their margins; and demands a two-way result, what the framework shows about the novel and what the novel's resistances show about the framework. The margin figures have not been fully theorized, which is what keeps the formulation open.

The theoretical area's characteristic failure is the application without resistance: "How can Gatsby be read through a feminist lens?" The inquiry invites the researcher to march the framework through the novel collecting confirmations, and the result is an exercise, not an inquiry. The repair is to build the resistance into the candidate from the start. "Where does a feminist reading of Daisy Buchanan encounter passages that resist it, and what do those resistances suggest about the limits of the framework?" The revised inquiry is researchable because the resistances are specific passages that can be named and argued over; it is honest because it treats the framework as a hypothesis rather than a verdict. The best theoretical questions are the ones the framework might lose, because a framework that cannot lose is not being tested.

A worked example in the psychoanalytic corner shows the same structure. Weak: "What does psychoanalysis say about Gatsby?" The inquiry is a topic with a question mark, and it invites summary rather than argument. Strong: "How does Gatsby's repeated return to the past in chapters 5 through 8 conform to and depart from the psychoanalytic account of repetition, and what do the departures imply about the novel's own theory of memory?" The strong version names the passage range, names the concept, repetition, and asks about both conformity and departure, with the departures carrying the originality. The researcher must know the concept well enough to recognize a departure, which is why theoretical questions demand real study of the theory, not just its slogans. A framework used at the level of slogans produces slogan-shaped answers.

The theoretical area also supports questions about the frameworks themselves as historical phenomena. "How did the dominant critical frameworks applied to the novel change between the mid-century formalist readings and the later historicist readings, and what does the change reveal about the profession's shifting questions?" This is a theoretical inquiry at one remove: its object is the criticism rather than the novel, its corpus is the body of published readings, and its payoff is an understanding of how questions themselves have histories. A researcher who sees that frameworks change will be less tempted to treat the current framework as the final one, and more attentive to what the next framework might notice.

A practice task for the theoretical area: choose one framework and write its three strongest predictions about a single scene, stated as falsifiable claims about what the scene must show. Then read the scene slowly and mark each prediction as confirmed, complicated, or contradicted, with the passage cited for each verdict. The contradicted and complicated predictions are the research formulation trying to be born: "Why does the framework fail here, and what does the failure reveal?" The exercise teaches the central discipline of theoretical work, which is letting the text win arguments against the theory. A good question is half the research, and in the theoretical area the good question is usually the one the theory cannot quite answer.

## How to Revise Weak Questions into Strong Ones

Every strong research candidate begins as a weak one. The researcher drafts something broad, something settled, or something unanswerable, and then revises it, often more than once, until the three marks hold. The revision is not a sign of failure; it is the method. What follows is a revision clinic: five weak questions walked through the full diagnostic, each with the failure named, the repair explained, and the strong revision stated. The patterns repeat across projects, so learning them on these five examples transfers to any inquiry a researcher will draft next.

The first patient is the broad inquiry: "What is the American Dream in The Great Gatsby?" The failure is scope. The inquiry asks about a theme across the entire novel, which means every passage is relevant and no passage is decisive. A researcher who starts here will read everything and argue nothing, because the formulation never says what would count as an answer. The repair is narrowing by mechanism and passage: choose one way the dream appears and one place to watch it. The revision: "How do the novel's descriptions of Gatsby's house in chapters 3 and 5 attach his dream to specific material objects, and what pattern emerges when those descriptions are read in sequence?" The revision names the mechanism, description of the house attaching the dream to objects; names the passage set, chapters 3 and 5; and asks about a pattern, which is something evidence can show. The topic is unchanged. The inquiry is new.

The second patient is the settled candidate: "What does the green light symbolize?" The failure is openness. The standard answers are absorbed into every survey of the novel, which means the researcher who pursues this inquiry will spend months rediscovering what the field already knows. The repair is movement: ask not what the symbol means but how its meaning changes. The revision: "How does the green light's significance shift between chapter 1, chapter 5, and chapter 9, and what does that movement imply about the novel's treatment of desire?" The three appearances give the researcher a defined corpus, the shift gives the researcher something to argue about, and the implication clause connects the local observation to the novel's larger concerns. A settled symbol becomes an open inquiry the moment the inquiry moves from meaning to change.

The third patient is the unanswerable inquiry: "What did Fitzgerald really intend by the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg?" The failure is evidence. Intention is a private mental state, and no document records it; the drafts show what Fitzgerald changed, not what he meant. A researcher who pursues intention will end in speculation, because there is nothing for the evidence to do. The repair is to aim the curiosity at documents that exist. The revision: "How do the surviving drafts and the published text differ in their presentation of the Eckleburg billboard, and what do the differences suggest about Fitzgerald's late revisions to the novel's symbolic economy?" The curiosity about the symbol's purpose survives, but it is now aimed at the revision sequence, which is a document the researcher can actually consult. The formulation trades the unanswerable "what did he mean" for the answerable "what did he change."

The fourth patient is the summary inquiry: "Why is Gatsby so wealthy?" The failure is subtle, because the candidate looks specific. It names an object, the wealth, and asks why. But the answer is plot summary: the novel explains the wealth through Gatsby's own account and through the business dealings the narration reveals. A researcher who pursues this inquiry will produce a paragraph of summary and then stop, because there is nothing to argue. The repair is to ask about the telling rather than the told: how the novel sequences and frames the revelations. The revision: "How does the novel's sequencing of revelations about Gatsby's wealth across chapters 4, 6, and 7 shape the reader's judgment of his legitimacy?" The wealth itself is settled; the reader's judgment is not, and the sequencing is the mechanism the researcher can analyze. Summary questions become research questions when the inquiry moves from what happened to how the telling shapes what the reader thinks.

The fifth patient is the moral-verdict inquiry: "Is Daisy Buchanan a bad person?" The failure is the evidence standard. Moral verdicts on fictional characters have no agreed procedure: which passages count, how they are weighted, and what "bad" means are all undefended. The researcher will collect impressions and call them an argument. The repair is to convert the moral curiosity into a textual mechanism. The revision: "What does Daisy's language in the Plaza Hotel confrontation reveal about how the novel distributes responsibility for its carelessness, and how does her diction there compare with her diction in the earlier reunion scene?" The moral inquiry about Daisy becomes a formulation about distribution, diction, and comparison, each of which has an evidence procedure. The researcher who wanted to judge Daisy can now argue about her, which is better.

These five revisions share a structure worth naming. Each one keeps the researcher's original curiosity and changes the object of the curiosity from something evidence cannot touch to something evidence can settle. The broad inquiry keeps its theme but gains a passage set. The settled candidate keeps its symbol but gains a movement. The unanswerable inquiry keeps its curiosity about purpose but gains a document. The summary inquiry keeps its plot interest but gains a mechanism. The moral inquiry keeps its character interest but gains a comparison. Revision is not abandonment; it is redirection, and the redirection always moves toward the evidence.

The table below puts the clinic's logic on one page. Each row pairs a weak formulation with the failure it exemplifies and a strong revision, so a researcher can match a draft inquiry to its nearest neighbor and borrow the repair.

| Weak question | Why it fails | Strong researchable revision |
|---|---|---|
| What is The Great Gatsby about? | Too broad; a summary request with no argumentative edge and no evidence that would settle it. | How does Nick Carraway's retrospective narration in chapters 1 and 9 frame Gatsby's parties as evidence of, rather than background to, his reinvention? |
| What does the green light symbolize? | Settled at the survey level; every standard reading is absorbed into the existing scholarship. | How does the green light's significance shift between chapters 1, 5, and 9, and what does that movement imply about the novel's treatment of desire? |
| What did Fitzgerald intend by the Eckleburg billboard? | Unanswerable; private intention leaves no document, so the inquiry ends in speculation. | How do the surviving drafts and the published text differ in presenting the billboard, and what do the differences suggest about the late revisions? |
| Why is Gatsby so wealthy? | Answerable by plot summary; the novel explains the wealth, leaving nothing to argue. | How does the sequencing of revelations about the wealth in chapters 4, 6, and 7 shape the reader's judgment of Gatsby's legitimacy? |
| Is Daisy Buchanan a bad person? | No evidence standard; moral verdicts on fictional figures have no agreed procedure. | What does Daisy's language in the Plaza Hotel confrontation reveal about how the novel distributes responsibility for its carelessness? |
| How does Fitzgerald use symbolism? | Unbounded; covers the whole novel with no passage set, mechanism, or comparison. | How does the color vocabulary of the valley of ashes passages differ from that of the East Egg passages, and what does the difference do? |
| What would Gatsby think of the later twentieth century? | Unanswerable; speculation about a fictional figure outside the text has no evidence. | What evidence in chapters 8 and 9 supports or resists the reading that Gatsby dies still believing in the dream? |
| Is the American Dream dead in the novel? | Broad and settled; a slogan rather than an inquiry, with no boundary and no document set. | Which passages attach Gatsby's dream to material objects, and what pattern emerges when those passages are read in sequence? |

A researcher using the table should treat it as a diagnostic instrument, not a menu. The procedure is to find the row whose failure matches the draft candidate's failure, study the repair that row performs, and then apply the same repair to the draft. The broad draft gets a passage set and a mechanism. The settled draft gets a movement or a comparison. The unanswerable draft gets a document. The summary draft gets a telling-mechanism. The moral draft gets a textual procedure. After one or two passes through the table, the researcher will start performing the repairs without it, which is the point: the table is training wheels, and the balance it teaches is the three-mark test applied by habit.

One caution about the table: a strong revision is a starting point, not a finished inquiry. The revisions above are researchable, but each one will change as the researcher meets the evidence. The color-vocabulary inquiry may discover that the real contrast is not between the valley and East Egg but between narration and dialogue; the sequencing inquiry may discover that chapter 6 matters less than expected and chapter 8 matters more. The formulation is allowed to move. What the revision provides is not a final form but a form good enough to begin, and beginning is what the weak inquiry could never do.

### How do you keep a research question from drifting during a project?

Write the candidate in its current form at the start of every work session and check whether the day's reading still serves it. If a fascinating passage does not serve the inquiry, note it in a separate later file and return to the inquiry. The inquiry is the anchor; the later file honors the curiosity without letting it steer.

Drift is the slow widening of the inquiry: the formulation about the Plaza confrontation quietly becomes an inquiry about Daisy, then about women in the novel, then about gender in the 1920s. Each widening feels like following the evidence, but the widening is usually following the excitement. The discipline is to let the anchor hold while the later file collects the temptations.

Drift has legitimate and illegitimate forms, and the researcher must learn to tell them apart. Legitimate drift is evidence-driven: the color-vocabulary candidate discovers that the dialogue-narration contrast matters more than the place contrast, and the inquiry is revised to follow the finding. Illegitimate drift is excitement-driven: the researcher reads a brilliant essay on something adjacent and the inquiry stretches to include it. The test is the direction of the movement. If the movement goes toward the evidence the inquiry named, it is revision. If the movement goes toward evidence the formulation never named, it is drift, and the inquiry should be restored. A research journal that records each revision with its reason makes the distinction visible; without the record, every drift feels like a discovery.

The practical discipline is a monthly candidate audit, and it takes twenty minutes. The researcher writes the current inquiry at the top of a page, lists the evidence gathered that month underneath, and draws lines connecting each item of evidence to the part of the inquiry it serves. Evidence that serves no part is either the seed of a new inquiry, to be filed in the later document, or a sign that the month's reading wandered. Then the researcher asks the three-mark test of the current formulation once more: still specific, still evidence-answerable, still open. Questions that pass the audit three months running are usually ready to become arguments. Questions that keep changing are telling the researcher something, and the something is usually that the original curiosity was a topic, not an inquiry, and the framing work was never finished.

## The Question's Working Life: From Draft to Final Form

A research inquiry is not written once; it is drafted, tested, revised, and eventually frozen, and each stage has its own discipline. The drafting stage is generative: the researcher produces candidates quickly, using the narrowing moves, without judging them. The testing stage is adversarial: each candidate faces the stranger test, the document test, and the afternoon test, and most candidates die. The revision stage is surgical: the survivors are narrowed, aimed at documents, and opened up until the three marks hold. The freezing stage is managerial: the researcher commits to one formulation for a defined period, usually a term, and allows only evidence-driven revisions. Skipping a stage is the usual failure. The researcher who drafts and freezes without testing commits to an untested inquiry; the researcher who tests without freezing never begins.

The drafting stage deserves a concrete method, because vague advice to "brainstorm" produces vague questions. The method is the twelve-candidate exercise: take a topic, apply the four narrowing moves, passage, mechanism, comparison, boundary, and produce three candidates per move. Twelve questions in thirty minutes. The speed matters, because it prevents the researcher from falling in love with the first candidate. Then the researcher ranks the twelve by a single criterion, which evidence is reachable this term, and keeps the top four. The other eight go into the later file. The exercise can be repeated with a different topic the next day, and after a week the researcher has a portfolio of candidates instead of a single fragile hope.

The testing stage is where the three-mark test earns its keep, and it should be done on paper, not in the head. For each surviving candidate, the researcher writes three lines: the passages or documents the inquiry names, the documents that could answer it and whether they are reachable, and the result of the afternoon with the scholarship. The written form matters because the head is generous to its own questions; the paper is not. A candidate that cannot name its documents on paper cannot name them in a proposal. A candidate whose afternoon test reveals a settled answer is dead, and the researcher should say so aloud: "This formulation is closed." The sentence is a small ritual, and it frees the researcher to work on the living ones.

A second sample research plan, this time for a historical inquiry, shows how the working life proceeds after freezing. The candidate: "How did the novel's earliest reviewers describe Gatsby's wealth, and what does their vocabulary reveal about what the 1925 audience found plausible?" Month one: assemble the corpus. The researcher locates the reviews published in the months after the novel's appearance, using periodical indexes and digital archives, and defines the corpus boundary explicitly, for example, reviews published within one year of publication in general-interest periodicals. Month two: extract every passage in the corpus that discusses the wealth, the parties, or Gatsby's origins, and build a table of the descriptive vocabulary. Month three: analyze the vocabulary for patterns, asking which words recur, which moral judgments attach to the wealth, and what the reviewers take for granted. Month four: compare the review vocabulary with the novel's own vocabulary for the wealth, asking where the reviewers' language matches the novel's and where it diverges. Month five: draft the argument about plausibility, letting the divergence do the work. The plan is possible because the frozen inquiry named its corpus in advance; a drifting inquiry would still be assembling corpora in month four.

The revision stage has its own characteristic moment, the evidence surprise, and the researcher should plan for it rather than fear it. The surprise is the discovery that the evidence does not behave as the inquiry expected: the color formulation finds the dialogue-narration contrast, the review inquiry finds that the earliest reviewers barely mention the wealth, the draft candidate finds that the most interesting revision is in a chapter nobody discusses. The correct response is to revise the inquiry toward the surprise, not away from it. The surprise is the research telling the researcher what it is actually about, and the questions that follow surprises are usually better than the questions that preceded them. The wrong response is to force the evidence back into the original formulation, which produces the strained readings that committees notice and distrust.

The freezing stage is the least glamorous and the most important for finishing. A frozen inquiry is one the researcher agrees not to change for a defined period except in response to evidence. The freeze is what makes the evidence log coherent: every item in the log serves the same inquiry, so the log accumulates instead of scattering. The freeze is also what makes the writing possible, because an argument is a frozen formulation with its answer attached. Researchers who never freeze have evidence logs that read like commonplace books, interesting and useless. The freeze can be lifted, and usually is, when the evidence demands it; but the lifting should be a decision, recorded in the journal with its reason, not a drift.

A practice task ties the working life together. Take one candidate inquiry and carry it through all four stages in a single week. Monday: draft twelve candidates from a topic and keep four. Tuesday: test the four on paper and keep two. Wednesday: revise the two until the three marks hold. Thursday: freeze one and write the one-page plan, naming the evidence, the method, and the first three tasks. Friday: do the first task, which is always the same, read the named passages slowly with the candidate in mind, and write down what the inquiry missed. The Friday reading is the beginning of the real research, and it is also the first evidence surprise. A researcher who runs this week once will never again mistake a topic for an inquiry, because the researcher will have felt the difference in the hands.

The freeze is not permanent, and the researcher should plan its thaw. A frozen inquiry typically holds for one term of steady work; at the term's end, the researcher runs the audit once more and decides whether to re-freeze, revise, or replace. Re-freeze when the evidence accumulated cleanly and the argument is drafting itself. Revise when the evidence surprised but the curiosity held, following the surprise toward the better formulation. Replace when the audits show the inquiry was a topic in disguise, which the researcher now recognizes because the framing habit has formed. The thaw is scheduled, not drifted into: put the term-end review on the calendar at the freeze date, so the candidate's working life has a rhythm the researcher controls rather than a sprawl the researcher discovers.

## Common Question Shapes and When to Use Each

Research questions come in a small number of shapes, and each shape suits a different kind of curiosity. Learning the shapes gives the researcher a repertoire: when the curiosity is about difference, reach for the comparison shape; when the curiosity is about change, reach for the movement shape; when the curiosity is about cause, reach for the mechanism shape. The shape is not decoration. It determines what evidence the inquiry needs and what an answer looks like, which is why choosing the shape deliberately is part of framing.

The comparison shape asks how two things differ and what the difference means. "How does the narration of the first party scene differ from the narration of the final gathering, and what does the difference imply?" The shape suits curiosities about contrast: East Egg versus West Egg, early chapters versus late chapters, dialogue versus narration, the novel versus its drafts. The evidence procedure is built in: collect the two sets, compare them feature by feature, and argue from the pattern of differences. The shape's characteristic failure is the unbalanced comparison, where one side gets a chapter and the other gets a paragraph; the repair is to give each side equal evidentiary weight, which usually means narrowing both sides to matched passage sets. The comparison shape is the most reliable shape for a new researcher, because the procedure is mechanical and the differences are checkable.

The movement shape asks how something changes across the novel and what the change signifies. "How does the green light's significance shift between chapters 1, 5, and 9?" The shape suits curiosities about development: a symbol's changing meaning, a character's shifting language, the narration's evolving reliability. The evidence procedure is sequential: establish the state at each point, describe the change, and argue about its significance. The shape's characteristic failure is the asserted arc, where the researcher claims a change without establishing the starting state; the repair is to document each point fully before arguing about the movement, which means the inquiry needs at least three points to show a pattern rather than a fluctuation. The movement shape is powerful because change is inherently argumentative: to claim that something changed is to claim something about the novel's design.

The mechanism shape asks how a textual device produces an effect. "How does Fitzgerald's color vocabulary in the valley passages produce the sense of desolation?" The shape suits curiosities about craft: how the narration creates suspense, how the dialogue characterizes, how the imagery persuades. The evidence procedure is analytic: identify the device's components, show them operating in the passages, and connect the operation to the effect. The shape's characteristic failure is the effect without a mechanism, where the researcher describes the desolation without showing how the language makes it; the repair is to name the linguistic features explicitly, the adjectives, the nouns they modify, the syntactic patterns, and to argue from the features rather than the impression. The mechanism shape is the most demanding, because it requires the researcher to be precise about language, but it is also the most rewarding, because its answers are the most checkable.

The distribution shape asks where something appears and what the pattern of appearances means. "Which passages attach Gatsby's dream to material objects, and what pattern emerges?" The shape suits curiosities about recurrence: where a word clusters, which characters get interiority, when the narration withholds information. The evidence procedure is survey-like: define the corpus, find every instance, tabulate, and argue from the distribution. The shape's characteristic failure is the cherry-picked instance, where the researcher argues from two examples while ignoring the other twenty; the repair is comprehensiveness within the defined corpus, which is why the shape requires the corpus boundary to be stated in advance. The distribution shape teaches the most important habit in textual research, which is looking at all the evidence, not just the convenient evidence.

The reception shape asks how readers at a particular time understood something and what their understanding reveals. "How did the earliest reviewers describe Gatsby's wealth?" The shape suits curiosities about the novel's life in the world: first reception, changing reputation, the history of a critical debate. The evidence procedure is documentary: assemble the corpus of responses, analyze their vocabulary and judgments, and argue about what the pattern shows. The shape's characteristic failure is the anachronistic corpus, where the researcher mixes reviews from different decades and treats them as one audience; the repair is period discipline, defining the corpus by date and reading it as a historical artifact. The reception shape connects the novel to its world, and it is the shape most likely to require real archival work.

The resistance shape asks where a framework or a standard reading breaks down and what the breakdown reveals. "Where does a feminist reading of Daisy encounter passages that resist it?" The shape suits curiosities about limits: the limits of a theory, the limits of a consensus, the passages that will not sit still. The evidence procedure is adversarial: state the framework's predictions, find the passages that complicate them, and argue from the friction. The shape's characteristic failure is the straw framework, where the researcher invents a weak version of the theory in order to defeat it; the repair is steelmanning, stating the framework at its strongest before showing where the text pushes back. The resistance shape is the most intellectually honest, because it builds the objection into the formulation, and it is often where originality lives.

A worked choice shows the repertoire in use. The curiosity: the researcher notices that Nick withholds the full account of Gatsby's criminal connections until late in the novel while hinting at them earlier, and wonders what the withholding does. The distribution shape suggests itself first, which passages hint and which reveal, but the mechanism shape is stronger, because the curiosity is about an effect, suspense and retrospective judgment, produced by a device, the timing of disclosure. The framed inquiry: "How does the timing of disclosures about Gatsby's business across chapters 4, 6, and 7 produce the reader's shifting judgment of his legitimacy?" The procedure follows from the shape: reconstruct what the reader knows at each point, mark each disclosure against the chronology, and argue from the gap between knowledge and telling. Choosing the shape first gave the curiosity its procedure in one move.

A researcher choosing among the shapes should match the shape to the curiosity, not to fashion. If the curiosity is "these two scenes feel different," the comparison shape is waiting. If the curiosity is "this symbol means something different at the end," the movement shape is waiting. If the curiosity is "how does this passage do what it does," the mechanism shape is waiting. If the curiosity is "this keeps happening," the distribution shape is waiting. If the curiosity is "did readers always see it this way," the reception shape is waiting. If the curiosity is "the standard reading cannot handle this passage," the resistance shape is waiting. The curiosity comes first; the shape gives it a procedure. A good question is half the research, and the shape is the candidate's skeleton: choose it deliberately, and the flesh of the argument will have something to hang on.

## Three Questions, Built from Draft to Plan

The craft this guide teaches is best seen in complete runs, from the first vague draft through the tests, the revision, and the research plan. What follows are three such runs, each starting where researchers actually start, with a curiosity that is not yet an inquiry, and each ending with a frozen inquiry and a plan for answering it. The runs are deliberately varied: one textual, one historical, one theoretical, so the reader sees the craft working across the question areas.

The first run begins with a textual curiosity: the researcher keeps noticing the word "careless" and its variants, and wonders what Fitzgerald is doing with it. The first draft is "What does carelessness mean in Gatsby?" The stranger test kills it immediately: asked what to read, the stranger says "the whole book." The document test is inconclusive: the evidence exists, but the formulation names no boundary. The afternoon test suggests the inquiry is half-closed: the standard readings of the novel's carelessness are well absorbed. The diagnosis is the broad candidate, and the repair is the distribution shape with a mechanism clause. The researcher builds a hand concordance of every occurrence of "careless," "carelessness," and "carelessly," noting the speaker and context of each, and the pattern that emerges is the revision's engine: the word clusters around the Buchanans, but it also appears in Nick's narration at moments of judgment, and the two uses do different work. The revised inquiry: "How does the distribution of 'careless' and its variants across characters and narration shape the novel's assignment of moral responsibility, and where does the pattern complicate the standard reading of the Buchanans as simply careless?" The inquiry is specific, it names the word set and the distribution; evidence-answerable, the concordance is buildable from the text; and genuinely open, because the complication clause points at the instances that resist the standard reading. The plan: week one, build the concordance from the chosen edition; week two, tabulate speaker, context, and immediate verbal environment for each instance; week three, compare the Buchanan-attached instances with the narration-attached instances; week four, draft the argument about distribution and responsibility. The curiosity about a single word has become a four-week project with a checkable answer.

The second run begins with a historical curiosity: the researcher wonders how the novel's first readers reacted to the valley of ashes, that strange wasteland between the Eggs and the city. The first draft is "How did early readers understand the valley of ashes?" The stranger test passes weakly: the stranger can name the corpus, the early reviews, but cannot say what in them to read. The document test raises the archive's silence: the earliest reviews may not discuss the image at all. The afternoon test is encouraging: the reception of this specific image in the first reviews is not a settled matter. The diagnosis is the corpus without a feature, and the repair is the reception shape with a defined vocabulary target. The researcher reframes around what the reviews do discuss: their descriptive language for the novel's settings generally, and whether anything in that language anticipates the later critical fixation on the valley. The revised inquiry: "What descriptive vocabulary do the novel's earliest reviews use for its settings, and how does that vocabulary compare with the symbolic language later criticism applies to the valley of ashes?" The formulation is specific, it names the corpus and the comparison; evidence-answerable, with a fallback built in, since the reviews' setting-language exists even if the valley goes unmentioned; and genuinely open, because the comparison has not been systematically made. The plan: month one, assemble the review corpus with an explicit boundary; month two, extract all setting-description passages; month three, build the vocabulary table and compare it with a defined sample of later criticism's valley-language; month four, draft the argument about when and how the image became symbolic. The archive's possible silence has been converted from a risk into a finding: if the earliest reviewers do not see the valley as symbolic, that absence is itself the discovery.

The third run begins with a theoretical curiosity: the researcher, working with narrative theory, suspects that Nick's narration is doing something unusual with time, presenting events out of order in ways that serve his retrospective judgment. The first draft is "How does narrative theory explain Nick's handling of time?" The stranger test fails: the inquiry names a framework but no passage set and no specific theoretical claim. The document test passes: the text is there. The afternoon test is mixed: the scholarship on Nick's temporality exists but leaves the retrospective-judgment angle underdeveloped. The diagnosis is the application without resistance, and the repair is the resistance shape with a named concept. The researcher chooses the narratological concept of order, the relation between the sequence of events and the sequence of their telling, and builds the candidate around the places where Nick's order departs from chronology. The revised inquiry: "Where does Nick's narration depart from chronological order in chapters 1 through 3, and how do those departures serve the retrospective judgments the narration is already making?" The inquiry is specific, it names the chapters and the concept; evidence-answerable, the departures are identifiable by collating event order against telling order; and genuinely open, because the function of the departures, serving judgment rather than suspense, is arguable. The plan: week one, reconstruct the chronological event sequence for the three chapters; week two, map the narration's actual order against it and mark every departure; week three, analyze each departure for its relation to the judgments Nick makes nearby; week four, draft the argument, including the departures that resist the thesis, treated as evidence rather than embarrassment. The theoretical curiosity has become an inquiry the theory might lose, which is what makes it research.

The three runs share a rhythm worth internalizing. Each begins with a curiosity that is real but shapeless. Each runs the three tests on paper and lets the tests kill or wound the draft. Each diagnoses the failure by its type, broad, corpus-without-feature, application-without-resistance. Each repairs by reaching for a shape, distribution, reception, resistance, that gives the curiosity a procedure. And each ends with a plan whose first task is always the same: go to the named evidence and read it slowly. The rhythm is the craft. A researcher who runs it three times will run it without thinking the fourth time, and at that point the researcher has the habit that this whole guide is trying to install. A good question is half the research, and these runs show the half being built, joint by joint, in the open.

## Question-Framing for Different Project Sizes

The same curiosity produces different questions at different project sizes, and mismatching the inquiry to the size is a quiet cause of failure. An essay candidate frozen at dissertation scale will sprawl past its deadline; a dissertation inquiry frozen at essay scale will exhaust its evidence in a month. The researcher should frame the inquiry for the project at hand, which means understanding how size changes what an inquiry must do. The three marks, specific, evidence-answerable, genuinely open, hold at every size; what changes is the scope of the evidence and the weight of the openness claim.

The essay-scale formulation serves a project of a few thousand words written in weeks. Its evidence is a handful of passages, its method is close reading, and its openness need only be local: the answer is not obvious from a first reading. A good essay-scale inquiry: "How does the clock scene in chapter 5 use the stopped mantel clock to stage Gatsby's attempt to stop time?" The candidate names one scene and one object, the evidence is a few pages, and the answer requires argument rather than summary. The essay-scale inquiry fails when it is really a dissertation inquiry in disguise: "How does the novel's treatment of time relate to modernist temporality?" That inquiry needs a corpus of modernist texts and a theoretical apparatus, which no essay can supply. The test for essay scale is the two-week test: can the evidence be gathered and the argument drafted in two weeks of steady work? If not, the formulation is oversized.

The thesis-scale inquiry serves a project of tens of thousands of words written over months. Its evidence is a defined corpus, several passage sets, a comparison, or a document collection, and its openness must survive the afternoon test against the scholarship. A good thesis-scale candidate: "How does Fitzgerald's color vocabulary differ across the novel's three main settings, the valley of ashes, West Egg, and East Egg, and what does the distribution imply about the novel's moral geography?" The inquiry names three passage sets, requires a systematic survey, and promises an argument about the novel's design. The thesis-scale inquiry fails in two directions. Undersized: "What does the green light mean in chapter 1?" The evidence is one passage, and the project will be padded. Oversized: "How does American literature of the 1920s represent wealth?" The corpus is a decade of novels, and the project will never close. The test for thesis scale is the chapter test: can the inquiry generate three to five chapters, each with its own evidence and argument, without repeating itself? If the chapters would all say the same thing, the formulation is undersized; if no chapter boundary suggests itself, it is oversized.

The dissertation-scale inquiry serves a project of years. Its evidence includes archives, multiple editions, reception corpora, or comparative sets; its openness must survive the literature review; and it must promise a contribution, something the field will know after the project that it does not know now. A good dissertation-scale candidate: "How did the novel's symbolic vocabulary, as constructed by criticism from the mid-century to the present, diverge from the descriptive vocabulary of the novel's earliest reviews, and what does the divergence reveal about the making of a classic?" The inquiry names two corpora, the early reviews and the critical tradition, spans decades, and requires both reception history and textual analysis. The dissertation-scale inquiry fails when it is an essay inquiry with ambition: "How does the green light's meaning change across the novel?" That is a chapter, not a dissertation; the evidence is three passages, and the researcher will spend four years padding. The test for dissertation scale is the gap test: can the researcher state, in one sentence, what the field does not know that this project will establish? If the sentence describes an interpretation, the formulation is essay-scale; if it describes a finding, it may be dissertation-scale.

A single curiosity can be framed at all three sizes, and seeing the same curiosity wear three questions teaches the scaling craft. Take the curiosity about Daisy's voice. Essay scale: "How does Daisy's reported speech in the reunion scene differ from her direct dialogue, and what does the difference conceal?" Thesis scale: "How does the novel distribute direct dialogue, reported speech, and narrated summary across its female characters, and what does the distribution imply about whose consciousness the narration admits?" Dissertation scale: "How did the novel's presentation of female speech change across its draft stages, and how does the revision history relate to the changing critical conversation about gender in the novel?" The curiosity is constant; the evidence, the method, and the openness claim scale with the project. The researcher who can perform this scaling can take any seminar interest and size it correctly, which is half of graduate planning.

A concrete evidence calendar shows the sizing discipline in action. Take the thesis-scale color inquiry. Months one and two: define the three passage sets and extract every color adjective into the table, noting the noun each modifies. Month three: analyze the clustering and draft the distribution argument. Month four: run the control comparison against the dialogue passages. Month five: check the pattern against the draft history for the most load-bearing passages. Month six: draft the chapters, one per setting plus the synthesis. Each month names its evidence and its deliverable, and the six months fit a two-term calendar with buffer. Shrink the same curiosity to essay scale: week one, extract the color adjectives from the valley passages; week two, compare them against the East Egg passages; week three, draft the argument. The calendar is the candidate's size made visible, and drawing it before the research begins keeps the essay from becoming a survey and the thesis from becoming padding.

The practical consequence is a rule: one project, one inquiry, at the right size. The researcher who tries to answer a dissertation inquiry in an essay will produce a survey; the researcher who tries to stretch an essay inquiry into a thesis will produce padding. The formulation should be reframed, not merely reused, when the project changes size, because the evidence base, the method, and the contribution all change. A good question is half the research, and the half must fit the project, like a key fits a lock: the right key at the wrong size opens nothing.

A final sizing discipline is the evidence calendar. For the chosen inquiry at the chosen size, the researcher writes the evidence-gathering tasks week by week until the corpus is covered, and checks that the calendar fits the project's deadline with buffer. An essay candidate whose calendar needs six weeks does not fit a four-week essay; the inquiry must be narrowed. A thesis inquiry whose calendar covers the corpus in a month is undersized; the inquiry must be widened or deepened. The calendar is the size test made visible, and it should be drawn before the research begins, because a formulation that cannot be scheduled cannot be finished.

## Verdict: Frame the Question Before You Chase the Answer

A good question is half the research, and the half can be built deliberately. The research questions worth asking about Gatsby are specific enough to name their evidence, answerable enough to be settled by documents a researcher can reach, and open enough that the answer is not known in advance. The confusion of a topic with a question is the standing temptation, at every level, and the defense is mechanical: the stranger test, the document test, the afternoon test, run on paper, before the inquiry is allowed to organize a project. The narrowing moves, by passage, by mechanism, by comparison, by boundary, turn any topic into a candidate. The question shapes, comparison, movement, mechanism, distribution, reception, resistance, give each candidate a procedure. The revision clinic and the table supply the repairs for the five standard failures. And the working life, draft, test, revise, freeze, carries the inquiry from curiosity to argument.

The honest note to end on is that no test and no table does the thinking. The craft in this guide manufactures answerable questions; it does not manufacture interesting ones. The interest comes from the researcher's own attention, the word that keeps catching the eye, the scene that will not sit still, the review that says something strange. The craft's job is to take that attention seriously enough to give it edges, documents, and a procedure, so the attention becomes research instead of reverie. Frame the question before chasing the answer, because the chase is long and the question is the map. A good question is half the research, and the map, drawn carefully, decides where the other half can go.

The first week's assignment makes the guide actionable. Day one: choose a topic and draft twelve candidates with the four narrowing moves. Day two: test the four most promising on paper and keep two. Day three: revise the two until the three marks hold, then freeze one. Day four: name the evidence, the method, and the first three tasks on a single page. Day five: read the named passages slowly and write down everything the question missed. By Friday the researcher will have a frozen inquiry, a plan, and the first evidence surprise, which is further than most projects get in a month.

## Study: Testing Questions Against the Text and the Scholarship

An inquiry sketch is a hypothesis until it meets the novel, and the meeting should happen early and repeatedly. Before committing to a formulation, the researcher should test every candidate against the text in a disciplined way: choose the passages the inquiry will depend on, read them slowly, and ask whether the evidence actually exists in the form the formulation assumes. The [annotated text on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html) is the place to test an inquiry against the text, because working through the novel with annotations at hand turns vague impressions into checkable claims about wording, structure, and narration. A candidate that cannot point to the passages it needs is an inquiry that has not yet earned its keep, and the annotated text is where that discovery should happen, in the first week, not the first year.

Once the inquiry survives the text, the next task is the scholarship, and the researcher needs to know what has already been asked and answered. The [research resources on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html) are the place to find scholarly and research resources for answering the inquiry, since the materials that support extended research work point toward the reference works, editions, and critical traditions a project will need. The afternoon test from this guide's method section is run here: spend the afternoon with the scholarship, learn whether the formulation is settled, and revise accordingly. The habit to build is scholarship-first framing. No inquiry should be frozen until its relationship to the existing conversation is mapped, because the map is what makes the openness claim honest.

The series this article belongs to offers neighboring resources that extend the guidance. A researcher ready to scale a candidate into a longer project will find the companion guide to [dissertation topics on Gatsby](/2031/02/03/gatsby-dissertation-topics/) useful for the next stage of planning. See also the [original thesis](/2031/07/28/gatsby-original-thesis/) guide, the [open research problems](/2031/07/21/gatsby-open-research-problems/) collection, and the [literature review](/2031/06/16/gatsby-literature-review/) guide.

The study routine this section recommends is compact enough to sustain. Draft the inquiry, test it against the annotated text, check it against the scholarship, revise it once, and then freeze it for the term. Keep the later file for the curiosities the inquiry excludes, keep the journal for the revisions and their reasons, and re-run the three-mark test at the start of every month. The routine is unglamorous, and that is its virtue: it replaces the anxious sprawl of the topic with the steady accumulation of the inquiry, and the accumulation, month by month, becomes the research.

The monthly check takes thirty minutes and needs only three documents: the frozen formulation, the evidence log, and the journal. First, re-run the three-mark test on the current formulation and note any drift in the margins. Second, count the evidence log's entries against the plan's calendar and confirm the pace. Third, read the month's journal entries and mark the recurring preoccupations, which are usually the project's real argument surfacing early. If all three check out, re-freeze for another month. The routine is the difference between a project that accumulates and a project that merely continues.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What research questions about Gatsby are worth asking?

The questions worth asking are the ones that are specific, answerable with evidence, and genuinely open. Specific means the inquiry names its passages, its comparison, or its corpus tightly enough that a stranger could tell what counts as relevant. Answerable with evidence means the answer must be reachable through the text, the drafts, the reviews, or another document set the researcher can actually consult, rather than through speculation. Genuinely open means the existing scholarship has not settled the matter and no single source answers it in an afternoon. Questions about how a symbol's meaning moves across chapters, how the narration distributes speech among characters, or how the earliest reviews described the novel's wealth all qualify, because each names evidence and admits rival answers. Questions that ask for summary, moral verdicts, or private intentions do not, because summary needs no research, verdicts have no evidence standard, and intentions leave no document.

### Q: What makes a question researchable?

Three marks make a candidate researchable, and each can be tested before any research begins. Specificity is tested by the stranger test: if someone who knows the novel can name what to read from the inquiry alone, it is specific enough. Evidence-answerability is tested by the document test: the researcher must be able to name the passages, editions, drafts, reviews, or datasets that could answer the inquiry and confirm they are reachable. Genuine openness is tested by the afternoon test: an afternoon with the scholarship should surface disagreement, partial answers, or silence, not a settled consensus. An inquiry missing specificity will sprawl; a formulation missing evidence-answerability will end in speculation; an inquiry missing openness will produce a summary of what is already known. The three marks are individually necessary and jointly close to sufficient, and running them on paper is the cheapest quality control in research.

### Q: What question areas exist?

Three broad areas cover nearly every researchable candidate about the novel. Textual questions ask about the novel's language, structure, and narration, and their evidence is the text itself in one or more editions; examples include questions about color vocabulary, narrative order, or the distribution of direct and reported speech. Historical questions ask about the world around the novel, its composition, its first reception, and its social context, and their evidence is documents outside the novel such as drafts, letters, reviews, and periodicals. Theoretical questions ask what happens when a framework is brought to the novel, and their evidence is the friction between the framework's predictions and the text's resistances. An inquiry should know which area it primarily inhabits, because the area decides what counts as evidence and what counts as an answer, though strong projects often work at the boundary between two areas.

### Q: How does a research question differ from a topic?

A topic is a noun phrase naming a field, such as the American Dream in Gatsby or narration in Gatsby, while an inquiry is that field with an interrogative edge: an interrogative verb, how, why, in what way, to what extent, plus a scope that evidence can address. The topic generates reading lists, which feel like progress; the inquiry generates an evidence log, which is progress. The practical difference shows in what the researcher does next. Given a topic, the researcher reads everything and argues nothing, because nothing says what would count as an answer. Given a formulation, the researcher knows which passages to collect, which comparison to run, and what a convincing answer would look like. Adding a question mark to a topic does not make it an inquiry; the test is whether the sentence names something that evidence can settle, and if removing the punctuation leaves a bare noun phrase, the framing work has not yet begun.

### Q: Why avoid broad, settled, or unanswerable questions?

Each of the three wastes a different resource. Broad questions waste time, because with no boundary the researcher reads everything and the evidence never disciplines the answer; the project sprawls until the deadline kills it. Settled questions waste effort, because the answer is already absorbed into the scholarship and the researcher spends months rediscovering what the field knows; the result is a summary, not a contribution. Unanswerable questions waste the inquiry itself, because with no document that could settle them, the researcher ends in speculation dressed as argument; questions about private intentions or counterfactuals belong here. The three failures also compound: a broad candidate is often unknowingly settled, since its very breadth means someone has answered some version of it, and an unanswerable inquiry is often broad, since no evidence constrains it. Running the three-mark test in the first week converts each predictable waste into a revision.

### Q: How does a strong question point to settling evidence?

A strong inquiry names its evidence in its own formulation, so the path from inquiry to document is visible before the research begins. It does this through the narrowing moves: naming the passage set, as in the clock scene in chapter 5; naming the mechanism, as in the color adjectives attached to the valley of ashes; naming the comparison, as in the first party scene against the final gathering; or naming the corpus boundary, as in reviews published within a year of the novel's appearance. Each naming is a promise the researcher can check: the passages exist, the comparison can be run, the corpus can be assembled. The formulation also implies what would count as an answer, a pattern in the distribution, a documented shift, a confirmed or contradicted prediction, so the researcher knows when the work is done. Weak questions gesture at evidence; strong questions appoint it.

### Q: How do you choose between several candidate questions?

Choose by scoring each candidate on three practical criteria: reachability of the evidence, survivability of the afternoon test, and fit with the project's size and deadline. Reachability asks whether the documents can be gathered in the time available; an inquiry needing inaccessible archives loses to one needing the novel and a library. Survivability asks whether the candidate stays open after honest contact with the scholarship; the candidate that collapses into a settled answer is out. Fit asks whether the evidence calendar matches the deadline with buffer; an oversized inquiry will not finish and an undersized one will pad. When two candidates tie, prefer the one whose evidence you have already begun to see, because the surprise of first contact is information: the inquiry that already shows texture is usually the livelier inquiry. Keep the losers in the later file; a rejected candidate is often next year's project.

### Q: What kinds of evidence can answer a Gatsby research question?

The evidence types divide by inquiry area. Textual questions use the novel's wording, structure, and narration, often across editions: the Cambridge edition edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli supplies the apparatus for variant-aware work, and the 2000 Cambridge edition of Trimalchio, the novel's early version, opens the draft dimension. Historical questions use documents outside the novel: surviving manuscripts, Fitzgerald's correspondence, contemporary reviews and periodicals, social records of 1920s Long Island, and publishing history. Theoretical questions use the text read against a framework's concepts, with the framework's own literature as secondary evidence. Quantitative questions, a newer arrival, use machine-readable texts and counts of linguistic features. The discipline is the same across types: name the evidence in the formulation, confirm it is reachable before freezing the inquiry, and let the evidence's actual behavior, including its silences, reshape the inquiry.

### Q: How do you tell whether a question is already answered?

Run the afternoon test with honest intent: spend a focused afternoon with the scholarship most likely to contain the answer, using surveys of the criticism, bibliographies, and the standard scholarly editions' notes. A settled candidate reveals itself quickly, through a consensus stated without qualification, an answer repeated across independent studies, or a note in a scholarly edition that closes the matter. Distinguish settled from merely discussed: an inquiry that the scholarship debates is open, while an inquiry the scholarship answers in passing, as background to something else, may still be open at the level of systematic investigation. Also check the negative space: if no study addresses the inquiry directly but the materials for answering it are well known, the formulation may be open through neglect rather than through difficulty, and neglect is a legitimate opening. Document the search, because the openness claim must be defensible to others.

### Q: How do you phrase a question so it invites an argument?

Phrase it so that more than one defensible answer is possible and the evidence could decide between them. Argument-inviting questions use verbs of explanation and comparison, how, why, in what way, to what extent, rather than verbs of identification, what, which, who, because identification questions close once the item is found. They name a tension the answer must resolve: a shift that needs explaining, a distribution that needs interpreting, a resistance that needs accounting for. "How does the green light's significance shift between chapters 1, 5, and 9?" invites argument because the shift can be characterized in rival ways; "In which chapters does the green light appear?" invites only a list. The test is to try writing two opposing one-paragraph answers: if both can be written and only evidence distinguishes them, the phrasing works. If only one answer is writable, rephrase toward the tension.

### Q: What makes a comparative question about Gatsby researchable?

A comparative inquiry is researchable when both sides get equal evidentiary weight and the comparison has a defined unit of analysis. "How does Gatsby compare with other novels of the 1920s?" fails on both counts: the comparator set is unbounded and no unit is named. The repair narrows both: "How does the narration of the party scenes in Gatsby differ from the narration of the party scenes in one named contemporary novel, along the dimensions of observed detail versus interior commentary?" The unit, the party scene, and the dimensions, detail versus commentary, make the comparison checkable, and giving each novel a full matched analysis keeps it fair. The candidate must also justify the pairing: why these two texts, and what does the comparison promise that single-text analysis cannot? Without that justification, the comparison is decorative. With it, the differences become evidence for a claim about the novel's distinctiveness.

### Q: How do you build a question from a passage that puzzles you?

Start by describing the puzzlement precisely, in one sentence, without explaining it away. "The narration calls Gatsby's smile reassuring and then immediately qualifies it" is a description; the puzzlement is the tension between the assertion and the qualification. Then ask what would have to be true for the tension to be deliberate: a pattern of qualified praise, a narrator hedging his judgments, a smile that the novel treats as performance. Each hypothesis suggests a search: collect the other qualified descriptions, collect Nick's hedges, collect the performance language. The inquiry emerges from whichever search returns a pattern: "How does the novel's pattern of qualified praise shape the reader's trust in Nick's judgments of Gatsby?" The discipline is to let the passage set the terms rather than importing a theme; puzzlement is attention, and attention, given a procedure, becomes research. Keep a file of puzzling passages; it is an inquiry bank.

### Q: When should you revise a research question mid-project?

Revise when the evidence surprises you, not when the excitement wanders. The legitimate triggers are all evidence-driven: the named corpus turns out to be silent, as when early reviews ignore the image under study; the pattern runs opposite to the hypothesis, as when the color contrast proves to be about dialogue rather than place; or the inquiry's key term proves unworkable, as when intention leaves no document. Each trigger points to a specific revision: widen the corpus, follow the real pattern, or trade the unanswerable term for a document. Do not revise to accommodate a fascinating adjacent reading, a new theory encountered mid-project, or the desire for a grander claim; those are drift, and the monthly audit exists to catch them. Record every revision with its reason in the research journal, because the record turns a wandering project into a deliberate one and shows a committee that the changes were decisions.

### Q: What is the difference between an interpretive question and an empirical one?

An interpretive formulation asks what a passage, pattern, or structure means and argues for one reading over rivals; its evidence is the text and its procedure is close reading. "How does the green light's shifting significance shape the novel's treatment of desire?" is interpretive: the answer is a reading, defended by evidence but not decided by counting. An empirical inquiry asks what is the case in a defined corpus and answers by survey; its evidence is the corpus and its procedure is comprehensive collection. "What descriptive vocabulary do the earliest reviews use for the novel's settings?" is empirical: the answer is a finding, checkable by anyone who assembles the same corpus. The two types need each other: empirical findings constrain interpretations, and interpretations give empirical findings their significance. A candidate should know which type it is, because the type decides the method section, the shape of the answer, and what counts as a refutation.

### Q: How do you turn a disagreement between critics into a research question?

Find the precise point of friction, not the general disagreement. Two published readings may both address a scene but differ on a specific claim, for example whether a passage shows the narrator judging or merely observing. State the rival claims as falsifiable predictions about the text: if the judging reading is right, certain linguistic features, evaluative adjectives, ironic framing, should cluster near the judgments; if the observing reading is right, they should not. The inquiry then becomes empirical at its core: "Do the linguistic features predicted by the judging reading actually cluster in the disputed passages?" The researcher collates the passages, checks the features, and argues from the result, which may support one reading, both partially, or neither. The discipline is steelmanning: state each rival at its strongest before testing, because defeating a weak version proves nothing. Name the published readings precisely, so the contribution is checkable.

### Q: How does the edition you use change a textual question?

It changes the evidence, because editions differ in wording, punctuation, and sometimes in substantive readings, and an inquiry that depends on exact language depends on the text that supplies it. An inquiry about the frequency or placement of a word must specify its text: the Cambridge edition edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli, published in 1991, is the scholarly standard and its apparatus records variants, while other editions may silently modernize or regularize. For draft-aware questions, the 2000 Cambridge edition of Trimalchio supplies the early version for comparison. The practical rule is to name the edition in the formulation's plan and to check any load-bearing quotation against it; a reading built on a wording that the standard edition does not contain is built on sand. Edition questions are themselves researchable: how the text changed across editions, and what the changes show, is a legitimate textual inquiry.

### Q: Why do some Gatsby questions stay open after a century of scholarship?

Some questions stay open because the evidence underdetermines the answer: the text supports rival readings and no document decides between them, as with the precise addressee of the novel's closing second person. Some stay open because each generation brings new frameworks that reopen settled matters; an inquiry closed under one theory reopens under the next. Some stay open through neglect: the materials exist but no study has assembled them, as with the systematic comparison of the early reviews' vocabulary against later criticism's. And some stay open because they were badly framed the first time, asked as topics rather than questions, and the scholarship answered the topic while the candidate waited. The lesson for the researcher is that openness has kinds, and the kind decides the strategy: underdetermination calls for better argument, framework change calls for theoretical awareness, neglect calls for archival diligence, and bad framing calls for revision.

### Q: How do you write a research question for a proposal?

Write it in one sentence, then support it with three short paragraphs: the gap, the evidence, and the method. The sentence states the inquiry with its scope visible: the passages, the corpus, or the comparison it names. The gap paragraph says what the field does not know, citing the nearest existing work and distinguishing the project from it on method, evidence, or scope rather than on brilliance. The evidence paragraph names the documents and confirms their reachability, because a committee's first doubt is always about sources. The method paragraph names the procedure, close reading of a defined passage set, collation of drafts, survey of a review corpus, and says what would count as an answer. The whole statement should fit on one page. A proposal inquiry that needs two pages to explain is not yet framed; run the narrowing moves again until the one-sentence version holds.

### Q: How do you test a question against a single chapter?

Use the chapter as a laboratory: state what the inquiry predicts about the chapter, then read the chapter slowly and mark each prediction as confirmed, complicated, or contradicted, with the passage cited. A formulation about narrative order predicts departures from chronology; the chapter test lists the chapter's events in chronological order, maps the telling order, and marks the departures. An inquiry about color vocabulary predicts clustering; the test extracts every color adjective in the chapter and checks the pattern. The single chapter cannot prove the candidate, but it can falsify it cheaply: if the predicted pattern is absent where it should be strongest, the inquiry needs revision before the researcher spends months on the full corpus. Choose the chapter where the inquiry's mechanism should be most visible; an inquiry that fails its best chapter is telling the researcher something important, and the something is usually that the mechanism was misidentified.

### Q: How do you keep a secondary source from answering the question for you?

Read secondary sources for orientation and then set them aside while working with the primary evidence, so the argument is built from the documents rather than borrowed from the criticism. The practical method is two-pass: first pass, survey the scholarship to map the conversation and run the afternoon test; second pass, close the secondary sources and build the evidence table, the concordance, or the corpus survey directly from the primary materials. Only then return to the secondary literature, to position the finding against it. The warning sign is language: if the draft's key terms, its metaphors, its structure of argument all echo one secondary source, the source has answered the formulation and the researcher is paraphrasing. The defense is the evidence log: every claim in the draft should trace to a primary document the researcher examined, not to a critic's summary of it. Secondary sources map the field; the researcher still has to walk it.
