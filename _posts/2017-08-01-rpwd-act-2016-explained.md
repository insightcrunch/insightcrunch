The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 turns on a distinction that most institutions applying it get wrong. The Act speaks to two populations in two different registers. To the first population, every person whose long term impairment interacts with barriers so as to hinder full and effective participation in society, it confers rights: the right not to be discriminated against, the right to reasonable accommodation, the right to live in the community, the right to hold property and make decisions, the right to protection from cruelty, abuse and exploitation. To the second and narrower population, persons certified as having not less than forty per cent of a specified disability, it confers those rights and something more: free education for children, reserved seats in higher education, reserved posts in government employment, and a first claim on a series of public schemes. The certificate that defines the second population is a gateway to the benefits. It was never drafted as a gateway to the rights.

![The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016, showing the two populations the statute creates and the rights that attach to each - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

Treating the certificate as the gateway to everything is the single commonest error in this field, and the Act's own text refutes it. A candidate denied a scribe in an examination because her assessed percentage fell below forty has the statute on her side, as the Supreme Court confirmed in Vikash Kumar v Union Public Service Commission. A tenant refused housing, an employee refused a screen reader, a passenger humiliated by an airline, none of them needs to prove a percentage before the rights chapters of this Act speak to their case. Yet the percentage is what administrators can verify, and so the percentage is what gets asked for. A statute containing two definitions of its own subject will be applied by administrators using whichever is easier to verify, and that is the tension this article keeps in view from beginning to end.

The article that follows works through the Act as a statute pillar should: what it replaced and why the replacement took the form it did, how the text is organised, the two definitions and the Schedule of twenty-one specified disabilities, the rights that attach to every person with disability, the additional entitlements that attach at the benchmark, the accessibility duties with their statutory deadlines, the certification machinery, the enforcement architecture of Commissioners, Special Courts and Funds, the offences, and the courts' contribution. It also gives the contrary case its full strength, because the forty per cent line has a serious defence and the honest reader should meet it. The comparison with the predecessor statute belongs to the article tracing [the move from the PWD Act 1995 to the RPwD Act 2016](/2017/07/15/pwd-act-1995-to-rpwd-2016/), and the applied treatment of reservation and accessibility belongs to its companion; this pillar states the provisions themselves and the distinctions they create.

## What the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act replaced, and why

The statute book before 2016 held the Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act, whose short title carries the year 1995 and which is, in the formal citation of the Gazette, Act 1 of 1996. That inversion is the first of this subject's name and year traps, and it is worth fixing at the outset: the predecessor is called the 1995 Act and is Act 1 of 1996; the statute explained here is called the 2016 Act, received assent on 27 December 2016, and came into force on 19 April 2017. A reader who holds those two facts separately will not be misled by either short title.

The 1995 Act recognised seven disabilities: blindness, low vision, leprosy cured, hearing impairment, locomotor disability, mental retardation and mental illness. Its architecture was that of a welfare statute administered through coordination committees at the Centre and the states, with duties framed as obligations of governments to act within the limits of their economic capacity and development. It reserved three per cent of government posts, one per cent each for three groups of disabilities, and it created the offices of Chief Commissioner and State Commissioners. What it did not do was define disability by reference to barriers, recognise discrimination as the central wrong, or confer enforceable individual rights in any developed form. Its definition of disability was a medical list with a percentage threshold, and a person whose condition fell outside the seven entries was, for the statute's purposes, not disabled at all.

The pressure for replacement came from outside the domestic statute book. The United Nations General Assembly adopted the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities on 13 December 2006. India signed the Convention and ratified it on 1 October 2007, among the earliest states to do so, and ratification carried the obligation to bring domestic law into line with the Convention's principles: respect for inherent dignity and individual autonomy, non-discrimination, full and effective participation and inclusion in society, accessibility, and equality of opportunity. The preamble of the 2016 Act recites those principles and states the purpose plainly: the Act exists to give effect to the Convention.

The drafting route was long. The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment appointed a committee chaired by Dr Sudha Kaul in 2010 to prepare a draft law, and the committee's work, contested at several points by disability organisations who objected to successive diluted drafts, eventually produced the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Bill of 2014. That Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 7 February 2014, went to the departmental Standing Committee, and lapsed scrutiny in one Parliament before being taken up in the next; in its final form the Bill covered nineteen conditions, a count Parliament raised to twenty-one before enactment. The Rajya Sabha passed the Bill on 14 December 2016 and the Lok Sabha on 16 December 2016, the President assented on 27 December 2016, the Act was published in the Gazette on 28 December 2016 as Act 49 of 2016, and the Central Government brought it into force on 19 April 2017 by notification. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules followed, notified on 15 June 2017, and those two dates, 19 April 2017 for the Act and 15 June 2017 for the Rules, are the ones from which several of the Act's own clocks run.

The deeper change was conceptual, and the Act's defenders and its critics agree on its direction. The 1995 Act asked what was wrong with a person's body and whether the state, within its means, would assist. The 2016 Act asks what barriers stand between a person and equal participation, and it answers with rights that others must respect rather than benefits that officials may distribute. Whether the machinery built to carry that answer is adequate is a separate question, taken up in the assessment sections of this article; the text itself made the move completely.

## From committee draft to enacted text: the choices that were contested

The drafting history matters to interpretation because three of the Act's most litigated features were deliberate choices made against opposition recorded in the process. The committee chaired by Dr Sudha Kaul, which reported in 2010, worked from the Convention outward: its draft took the social definition of disability as the organising idea, treated legal capacity as the default rather than the exception, and framed entitlements as rights with remedies attached. The Bill introduced in the Rajya Sabha in February 2014 carried much of that architecture, including the expanded list of conditions and the new guardianship model, but it departed from the committee draft at points the disability movement contested in the years that followed. Two departures drew sustained objection. The first was guardianship: the Bill as introduced retained a place for plenary guardianship alongside the limited form, and organisations of persons with disabilities argued that a statute implementing a Convention whose Article 12 affirms equal legal capacity could not coherently preserve a mechanism by which a court extinguished a person's legal personhood in favour of a substitute. The enacted Act is the record of that argument succeeding in substance: Section 14 knows only limited guardianship, and the residual possibility of total support enters through the proviso to that section, bounded and reviewable, rather than as a freestanding plenary institution. The second departure was the treatment of discrimination: the Bill's justification exception, for acts shown to be a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim, survived into the enacted Section 3(3), over objections that a justification clause inside a rights provision invites precisely the administrative reasoning the clause then has to be policed against, and it remains the provision through which a respondent establishment will ordinarily attempt to defend differential treatment.

The numbers in Chapter VI were likewise negotiated rather than inherited. The three per cent reservation of the predecessor, spread across three categories, became four per cent across five streams, with the newly recognised conditions of the intellectual and multiple disability groups given a shared stream rather than separate ones, a compromise between the claim of each group to its own quota and the administrative objection that multiplying streams multiplies unfilled vacancies. The higher education reservation rose from three per cent to five per cent and was extended in terms from government institutions to aided ones. The list of conditions grew from the nineteen of the Bill to the twenty-one of the Act during the parliamentary passage, as conditions pressed by medical and parent organisations, among them the blood disorder group, were taken into the Schedule. Each of these choices is a datum for the interpreter: the forty per cent threshold was retained by a Parliament that had the committee's more individualised approach before it and chose a measurable criterion for benefits, which is the strongest textual argument that the threshold's retention was a considered distributive decision and not a survival from the medical model the Act elsewhere discards. The same history explains the statute's one deliberate untidiness, the coexistence of the Act's guardianship with the surviving guardianship of the National Trust statute, addressed in the legal capacity section: Parliament reformed the general law and left the specialist statute standing, and the friction between them is a consequence of that sequencing rather than of any judgment that two guardianship regimes are better than one.

## How the Act is organised, and who it binds

The Act contains one hundred and two sections arranged in seventeen chapters, and its shape repays attention because the order of the chapters is itself an argument. After the preliminary chapter of definitions, Chapter II sets out rights and entitlements in general terms: equality, community life, protection, family, reproductive rights, voting, justice and legal capacity. Chapter III covers education, Chapter IV skill development and employment, Chapter V social security, healthcare, rehabilitation and recreation. Chapter VI then isolates the special provisions for persons with benchmark disabilities, which is where the reservation and free education provisions sit, and a short Chapter VII adds special provisions for persons with high support needs. Chapter VIII lists the duties of appropriate governments, Chapter IX requires the registration of institutions for persons with disabilities, and Chapter X deals with certification. Chapters XI to XV build the institutional machinery: advisory boards at the Centre, in the states and in districts, the Chief Commissioner and State Commissioners, Special Courts, and the National and State Funds. Chapter XVI creates the offences and penalties, and Chapter XVII carries the miscellaneous provisions, including the repeal of the 1995 Act.

The duties fall on a wide field. The expression "appropriate Government" divides responsibility between the Central Government and the State Governments according to the establishment concerned. "Establishment" is defined to include both government establishments and private establishments, and a company, a firm, a society, a trust, a factory or an organisation can each be a private establishment for the Act's purposes. That breadth matters for the rights chapters: the bar on discrimination in employment in Section 20 and the duty to notify an equal opportunity policy in Section 21 are addressed to establishments generally, not only to the state. At the same time the Act is careful about which of its commands bind whom. The reservation percentages bind government establishments and government or aided institutions of higher education. The accessibility deadlines bind public buildings and service providers, government or private. The offence provisions bind everyone. Part of reading the Act accurately is tracking, provision by provision, which class of duty-bearer each sentence addresses, and the sections that follow do that tracking expressly.

The division inside "appropriate Government" is set by Section 2(a): in relation to the Central Government or an establishment wholly or substantially financed by it, or a Union territory, the Central Government; in relation to a State Government or an establishment wholly or substantially financed by it, the State Government. The definition decides practical questions that arise constantly under the Act: which government must frame the scheme, which Commissioner receives the complaint about a given establishment, which government answers for a reserved vacancy, and which fund, national or state, a benefit is charged against. A complaint filed with the wrong Commissioner is not defeated by the error, since the offices correspond and transfer papers in practice, but the applicant who reads Section 2(a) first starts in the right office, and in a statute where delay is the commonest injury, that is worth the paragraph it takes to say.

The field this statute governs sits inside a wider body of welfare legislation, statutes on maintenance, social security, health and education that share its beneficiaries without sharing its rights-based grammar. That wider field is mapped in [the complete guide to welfare legislation in India](/2017/07/01/welfare-laws-india-complete-guide/), which takes the connective view; the present article stays with the single statute and its internal distinctions.

## Two definitions, two populations

Everything in the Act descends from Section 2, and three of its clauses do the decisive work. Clause (s) defines a "person with disability" as a person with long term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairment which, in interaction with barriers, hinders his full and effective participation in society equally with others. Clause (c) defines "barrier" to mean any factor, communicational, cultural, economic, environmental, institutional, political, social, attitudinal or structural, which hampers that participation. Clause (r) defines a "person with benchmark disability" as a person with not less than forty per cent of a specified disability where the specified disability has not been defined in measurable terms, and as including a person with a disability, where the specified disability has been defined in measurable terms, as certified by the certifying authority. Clause (zc) then ties the second definition to the Schedule: a "specified disability" is a disability specified in the Schedule to the Act.

Read the two definitions against each other and their different logics come into focus. The first definition contains no percentage, no list and no certificate. It asks two questions: does the person have a long term impairment of one of the four kinds named, and does that impairment, meeting the barriers around it, hinder participation on an equal basis? A person with a chronic neurological condition that intermittently prevents writing, a person with a psychosocial disability whose impairment is real but assessed at a low percentage, a person with a sensory impairment that no notified scale measures well: each can fall squarely within clause (s) while falling outside clause (r). The second definition is deliberately narrower and deliberately bureaucratic. It requires a specified disability, one of the entries in the Schedule, and it requires the forty per cent threshold, proved in the manner the certification chapter prescribes. The first definition describes a relationship between a person and their environment. The second describes a file that an administration can open, verify and count.

The Act distributes its content between the two definitions with care. Chapter II's rights are conferred on persons with disabilities, the wide class. The distributive provisions of Chapter VI are confined to persons with benchmark disabilities, the narrow class. When a dispute arises under the Act, the first analytical step is always to ask which definition the provision in question uses, because the answer decides whether a certificate is relevant at all. Much confused advice in this field consists of applying Chapter VI's threshold to Chapter II's rights, and the statute does not authorise that move.

How the percentage is produced deserves attention, because the benchmark reads like a natural fact and is in truth an artefact of the guidelines. For conditions the Schedule defines in measurable terms, the measurement is the assessment: blindness is total absence of sight, or visual acuity less than 3/60 or less than 10/200 (Snellen) in the better eye with best possible correction, or a field of vision subtending an angle of less than 10 degrees; low vision spans the band between that floor and the ceilings the Schedule states; deafness is a hearing loss of 70 decibels in speech frequencies in both ears, and hard of hearing the band from 60 to 70 decibels. A certifying specialist applies those measures directly. For conditions the Schedule defines descriptively, locomotor disability, intellectual disability, mental illness and the neurological and blood disorder groups, the guidelines notified under Section 56 convert clinical findings into percentages through prescribed instruments and tables, and the certificate records the resulting figure. Two consequences follow. The percentage of a measurable condition is reproducible between districts in a way the percentage of a described condition is not, because instruments differ in administration and borderline judgments differ between boards; and the forty per cent line therefore operates with different real strictness at different points of the Schedule, a qualification to the uniformity argument for the threshold that its defenders must carry. The certification rules also provide for certificates that record a period of validity where the disability is likely to vary, with reassessment at the end of that period, alongside certificates of a permanent character where variation is not expected, so benchmark status itself can be time limited even though Chapter VI speaks of it as a settled status.

### Does a person whose disability is below forty per cent have any rights under the RPwD Act?

Yes. The rights chapters of the Act are conferred on every person with disability, defined by impairment interacting with barriers, and contain no percentage threshold. Non-discrimination, reasonable accommodation, community life, legal capacity and protection from abuse all attach below forty per cent. The threshold governs only the distributive benefits reserved to persons with benchmark disabilities.

The point is not a technicality, because institutions behave as though the opposite were true. Examination bodies have demanded benchmark certificates before providing a scribe; employers have treated a sub-forty assessment as an answer to an accommodation request; landlords and transport operators have treated the absence of a certificate as the absence of a right. Each of those responses imports into a rights provision a condition that Parliament placed only in the benefits provisions. The Supreme Court addressed the examination version directly in Vikash Kumar, discussed in the judicial section of this article, and held that the facility of a scribe, as an instance of reasonable accommodation, could not be confined to benchmark disability. The reasoning travels: where the right claimed is one Chapter II confers, the claimant's percentage is beside the point.

There is a third category that sits inside the benchmark class and should not be confused with either definition. A "person with disability having high support needs" is defined in clause (t) as a person with benchmark disability, certified in the manner the certification chapter prescribes, who needs high support, and "high support" is itself defined as intensive support, physical, psychological and otherwise, that a person may require for daily activities, for taking independent and informed decisions, and for access to facilities and participation in all areas of life. Section 38 then builds a specific procedure on that category, examined later in this article. The three concentric classes, all persons with disabilities, persons with benchmark disabilities among them, and persons with high support needs among those, are the Act's population structure, and nearly every operative provision can be located by asking which ring of the three it addresses.

## The Schedule: twenty-one specified disabilities

The Schedule is short enough to state in full and important enough that approximations of it mislead. It groups the specified disabilities under five heads. The first head is physical disability, itself divided into locomotor disability, visual impairment, hearing impairment, and speech and language disability. Under locomotor disability the Schedule names the leprosy cured person, cerebral palsy, dwarfism, muscular dystrophy and acid attack victims, and locomotor disability itself is defined in terms of an inability to execute distinctive activities associated with movement, whether from affliction of the bones, joints, muscles or nerves. Visual impairment contributes two entries, blindness and low vision, each defined by acuity and field-of-vision measures. Hearing impairment contributes two: "deaf", meaning persons having seventy decibels of hearing loss in conversational frequencies in both ears, and "hard of hearing", meaning sixty to seventy decibels of loss. Speech and language disability completes the physical group.

The second head is intellectual disability, a condition characterised by significant limitation both in intellectual functioning and in adaptive behaviour, and under it the Schedule places specific learning disabilities, meaning a group of conditions in which there is a deficit in processing language, spoken or written, and autism spectrum disorder, a neuro-developmental condition appearing in the first three years of life that significantly affects a person's ability to communicate and to understand and relate to others. The third head is mental behaviour, whose single entry is mental illness, defined as a substantial disorder of thinking, mood, perception, orientation or memory that grossly impairs judgment, behaviour, capacity to recognise reality or ability to meet the ordinary demands of life, and expressly stated not to include what the Schedule, following older vocabulary it inherited and did not modernise, calls retardation. The fourth head covers disability caused by other conditions: chronic neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease, and the blood disorders haemophilia, thalassemia and sickle cell disease. The fifth head is multiple disabilities, meaning more than one of the specified disabilities, including deaf-blindness. Counted as the Schedule enumerates them, the entries come to twenty-one, against the seven the 1995 Act recognised.

Two features of the Schedule carry practical consequences beyond the count. The first is that several entries are defined in measurable terms and several are not, and Section 2(r) is drafted to handle both: where a disability has been defined in measurable terms, benchmark status follows certification against those measures; where it has not, the forty per cent is assessed on the guidelines the certification chapter authorises. The second is the power Section 2(zc) read with the Schedule's closing words reserves to the Central Government: the government may, by notification, add further disabilities to the Schedule. A person whose condition is absent from the list is therefore not in the position of someone excluded by a statute that only Parliament can change. The list is expandable by executive notification, and campaigns for the inclusion of further conditions are addressed to that power. What the notification power cannot do is dispense with the list altogether for benchmark purposes: outside the Schedule there is no specified disability, and so no benchmark status, however disabling the condition. The barriers definition in clause (s) may still reach such a person for rights purposes, which is one more reason the two definitions must be kept apart.

Two of the Schedule's inclusions changed the law's reach in ways a bare count understates. The recognition of specific learning disabilities as a specified disability brought a large population of school and examination candidates, whose conditions had previously been handled by board level concessions of uneven generosity, inside a statutory framework with certification, benchmark status and the inclusive education duties of Sections 16 and 17 attached; the examination litigation described later in this article is unintelligible without that entry. The recognition of acid attack victims within locomotor disability connected this Act to the criminal law reforms on acid attacks and gave survivors a route into reservation and scheme benefits that a general impairment category would not have stated as clearly. Conversely, the Schedule's silence is also law: a condition with substantial participation effects that appears under no head, and has not been notified under Section 99, produces a person who may hold every Chapter II right and no Chapter VI benefit, and the statute intends that result rather than overlooking it.

## Which right needs a benchmark: the Act's central table

The distinction drawn so far resolves, in one view, the question readers and institutions ask most often about this statute: does this entitlement require a forty per cent certificate, or does it not?

| Right or entitlement | Who holds it | Provision | What must be shown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-discrimination | Any person with disability | Section 3 | Disability within Section 2(s); no certificate or percentage is prescribed |
| Reasonable accommodation | Any person with disability | Sections 2(y) and 3; denial is discrimination under Section 2(h) | Disability and a modification that is necessary and appropriate without disproportionate or undue burden |
| Accessibility of buildings, transport and information | Any person with disability | Sections 40 to 46 | Nothing by the individual; the duty rests on establishments and service providers |
| Community life | Any person with disability | Section 5 | Disability; the duty is that the person not be obliged to live in a particular arrangement |
| Legal capacity | Any person with disability | Sections 13 and 14 | Nothing for capacity itself; limited guardianship requires an order of the District Court or designated authority |
| Free education, ages six to eighteen | Persons with benchmark disabilities only | Section 31 | Certification of benchmark disability |
| Reservation in higher education | Persons with benchmark disabilities only | Section 32 | Certification of benchmark disability |
| Reservation in government employment | Persons with benchmark disabilities only | Section 34 | Certification of benchmark disability and identification of the post under Section 33 |
| Scheme benefits and special schemes | Persons with benchmark disabilities only | Sections 35 to 38 | Certification; high support provision under Section 38 requires certification of high support needs |

The table is the article's argument in miniature. The upper rows, the rights, are certificate-free. The lower rows, the distribution, are certificate-gated. An administrator who demands a certificate in an upper-row case has misread the statute, and a claimant who demands a reserved post in a lower-row case without certification has misread it the other way.

## The rights held by every person with disability

Chapter II is the Act's centre of gravity, and it begins with the equality provision. Section 3 requires the appropriate Government to ensure that persons with disabilities enjoy the right to equality, life with dignity and respect for integrity equally with others, and to take steps to utilise their capacity by providing an appropriate environment. Section 3(3) then states the operative prohibition: no person with disability shall be discriminated on the ground of disability, unless it is shown that the impugned act or omission is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. That closing clause matters. The Act does not enact an absolute bar on disability-based distinction; it enacts a justification test, and places the burden of meeting it on the person defending the distinction, since the words are "unless it is shown". Section 3(4) adds that no person shall be deprived of personal liberty only on the ground of disability, and Section 3(5) obliges the appropriate Government to take necessary steps to ensure reasonable accommodation.

The justification test in Section 3(3) is the provision through which most contested distinctions will be fought, and its structure deserves precision. It has three elements, all of which the defender of the distinction must establish: a legitimate aim, which excludes aims that are themselves discriminatory or merely administrative convenience dressed as policy; a means that is proportionate to that aim, which imports the familiar inquiries into suitability, necessity and balance, whether the measure actually serves the aim, whether a less restrictive measure would serve it as well, and whether the burden imposed on persons with disabilities is out of proportion to the gain; and the burden of showing both, which the clause places on the respondent by the words "unless it is shown". A blanket exclusion illustrates the test at work: an establishment that bars all persons with a stated condition from a class of posts, citing safety, must show that the safety aim is real for the posts in question, that individual assessment would not serve it, and that the exclusion's breadth is proportionate; the Act's scheme, which elsewhere insists on post identification by category in Section 33 and individual assessment for high support in Section 38, makes blanket rules difficult to defend, and establishments drafting policies under the Act should assume that a distinction drawn by diagnosis rather than by function will fail the necessity limb. The clause is not a weakness in the prohibition; properly applied, it is the prohibition's method.

Reasonable accommodation is defined in Section 2(y) as necessary and appropriate modification and adjustments, without imposing a disproportionate or undue burden in a particular case, to ensure to persons with disabilities the enjoyment or exercise of rights equally with others. Two elements of the definition decide real disputes. The first is that accommodation is individual: the words "in a particular case" require the adjustment to be fitted to the person, not selected from a standard list, so a duty-holder who offers a generic facility that does not meet the individual's barrier has not discharged the duty. The second is the burden qualifier, which is the defence built into the right: an accommodation that would impose a disproportionate or undue burden is outside the definition. What makes the concept a duty rather than a courtesy is Section 2(h), which defines discrimination to include denial of reasonable accommodation. The Act thus completes a syllogism: discrimination is prohibited, denial of reasonable accommodation is discrimination, and so a refusal to accommodate, where the accommodation is within the definition, is unlawful discrimination unless the Section 3(3) justification is made out. The burden question inside the definition is genuinely underdeveloped in Indian case law, and the counter-reading section of this article returns to it.

Section 4 addresses women and children with disabilities, requiring measures to ensure they enjoy their rights equally with others and that children with disabilities can express their views on matters affecting them with appropriate support. Section 5 confers the right to live in the community: persons with disabilities shall not be obliged to live in any particular living arrangement, and governments must endeavour to give access to a range of in-house, residential and other community support services, including personal assistance. The institutional model, under which disability was answered with segregation, is what this section displaces in law, whatever the pace of displacement in fact.

### Can a person with disability be required to live in an institution?

No. Section 5 gives persons with disabilities the right to live in the community and provides that they shall not be obliged to live in any particular living arrangement. The appropriate Government must instead endeavour to provide a range of in-house, residential and community support services, including personal assistance, so that community life is practically possible.

Sections 6 and 7 build the protection layer. Section 6 obliges the appropriate Government to take measures to protect persons with disabilities from torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, and bars research on a person with disability without free and informed consent given through accessible modes of communication and the prior permission of a committee constituted for research on disability, at least half of whose members must be persons with disabilities or members of registered organisations. Section 7 addresses abuse, violence and exploitation directly: governments must take cognizance of incidents, prescribe reporting procedures, arrange rescue, protection and rehabilitation of victims, and create public awareness. Any person or registered organisation that has reason to believe abuse, violence or exploitation has been, is being or is likely to be committed against a person with disability may report it to the Executive Magistrate of the area, who must take immediate steps, which can include authorising rescue and safe custody, directing protective custody where the person desires it, and providing maintenance. This is a fast, non-criminal protective route that exists alongside the offence provisions, and it is one of the Act's least known mechanisms.

Section 8 extends protection to situations of risk, covering armed conflict, humanitarian emergencies and natural disasters, and requires the authorities responsible for disaster management to take account of persons with disabilities in their planning and to maintain records of persons with disabilities in the affected areas so that relief reaches them. Section 9 protects home and family: a child with disability shall not be separated from parents except on an order of a competent court where separation is in the child's best interests, and where parents are unable to care for a child with disability the court works to place the child with family or, failing that, in a shelter home run by the government or a registered organisation, rather than with an unrelated institution by default. Section 10 protects reproductive rights, and provides in terms that no person with disability shall be subjected to any medical procedure which leads to infertility without his or her free and informed consent. Section 11 requires the Election Commission and the State Election Commissions to ensure that polling stations are accessible and that electoral materials and facilities are within the reach of persons with disabilities, so that the franchise itself is usable. Section 12 addresses access to justice: governments must ensure that persons with disabilities can exercise the right to access any scheme, programme, facility or service offered by a court, tribunal, authority or commission, with the option of filing in accessible formats and with support where required, and police and judicial officers are to receive training under the human resource development provisions that follow later in the Act.

The rights in Chapter II are framed as entitlements the appropriate Government must ensure, and a reader is entitled to ask what happens when the ensuring fails. The Act answers in three registers. Against a private actor, the anti-discrimination duty in Section 3 speaks to conduct generally, and the offence and penalty provisions give specified denials a criminal and financial consequence. Against any actor, the Commissioners stand open: a denial of a Chapter II right is a deprivation of rights within their inquiry jurisdiction, and the complainant needs no certificate, no fee framework of the kind litigation carries, and no standing beyond being aggrieved. Against the state itself, the rights overlap the fundamental rights in Articles 14, 19 and 21 of the Constitution, and the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court is the route the major cases under this statute and its predecessor have taken, from the National Federation of the Blind litigation under the 1995 Act to Vikash Kumar and Rajive Raturi under this one. The structure is deliberate: the certificate-free rights would be worth little if the only forum for them were a court, and the Commissioners' inquiry power, examined in the enforcement section, is the forum the Act builds for them.

## Legal capacity and limited guardianship

Sections 13 to 15 contain the Act's most quietly radical provisions, because they address not what the state must give but what the law must stop taking away. Section 13(2) requires the appropriate Government to ensure that persons with disabilities enjoy legal capacity on an equal basis with others in all aspects of life and have the right to equal recognition everywhere as persons before the law. Section 13(1) spells out the economic content: the right, equally with others, to own or inherit property, movable or immovable, to control their financial affairs, and to have access to bank loans, mortgages and other forms of financial credit. The remainder of the section builds a supported-decision framework around that capacity. A person with disability may take support for a transaction; a supporting person who has a conflict of interest in a particular transaction must abstain from supporting in that transaction, though kinship alone raises no presumption of conflict; the person may alter, modify or dismantle any support arrangement and seek another, prospectively and without disturbing third party transactions already entered; and any person providing support shall not exercise undue influence and shall respect the supported person's autonomy, dignity and privacy.

The practical importance of Section 13 is best seen in the transactions it governs. A bank account, a loan, a mortgage, the purchase and sale of property, the inheritance of a share in family property: each is an exercise of legal capacity, and before statutes of this kind, each was a point at which a person with an intellectual or psychosocial disability, or a person whose communication was unconventional, could be met with a demand for a guardian's authority that the law had made routine. Section 13 removes the legal basis of that demand in terms, by providing that persons with disabilities enjoy legal capacity on an equal basis with others in all aspects of life and have the right, equally with others, to own or inherit property, to control their financial affairs and to have access to bank loans, mortgages and other forms of financial credit, and by requiring the appropriate Government to ensure that this is so. Where a transaction is genuinely beyond the person's ability to conduct even with support, Section 14's limited guardianship is the only route the Act provides, and it produces a joint decision maker for specified decisions, not a substitute for the person. Sector reporting has documented the persistence of the older practice at bank counters, which is why the section's enforcement matters: a refusal grounded in disability is discrimination within Section 2(h) where it denies equal treatment, and a refusal grounded in the absence of a guardianship order the Act does not require is an error a Commissioner can correct on complaint.

### Can a person with disability own property and operate financial accounts in their own name?

Yes. Section 13 guarantees legal capacity on an equal basis with others, including the right to own or inherit property, control financial affairs and access bank loans, mortgages and other forms of financial credit. Support arrangements are the person's to choose, alter or end, and a supporter must not exercise undue influence over the decision supported.

Section 14 then deals with the case where support is not enough. Where a District Court, or a designated authority notified by the State Government, finds that a person with disability, who had been provided adequate and appropriate support, is unable to take legally binding decisions, the person may be provided the further support of a limited guardian to take legally binding decisions on his behalf in consultation with him. The Explanation defines limited guardianship as a system of joint decision making which operates on mutual understanding and trust between the guardian and the person with disability, limited to a specific period and to specific decisions and situations, and operating in accordance with the will of the person with disability. Three features distinguish it from the plenary guardianship it displaced. It is joint rather than substitutionary, the guardian decides with the person, not instead of the person. It is bounded, confined to the period, decisions and situations the order specifies, rather than a general transfer of legal personality. And it is residual: it is available only after adequate and appropriate support has been provided and has not sufficed. A proviso allows the court or authority to grant total support where the person requires it or where limited guardianship would have to be granted repeatedly, with the arrangement then subject to review. Section 14(2) performs a wholesale conversion: every guardian appointed under any other law for a person with disability is deemed, from the commencement of this Act, to function as a limited guardian. An appeal lies under Section 14(3) to an appellate authority notified by the State Government. Section 15 completes the scheme by requiring governments to designate authorities to mobilise the community, create awareness for the exercise of legal capacity, and set up support arrangements, including for persons living in institutions and persons with high support needs.

The interaction with mental healthcare law is the live edge of these provisions. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017, whose own architecture of advance directives and nominated representatives is set out in [the pillar on the mental healthcare statute](/2015/09/15/mental-healthcare-act-2017-explained/), proceeds from the same premise of capacity presumed and support preferred, and a person may find both statutes engaged by the same set of facts. The developing jurisprudence on legal capacity, in which courts have been pressed to read Section 14's joint model into older plenary arrangements and to treat the person's will as the governing consideration, moves in the Convention's direction; the cautious statement of the position is that Section 14 supplies the statutory standard against which guardianship orders affecting persons with disabilities are increasingly measured, while plenary guardianship surviving under other enactments, notably for persons with the conditions covered by the National Trust Act 1999, has not been swept away in terms. The Act's own conversion clause reaches guardians "appointed under any provision of any other law", and the friction between that sweep and the National Trust scheme is a genuine unresolved point of Indian disability law, better flagged than papered over.

The machinery that Section 15 builds around guardianship completes the picture. State Governments must designate authorities to mobilise the community, create awareness of the Act's legal capacity provisions, and set up suitable support arrangements for persons with disabilities living with families, including informing persons with high support needs of their rights. The designated authority is also the point at which families encounter the new model in practice: an application for limited guardianship is not an application to have a person declared incapable, but a request for a bounded, joint arrangement for specified decisions, and the authority or court considering it must record why support short of guardianship was inadequate, because Section 14's sequence, support first, guardianship on its failure, is a condition of the order and not a courtesy. The review obligation in the proviso, requiring the authority, when it finds total support necessary, to review the need for such support at a later stage, converts what older law treated as a permanent status into a revisable assessment, and families should understand both halves of that sentence: the Act contemplates orders at the far end of support, and it refuses to let them harden into finality.

## Education, employment and social security: the service chapters

Chapters III to V confer their benefits on persons with disabilities in the wide sense, with benchmark-specific elements appearing only where the text says so, and a pillar treatment summarises them at the level the cluster's other articles do not claim. Chapter III opens with Section 16, which obliges the appropriate Government and local authorities to endeavour that all educational institutions funded or recognised by them provide inclusive education: admission without discrimination, accessible buildings and facilities, reasonable accommodation according to individual requirements, individualised support, education for blind, deaf and deaf-blind students in the most appropriate languages and modes of communication, early detection of specific learning disabilities, monitoring of participation and attainment, and transport for children with disabilities and attendants for those with high support needs. Section 17 adds the machinery: a survey of school going children every five years, the first within two years of commencement, teacher training institutions and teachers qualified in sign language and Braille, resource centres, books and assistive devices free of cost to students with benchmark disabilities up to the age of eighteen, scholarships, and modifications to curriculum and examinations including extra time, scribes and exemption from second and third language courses. Section 18 covers adult and continuing education. These provisions operate beside, and are expressly stated to operate notwithstanding nothing in, the general education code; the relationship with the school education statute is traced in [the guide to the Right to Education Act](/2015/01/01/right-to-education-act-2009-guide/).

The mechanics of Section 16 deserve a sentence of their own because they convert a philosophy into an audit trail. The duties include conducting surveys of school going children in every five years, the first within two years of commencement, for identifying children with disabilities and ascertaining their special needs; establishing an adequate number of teacher training institutions and training staff; training professionals and staff to support inclusive education at all levels of school education; establishing an adequate number of resource centres to support educational institutions; promoting the use of appropriate augmentative and alternative modes of communication, Braille and sign language; providing books, learning materials and assistive devices free of cost to students with benchmark disabilities up to the age of eighteen; and detecting specific learning disabilities at the earliest and taking suitable pedagogical measures. Section 17's specific measures add the individual entitlements that sit inside that machinery, including reasonable accommodation according to the child's requirements, individualised support, education in the most appropriate languages and modes of communication, scholarships in appropriate cases, and modification of the examination system to eliminate pure mathematical calculation for children with blindness, alongside scribe facilities and extra time. The distinction between the two sections mirrors the article's central one in miniature: Section 16 builds the system, Section 17 confers the measures, and a complaint about a child is usually a complaint that the second has been refused because the first was never built.

Chapter IV addresses work. Section 19 requires schemes for vocational training and self-employment, including concessional loans and inclusion in mainstream skill programmes. Section 20 prohibits discrimination in employment by government establishments in any matter relating to employment, subject to a notification power by which the appropriate Government may exempt an establishment having regard to the type of work carried on; it also protects a person with disability from being dispensed with or reduced in rank on acquiring disability during service, a protection with a long pedigree in Indian service law, and requires governments to provide reasonable accommodation and a barrier free environment in government workplaces. Section 21 requires every establishment to notify an equal opportunity policy detailing the measures it will take, and requires government establishments to register a copy with the Chief Commissioner or State Commissioner. Sections 22 and 23 add record keeping and the appointment of a grievance redressal officer in government establishments.

Two of these provisions repay closer statement because they are the ones employees actually invoke. Section 20(4) provides that no government establishment shall dispense with, or reduce in rank, an employee who acquires a disability during his service; that if an employee, after acquiring disability, is not suitable for the post he was holding, he shall be shifted to some other post with the same pay scale and service benefits; and that if it is not possible to adjust the employee against any post, he shall be kept on a supernumerary post until a suitable post becomes available or the employee attains the age of superannuation, whichever is earlier. The provision converts what service law once treated as a medical board question about fitness into a statutory sequence: retain, redeploy on equal terms, hold supernumerary. Section 23 completes the internal machinery by requiring every government establishment to appoint a grievance redressal officer and to register a complaint with that officer within two days of its receipt, the officer to enquire and take up the matter with the head of the establishment, who must act within the time the framework prescribes and maintain a register of complaints. The grievance officer is the Act's first instance forum inside the workplace, and the Commissioners' jurisdiction stands behind it when the internal route fails.

Around these provisions sits an older employment machinery that the Act retains and reorients. Section 22 requires every establishment to maintain records relating to persons with disabilities employed in it, in the form the Central Government prescribes, open to inspection by the prescribed authority; the records are what make reservation and non-discrimination auditable rather than declarative. Section 36 preserves the special employment exchange: the appropriate Government may by notification require employers in every establishment to furnish information or returns about vacancies appointed for persons with benchmark disabilities, occurring or about to occur, to the notified special employment exchange, and the establishment must comply. Section 24's unemployment allowance, payable to a person with disability registered with the Special Employment Exchange for more than two years who could not be placed in gainful occupation, is tied to the same register. The machinery is inherited from an era when the employment exchange was the labour market's clearing house, and its position inside a rights statute is instructive: Parliament kept the register because the distributive provisions of Chapter VI cannot be administered without a list of vacancies and claimants, even as the Act's centre of gravity moved to rights that need no register at all.

Chapter V gathers the welfare provisions: Section 24 on social security schemes within economic capacity and development, Section 25 on healthcare, including barrier free access to healthcare institutions, priority in attendance and treatment, and measures for prevention of disabilities; Section 26, by which the appropriate Government shall, by notification, make insurance schemes for its employees with disabilities; Section 27 on rehabilitation; Section 28 on research; Section 29 on culture and recreation; and Section 30 on sporting activities. The "within the limit of economic capacity and development" qualifier recurs across this chapter and is the textual hinge on which much of the chapter's enforceability turns; it is the 1995 Act's grammar surviving inside the 2016 Act's rights framework, and readers should register where it appears, because provisions carrying it are framed as programme duties rather than individual entitlements.

Section 25 repays separate statement because healthcare is where most citizens meet the Act outside education and employment. The appropriate Government and local authorities must take necessary measures for persons with disabilities to provide free healthcare in the vicinity, especially in rural areas, subject to such family income as may be notified; barrier free access in all parts of government and private hospitals and other healthcare institutions and centres; and priority in attendance and treatment. A second limb turns to prevention: governments must take measures for the prevention of the occurrence of disabilities, including surveys, investigation and research into the causes of disability, promotion of methods of prevention, screening of children for the purpose of identifying at risk cases, measures for pre-natal, perinatal and postnatal care of mother and child, and education and awareness. Two features distinguish the section from the chapter around it. The barrier free access duty is stated for private hospitals in terms, one of the points at which the Act reaches private service providers directly and without an economic capacity qualifier in the operative clause; and the prevention limb, inherited from the public health grammar of the predecessor statute, sits in visible tension with the rights chapters, since a state that speaks of preventing disability while guaranteeing the rights of disabled persons must be careful, as the Act's own structure is careful, to direct prevention at conditions and causes and never at persons. Section 24's social security schemes complete the material floor of the chapter, and the section contains one of the Act's few quantified welfare promises: the quantum of assistance to persons with disabilities under such schemes shall be at least twenty-five per cent higher than the similar schemes applicable to others. The schemes themselves must provide for community centres, facilities for persons with disabilities who have no family or have been abandoned, support during disasters and in areas of conflict, support to women with disability for livelihood and for the upbringing of their children, aids and appliances, medicine and diagnostic services and corrective surgery free of cost subject to a notified income ceiling, disability pension subject to a notified income ceiling, an unemployment allowance for persons registered with the Special Employment Exchange for more than two years who could not be placed, a care-giver allowance for persons with disabilities with high support needs, and an insurance scheme, of the breadth Section 24(3)(j) describes, for persons with disability not covered by the Employees' State Insurance schemes or other statutory or government sponsored insurance.

## Rehabilitation, culture, sport and research: the wider canvas of Chapter V

Chapter V is wider than its healthcare and social security provisions, and its remaining sections explain the kind of life the Act imagines its beneficiaries leading. Section 27 requires the appropriate Government and the local authorities, within their economic capacity and development, to undertake or cause to be undertaken services and programmes of rehabilitation, particularly in health, education and employment, for all persons with disabilities. Section 29 turns to culture and recreation: governments must take measures to promote and protect the rights of all persons with disabilities to have a cultural life and to participate in recreational activities on an equal basis, including access to cultural venues, the promotion of cultural activity in accessible formats, and the sponsorship of disability specific cultural programming. Section 30 addresses sport in the most operational language of the three, requiring measures to promote participation, including the restructuring of courses and programmes and the redesign of infrastructure, with duties laid on the sports authorities themselves to recognise the right of persons with disabilities to participate in sports and to provide for their inclusion in sporting schemes and programmes, and with the development of talent among its objects. These are programme duties carrying the economic capacity grammar of the chapter, and no one should mistake them for individual entitlements to a place in a team; but their presence in a statute whose predecessor confined itself to prevention, employment and aids marks the expansion of the statutory imagination from maintenance to participation, and the Commissioners treat exclusion from public cultural and sporting infrastructure as within their remit on the strength of them.

Research occupies a distinctive place in the design, because the Act distrusts it even while requiring it. Section 6, already noticed, bars research on a person with disability without free and informed consent and the prior permission of a committee on which persons with disabilities or their organisations hold at least half the seats, a direct response to a history in which disabled bodies were material for inquiry they did not govern. Section 28, on research and development, runs the constructive side: the appropriate Government must initiate or cause to be initiated research and development through individuals and institutions on issues which enhance habilitation and rehabilitation and on such other issues as are necessary for the empowerment of persons with disabilities, and Section 47, on human resource development, must build the professional pipeline, rehabilitation professionals, special educators, accessibility specialists, sign language interpreters, without which the service chapters are statements of intent. The definition of rehabilitation in Section 2(za) is itself programmatic: a process aimed at enabling persons with disabilities to attain and maintain optimal physical, sensory, intellectual, psychological and social function levels, a statement of ends in terms of the person's functioning in life rather than of the services administered to them. A pillar account should register the pattern: even where the Act legislates for services, it defines their ends in the vocabulary of participation rather than of cure.

## What the benchmark adds: Chapter VI and high support

Chapter VI is where the forty per cent threshold operates, and its provisions should be stated exactly, since percentages in this area are quoted from memory more often than from the text. Section 31 provides that, notwithstanding anything in the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act 2009, every child with benchmark disability between the ages of six and eighteen years shall have the right to free education in a neighbourhood school, or in a special school, of his choice, and the appropriate Government and local authorities shall ensure access to free education in an appropriate environment until the child attains eighteen. The choice between neighbourhood and special school is the statute's own, a deliberate departure from an uncompromising inclusion mandate, and it is placed with the child rather than the administration. Section 32 requires all government institutions of higher education and other higher education institutions receiving aid from the government to reserve not less than five per cent of seats for persons with benchmark disabilities, with an upper age relaxation of five years for admission. Section 33 requires the appropriate Government to identify the posts that can be held by each category of persons with benchmark disabilities, through an expert committee including representation of persons with benchmark disabilities, and to review the identified posts at intervals not exceeding three years. Section 34 then sets the employment reservation: every appropriate Government shall appoint in every government establishment not less than four per cent of the total number of vacancies in the cadre strength in each group of posts meant to be filled with persons with benchmark disabilities, one per cent each for blindness and low vision, for deaf and hard of hearing, and for locomotor disability including cerebral palsy, leprosy cured, dwarfism, acid attack victims and muscular dystrophy, and one per cent shared between autism, intellectual disability, specific learning disability and mental illness on the one hand and multiple disabilities, including deaf-blindness in the identified posts, on the other. A proviso leaves reservation in promotion to the instructions of the appropriate Government. Section 34(2) supplies the carry-forward rule where a vacancy cannot be filled for want of a suitable candidate, with interchange among the categories before a post may go to a person without disability.

Three qualifications complete the section's accurate statement. The second proviso to Section 34(1) permits the appropriate Government, in consultation with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner, to exempt a government establishment from the reservation, by notification and having regard to the type of work carried on in it; the exemption power is therefore inside the section, consultative and notified, not an administrative practice that can grow up unrecorded. Section 34(3) permits the appropriate Government to relax the upper age limit for employment of persons with benchmark disabilities by notification. And the identification duty in Section 33 is the load bearing wall of the whole structure: reservation attaches to vacancies in identified posts, so a government that delays its expert committee, or lets the three year review lapse, shrinks the reservation in practice without touching the percentage in the text, which is why the Supreme Court's reasoning under the predecessor statute, that failure to identify cannot defeat reservation, retains its importance under this Act.

The remaining sections of the chapter widen the preference beyond posts and seats. Section 35 requires the appropriate Government and local authorities, within the limit of their economic capacity and development, to provide incentives to employers in the private sector to ensure that at least five per cent of their workforce is composed of persons with benchmark disabilities. Section 36 concerns special employment exchanges. Section 37 requires schemes providing, among other things, five per cent reservation in allotment of agricultural land and housing in all relevant schemes and development programmes, five per cent reservation in all poverty alleviation and developmental schemes with priority to women with benchmark disabilities, and five per cent reservation in allotment of land at concessional rates where land is allotted for housing, shelter, occupation, business or enterprise, recreation centres and production centres. These are the provisions a benefits claimant actually lives on, and the detail of how the reservation is administered, carried forward and litigated belongs to [the article on reservation and accessibility under the RPwD Act](/2017/08/15/rpwd-reservation-accessibility-rights/), which owns that applied ground; the pillar's task is to fix the percentages and their statutory locations, which the preceding paragraphs have done.

Chapter VII consists of Section 38, the special provision for persons with disability having high support needs. Such a person, or any person or organisation on his behalf, may apply to an authority notified by the appropriate Government, and the authority refers the case to an Assessment Board constituted for the district or the state, which assesses the extent and type of support required and reports to the authority, which then arranges the support. The provision is brief and procedural, but it marks an important boundary in the Act's scheme: high support is the point at which the statute moves from rights and reservations to an individualised, assessed entitlement to support itself, and it is the provision families of persons with the most intensive needs reach for first.

## Duties of governments and the registration of institutions

Chapter VIII collects the obligations of the appropriate governments as programme duties: awareness campaigns run with the involvement of persons with disabilities, so that the values of the Act reach the public whose attitudes the definition of "barrier" includes; a social audit of all general schemes and programmes involving persons with disabilities under Section 48, conducted so that the schemes are assessed for their impact; and the framing of schemes to give effect to the Act. Chapter IX then builds a registration regime for institutions: no person may establish or maintain an institution for persons with disabilities without a certificate of registration from the competent authority appointed under Section 49, save for institutions maintained by the appropriate Government, and the chapter provides for application, grant, revocation and appeal. Existing institutions were deemed registered in the circumstances the chapter prescribes, and a person who maintains an institution without registration, or in contravention of the chapter, commits an offence dealt with in the penalties chapter. The registration chapter is the Act's quality gate over the residential and care sector, and its existence explains why the protection provisions of Chapter II and the registration provisions have to be read together when abuse inside an institution is alleged.

The chapter's mechanics repay statement, because they are the standards by which the sector is judged when a scandal breaks. Section 50 states the bar: no person shall establish or maintain an institution for persons with disabilities except in accordance with a certificate of registration issued by the competent authority appointed under Section 49, government institutions established or maintained by the Central or a State Government being exempted from the chapter by Section 54. Section 51 governs the grant: application in the form prescribed by the State Government, enquiry by the competent authority, and grant within ninety days of receipt where the authority is satisfied that the applicant has complied with the Act and the rules, no certificate being granted unless the institution is in a position to provide the facilities and meet the standards prescribed. The certificate runs for the prescribed period, renewal is to be sought not less than sixty days before expiry, and a copy must be displayed in a conspicuous place in the institution, a small provision with real content, since a family placing a relative is entitled to see the licence on the wall. Revocation follows where the holder has made a false statement in the application, has contravened the Act or the rules, or where the institution's functioning requires it, but only after an opportunity to show cause; on revocation the institution must cease to function, the competent authority may direct where the inmates are to be received, and the certificate must be surrendered. An appeal lies against refusal or revocation to the appellate authority the State Government notifies, whose order is final, and Section 55 empowers governments, within their economic capacity, to grant financial assistance to registered institutions. Read as a whole, the chapter converts registration from a formality into a continuing condition: standards at entry, a licence of limited duration, display, a show cause route to closure with protection for the residents on closure, and public money tied to registered status.

## Accessibility: duties with deadlines

Chapter VIII's second half, Sections 40 to 47, contains the Act's accessibility code, and it is drafted in mandatory terms. Section 40 empowers and directs the Central Government, in consultation with the Chief Commissioner, to formulate rules laying down standards of accessibility for the physical environment, transportation, and information and communication technology, and for other facilities and services. Section 41 requires measures for safe, accessible and affordable transport, including retrofitting of vehicles and personal mobility assistance. Section 42 requires that content available in audio, print and electronic media be made accessible, that electronic media provide audio description, sign language interpretation and close captioning, and that everyday electronic goods be available in universal design. Section 43 promotes the development and distribution of universally designed consumer products. Section 44 gives the standards their teeth at the planning gate: no establishment shall be granted permission to build any structure if the building plan does not adhere to the rules formulated under Section 40, and no establishment shall be issued a certificate of completion or allowed to take occupation of a building unless it has adhered to them.

The scope of the code is fixed by two definitions that readers habitually skip. A public building, under Section 2(w), is a government or private building used or accessed by the public at large, including buildings used for educational or vocational purposes, workplaces, commercial activities, public utilities, religious, cultural, leisure or recreational activities, medical or health services, law enforcement agencies, reformatories and judicial forums, railway stations, bus stands, airports and waterways; private ownership is no answer to the duty, because the trigger is public use. Public facilities and services, under Section 2(x), sweep in housing, education and training, employment and career advancement, shopping, religious and cultural activity, medical and rehabilitation services, banking, finance and insurance, communication and postal services, access to justice, public utilities and transportation. Sections 41 to 43 then work the code outward from buildings: Section 41 requires facilities at bus stops, railway stations and airports conforming to the accessibility standards, access to all modes of transport conforming to design standards including retrofitting of old modes where technically feasible and safe, economically viable and without major structural change, accessible roads, and schemes for personal mobility at affordable cost through incentives, retrofitting of vehicles and personal mobility assistance. Section 42 addresses information and communication: content in audio, print and electronic media in accessible format, access to electronic media through audio description, sign language interpretation and close captioning, and everyday electronic goods and equipment available in universal design, that expression itself being defined in Section 2(ze) as design usable by all people to the greatest extent possible without adaptation or specialised design. Section 43 requires measures to promote universally designed consumer products and accessories. The architecture is cumulative: standards under Section 40, a planning gate under Section 44, deadlines under Sections 45 and 46, and a perimeter under the definitions that reaches the private mall, the private hospital and the private broadcaster as surely as the government office.

Sections 45 and 46 then set the two clocks that dominate accessibility practice. Section 45 requires all existing public buildings to be made accessible in accordance with the rules within a period not exceeding five years from the date of notification of the rules, the Central Government being empowered to grant extensions to states case by case on grounds of preparedness, and requires governments and local authorities to publish a prioritised action plan covering essential services such as primary health centres, civil hospitals, schools, railway stations and bus stops. Section 46 requires service providers, whether government or private, to provide services in accordance with the accessibility rules within two years from the date of notification of those rules, again subject to extension for categories of services in consultation with the Chief Commissioner. The Rules were notified on 15 June 2017, so the service provider period ran to 15 June 2019 and the existing buildings period to 15 June 2022, subject to any extension granted. Section 47 adds the human resource obligations: mandatory training on disability in programmes for legislators, administrators, police, the judiciary and lawyers, the inclusion of disability and accessibility in educational curricula, capacity building for families and care-givers, and independence training for persons with disabilities.

### What is the deadline for making an existing public building accessible?

Section 45 requires all existing public buildings to be made accessible in accordance with the rules formulated under Section 40 within a period not exceeding five years from the notification of those rules. The Rules were notified on 15 June 2017, so the statutory period expired on 15 June 2022, subject to case by case extensions for states.

The deadlines would matter less if the standards against which they run were beyond dispute, and for years they were not. Rule 15 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules 2017 incorporated accessibility guidelines issued by various ministries, and did so in language the Supreme Court eventually held to be recommendatory rather than mandatory. In Rajive Raturi v Union of India, a public interest petition filed in 2005 by a petitioner with visual impairment and pursued across nearly two decades of compliance orders, a bench of Chandrachud CJ, Pardiwala and Misra JJ held on 8 November 2024 that several of the guidelines prescribed in Rule 15 were recommendatory guidelines under the garb of mandatory rules, that Rule 15(1) was accordingly ultra vires the scheme and legislative intent of the Act, which creates a mechanism for mandatory compliance, and that creating a minimum floor of accessibility cannot be left to what the Court called the altar of progressive realisation. The Union Government was directed to delineate the mandatory rules required by Section 40 within three months, segregating the non-negotiable rules from the wider guidelines, in consultation with stakeholders and with the Centre for Disability Studies at NALSAR University of Law, and the Court directed that once the mandatory rules were prescribed, the consequences in Sections 44, 45, 46 and 89, including the withholding of completion certificates and the imposition of fines, be implemented in cases of non-compliance. The judgment matters beyond its directions: it locates the Act's accessibility failure neither in Parliament's drafting nor primarily in the states, but in a rule-making exercise that had converted a mandatory statutory duty into guidance, and it did so on the Act's own text. The applied and litigated dimensions of accessibility are developed in the companion article already linked from the benchmark chapter; the pillar records the duties, the dates and the holding.

The accessibility chapter does not stand on sanction alone, and two companion provisions complete its design. Section 39 requires the appropriate Government, in consultation with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner, to conduct and promote awareness campaigns and sensitisation programmes, whose content the section specifies: the programmes must promote values of inclusion, tolerance, empathy and respect for diversity, advance recognition of the skills and abilities of persons with disabilities, foster respect for the decisions persons with disabilities make on family life and relationships, and carry orientation on disability rights into schools, colleges, universities and professional training, including the curricula of universities, colleges and schools. Section 48 requires social audit of all general schemes and programmes involving persons with disabilities, to ensure that the schemes do not impact them adversely and that their requirements and concerns are met. Read together, the chapter's logic is that barriers are physical, informational and attitudinal at once, and that a state which ramps its buildings while leaving its curricula and its audit practice untouched has complied with the visible third of the duty. The Accessible India Campaign, the access audit driven programme of the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities that preceded the Act and was absorbed into its implementation, is the administrative expression of the same idea: audit first, retrofit against the audit, and report against the deadlines the statute then supplied.

## Certification: how benchmark status is proved

Chapter X, Sections 56 to 59, builds the machinery by which the narrower population is identified. Section 56 requires the Central Government to notify guidelines for evaluation and assessment of the specified disabilities, the clinical instruments by which a percentage is arrived at for conditions defined in measurable terms and by which assessment proceeds for conditions that are not. Section 57 provides for the designation of certifying authorities: the appropriate Government designates, by notification, the medical authorities competent to issue certificates for particular districts and particular disabilities. Section 58 prescribes the procedure: an application by the person with disability, or by a parent or guardian in the cases covered, assessment by the certifying authority, and issue of a certificate of disability in the prescribed form recording the percentage and the nature of the disability. Section 59 provides the appeal: a person aggrieved by a decision of the certifying authority may appeal to an appellate authority designated by the appropriate Government, whose decision is final within the scheme. Certificates for benchmark status record forty per cent or more, and the certificate is the document that Chapter VI's benefits are administered against. Section 58 also disciplines the negative case: where the assessment finds no specified disability, the certifying authority must inform the applicant in writing, so that a refusal is a reasoned, appealable outcome and not a silence the applicant cannot challenge.

The machinery in daily operation is medical and district based. Certifying authorities are the notified medical authorities, in practice the district medical boards and designated government hospitals, with specialists in the relevant discipline assessing the condition presented. That structure produces the system's known geography: an applicant whose district lacks the relevant specialist, or whose board sits infrequently, waits; an applicant near a major government hospital is assessed quickly; and identical conditions can meet boards of different composition in different districts. The Act's answer to error within that structure is the Section 59 appeal, and its answer to inconsistency is the uniformity of the Section 56 guidelines, which bind every board to the same instruments. What the scheme does not contain is any time limit in the Act itself for the disposal of a certificate application, a silence that matters because every Chapter VI benefit queues behind the document: a delayed certificate delays free education admission, reservation in a recruitment cycle that will not reopen, and scheme benefits with their own application windows. The Commissioners' complaint jurisdiction is the remedy applicants in fact use when certification stalls, since a stalled certificate is, in substance, a denial of the benefits the certificate gates, and State Commissioners have treated certification delay as within their inquiry remit on that footing.

Alongside the statutory certificate runs an administrative project that readers encounter constantly and should not confuse with it. The Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities administers the Unique ID for Persons with Disabilities project, which issues a UDID card, also called the Swavlamban card, generated through a national portal on the basis of the disability certificate, and designed to function as a single document of identity and disability recognised across states and across schemes. The distinction matters in both directions. The UDID card does not replace the certificate as the legal proof of benchmark status; it is built on the certificate and carries its data. And a person who holds a certificate but not the card holds the substantive entitlement, since the certificate, not the card, is what the Act prescribes. For a person pursuing a reserved post, a scholarship or a scheme benefit, the paperwork is evidence in a legal claim, and keeping the certificate, the application records, the medical papers and the correspondence together, in the kind of private file a reader might keep [organised and source protected on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), is the practical difference between an entitlement that can be proved on demand and one that has to be reconstructed under time pressure.

Two features of certification deserve emphasis because they generate a large share of real grievances. The first is that the percentage is an assessment outcome, not a measurement the applicant controls: guidelines assign percentages to defined conditions and degrees, and for conditions not defined in measurable terms the assessment is a clinical judgment recorded against the guidelines, which is why Section 2(r) is drafted in the alternative. The second is that certification is district anchored while lives are not: a person certified in one district who moves faces the practical question whether the certificate travels. The Act's scheme treats the certificate as the record of an assessment under a national statute, and the UDID project exists precisely to make that record portable in practice; where an authority refuses to recognise a certificate from another district or state, the refusal is an administrative act open to challenge before the Commissioners discussed in the next section, and the general information in this article is no substitute for advice on the facts of a particular refusal.

## The subordinate legislation: what the Rules and guidelines carry

Much of the Act's operative content sits one level below the sections, and a pillar treatment should map that level accurately. Sections 100 and 101 divide the rule making power: the Central Government makes rules on, among other subjects, the manner of notifying equal opportunity policies, the maintenance of records and complaint registers, the returns establishments must furnish to special employment exchanges, the composition and assessment manner of Assessment Boards under Section 38, the accessibility standards under Section 40, the form and manner of applications for certificates and the form of the certificate itself, the advisory boards' procedure, the Commissioners' conditions of service, and the procedure, utilisation and management of the National Fund. State Governments make rules on, among other subjects, the manner of providing support for limited guardianship, the registration of institutions and the facilities and standards they must meet, the appeal periods under the certification chapter, State Commissioner conditions, Special Public Prosecutor fees, and the constitution, utilisation and management of the State Fund, and Section 101 required those state rules within six months of commencement. Two instruments below even the Rules carry decisive weight. The guidelines for evaluation and assessment notified under Section 56 are, in substance, the benefits code of the Act, since the percentages they assign decide benchmark status for every described condition; and the standards formulated under Section 40, whose conversion into mandatory rules the Supreme Court directed in 2024, decide what accessibility means in concrete. A reader advising on this statute must therefore work at three levels: the sections for the structure, the rules for the procedure, and the guidelines for the numbers. Both levels of rules are subject to legislative laying, the Central rules before Parliament and the State rules before the State Legislature, with modification or annulment possible in the manner Sections 100 and 101 prescribe, so the subordinate code is neither unaccountable nor easily displaced.

## The enforcement architecture: boards, Commissioners, Special Courts and Funds

Chapters XI to XV build the institutions, and their design tells the reader where the Act expects pressure to be applied. Chapter XI establishes advisory boards: a Central Advisory Board on Disability under Section 60, chaired by the Union Minister in charge of the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities and including state ministers, parliamentarians, experts and representatives of persons with disabilities, charged under Section 65 with the national level functions of advising on policy, developing a national policy for persons with disabilities, and reviewing and coordinating the work of departments and organisations; State Advisory Boards under Section 66 performing the corresponding functions within states; and District-level Committees on disability under Section 72, constituted by State Governments to perform prescribed functions, which include the local monitoring of the Act's schemes. These bodies advise and coordinate; they do not adjudicate, and confusing their policy role with the Commissioners' quasi-judicial role is a recurring source of misdirected complaints.

The composition of the two Commissioner offices is part of their design and worth stating. The Chief Commissioner is supported by two Commissioners, at least one of whom must be a person with disability, and by an advisory committee of experts drawn from the different fields of disability, whose composition the Act leaves to appointment by the Central Government; the State Commissioner is supported by a corresponding state advisory committee. The qualifications regime looks to experience in disability and rehabilitation, and the tenure and conditions of service are prescribed. In practice the offices combine three kinds of work that sit uneasily together: case work on individual complaints, systemic review of laws and schemes, and the ceremonial and awareness functions that Section 39's campaigns draw them into. The advisory boards, by contrast, exist to keep disability inside the ordinary policy process of governments: the Central Advisory Board's membership spans the ministries whose decisions create barriers, finance, education, health, transport, housing, labour and others, with experts and representatives of persons with disabilities, and its statutory functions include advising the Central Government on policies and programmes, developing a national policy, and reviewing and coordinating the activities of departments and of non-governmental organisations dealing with disability. The district committee is the design's local terminus, charged with the prescribed district functions, and it is the body a district administration is expected to convene when a scheme, a survey of school children under Section 16, or a disaster record under Section 8 needs local execution.

Chapter XII creates the Commissioners. The Central Government appoints a Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities and two Commissioners under Section 74, at least one of the Commissioners being a person with disability, and the State Governments appoint State Commissioners under Section 79. The functions of the Chief Commissioner under Section 75 include identifying, suo motu or otherwise, provisions of any law or policy or programme inconsistent with the Act and recommending corrective steps; inquiring, suo motu or on complaint, into deprivation of rights of persons with disabilities and non-implementation of laws, rules and schemes; reviewing the safeguards provided by the Constitution and by law; monitoring the implementation of the Act and of schemes and programmes; and promoting research and awareness. Section 76 gives the recommendation a timetable: whenever the Chief Commissioner makes a recommendation to an authority, that authority shall take necessary action on it and inform the Chief Commissioner of the action taken within three months from the date of receipt of the recommendation; and where an authority does not accept a recommendation, it must convey its reasons for non-acceptance to the Chief Commissioner within that period and inform the person aggrieved. Sections 78 and 83 complete the accountability loop by requiring annual and special reports from the Chief Commissioner and the State Commissioners, to be laid before Parliament and the State Legislatures respectively, so that the inquiry work of the offices enters the public record of the legislature that created them. Section 77 gives the teeth: the Chief Commissioner, while inquiring into any matter, has the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908, including summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, requiring the discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records, and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses and documents, and proceedings before the Chief Commissioner are deemed to be judicial proceedings for the purposes the section names. The State Commissioner exercises the corresponding functions and powers within the state under Sections 80 to 82. The offices are therefore neither courts nor mere ombudsmen: they are inquiry bodies with compulsory process, whose recommendations carry institutional weight but whose orders do not execute themselves, a design whose strengths and limits the assessment section weighs.

Chapter XIII addresses criminal enforcement. Section 84 requires the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to specify for each district a Court of Session as a Special Court to try offences under the Act, for the purpose of speedy trial, and Section 85 provides for a Special Public Prosecutor for every Special Court, specified or appointed from advocates of not less than seven years' practice. Chapter XIV constitutes the National Fund for persons with disabilities under Section 86, into which are credited the sums previously held in the former National Fund under the repealed regime, moneys provided by Parliament, contributions and grants, and income from investments, the Fund being applied to the welfare purposes the chapter and the rules prescribe and audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General. Chapter XV constitutes a State Fund on the corresponding pattern under Section 88, to be constituted by each State Government in the prescribed manner, utilised and managed as prescribed, and likewise audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General with accounts laid before the State Legislature.

## The offences

Chapter XVI converts specified conduct into crime, and its provisions divide into four tiers that readers should keep distinct. Section 89 is the general penalty: a person who contravenes any provision of the Act or of the rules or regulations made under it is punishable, for a first contravention, with fine which may extend to ten thousand rupees, and for a subsequent contravention with fine which shall not be less than fifty thousand rupees but which may extend to five lakh rupees. The general penalty is financial only, and its modesty is a standing criticism of the Act, though Section 89 is a residual provision and the grave conduct is dealt with elsewhere in the chapter. Section 90 attributes offences committed by companies to the persons in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business, subject to the due diligence defence the section contains. Section 91 punishes the fraud that the benchmark system invites: whoever fraudulently avails or attempts to avail any benefit meant for persons with benchmark disabilities is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, or with both.

Section 92 is the chapter's centre: the offences of atrocity against a person with disability. It punishes whoever intentionally insults or intimidates with intent to humiliate a person with disability in any place within public view; assaults or uses force against a person with disability with intent to dishonour him or to outrage the modesty of a woman with disability; having the actual charge or control over a person with disability, voluntarily or knowingly denies food or fluids to him or her; being in a position to dominate the will of a child or woman with disability, uses that position to exploit her sexually; voluntarily injures, damages or interferes with the use of any limb or sense or any supporting device of a person with disability; or performs, conducts or directs a medical procedure on a woman with disability which leads to or is likely to lead to termination of pregnancy without her express consent, save in severe cases with the opinion of a registered medical practitioner and the consent of her guardian. The punishment for each is imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than six months but which may extend to five years, and fine. Two observations belong in a pillar treatment. The offence protects any person with disability, not only a person with benchmark disability; the victim's percentage is again irrelevant to liability. And the offence overlaps the general penal code without displacing it, Section 96 preserving the operation of other laws, so that conduct may be prosecuted under the general law, under this Act, or under both regimes, and Section 95 provides that where an act or omission is an offence under this Act and under another law, the offender found guilty is liable to punishment only under the law that provides the punishment greater in degree. Section 93 punishes failure to furnish information, returns or documents required under the Act with fine which may extend to twenty-five thousand rupees, with a further daily fine for continuing failure, and Section 94 requires the previous sanction of the appropriate Government before a court takes cognizance of an offence under the chapter alleged against an employee of that government, except where the complaint is filed by an authorised officer.

The chapter's balance has been criticised from both directions, and the pillar should record the criticism accurately. From one side, the general penalty in Section 89, ten thousand rupees for a first contravention of any provision of the Act, is said to price non-compliance for a large establishment at a figure no finance department will notice, leaving the Commissioners' recommendations and the Section 44 completion certificate lever to do the real work of enforcement; the five lakh ceiling for repeat contraventions mitigates but does not answer the point. From the other side, Section 94's sanction requirement for prosecuting government employees is said to shield precisely the officials whose defaults, an unbuilt ramp, an unfilled reserved vacancy, an unexamined certificate application, constitute the commonest violations, by routing prosecution through the government complained against. The countervailing considerations are real in both cases: Section 89 is a residual penalty behind the atrocity and fraud provisions, which carry imprisonment, and sanction requirements are a standard protection against vexatious prosecution of officials for acts in discharge of duty. What is harder to defend is the chapter's silence on compensation: unlike several comparable welfare statutes, the Act gives the victim of a Section 92 offence no statutory compensation route within the Act itself, leaving reparation to the general criminal procedure and to constitutional tort claims of the kind made in Jeeja Ghosh.

## What the courts have added

The statute's two-population structure made one judicial question inevitable: when an institution confines a facility to benchmark disability, and the claimant's right is a Chapter II right, which definition governs? The Supreme Court answered in Vikash Kumar v Union Public Service Commission, (2021) 5 SCC 370, decided on 11 February 2021 in a judgment authored by Dr D. Y. Chandrachud J. The petitioner had a chronic neurological condition, writer's cramp, which made writing by hand extremely difficult, and sought a scribe for the civil services examination; the facility was refused because he did not have a benchmark disability, and the examination rules restricted scribes to specified categories. The Court held that the provision of a scribe fell within reasonable accommodation, that reasonable accommodation is a substantive duty and a component of the right to equality rather than a concession dispensed as charity, that the definition of discrimination, which includes denial of reasonable accommodation, is not confined to persons with benchmark disabilities, and that to restrict the facility to the benchmark class would contradict the scheme of a statute that confers the anti-discrimination right on persons with disabilities generally. The judgment is the pillar's most important case because it validates the article's central reading from the highest authority: the benchmark line sorts benefits, and it does not gate rights.

The reasoning repays attention because it supplies the method for the next case. The Court treated equality under the Act as substantive rather than formal: a rule that distributes an examination facility by certificate category is not neutral merely because it applies to everyone in the category, since the facility exists to neutralise a disadvantage that the category does not measure. It located reasonable accommodation inside the definition of discrimination itself, so that a respondent cannot concede the duty and contest the remedy; denial is the discrimination. It drew on the Convention and on comparative practice to hold that the benchmark classification, valid for the distributive provisions for which Parliament designed it, could not be imported into a rights provision whose text contains no such limit; and it overruled the contrary Madras view in V. Surendra Mohan v Union of India, (2019) 4 SCC 237, which had sustained a benchmark linked restriction. The directions that followed, requiring the examination body to frame its scribe policy accordingly, illustrate the remedial pattern of the case law under this Act: the courts rarely award the individual facility and stop; they require the policy that produced the refusal to be rewritten, which is why single judgments under this statute tend to change the position of whole cohorts.

Avni Prakash v National Testing Agency, decided the same year, applied the same structure to compensatory time and scribe facilities in a professional entrance examination for a candidate whose disability was likewise below the benchmark framing the agency had applied. The line of examination cases is best understood as the courts policing the boundary between the two definitions at the exact point where administrators most often blur it, because an examination body experiences the certificate as a fraud control and the Court experiences the same demand as a rights violation where the facility sought is an accommodation.

Two further decisions fill out the picture. In Jeeja Ghosh v Union of India, (2016) 7 SCC 761, decided before the Act commenced but on the rights logic it codifies, the Court awarded compensation to a passenger with cerebral palsy who had been offloaded from a flight, treating the episode as a violation of dignity and equality and directing attention to the regulatory framework for carriage of passengers with disabilities; the case is routinely cited for the proposition that dignitary harm to a person with disability is a legal injury, not a discourtesy. In Disabled Rights Group v Union of India, (2018) 2 SCC 397, the Court issued directions on accessibility in institutions of higher education, including the reservation of seats and accessible infrastructure, pursuing the implementation of disability entitlements through continuing supervision of the responsible authorities. Under the predecessor statute, Union of India v National Federation of the Blind, (2013) 10 SCC 772, had already established that the three per cent reservation of the 1995 Act bound the state in promotions as the Court read that Act and that the state could not defeat the reservation by failing to identify posts, a discipline the 2016 Act absorbed by imposing the identification duty in terms in Section 33. The Rajive Raturi judgment of 8 November 2024, set out in the accessibility section, completes the sequence on the standards question. Taken together, the case law under and around this statute does one consistent thing: it refuses to let administrative convenience rewrite the distribution of rights and benefits that the text enacts.

One further line of authority concerns the promotion proviso in Section 34(1), which left reservation in promotion to instructions of the appropriate Government. In State of Kerala v Leesamma Joseph, decided in 2021, the Supreme Court held that persons with disabilities are entitled to reservation in promotion under the Act, reading the proviso as regulating the manner of a reservation that the statute's scheme, and India's obligations under the Convention, require, rather than as leaving the existence of the reservation to executive choice; a Constitution Bench in Siddaraju v State of Karnataka, decided in 2020, had earlier upheld the permissibility of reservation in promotion for persons with disabilities under the predecessor framework. The pair of decisions matter for the pillar because they fix the proviso's meaning: an instruction dependent provision in a benefits chapter is to be read, where the Act's scheme supports it, as a direction to regulate rather than a licence to withhold, and governments that issue no instructions do not thereby suspend the entitlement the Court found in the scheme.

## The argument over the forty per cent line, stated fairly

The threshold has a serious defence, and the stance of this article requires it to be put properly. Distributive benefits are scarce and rivalrous: a reserved seat given to one candidate is not available to another, and a scheme benefit paid to one claimant is not paid to another. Distribution on that scale requires a criterion that can be verified by thousands of officials, applied uniformly across districts, and defended against fraud, and a percentage assessed by a medical authority against notified guidelines is such a criterion in a way that an individualised barriers assessment is not. A barriers test for benefits would invite inconsistency between districts, litigation over every assessment, and capture by the articulate; a percentage, for all its crudity, is auditable. Defenders of the threshold add that the Act does not leave the sub-benchmark person remediless: the entire rights apparatus, which is where most disability disadvantage is actually produced, in inaccessible buildings, refused accommodations and discriminatory refusals, is available without a certificate. On this view the forty per cent line is not a judgment about whose disability counts but an administrative device for rationing the benefits that cannot be universal, and the correct reform target is the generosity of the benefits and the accuracy of assessment, not the existence of the line.

The genealogy of the figure sharpens both sides of the argument. Forty per cent is not a number the 2016 Parliament derived from the Convention, which contains no percentage at all; it is inherited. The 1995 Act defined a person with disability as a person suffering from not less than forty per cent of any disability as certified by a medical authority, and for twenty years that single, certificate based definition did all the work: below it, a person was outside the statute altogether. The 2016 Act's originality lies not in choosing the figure but in demoting it. Parliament retained forty per cent as the key to the benefits it had to ration, the function the figure had always performed in practice, and stripped it of the function it should never have performed, defining who counts as disabled in law. Critics of the threshold must therefore aim at the right target: the objection is not that Parliament invented a medical gate in 2016, but that it kept one, when the committee process had put more individualised assessment models for benefits on the table. Defenders reply that the models on the table were never costed at national scale, and that a known, imperfect gate is preferable to an elegant test that collapses in the district. The pillar records the disagreement without pretending the text resolves it, because the text takes a side only on the prior question: whatever the benefits test, it is not the definition of disability.

The objection answers at the level of the statute's own logic. Section 2(s) defines disability as an interaction: the same impairment is disabling or not depending on the barriers it meets. A percentage measures impairment only. The benefit criterion and the statutory definition therefore measure different things, and the mismatch produces the hard cases at the boundary: a person with a modest measured impairment whose work is destroyed by a specific barrier, and a person with a high measured impairment whose environment accommodates it fully. The first is precisely the claimant the barriers definition was written to capture, and the benefits chapter passes him by. Critics add two practical points. Assessment under the notified guidelines is itself uneven between districts and specialties, so the claimed uniformity of the percentage is partly an aspiration; and percentages harden into identity, so that the certificate, designed as a rationing device for Chapter VI, migrates in administrative practice into a precondition for Chapter II rights, which the Supreme Court had to correct in Vikash Kumar. The verdict this article reaches is that the mismatch is real but structural rather than accidental: a rights-based definition and a rationed benefits system cannot be fused into one test, and Parliament's solution, two tests kept apart, is defensible in design. What is not defensible is the administrative habit of collapsing the two, and every institution applying the Act can be audited against that single distinction.

A third line of criticism, distinct from both, targets not the line's location but the instrument that draws it. Certification is a medical event: the applicant attends a board, is examined, and receives a number. For conditions that are episodic, fluctuating or primarily psychosocial, the event measures the person on the day, and repeated assessment for time limited certificates imposes a recurring burden on precisely the claimants least equipped to carry it, a burden documented in the sector's reporting on certification camps and district board availability. The defence of the instrument is the defence of the line itself, that no alternative scales; but the criticism identifies a genuine design cost, and the Act's own answer, the appeal in Section 59 and the high support assessment in Section 38, which looks at the support a person needs rather than only the percentage a board assigns, shows the statute reaching, at its margins, for the functional measure its benefits chapter declined to adopt as the general test.

The burden qualifier inside reasonable accommodation deserves the same candour. The definition excuses an accommodation that would impose a disproportionate or undue burden, and Indian authority gives duty-holders and rights-holders very little guidance on how that burden is to be measured: against what baseline of resources, over what period, with what weight given to the size of the establishment and the availability of public support. A large government establishment and a small private firm face the same verbal formula with radically different real capacities, and until appellate courts or rules give the qualifier content, both sides of an accommodation dispute negotiate in fog. Vikash Kumar settles that accommodation is a duty; it does not settle the duty's outer edge, and honest exposition should say so.

## The implementation record

A pillar article must record what the record shows, with attribution, and the pattern across the Act's own reporting is consistent enough to state without manufacturing figures. The Census of 2011, conducted under the predecessor regime but measuring the population this Act serves, counted 2.68 crore persons with disabilities, 2.21 per cent of the population, a proportion widely regarded in the sector as an undercount against international prevalence estimates, which is itself relevant to how scheme budgets are set. On the machinery, reports of the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities and answers given in Parliament over the years since commencement have repeatedly recorded vacancies in, and delayed appointments to, the offices of State Commissioners in a number of states, additional charge arrangements substituting for dedicated appointments, and Special Courts under Section 84 constituted in many districts by designating an existing Court of Session rather than by creating a dedicated forum, which satisfies the section's words while diluting its purpose of speedy, sensitised trial. Advisory boards in several states have met irregularly. The accessibility deadlines in Sections 45 and 46 expired with compliance far from complete, a state of affairs the Supreme Court's continuing supervision in Rajive Raturi documents in detail, and the deeper defect the Court identified in 2024 was not municipal laziness but the conversion of mandatory standards into guidelines at the rule-making stage.

The certification system shows the same pattern of a sound scheme under administrative strain: assessment depends on notified medical boards whose availability varies sharply by district, the guidelines assign percentages with varying precision across the twenty-one entries, and the UDID project, designed to produce one portable record, has had to be built on top of a certificate base that was itself incomplete. None of this is an argument that the Act was misconceived. It is the record of an enforcement gap between a statute whose rights are well specified and an administrative apparatus whose staffing, funding and habits were formed under a welfare statute with weaker demands.

The federal design of the Act explains part of the variation. Relief of the disabled is a subject on which states have historically legislated, and Parliament enacted this statute to implement an international convention, the route by which the law operates uniformly across the Union while leaving implementation, state rules, state funds, state advisory boards and state Commissioners, to the states. Section 101 required State Governments to make their rules not later than six months from the commencement of the Act, and the record of the years that followed is of states notifying rules at very different speeds, with the consequence that provisions dependent on state prescription, from the manner of limited guardianship applications to the constitution of the State Fund, operated on different timetables in different states under a single national text. Implementation status reporting by the National Centre for Promotion of Employment for Disabled People, the sector's longest running monitoring exercise, has documented the same unevenness across successive assessments: states differ by years in constituting their advisory boards, in appointing dedicated State Commissioners, and in notifying the expert committees on which Section 33 identification depends. For the citizen the consequence is concrete: the rights in Chapter II are national and uniform, while the benefits machinery that delivers Chapter VI runs at the speed of the state the citizen lives in, and a pillar account of the Act that ignored that variation would misdescribe the law in action. The gap is the Act's central practical fact, and it is why the Commissioners' inquiry powers, the Commissioners being the one institution in the Act designed to generate pressure continuously and on their own motion, matter more in practice than their recommendatory status suggests.

External review has recorded the same gap from the treaty side. The Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, in its concluding observations on the initial report of India adopted in 2019, welcomed the Act while expressing concern at, among other matters, the persistence of guardianship regimes permitting substituted decision making, the pace of accessibility implementation, and the situation of persons with disabilities in institutions, and it recommended movement toward supported decision making and strengthened enforcement. The observations are not law, and this article does not treat them as such; their value to the reader is diagnostic, since the three concerns the treaty body selected are the same three this pillar's own reading of the domestic record identifies: legal capacity where the National Trust regime survives, accessibility where deadlines have run, and institutional living where Section 5's community right meets an inherited estate of residential institutions.

## The Act in one life: a worked passage through the statute

The two-population structure is easiest to audit in a single worked passage, following one citizen through provisions drawn from both registers. Take a student with a specific learning disability, certified at forty per cent. Her benchmark status opens Chapter VI: Section 31 gave her free education to eighteen in a school of her choice, and Section 32 reserves five per cent of seats in the government and aided institutions of higher education to which she applies, with five years of age relaxation. But the facilities she needs while studying sit in the rights register: the inclusive education duties of Section 16 bind her institution to admit her without discrimination and to make the physical and learning environment accessible, Section 17's measures include the provision of scribes and compensatory time in examinations, and if an examination body were to refuse a scribe because her certificate recorded a percentage the body considered the wrong kind, Vikash Kumar and the examination cases answer the refusal without any reference to Chapter VI at all. Her status under the Act changes as the question changes, and the institutions dealing with her err when they treat the certificate as answering every question.

Take a second citizen, a government clerk who acquires a locomotor disability in service, assessed at thirty per cent. He holds no benchmark status and Chapter VI is closed to him, and almost everything he needs is still in the Act. Section 20(4) forbids his establishment to dispense with his services or reduce him in rank; if he cannot hold his former post he is to be shifted to another post on the same pay scale and service benefits, and if no post is available he is held on a supernumerary post until one is or until he superannuates. Section 21's equal opportunity policy, notified by his establishment and registered with the Commissioner, states the measures the establishment has undertaken; Section 23's grievance redressal officer is his first forum; Section 3 and the definition of discrimination in Section 2(h) govern any refusal of the reasonable accommodation he requests, a reallocated desk, an accessible workstation, a transfer to a ground floor office. If his office building is an existing public building, Section 45's period governed its retrofit, and his complaint about a lift that was never installed lies to the State Commissioner whether or not he holds any certificate. The two worked passages make the article's claim in concrete form: the certificate sorted the first citizen into a benefits class and was irrelevant to nearly every protection the second citizen needed, and an administrator who asked the second citizen for a benchmark certificate before acting on Section 20(4) would have invented a condition the statute does not contain.

## The verdict of this pillar

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 is a genuinely two-part statute, and it should be read, taught and administered as one. Its rights part is universal within the class of persons with disabilities and is enforceable without any certificate; its benefits part is rationed by certification at forty per cent of a specified disability and is meaningless if the certification machinery fails. The Act's drafting sustains that reading at every point the courts have tested, and the Supreme Court's contributions, in Vikash Kumar on the boundary, in Rajive Raturi on mandatory standards, and across the examination cases, have been to hold administrators to the distribution the text enacts. The statute's weaknesses are of two kinds only: the burden qualifier in reasonable accommodation, which awaits content, and the implementation gap, which awaits administrative will. Neither weakness is a drafting failure of the two-population design itself. The reader who leaves with one rule has the Act: rights need no benchmark; benefits do; and any demand for a certificate in a rights case is an error that the statute, and the Court, have already corrected.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which disabilities are specified under the RPwD Act?

The Schedule to the Act specifies twenty-one disabilities in five groups. Physical disability covers locomotor disability, including leprosy cured persons, cerebral palsy, dwarfism, muscular dystrophy and acid attack victims; visual impairment, comprising blindness and low vision; hearing impairment, comprising deaf and hard of hearing; and speech and language disability. Intellectual disability includes specific learning disabilities and autism spectrum disorder. Mental behaviour contributes mental illness. Disability caused by chronic neurological conditions includes multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease, and disability caused by blood disorders includes haemophilia, thalassemia and sickle cell disease. Multiple disabilities, including deaf-blindness, complete the list. The Central Government may notify further disabilities.

### Q: What is a person with benchmark disability?

Section 2(r) defines a person with benchmark disability as a person with not less than forty per cent of a specified disability. Where the specified disability has been defined in measurable terms, in the Schedule or under the assessment guidelines, the percentage is certified by the certifying authority designated under the Act. Where it has not been defined in measurable terms, the forty per cent is assessed against the notified guidelines for evaluation and assessment. Benchmark status is the gateway to the distributive provisions of Chapter VI: free education for children between six and eighteen, reservation of five per cent of seats in government and aided higher education, and reservation of four per cent of vacancies in government employment.

### Q: What is reasonable accommodation and is it a legal duty?

Reasonable accommodation is defined in Section 2(y) as necessary and appropriate modification and adjustments, without imposing a disproportionate or undue burden in a particular case, to ensure that persons with disabilities enjoy or exercise rights equally with others. It is a legal duty, not a concession. Section 2(h) defines discrimination to include denial of reasonable accommodation, and Section 3 prohibits discrimination on the ground of disability. In Vikash Kumar v Union Public Service Commission, (2021) 5 SCC 370, the Supreme Court held that reasonable accommodation is a substantive component of equality, that its denial is discrimination, and that the duty is owed to persons with disabilities generally and not only to persons with benchmark disabilities.

### Q: What rights does the RPwD Act give in healthcare and family life?

Section 25 requires measures for healthcare, including barrier free access to public healthcare institutions, priority in attendance and treatment, and steps for the prevention of disabilities. Section 9 protects home and family: no child with disability shall be separated from parents on the ground of disability except by order of a competent court in the child's best interest, and where parents cannot care for the child the court places the child with near relations or within the community in a family setting. Section 10 guarantees access to information on reproduction and family planning and provides that no person with disability shall be subjected to any medical procedure leading to infertility without free and informed consent.

### Q: How does limited guardianship differ from full guardianship?

Under the plenary or full guardianship displaced by the Act, a guardian was substituted for the person and took decisions on the person's behalf across the whole field of life. Limited guardianship under Section 14 is a system of joint decision making which operates on mutual understanding and trust, is limited to a specific period and to specific decisions and situations, and operates in accordance with the will of the person with disability. It is granted by a District Court or designated authority only where adequate and appropriate support has been provided and the person remains unable to take legally binding decisions. Every guardian appointed under any other law is deemed to function as a limited guardian from the Act's commencement.

### Q: Who enforces the RPwD Act and what powers do they have?

Enforcement is led by the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities at the Centre and State Commissioners in the states, appointed under Sections 74 and 79. They may inquire, on their own motion or on complaint, into deprivation of rights and non-implementation of the Act, review safeguards, monitor schemes and recommend corrective steps. While inquiring, each has the powers of a civil court: summoning witnesses, requiring production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits and requisitioning public records, and proceedings before them are judicial proceedings. Offences are tried by Special Courts, being Courts of Session specified for each district under Section 84, with Special Public Prosecutors. Executive Magistrates hold the emergency protection powers under Section 7.

### Q: What offences does the RPwD Act create?

Section 89 imposes a general penalty for contravention of the Act or its rules: a fine up to ten thousand rupees for a first contravention and, for a subsequent contravention, a fine of not less than fifty thousand rupees extending to five lakh rupees. Section 91 punishes fraudulently availing a benefit meant for persons with benchmark disabilities with imprisonment up to two years, or fine up to one lakh rupees, or both. Section 92 creates the offences of atrocity: intentional insult or intimidation to humiliate in public view, assault, denial of food or fluids by a person in charge, sexual exploitation by a person dominating the will of a child or woman with disability, injury to a limb, sense or supporting device, and a medical procedure terminating pregnancy without express consent, punishable with imprisonment of six months to five years and fine.

### Q: How is a disability certificate obtained?

A person applies to the certifying authority designated by the appropriate Government for the district and the disability concerned, under Sections 57 and 58. Certifying authorities are medical authorities notified for the purpose, and assessment follows the guidelines for evaluation and assessment of specified disabilities notified under Section 56, which assign percentages to conditions defined in measurable terms and govern assessment of conditions that are not. The authority issues a certificate of disability in the prescribed form recording the nature and percentage of the disability. A person aggrieved by the certifying authority's decision may appeal to the appellate authority designated under Section 59. The certificate recording forty per cent or more is the proof of benchmark disability for Chapter VI benefits.

### Q: When did the RPwD Act come into force, and which statute did it repeal?

The Act received the assent of the President on 27 December 2016 and was published in the Gazette on 28 December 2016 as Act 49 of 2016. It was brought into force on 19 April 2017 by notification of the Central Government under Section 1(2), and the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules were notified on 15 June 2017. Section 102 repealed the Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act, whose short title carries the year 1995 and which is formally Act 1 of 1996, with savings for action taken under the repealed statute. The interval between a 2016 short title and a 2017 commencement is a recurring source of error in this field.

### Q: Why does the RPwD Act use two definitions, one for rights and one for benefits?

The two definitions do different work. Section 2(s) defines a person with disability by the interaction between a long term impairment and barriers, because the rights in Chapter II respond to that interaction: discrimination, refused accommodation and inaccessible environments can harm a person whatever the measured percentage. Chapter VI distributes scarce and rivalrous benefits, reserved seats, reserved posts and scheme preferences, and distribution at national scale needs a verifiable criterion, which the forty per cent certificate supplies. Parliament therefore conferred rights on the wide class and gated benefits by the narrow one. Confusing the two, by demanding a certificate for a right or expecting a benefit without one, is the commonest error made in applying the Act.

### Q: What is an equal opportunity policy, and which establishments must adopt one?

Section 21 requires every establishment to notify an equal opportunity policy detailing the measures it proposes to take for persons with disabilities in pursuance of the Act. Establishments include government establishments and private establishments: companies, firms, societies, trusts, factories and organisations notified as such. The policy addresses, in practice, facilities and amenities, training, promotion and the conditions under which persons with disabilities work in the establishment. Government establishments must also register a copy of the policy with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner, as the case may be. The policy is the establishment's own published statement of compliance, and the Commissioners may call for it when a complaint about that establishment is examined.

### Q: Does a child with a benchmark disability have a right to free education up to the age of eighteen?

Yes. Section 31 provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act 2009, every child with benchmark disability between the age of six and eighteen years shall have the right to free education in a neighbourhood school, or in a special school, of his choice, and the appropriate Government and the local authorities shall ensure that every such child has access to free education in an appropriate environment until he attains the age of eighteen years. The choice between the neighbourhood school and the special school belongs to the child, and the provision extends free education beyond the fourteen year ceiling of the general education statute.

### Q: What can a person with disability do when a public building remains inaccessible?

The person may complain to the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner, who may inquire on complaint or on their own motion, summon the responsible authority, require documents and recommend corrective steps, which the authority must act on or answer under Sections 76 and 81. The underlying duty is mandatory: existing public buildings had to be made accessible within five years of the notification of the Rules under Section 45, and a building cannot lawfully receive a completion certificate without adhering to the accessibility rules under Section 44. In Rajive Raturi v Union of India, decided on 8 November 2024, the Supreme Court held the recommendatory accessibility guidelines in Rule 15 ultra vires and directed that mandatory standards be framed and the Act's consequences applied.

### Q: Is a disability certificate issued by one state valid across India?

Yes. Section 58(3) provides in terms that the certificate of disability issued under the Act shall be valid across the country. The certificate is issued by a certifying authority designated under a central statute, against assessment guidelines notified by the Central Government, and it records an assessment under the Act rather than under any state scheme, so no state may lawfully demand a fresh assessment as a condition of recognising it. The Unique ID for Persons with Disabilities project gives that portability a single documentary form: the UDID card is generated from the certificate and is designed to be recognised by authorities in every state. Where an authority still declines to recognise a certificate issued elsewhere, the refusal can be taken to the State Commissioner of the state concerned, who has power to inquire into deprivation of rights under the Act.

### Q: What is the UDID card, and how does it differ from a disability certificate?

The disability certificate is the legal document prescribed by Chapter X of the Act: it is issued by a certifying authority after assessment and records the nature and percentage of the person's disability, and it is the proof on which benchmark status and Chapter VI benefits depend. The UDID card, also called the Swavlamban card, is an administrative document issued under the Unique ID for Persons with Disabilities project of the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities. It is generated from the certificate's data and serves as a single identity and disability document intended to work across states and schemes. The card carries the certificate's authority; it does not add to it, and the certificate remains the operative proof.

### Q: Can the Central Government add further disabilities to the Schedule of the RPwD Act?

Yes. A specified disability is one specified in the Schedule, and Section 99 empowers the Central Government, on the recommendations of the appropriate Government or otherwise, to amend the Schedule by notification, so the list of twenty-one is expandable without an amendment Act, each notification being laid before Parliament. Conditions absent from the Schedule cannot ground benchmark status, because Chapter VI benefits run only on specified disabilities, but the notification power is the route by which groups representing an unlisted condition pursue recognition. A person with an unlisted condition may still be a person with disability under Section 2(s), if a long term impairment interacts with barriers to hinder participation, and may claim the Chapter II rights which require no entry in the Schedule at all.

### Q: What does the RPwD Act require in situations of risk and humanitarian emergencies?

Section 8 provides that persons with disabilities shall have equal protection and safety in situations of risk, armed conflict, humanitarian emergencies and natural disasters. The National and State Disaster Management Authorities must take appropriate measures to include persons with disabilities in their disaster management activities. The District Disaster Management Authority must maintain a record of the details of persons with disabilities in the district and take suitable measures to inform such persons of situations of risk so as to enhance preparedness. Authorities engaged in reconstruction after a disaster must undertake the work in consultation with the State Commissioner and in accordance with the accessibility requirements of persons with disabilities, so that reconstruction does not rebuild the barriers the Act requires to be removed.

### Q: Does the RPwD Act say anything about insurance for persons with disabilities?

Section 26 provides that the appropriate Government shall, by notification, make insurance schemes for its employees with disabilities. The provision is directed at governments as employers and is framed as a scheme obligation rather than an individual entitlement to a policy, and unusually for Chapter V it carries no economic capacity qualifier in its own terms. Insurance appears elsewhere through Section 24, under which social security schemes must provide for an insurance scheme for persons with disability not covered under the Employees' State Insurance schemes or any other statutory or government sponsored insurance schemes, and healthcare entitlements run through Section 25. The Act does not regulate commercial insurers or prescribe the terms of private policies; its insurance provisions are duties owed by governments, as employers and as scheme makers.

### Q: What is high support, and what special provision does the RPwD Act make for persons who need it?

High support is defined in Section 2(l) as intensive support, physical, psychological and otherwise, which may be required by a person with benchmark disability for daily activities, to take independent and informed decisions, to access facilities and to participate in all areas of life, including education, employment, family and community life, and treatment and therapy. A person with disability having high support needs is a person with benchmark disability, so certified, who needs such support. Section 38, the whole of Chapter VII, provides that such a person, or any person or organisation on his behalf, may apply to the authority notified by the appropriate Government; the authority refers the case to an Assessment Board, which assesses the extent and the type of support required and reports, and the authority then takes steps to provide the support assessed.

### Q: Does the RPwD Act impose duties on private establishments, or only on government?

Both. The definition of establishment covers private establishments, and several duties run against them directly: service providers, government or private, had to make their services accessible within two years of the notification of the Rules under Section 46; building permissions and completion certificates under Section 44 turn on compliance for any establishment; every establishment must notify an equal opportunity policy under Section 21; and the offence provisions, including the atrocity offence in Section 92 and the general penalty in Section 89, bind everyone. What is confined to the public sector is the distribution side: reservation under Sections 32 and 34 binds government and aided institutions and government establishments, and the employment non-discrimination provision in Section 20 is addressed to government establishments in terms.
