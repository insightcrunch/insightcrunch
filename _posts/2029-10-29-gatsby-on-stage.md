## The Problem a Stage Has to Solve

The Great Gatsby on stage is nearly as old as the novel itself. A Broadway adaptation opened in February 1926, thirteen months after publication, and theatrical versions have appeared in every subsequent generation, culminating in an unusually crowded present in which immersive productions, full-length recitations, and competing musicals have all found audiences. That persistence is worth explaining, because the novel presents theater with a harder problem than it presents cinema.

![The Great Gatsby on stage: Broadway, immersive theater, and stage adaptations of Fitzgerald's novel - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-50.webp)

The problem is that this book is a voice. Nick Carraway is not a camera; he is a man reconstructing a summer from a distance, editing as he goes, and the novel's meaning lives in the gap between what he claims and what he reveals. He opens by reporting his father's advice and announcing that he is inclined to reserve all judgements, and he tells us within two pages that only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction. A film can put that in voiceover and lose the irony, because a voice speaking directly into your ear registers as sincere. Theater has fewer places to hide and, as it turns out, more places to put a narrator.

The claim this article defends is that the theatrical history of this novel is a record of inventive solutions rather than a series of lesser attempts. The stage keeps returning because it can do two things cinema cannot: it can put a speaker in the room with the audience and make the act of telling visible, and it can make the audience physically present at the party rather than seated in front of it. Every major production of the last century has exploited one of those two capacities, and the split between them organizes the whole history.

## The 1926 Broadway Production

The first adaptation of any kind was a play, not a film. Owen Davis, a prolific and Pulitzer-winning dramatist, adapted the novel for the stage. George Cukor, early in a career that would become one of the most distinguished in American directing, staged it. William A. Brady produced. The production opened at the Ambassador Theatre in New York on February 2, 1926, with James Rennie as Gatsby and Florence Eldridge as Daisy, and it ran for more than a hundred performances.

Two facts about this production matter more than its reviews. The first is commercial: the play's success is what generated the film sale, and the silent picture released later in 1926 followed directly from it. The novel's slow initial sales are a familiar part of the Fitzgerald story, and the stage version is a significant part of why the book earned him money at all in the year after publication. Fitzgerald was living abroad during the run and did not see it, which produces the odd situation that the author of the most theatrically adapted American novel of the century never watched his own book performed.

The second fact is structural, and it set a pattern that has held for a century. A conventional three-act play of the period had to convert Nick's retrospective narration into present-tense scenes with entrances and exits. That conversion does something specific to the story: it makes Gatsby the protagonist. In the novel Gatsby is an object of attention, assembled by Nick out of rumor, observation, and one long confession, and the reader's experience is of a man being gradually constructed. Put him on stage in real time and he becomes simply a man in a room who wants a woman back. The plot survives the transfer intact. The epistemology does not.

Every subsequent adaptation has had to decide whether to accept that trade or to fight it, and the interesting productions are the ones that fight.

### What was the first stage version of The Great Gatsby?

Owen Davis adapted the novel for Broadway in 1926, directed by George Cukor and produced by William A. Brady. It opened at the Ambassador Theatre that February with James Rennie as Gatsby and Florence Eldridge as Daisy, ran over a hundred performances, and led directly to the silent film sale.

## The Stage Table

Laying the major productions against their solution to the novel's central difficulty makes the pattern legible. This table pairs each with the approach it takes to Nick's narration and to the spectacle of the parties.

| Production | Approach to narration | Approach to the parties | What the solution costs or gains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owen Davis, Broadway 1926 | Converted to present-tense dramatic scenes | Staged conventionally offstage and on | Gains clarity and plot momentum; loses the retrospective frame entirely |
| Simon Levy, from 2006 | Nick addresses the audience directly as narrator | Suggested rather than depicted, with a small cast | Gains the voice; keeps the scale modest, which suits schools and regional houses |
| Gatz, Elevator Repair Service | The entire novel read aloud, word for word, over roughly eight hours | An office slowly becomes the world of the book | Gains every sentence Fitzgerald wrote; demands enormous audience commitment |
| Immersive productions, from 2015 | Distributed among characters who speak to individual audience members | The audience attends the party in costume and moves through rooms | Gains the felt experience of the party; loses Nick's estrangement from it |
| Musical adaptations, from 2023 | Song takes over the interior monologue | Full production numbers | Gains emotional directness; song resolves the ambiguity Nick's prose sustains |

The table's most useful column is the last one. Every solution buys something and pays for it, and no production has yet found a version that pays nothing. That is not a failure of theatrical imagination. It is a property of the source.

## Gatz and the Recitation Solution

The most radical answer to the narration problem is also the simplest: do not adapt the novel at all. Perform it.

Gatz, created by the New York company Elevator Repair Service and directed by John Collins, stages the complete text of The Great Gatsby, every word of it, spoken aloud. The conceit is that an office worker in a shabby, failing workplace finds a paperback copy in his desk and begins to read it out loud when his computer will not start. Over the course of the performance the office and its occupants gradually take on the roles of the novel's characters, so that the reader becomes Nick and the surrounding drudgery becomes Long Island. The production runs roughly eight hours including intermissions and a dinner break. It premiered in the mid-2000s and, after rights complications kept it out of New York for years, had its celebrated run at The Public Theater in 2010.

What makes Gatz interpretively important rather than merely a stunt is that it identifies the correct problem and refuses the usual compromise. If the novel's value is in its sentences, then any adaptation that paraphrases them has thrown away the asset. Gatz keeps every sentence, including the ones no dramatization would retain: the guest list in chapter four, the descriptions of the caterers and the crates of oranges and lemons, the passages of pure retrospective commentary. The result is a production in which the audience hears the prose at the pace of speech, which is slower than most people read, and hears therefore how carefully it is built.

It also solves the unreliability problem almost by accident. Because a specific man is holding a book and speaking, the audience is continuously aware that this is an account being delivered rather than events being witnessed. The mediation is not hidden; it is the entire staging. When the reader says that reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope, the audience is watching someone say it, in a room, to them, which reintroduces exactly the distance a film's voiceover erases.

The cost is obvious and is not trivial. An eight-hour commitment excludes most audiences most of the time, and the production cannot tour or license the way a conventional play can. Gatz is a demonstration rather than a repeatable model. But what it demonstrates is worth knowing: the novel does not need to be dramatized to work in a theater. It needs to be spoken.
## The Immersive Turn

The second great theatrical solution attacks the other half of the problem. If the parties are the novel's most famous element and a proscenium stage cannot deliver their scale, then remove the proscenium and put the audience inside.

Immersive Gatsby productions began appearing in the mid-2010s, most influentially in a version created by the Guild of Misrule that originated in Leeds in 2015 and later established a long-running London presence. The format is now familiar: audiences arrive in period dress, move through a series of rooms rather than sitting in a fixed position, encounter characters individually as well as in scenes, and can sometimes choose which thread of the story to follow. Cocktails are served. Dancing is taught. The event is a party that contains a play rather than a play about a party.

The interpretive case for this format is strong and is usually stated badly. It is not that the parties were fun and therefore the audience should have fun. It is that Fitzgerald wrote the party chapter as an experience of disorientation, and disorientation is exactly what a fixed seat prevents. Nick's account of his first party is a sequence of small confusions: he cannot find his host, he does not know whose invitation he holds or why he alone received one, he attaches himself to Jordan out of social desperation, he overhears contradictory rumors about the man whose champagne he is drinking. He describes himself as within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life. An audience wandering through rooms, catching fragments of conversation, unsure which door leads where, is having a structurally similar experience.

The interpretive case against is equally real. An immersive production has a commercial obligation to be enjoyable, and the novel's parties are, on close reading, not enjoyable. Nick leaves his first one having watched a woman weep and a new coupe end up in the ditch beside the road, violently shorn of one wheel, with a driver too drunk to grasp what has happened. The guests are catalogued in chapter four as a kind of casualty list. Whatever an audience in a feathered headband is experiencing while a cocktail is placed in their hand, it is not Nick's growing sense of waste. The format delivers the immersion and drops the estrangement, and the estrangement is the point. The broader migration of the novel's imagery into party culture, detached from the book's judgement of it, is traced in [the article on Gatsby-themed parties and the aesthetic](/2030/01/21/gatsby-themed-parties/).

The honest verdict is that immersive theater solves the party problem and creates the Nick problem in an acute form. Some productions have responded intelligently by giving Nick a distinct function, positioning him as a guide who keeps commenting rather than as a fellow reveller, which restores some of the distance. The productions that treat him as one more character at the party lose the novel entirely and become a themed evening with a plot attached.

### What does an immersive Gatsby production do with the audience?

It places them inside the party in period dress, moving through rooms rather than sitting, encountering characters individually and following whichever thread they choose. The format reproduces Nick's disorientation at his first party unusually well, though it struggles to reproduce his estrangement, since a paying guest is having a good time.

## The Public Domain and the Present Crowd

The novel entered the United States public domain on January 1, 2021, and the theatrical consequences were immediate and large.

Before that date, staging The Great Gatsby required permission. The Fitzgerald estate authorized a limited number of versions, most significantly Simon Levy's adaptation, which premiered at the Guthrie Theater in 2006 and became the standard licensed script for schools, colleges, and regional companies. Levy's version is a sensible piece of dramaturgy: it uses a small cast, suggests rather than depicts the spectacle, and keeps Nick as a direct-address narrator so that the retrospective frame survives. For a decade and a half it was, for most theaters, the only legal option.

After 2021 anyone could adapt the book without asking. The result has been a proliferation rather than a single successor. Musical versions have been the most visible: one, with a score by Jason Howland and lyrics by Nathan Tysen and a book by Kait Kerrigan, developed at the Paper Mill Playhouse in 2023 and opened on Broadway in 2024. Another, developed at the American Repertory Theater with music and lyrics by Florence Welch and Thomas Bartlett and a book by Martyna Majok, took a markedly different tone. That two substantial musicals of the same novel could be in development simultaneously, with opposed sensibilities, is a fact about copyright law before it is a fact about theater, and it produced an unusual natural experiment: the same source, the same medium, the same moment, two incompatible readings.

The public-domain era also lowered the barrier for small companies, university theaters, and experimental groups, which means the current stage history of this novel is being written faster than it can be surveyed. What can be said with confidence is that the productions divide along the same axis they always have. Some pursue the voice and some pursue the party, and the ones that attempt both usually find that the party wins, because spectacle is louder than commentary.

## The Narration Problem, Stated Precisely

It is worth being exact about what theater is trying to solve, because the difficulty is often described vaguely as the novel being interior.

The novel is not especially interior. Very little of it takes place inside anyone's head. What it has instead is a narrator with a documented gap between his self-description and his behavior, and the reader's job is to notice the gap. Nick tells us he is inclined to reserve all judgements and that reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope. He then judges Tom, Daisy, Jordan, Myrtle, the guests, and himself, continuously and often harshly. He describes himself as one of the few honest people that I have ever known while giving an account that withholds the timing of what he knew and softens his own conduct. He announces at the outset that Gatsby represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn and then declares that no Gatsby turned out all right at the end, blaming instead what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams.

That structure requires the audience to hold two things at once: the account and a growing skepticism about the accountant. Prose does this easily because the reader controls the pace and can reread. Film does it badly because voiceover flattens irony into sincerity. Theater does it better than film for a specific and underappreciated reason: on a stage, a narrator is visibly a person performing an act of narration, and the audience can see the performance as a performance. There is no cinematic illusion of unmediated access. Everything is admittedly staged, which means an admittedly staged account fits the medium's grammar rather than fighting it.

This is the strongest argument that theater is not the lesser medium for this book. Cinema is better at spectacle and worse at mediation. Fitzgerald's novel is a mediated account of a spectacle, and it is at least arguable that the mediation is the more important half. The full case for why every medium struggles here is developed in [the article on why The Great Gatsby resists adaptation](/2029/12/10/why-gatsby-resists-adaptation/), and the film versions are tested against the novel in [the comparison of the novel against its film adaptations](/2028/11/27/gatsby-novel-vs-films/).
## Staging the Parties Without a Budget

Most theaters cannot build a mansion, hire an orchestra, or fill a stage with two hundred guests, and the constraint has forced some of the more intelligent thinking in this adaptation history.

Fitzgerald's own technique offers the way out, because the novel's parties are not actually described as crowds. They are described as inventories and as sounds. Five crates of oranges and lemons arrive every Friday and leave every Monday as a pyramid of pulpless halves. Eight servants spend a day repairing the damage. The orchestra is no thin five-piece affair, but a whole pitful of oboes and trombones and saxophones and viols and cornets and piccolos, and low and high drums. Nick tells us the lights grow brighter as the earth lurches away from the sun and the opera of voices pitches a key higher. Almost none of this requires bodies on stage. It requires a list, a sound design, and a speaker.

This is why the small-scale licensed versions frequently work better than their budgets suggest. A stage that gives you one servant, one lit doorway, and a narrator reciting the logistics can produce the sense of an operation running at scale more efficiently than a stage crowded with twenty extras, because twenty extras look like twenty people and a list of two hundred oranges sounds like an industry. The novel's excess is quantitative rather than visual, and quantity is cheap to say.

The same principle governs the ending of the party sequence, which is where most productions falter. Fitzgerald does not end chapter three with a climax. He ends it with a mess in a ditch and a general dispersal, and later, when the parties stop, he writes that a sudden emptiness seemed to flow now from the windows and the great doors. The theatrical equivalent of that sentence is a lighting change and an empty stage, which any company can afford and which lands harder than any number of dancers. The symbolic weight the parties carry across the whole novel is worked through in [the analysis of Gatsby's parties as symbol and spectacle](/2025/05/26/gatsby-parties-symbol/).

## Staging the Objects: The Light, the Shirts, the Pool

Three physical properties carry disproportionate meaning in this novel, and how a production handles them is a fast diagnostic of its intelligence.

The green light is the hardest, because it is already a stage effect in the book. Gatsby stands on his lawn with his arms stretched toward the dark water and there is a single green light, minute and far away. A theater can simply do this, and the danger is that it does it too well. The novel's green light works because it is small and barely visible, and because Nick, telling the story afterward, knows what it turned out to mean and the reader does not yet. A production that lights it brightly and holds on it has explained the symbol before the audience has met it. The best staging is the stingiest one.

The shirts are the opposite problem: an object that theater can deliver better than film. When Gatsby throws his shirts down and the soft rich heap mounted higher until Daisy bends her head into them and cries stormily, the scene needs the physical fact of an absurd quantity of cloth accumulating in a room. On a stage this is tactile and slightly ridiculous in exactly the right proportion, and the audience is close enough to register the fabric as fabric rather than as a shot.

The pool is where theater's limitations are least escapable and most productively so. Fitzgerald does not show the killing. He gives the reader a chauffeur who heard the shots, the laden mattress moving irregularly down the pool, and the flat sentence that the holocaust was complete. The event is reported, not witnessed. Theater is well suited to reported violence, having done it since the Greeks, and productions that resist the urge to stage the shooting and instead let someone describe it are closer to Fitzgerald than any film that shows the body.

### How does the stage handle the deaths?

Usually by reporting them, which matches the novel. Fitzgerald never shows the shooting: a chauffeur hears the shots, the laden mattress moves irregularly down the pool, and Nick records that the holocaust was complete. Theater has staged reported violence since the Greeks, so the medium's convention and the book's method align unusually well here.

## Is the Stage the Lesser Medium?

The assumption that film is the natural home of an adaptation and theater a compromise is worth examining, because in this particular case it is close to backwards.

Cinema's advantages here are real: scale, period reconstruction, the ability to move between Long Island and the valley of ashes and Manhattan without a scene change, and the capacity to render Fitzgerald's imagery photographically. No stage will ever match a film's rendering of the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg looking out over a landscape where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and finally of ash-grey men.

Theater's advantages are less obvious and, for this novel, arguably weightier. First, the narrator can be present as a narrator without the medium objecting, because everything on a stage is admittedly a performance. Second, the audience shares physical space with the actors, which makes an immersive party genuinely immersive rather than depicted. Third, theater is comfortable with reported action, which suits a novel whose central events, the affair's beginning, the accident, the killing, are all delivered secondhand. Fourth, and least discussed, theater can hold silence in a way film usually will not permit, and this novel's last chapters are almost entirely quiet.

The strongest evidence for theater's suitability is Gatz. No film has ever proposed to deliver the complete text of the novel, and no film could, because eight hours of a man reading is not a film. The medium that can absorb Fitzgerald's actual sentences without paraphrase is the one with a live speaker in it.

### Is stage adaptation inferior to film?

Not for this novel. Cinema wins on scale and period imagery, but theater accommodates a visible narrator without breaking its own grammar, shares physical space with the audience, is comfortable with reported violence, and tolerates silence. Since Fitzgerald's book is a mediated account of a spectacle, the mediation suits a stage.

## What the Stage History Teaches a Reader

A century of theatrical attempts amounts to a large body of evidence about where the novel's weight actually sits, and three findings emerge.

The first is that the plot is not the asset. Every stage version preserves the plot, and the versions differ enormously in quality, so the plot cannot be what makes the book work. This is a useful thing for a student to internalize before writing an essay, because plot summary is the default failure mode and the adaptation record demonstrates its insufficiency empirically.

The second is that Nick is the asset, and that this is visible precisely because it is what productions lose. The versions that keep him as a speaking, commenting presence feel like the novel. The versions that dissolve him into the action feel like a melodrama about a rich woman and a bootlegger. The experiment has been run many times with consistent results.

The third is that the parties are a trap. They are the most adaptable element and the most misleading one. A production that succeeds at the parties has succeeded at the thing the novel is criticizing, and unless it builds a counterweight it will send its audience home having enjoyed exactly what Nick could not. Fitzgerald put the estrangement in the narration, which is why removing the narration removes the critique and leaves the champagne.
## The Licensed Era and the School Production

Between the 1926 Broadway run and the public-domain flood, most people who saw The Great Gatsby on a stage saw a licensed adaptation in a school hall, a college studio, or a regional theater with a modest budget. That body of production is rarely discussed and it is where the novel has reached the largest number of live audiences.

Simon Levy's adaptation, premiered at the Guthrie Theater in 2006 and published for licensing, became the default for good reasons. It is built for constraint. The cast is small, the settings are suggested rather than constructed, and Nick speaks to the audience directly, which means the retrospective frame survives a production with three flats and a lighting board. The script accepts that a school cannot build West Egg and reasons that it does not need to, because Fitzgerald's descriptions are verbal and can simply be spoken.

That choice has a consequence worth naming. A generation of students encountered the novel on stage in a version that foregrounded Nick, which is a more accurate introduction to the book than most of them received from the films. The licensed versions have been quietly better custodians of the novel's structure than the expensive productions, because poverty forced them toward the narrator and away from the party.

There is a broader lesson here about what constraint does to adaptation. A company with money will build the mansion, hire the dancers, and spend its resources on the spectacle, because spectacle is what money buys. A company without money must decide what the novel is actually made of, and the answer it arrives at is almost always the voice. The most reliable predictor of whether a stage production will feel like The Great Gatsby is not its budget but whether Nick keeps talking.

The school production has one further advantage that is easy to overlook. Its audiences are frequently reading the book at the same time, which means they arrive able to notice what has been cut and what has been kept. An adaptation watched by people who know the source is doing something a film in a cinema cannot: it is being continuously checked. That checking is exactly the critical activity the novel rewards, and the modest stage version, watched by a hall of students who read chapter three last week, may be the single most effective teaching instrument in the whole adaptation record.

## Two Musicals, One Novel

The most instructive event in the recent theatrical history is that two substantial musical adaptations developed at the same time with opposite instincts, which produced a comparison no one designed and everyone can learn from.

The Broadway version, developed at the Paper Mill Playhouse in 2023 with a score by Jason Howland, lyrics by Nathan Tysen, and a book by Kait Kerrigan, took the romantic reading. In that reading Gatsby is a lover, the tragedy is the failure of a great love against social barriers, and the score's job is to make an audience feel the size of the longing. This is the reading that has dominated popular reception of the novel for decades and it has real textual support, particularly Nick's verdict that Gatsby possessed an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again.

The American Repertory Theater version, with music and lyrics by Florence Welch and Thomas Bartlett and a book by Martyna Majok, took the darker reading. That reading treats the novel as a critique of the machinery that produces men like Gatsby, and it foregrounds the wreckage: Myrtle, Wilson, the valley of ashes, the carelessness of people who smash things and retreat into their money. Its textual support is equally strong, and arguably stronger, because it takes seriously the second half of Nick's verdict, which is that what preyed on Gatsby and what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams is the thing that closed out his interest in the sorrows and elations of men.

Neither is a misreading. Both sentences are in the same paragraph on the novel's third page, and Fitzgerald put them there together. He gives the reader an unqualified admiration and an unqualified indictment within a few lines of each other and never chooses between them.

What the musicals demonstrate, therefore, is that the novel's famous ambiguity is not a critical construction. It is load-bearing enough to sustain two full evenings of theater pulling in opposite directions from the same source, developed by serious professionals in the same season. A student arguing that Fitzgerald deliberately withheld a verdict on his protagonist now has a piece of external evidence that is unusually hard to dismiss, because the disagreement was expensive.

Song creates one difficulty neither version escapes. A sung emotion is a declared emotion. When a character sings about longing, the audience is told what the character feels, and the telling is sincere by convention. The novel's method is the opposite: it withholds interiority and lets the reader infer it from surfaces, gestures, and Nick's suspicion. Musical theater's greatest strength is therefore misaligned with this novel's greatest technique, which is why the form has to work harder here than it does with material built on declared feeling.

### Why do the two musicals disagree so completely?

Because Fitzgerald put both readings on the same page. He calls Gatsby's hope an extraordinary gift and a romantic readiness, then says what preyed on him and what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams closed out Nick's interest in other men's sorrows. Admiration and indictment sit a few lines apart.

## What Every Stage Version Has to Cut

Studying the cuts is more revealing than studying the additions, and the pattern across a century of productions is remarkably consistent.

The guest list goes first. Chapter four opens with two pages of names, the people who came to Gatsby's house that summer, recorded by Nick on a timetable and delivered without commentary. It is the novel's driest passage and one of its most devastating, because the accumulation of names is the accumulation of people who took his hospitality and will not attend his funeral. No dramatization retains it, because a list of names is not a scene. Gatz retains it, which is one of the clearest arguments for what that production is doing.

Owl Eyes usually goes next, and productions that keep him tend to be the intelligent ones. He appears in the library during the party, drunk and amazed that the books are absolutely real and have pages and everything, treating the thoroughness of Gatsby's construction as evidence of something admirable and something alarming at once. He returns at the funeral as almost the only guest. Cutting him removes the novel's most compressed statement of its theme.

Henry Gatz is a frequent casualty, and losing him removes the origin of Gatsby's self-invention. He arrives after the death with a photograph of the house, cracked in the corners and dirty with many hands, and a ragged old copy of a book called Hopalong Cassidy with a boyhood schedule and a list of general resolves written on the back flyleaf. That schedule is the mechanism of the whole character laid bare, and productions that cut it make Gatsby mythic by removing his manufacture.

Klipspringer goes almost always. He lives at the mansion, plays the piano for Gatsby and Daisy, and telephones after the death not to ask about the funeral but about a pair of tennis shoes he left behind. He is the exact measure of what the guests took and what they returned, and he is comic, which makes him easy to cut and expensive to lose.

The pattern is unmistakable. What gets cut is the material that makes the novel's judgement specific rather than atmospheric. A production can convey that Gatsby was used through empty rooms and a lit stage. It takes the guest list, Owl Eyes, Henry Gatz, and Klipspringer to convey exactly how, and those four cost cast members and stage time that a production usually prefers to spend on the party. The result is that live versions, like the films, are consistently stronger on the novel's spectacle than on its accounting.

### What do stage productions usually cut?

The material that makes the novel's judgement precise: the guest list in chapter four, Owl Eyes in the library and at the funeral, Henry Gatz with his son's boyhood schedule, and Klipspringer telephoning about tennis shoes. All four are cheap to cut and expensive to lose, since each specifies exactly how Gatsby was used.

## The Sound of a Room: What Live Performance Does to the Prose

One effect of the stage that has no equivalent in any other medium deserves separate treatment, because it changes how the novel's sentences behave.

Fitzgerald's prose is rhythmically organized to a degree that silent reading conceals. His long sentences accumulate through parallel clauses and then close on a short one, and the closing short one is where the judgement usually sits. Read silently at speed, the accumulation registers as atmosphere and the closing clause slides past. Delivered aloud, the accumulation takes real time and the audience is waiting when the short clause lands.

The description of the parties works this way. The catalogue of the orchestra runs long, listing oboes and trombones and saxophones and viols and cornets and piccolos, and low and high drums, and the length is the point, because the list is doing what the party does, which is going on past the point of sense. Spoken, that sentence takes fifteen seconds, and fifteen seconds of instruments is an experience rather than a description.

The endings of chapters behave the same way. When Gatsby's household is dismissed and the parties stop, Nick reports that a sudden emptiness seemed to flow now from the windows and the great doors, and then adds, of the whole apparatus falling apart under Daisy's disapproval, that the whole caravansary had fallen in like a card house at the disapproval in her eyes. Delivered in a room, the metaphor of the card house arrives as a small sound after a large one, and the collapse is audible.

This is the argument for recitation stated at the level of technique rather than at the level of principle. The novel is built for a voice. It was written by a man with a strong ear in a period when reading aloud was ordinary, and its most admired passages are the ones whose effects are rhythmic. A medium that supplies a voice and a room is not making a compromise with this text. It is supplying something the page can only imply, and the century of theatrical attempts is, at bottom, a century of people discovering that.

## The Audience as a Character

There is one more thing a stage can do with this novel that has barely been attempted, and naming it clarifies what the existing productions are and are not achieving.

Fitzgerald's parties have an audience problem built into them. The guests are not invited. They arrive, consume, and leave, and Nick notes that he was one of the few guests who had actually been invited, a line that quietly indicts everyone else in the garden. The novel therefore contains a crowd whose defining quality is that it takes without being asked, and whose collective behavior is the book's central indictment of the culture it describes.

An immersive production puts a real crowd in the building, and that crowd behaves exactly like the fictional one. They arrive in costume, consume the drinks, enjoy the spectacle, and go home. The parallel is exact and almost entirely unexploited. A production that pressed on it, that at some point made the paying audience aware that they had spent an evening doing what the guests did, would have found the theatrical equivalent of Nick's estrangement without needing Nick to state it.

The material for this is already in the text and it is placed at the end. Owl Eyes, standing at the grave in the rain, delivers the verdict on everyone who came to the house and did not come to the funeral. Nick, dressing to attend, finds almost nobody there. A production that has spent two hours letting an audience enjoy Gatsby's hospitality and then stages the funeral has an available effect no film can reach, because the film's audience never accepted the hospitality.

That gap between what the form makes possible and what productions have so far done with it is the most promising thing about the current moment. The public domain has removed the permission problem, the immersive format has solved the spectacle problem, and the recitation experiments have proved that the prose survives being spoken. What remains unattempted at scale is a production that uses the audience's own complicity as the instrument of judgement, which is precisely the move Fitzgerald makes on his reader in the last twenty pages.
## Writing About a Stage Production in an Essay

Stage adaptations are unusually good essay material because they are less familiar than the films, which means an examiner has probably not read the same observation forty times, and because the constraints of live performance force interpretive decisions into the open.

The weak use is the review, and it is the same weak use that afflicts writing about the films. Describing a production, praising or criticizing it, and concluding that it was faithful or unfaithful produces an opinion about an evening in a theater rather than an argument about a novel.

The first strong use is the constraint argument. Take a limitation the medium imposes, show how a production resolved it, and argue that the resolution exposes something structural in the book. The strongest instance is the narration. A stage cannot supply Nick's retrospective commentary through the camera, so a production must either give him direct address or lose the frame, and tracking what disappears when the frame goes is a rigorous way of demonstrating how much of the novel's judgement lives in its telling rather than its events.

The second strong use is the comparison of solutions. Set two productions with opposite strategies against each other, such as a full-text recitation and an immersive party, and argue that they optimize for different halves of the same book. One preserves the sentences and sacrifices the spectacle; the other preserves the spectacle and sacrifices the voice. The claim that follows is that no single production can do both, and that the reason is a genuine tension in Fitzgerald's design between a seductive surface and a narrator who refuses to be seduced.

The third strong use is the reported-action argument. Note that the novel's decisive events, the beginning of the affair, the accident on the road, the shooting at the pool, all reach the reader secondhand. Then observe that theater has staged reported violence for two and a half thousand years, and argue that this convergence of convention explains why the stage handles the novel's ending better than the screen does. This move is stronger than it looks because it grounds a claim about adaptation in a claim about the text's narrative method.

Whichever route the essay takes, the paragraph has to land on Fitzgerald's prose. ## Verdict

The theatrical history of The Great Gatsby is not a footnote to the film history. It is the older tradition, the more inventive one, and in several respects the more successful one.

The record shows a single problem attacked from two directions for a century. Either a production pursues the voice, which produces the Owen Davis line of dramatizations refined into the direct-address versions and taken to its logical extreme in a complete recitation, or it pursues the party, which produces the immersive productions and the musicals. The versions that pursue the voice are more faithful to what makes the novel work and are harder to sell. The versions that pursue the party are easier to sell and risk becoming the thing the novel is critical of.

The best productions are the ones that keep a counterweight. An immersive evening that positions Nick as a commenting presence rather than a fellow guest retains the estrangement. A modest licensed staging that spends its resources on the narrator rather than on extras produces more of Fitzgerald than a lavish one that cuts him. The governing principle is that this book's judgement lives in its telling, so whatever else a production spends money on, it should not economize on the man who is telling it.

For a reader, the stage history is worth knowing because it functions as a long controlled experiment. The plot is held constant across every version and the results vary wildly, which is the clearest available proof that the plot is not where the value is. Nick is added and removed repeatedly, and the productions with him feel like the novel while the productions without him feel like a story about a party. That is not an aesthetic preference. It is a finding, and a reader can use it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What stage adaptations has The Great Gatsby had?

The theatrical line began almost immediately. Owen Davis adapted the novel for Broadway in 1926, directed by George Cukor, and the production ran for over a hundred performances. For most of the following century, staging required permission from the Fitzgerald estate, and Simon Levy's adaptation, which premiered at the Guthrie Theater in 2006, became the standard licensed script for schools and regional companies. Elevator Repair Service created Gatz, which performs the complete text of the novel aloud. Immersive productions began appearing in the mid-2010s, most influentially a Guild of Misrule version originating in Leeds in 2015. After the novel entered the public domain in 2021, multiple musicals arrived, including one that opened on Broadway in 2024 and another developed at the American Repertory Theater.

### Q: What was the early stage version of the novel?

Owen Davis, a prolific Pulitzer-winning dramatist, adapted the book for Broadway barely a year after publication. George Cukor directed, early in what became one of the most distinguished careers in American directing, and William A. Brady produced. The production opened at the Ambassador Theatre in New York on February 2, 1926, with James Rennie as Gatsby and Florence Eldridge as Daisy, and ran for more than a hundred performances. Its commercial success generated the sale that produced the silent film released later the same year. Structurally it set the pattern that has held ever since: converting Nick's retrospective account into present-tense scenes makes Gatsby the protagonist, which preserves the plot and dissolves the novel's epistemology.

### Q: What modern and immersive productions exist?

Two strands dominate the recent record. The recitation strand is represented by Gatz, created by Elevator Repair Service and directed by John Collins, which stages the complete text of the novel spoken aloud over roughly eight hours in a shabby office that gradually becomes Long Island. The immersive strand began with a Guild of Misrule production that originated in Leeds in 2015 and later established a long London run, in which audiences arrive in period dress, move through rooms rather than sitting, and encounter characters individually. Since the novel entered the public domain in 2021, musical versions have joined them, including one that reached Broadway in 2024 and another with a markedly different sensibility developed at the American Repertory Theater.

### Q: How does the stage handle the narration and the parties?

By choosing one and paying for the other. Productions that pursue the narration keep Nick as a direct-address presence, or in the case of a full recitation simply let a man read the book aloud, which preserves the gap between what Nick claims and what he reveals. Productions that pursue the parties dissolve him into the action and put the audience inside the spectacle, which delivers the disorientation of chapter three and loses the estrangement. Fitzgerald's own method offers a middle path that small companies exploit well: the parties in the novel are described as inventories and sounds rather than as crowds, so a list, a sound design, and a speaker can convey scale more cheaply and more accurately than extras can.

### Q: Is stage adaptation inferior to film?

Not for this novel, and arguably the reverse. Cinema has the clear advantages of scale, period reconstruction, and the ability to render the valley of ashes photographically. But theater accommodates a narrator without breaking its own grammar, because everything on a stage is admittedly a performance and an admittedly constructed account therefore fits the medium rather than fighting it. Theater also shares physical space with the audience, which makes an immersive party genuinely immersive; it is comfortable with reported violence, which suits a book whose decisive events all arrive secondhand; and it tolerates silence, which the novel's last two chapters require. Film is better at spectacle and worse at mediation, and this is a mediated account of a spectacle.

### Q: What distinct solutions does theater offer?

Four that cinema cannot match. The first is the visible narrator: a stage can put a man in a room holding a book and speaking, which makes the act of telling part of the staging rather than something the medium has to conceal. The second is shared space, which lets an audience attend the party rather than watch one. The third is reported action, a convention theater has used since the Greeks and which matches Fitzgerald's refusal to show the shooting, giving the reader instead a chauffeur who heard the shots and the laden mattress moving irregularly down the pool. The fourth is silence, which film convention tends to fill and which the novel's ending depends on.

### Q: Who wrote the 1926 Broadway play?

Owen Davis wrote the adaptation. He was among the most prolific American dramatists of his era and had won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama a few years earlier, so the novel was handed to an established professional rather than to a novice. The production was directed by George Cukor and produced by William A. Brady, and it opened at the Ambassador Theatre in February 1926 with James Rennie and Florence Eldridge in the leads. Davis's dramaturgical problem was the one every subsequent adapter has faced: a conventional play of the period needed present-tense scenes with entrances and exits, and converting Nick's retrospective account into that form necessarily promotes Gatsby from an object of reconstruction to a man in a room.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald see the stage version?

He did not. Fitzgerald was living abroad during the Broadway run, which produces the curious situation that the author of the most frequently staged American novel of the century never watched his own book performed. The production mattered to him financially rather than artistically. His novel had sold modestly against his expectations, and the play's success generated the film sale that followed later in 1926, so the theatrical adaptation is a significant part of why the book produced income for him in its first years. The commercial and critical history of the novel's slow start, and the reversal that came after his death, remains one of the more instructive stories in American publishing.

### Q: What is Gatz and how long does it run?

Gatz is a production by the New York company Elevator Repair Service, directed by John Collins, that performs the complete text of The Great Gatsby aloud, every word of it. The conceit is that an office worker in a failing workplace finds a paperback in his desk when his computer will not start and begins reading it out loud, and over the course of the performance the office and its occupants gradually assume the roles of the novel's characters. It runs roughly eight hours including intermissions and a dinner break. It premiered in the mid-2000s and, after rights complications delayed a New York staging, had a celebrated run at The Public Theater in 2010.

### Q: Is there a Gatsby musical?

There are several, and their simultaneous existence is a consequence of copyright rather than of theatrical fashion. Once the novel entered the public domain on January 1, 2021, anyone could adapt it without seeking permission, and multiple musical versions moved into development at once. One, with music by Jason Howland, lyrics by Nathan Tysen, and a book by Kait Kerrigan, developed at the Paper Mill Playhouse in 2023 and opened on Broadway in 2024. Another, with music and lyrics by Florence Welch and Thomas Bartlett and a book by Martyna Majok, was developed at the American Repertory Theater with a distinctly different tone. The same source, the same medium, the same moment, two incompatible readings.

### Q: How did the 2021 public domain entry change theatrical Gatsby?

It removed the gatekeeper. Before 2021, staging the novel in the United States required permission from the Fitzgerald estate, which authorized a limited number of versions; Simon Levy's 2006 adaptation was for many theaters the only legal option, and its dramaturgical choices, a small cast, suggested spectacle, and Nick as direct-address narrator, therefore shaped a generation of school and regional productions. After the copyright expired, the barrier fell for musicals, experimental companies, university theaters, and small immersive operations alike. The result is a proliferation being written faster than it can be surveyed, and an unusual natural experiment in which competing interpretations of the same text run concurrently.

### Q: Can a play keep Nick's retrospective distance?

Yes, and this is theater's strongest advantage over film. On a stage everything is admittedly a performance, so a man standing before an audience narrating an account he has already lived through does not violate the medium's grammar; it uses it. Film struggles because voiceover flattens irony into sincerity, since a voice speaking directly into your ear registers as trustworthy by default. A stage narrator is visibly performing an act of narration, which lets an audience hold the account and their skepticism about the accountant at the same time. That is precisely the double awareness the novel requires when Nick claims that reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope and then judges everyone he meets.

### Q: How does theater stage the green light?

Ideally, sparingly. The green light is already a stage effect in the novel: Gatsby stretches his arms toward the dark water and there is a single green light, minute and far away. A theater can simply produce this, and the trap is producing it too well. The image works on the page because it is small, barely visible, and unexplained at the moment of its appearance, with Nick withholding what it will come to mean. A production that lights it brightly and lingers has interpreted the symbol before the audience has encountered it. The stingiest staging is the most faithful one, since the novel's power here comes from insufficiency rather than from spectacle.

### Q: How does the stage handle the car crash and the shooting?

By reporting them, which is exactly what Fitzgerald does. The novel never shows the killing. Nick gives the reader a chauffeur who heard the shots and thought little of them, the laden mattress moving irregularly down the pool with its accidental burden, and the flat conclusion that the holocaust was complete. The accident on the road reaches the reader through witnesses and reconstruction rather than through direct depiction. Theater has staged reported violence since Greek tragedy, so the medium's oldest convention and the book's narrative method coincide. Productions that resist the urge to show the body are closer to the source than any film that shows it, and the restraint costs nothing.

### Q: Which stage version is the most faithful?

Faithfulness splits into two incompatible kinds, and no production has achieved both. Gatz is the most faithful to the text, since it delivers every sentence Fitzgerald wrote without paraphrase, including the guest list and the caterers and the passages of pure commentary that no dramatization retains. The licensed direct-address versions are the most faithful to the novel's structure, keeping Nick's retrospective frame intact at modest scale. The immersive productions are the most faithful to the felt experience of chapter three and the least faithful to the judgement the novel passes on it. Choosing among these is choosing which half of the book matters more, which is a real interpretive decision rather than a ranking.

### Q: Should I see a production before writing an essay?

It can help, provided the essay uses the production as evidence rather than as subject. Live performance is a good diagnostic instrument because the constraints force interpretive decisions into the open: a stage cannot supply Nick's commentary through a camera, so a production must either give him direct address or lose the frame, and watching what disappears when the frame goes demonstrates how much of the novel's judgement lives in its telling. What an essay must avoid is the review, which describes an evening in a theater and calls it analysis. The paragraph has to end on Fitzgerald's prose, not on a performance.

### Q: What does live performance reveal that print hides?

Chiefly pace. Reading is faster than speech, and readers skim, so the architecture of Fitzgerald's sentences is easy to pass over. Hearing the prose delivered aloud at the speed of a voice exposes how carefully it is constructed, which is the central discovery of the full-text recitation approach. Live performance also exposes the novel's proportions. Passages that feel brief on the page, such as the party inventories and the guest list, take real time in a room, and the audience registers how much of the book is spent cataloguing rather than dramatizing. That imbalance is invisible in print and is one of the surer signs that the novel's business is judgement rather than incident.

### Q: Why do theatermakers keep returning to this novel?

Because it offers a rare combination: a compact plot, a small cast of vivid characters, an enormous party, and an unsolved formal problem. The plot and the characters make it producible. The party makes it sellable. The formal problem, how to keep a narrator whose reliability is the point, makes it interesting to a director who wants something to solve rather than something to execute. Add a century of cultural saturation, so audiences arrive knowing the images, and the removal of copyright restrictions in 2021, and the incentives all point the same way. Every generation gets to attempt a solution that the previous one did not quite reach, which is an unusually durable invitation.
