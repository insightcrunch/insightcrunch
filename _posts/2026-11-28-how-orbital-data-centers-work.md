## What an Orbital Data Center Is and What It Is Not

An orbital data center works by placing servers, power systems, and radiators on satellites that generate electricity from sunlight, process data in orbit, exchange results through optical links, and reject every watt of waste heat to space by radiation. Computing in orbit is governed less by computing than by heat, because every watt of computation must be radiated away, so radiator area and mass set the real capacity of any system flown. The machine is therefore a thermal system with computers attached, not a terrestrial building lifted upward. Its servers resemble ground servers in logic and differ in almost every physical support system around that logic.

![An orbital data center satellite with large radiators and solar arrays above Earth - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

A terrestrial data center assumes cheap mass, abundant air or water for cooling, grid power on demand, and technicians who can replace a failed board within hours. None of those assumptions survives launch. Mass must be paid for in propellant. Air does not exist outside the pressure vessel. Water cannot be dumped overboard without consuming a finite store. Repair means either robotic servicing, redundancy built in before launch, or acceptance that a failed unit stays failed. The design problem shifts from maximizing servers per floor to maximizing reliable computation per kilogram and per square meter of radiator.

The distinction matters for what counts as an orbital data center. A communications satellite that routes packets performs computation, but routing alone does not make it a data center. A scientific instrument that processes images before downlink performs computation, but a dedicated instrument serves one payload and one mission. An orbital data center, by contrast, offers general purpose processing and storage to multiple workloads, accepts tasks uplinked from the ground or from other spacecraft, and returns results rather than raw sensor streams.

Regulatory history illustrates the scale that the definition now has to cover. The Federal Communications Commission authorization DA 26-1273, adopted November 25, 2026, granted authority in part for a 1,000,000-satellite orbital data center constellation, with shells at 500 to 1,002 kilometres and optical links as the primary means of moving data between spacecraft. A constellation of that size is not a single large building in space. It is a distributed system in which capacity comes from aggregation across many identical nodes, each node limited, each node replaceable in principle by launching another, and the network rather than any one satellite carrying the service.

Aggregation brings its own mechanism. A task arrives by radio or optical uplink, a scheduler assigns it to a node with free capacity, free power, and sufficient thermal margin, the node executes it while its radiators shed the resulting heat, and the answer leaves by optical crosslink or downlink. If the assigned node passes into eclipse or its radiator temperature rises, the scheduler migrates the task or waits. Scheduling therefore tracks sunlight and temperature as first class resources alongside processor cycles and memory.

What an orbital data center is not is equally instructive. It is not a route to unlimited free energy, because sunlight in orbit still requires solar array area, batteries for eclipse, and power conditioning mass. The value case rests instead on specific advantages: continuous sunlight at favorable altitudes, processing of space generated data without downlinking raw streams, and capacity placed where no ground facility can sit. Judged on those terms, the concept is an engineering tradeoff with clear physical limits, and the limits begin with the cost of lifting mass.

## The Rocket Equation Prices Every Kilogram of Compute

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published the rocket equation in 1903, and every orbital data center design still pays its price. The equation states that the change in velocity a rocket can achieve equals its exhaust velocity multiplied by the natural logarithm of the ratio between starting mass and final mass. The logarithm is the punishment. Doubling the required velocity change does not double the propellant needed. It multiplies the mass ratio, so each additional increment of performance demands propellant to lift propellant that exists only to lift more propellant. Engineers call the surviving fraction the mass fraction: the share of liftoff mass that remains as structure, payload, and usable systems once the propellant is gone.

The mechanism works step by step. A launcher leaves the pad mostly as propellant by mass. As the engines burn, the vehicle sheds mass as exhaust, accelerates the remainder, burns more propellant to accelerate what remains, and stages away empty tanks to stop paying for dead structure. By the time a payload reaches low Earth orbit at roughly 7.8 kilometres per second of velocity change from the surface, only a small percentage of the liftoff mass remains in orbit. The Space Shuttle system, documented by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration across its 1981 to 2011 flight history, delivered payloads that represented a low single digit share of the roughly 2,000,000-kilogram liftoff stack. Modern reusable vehicles improve cost per kilogram without repealing the underlying ratio.

That ratio decides what gets left behind. A ground server assumes a steel chassis, generous heat sinks, redundant power supplies, and cabling with slack. In orbit each of those choices competes directly with propellant. A kilogram spent on a heavier chassis is a kilogram denied to solar array, battery, radiator, shielding, or propellant for station keeping. Designers therefore strip a space server to functions that earn their mass. Heat sinks shrink because a massive heat sink only delays overheating unless a radiator ultimately rejects the heat. Redundancy concentrates on components whose failure ends the mission, while components that can fail gracefully are flown singly.

Mass also compounds across subsystems in a way ground design never faces. Adding 100 kilograms of servers requires more electrical power, which requires larger solar arrays, which require stronger structure and larger attitude control capacity, which require more propellant, which requires still more launch mass at a multiple of the original addition. Systems engineers call this growth factor, and for spacecraft it commonly means that one kilogram added late in design costs several kilograms at liftoff. The discipline that follows is unforgiving accounting from the first sketch. Every subsystem carries a mass budget, every budget carries a margin held by the chief engineer, and any team that exceeds its budget must remove mass elsewhere or persuade another team to surrender margin.

Launch cost per kilogram translates the physics into economics. A data center operator on the ground compares server price against electricity and building cost over the hardware life. An orbital operator compares server mass against launch price, replacement cadence, and the revenue a kilogram of computing can earn before radiation or propellant exhaustion retires it. A cheaper processor that runs hotter can lose to a costlier efficient processor once radiator mass and array mass enter the ledger.Tsiolkovsky wrote the equation for exploration, but its commercial meaning is simple. Orbit charges rent in kilograms, collected in advance, at logarithmic rates. An orbital data center succeeds only if each kilogram flown returns enough computation, over enough years, to justify the propellant burned to place it and the propellant that a replacement will burn when its life ends.

## Power in Orbit: Sunlight, Eclipse and the Battery Tax

Sunlight in low Earth orbit is both stronger and less reliable than sunlight on a rooftop. Outside the atmosphere a spacecraft receives the full solar flux, measured by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration at roughly 1,361 watts per square meter at Earth distance, with no clouds and no atmospheric absorption. The catch is geometry. A satellite in low orbit circles Earth in roughly 90 minutes, and on many orbits Earth itself blocks the Sun for a substantial part of each revolution. The spacecraft alternates between full illumination and total eclipse dozens of times across a single ground day, and its power system must bridge every dark interval without interrupting computation.

### How much power can a satellite actually generate?

A satellite generates power equal to its solar array area multiplied by the solar flux and the cell efficiency, minus losses in wiring and conversion. A 10-square-meter array using cells at 30 percent efficiency in full sunlight produces roughly 4,000 watts before losses, and produces nothing at all during eclipse.

The step by step mechanism begins with photovoltaic conversion. Solar cells turn a fraction of incident sunlight into direct current electricity. Triple junction cells flown on many spacecraft convert around 30 percent of the flux, a figure the National Renewable Energy Laboratory has tracked through its efficiency records as commercial space cells improved across decades. The array must point toward the Sun to reach that output, so the spacecraft either rotates the array on a drive mechanism or accepts lower output at an angle. Generated power then divides three ways during sunlight: running the computers, running the spacecraft bus systems such as radios and attitude control, and recharging the batteries that eclipse will drain.

Eclipse imposes the battery tax. Energy used in darkness must first be stored during light, and storage is neither free nor lossless. Lithium ion batteries flown in orbit store energy at a specific energy far below that of propellant or of sunlight collected directly, and each charge and discharge cycle consumes a small part of battery life. A low orbit satellite may cycle its batteries more than 5,000 times per year, a cycling rate no ground backup battery ever faces. Battery designers therefore limit depth of discharge, using only part of the stored capacity on each eclipse to extend life, which means the installed battery must be substantially larger than the energy actually drawn each orbit. Mass grows again: the usable watt hour costs several watt hours of installed capacity and the kilograms that capacity weighs.

Batteries lose capacity when cold and degrade faster when hot, so heaters and radiators serve the power system before they serve the computers. Solar arrays also degrade under radiation and micrometeoroid damage, losing a few percent of output per year depending on altitude and shielding, a decline the European Space Agency documents in its space environment reports for photovoltaic assemblies. A constellation designed for a five year life must launch with excess array area sized for end of life output, not for the generous output of its first month. That excess is mass that earns nothing at first except survival later.

Power therefore sets a hard ceiling. The average power across a full orbit, sunlight and eclipse combined, after bus loads, battery losses, and degradation margins, is the power the computers may use on a sustained basis. Peak power in sunlight can exceed that average, and schedulers can burst work into illuminated intervals, but a workload that demands constant power must fit inside the orbital average. In orbit, electricity is a harvested and stored ration, delivered in pulses, taxed at every storage step, and shrinking slowly across the mission.

## The Thermal Wall: Rejecting Heat With Radiation Alone

Heat leaves a terrestrial data center by moving air or water, carrying thermal energy away in a flowing medium. Orbit offers no medium. Conduction moves heat inside the spacecraft, from chip to cold plate to transport loop, but conduction stops at the hull. Convection requires a fluid outside the surface, and outside there is vacuum. The only mechanism that crosses vacuum is radiation: a warm surface emits infrared energy directly to space, photon by photon, with no contact and no carrier. Every watt a server consumes becomes a watt of heat that a radiator must emit, and the physics of that emission was quantified in the nineteenth century.

Josef Stefan established the empirical relationship in 1879, and Ludwig Boltzmann derived it from thermodynamics in 1884. The Stefan-Boltzmann law states that the power radiated per unit area rises with the fourth power of absolute temperature, multiplied by the Stefan-Boltzmann constant of 5.67 times 10 to the power of negative eight watts per square meter per kelvin to the fourth power. The fourth power cuts both ways. A radiator running hotter emits far more power per square meter, which tempts designers toward high temperatures. The same law punishes low temperatures severely: halve the absolute temperature and the emitted power falls by a factor of 16. Electronics, however, prefer to run cool, and the gap between comfortable chip temperatures and efficient radiator temperatures defines the central design struggle.

A worked scale shows the consequence. A black surface at 300 kelvin, roughly room temperature, radiates about 459 watts per square meter under the Stefan-Boltzmann law, and a real radiator with imperfect emissivity and a view partly blocked by Earth radiates less in useful directions. A computing node drawing 10,000 watts therefore needs on the order of 25 to 30 square meters of effective radiator once margins, emissivity below unity, and Earth infrared input enter the calculation. Scale the node to 100,000 watts and the radiator grows toward the size of a tennis court, all of it launched, deployed, and kept pointed away from the Sun. Radiator area, not floor space, becomes the floor plan of the facility.

The International Space Station provides the flown precedent. The station rejects heat through ammonia cooling loops described in National Aeronautics and Space Administration system documentation: coolant collects heat from internal water loops through heat exchangers, pumps circulate the ammonia to large external radiator panels, and the panels emit the heat to space as they rotate to favorable angles. Ammonia serves the external loop because it remains fluid across the station temperature range and transports heat efficiently, while water serves internal loops where crew safety rules out ammonia leaks. The station radiators span areas measured in hundreds of square meters to reject heat loads far smaller than a commercial computing constellation proposes per unit of mass, which is precisely why the precedent instructs. The mechanism is proven. Its area and mass cost is also proven.

Getting heat from chip to radiator adds further steps, each with a temperature drop. Heat conducts from the processor into a cold plate, transfers into a pumped fluid or heat pipe, travels to the radiator panel, spreads across the panel, and radiates away. Each interface drops temperature, so the radiator runs cooler than the chip by the sum of those drops, and a cooler radiator needs more area under the fourth power law.

The thermal wall is a wall because no invention repeals a fourth power law of nature. Better materials raise emissivity toward its maximum of one. Higher operating temperatures shrink area at the cost of electronics life and efficiency. Heat, not logic, draws the blueprint.

## Radiation, Bit Flips and the Price of Trustworthy Answers

Beyond the atmosphere and partly beyond the protection of Earth magnetosphere, energetic particles cross spacecraft electronics continuously. Cosmic rays and solar particles strike memory cells and logic transistors, deposit charge along their paths, and occasionally deposit enough charge in one place to flip a stored bit from zero to one or one to zero. Engineers call each such event a single event upset. The upset rarely damages hardware permanently. The bit simply changes value, silently, and a computation that trusted that bit produces a wrong answer with no alarm attached. For a data center selling trustworthy answers, silent wrong answers are the most expensive failure mode available.

The mechanism proceeds in steps. A high energy particle enters a memory array, ionizes atoms along its track, and frees charge carriers inside the silicon. If the collected charge at a storage node exceeds the critical charge that defines its state, the node flips and holds the new wrong value until rewritten. Smaller transistors, built for density and low power, store less charge per bit and therefore flip more easily, which means the most modern ground processors are often the most vulnerable when flown unchanged. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration documented upset rates across decades of missions in its radiation effects research, with rates rising sharply at higher altitudes and during solar particle events, when the Sun floods near Earth space with energetic protons.

Altitude sharpens the tradeoff. Lower orbits benefit from greater magnetic shielding and from the atmosphere absorbing some particle flux above the spacecraft, while higher orbits within the range under discussion pass closer to or through regions of the Van Allen belts where trapped particles concentrate. A shell chosen to reduce atmospheric drag and extend orbital life can therefore raise the upset rate that the computing hardware must survive. The same design decision that saves propellant spends error correction capacity, and the accounting must be done jointly rather than by separate teams optimizing separate variables.

Defenses exist, and each charges rent. Shielding with added material stops lower energy particles but adds launch mass, and against the highest energy cosmic rays shielding can even worsen matters by producing secondary particles in the shielding itself. Error correcting code memory adds extra bits to each word, computes check values on every write, verifies them on every read, and corrects single bit errors while detecting double bit errors, at the cost of memory capacity, power, and processor cycles. Triple modular redundancy runs the same computation on three processors and votes on the result, tripling hardware and power for the protected function. Periodic scrubbing rereads and corrects memory before errors accumulate beyond what the code can repair. Each layer consumes exactly the resources the earlier sections rationed: mass, power, and thermal margin.

The HPE Spaceborne Computer experiment, installed on the International Space Station in 2017 by Hewlett Packard Enterprise and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, tested whether commercial servers could survive this environment through software protection rather than radiation hardened hardware. The system ran for more than a year in orbit, endured power and radiation conditions, and demonstrated that commodity processors with software based fault tolerance could complete useful workloads, while also recording the errors and throttling events that protection had to absorb. The lesson cuts precisely: trustworthy orbital computing is possible, and its price is paid in overhead that a ground facility never budgets.

That price changes the economics of the product. A customer buying orbital computation buys corrected computation, and the correction consumes a measurable share of the raw capacity launched. Trust, in orbit, is a line item with a mass and a power bill.

## Choosing the Shell: Altitude, Inclination and a Crowded Sky

Low Earth orbit is the region of space above the sensible atmosphere and below the Van Allen radiation belts core, conventionally bounded at altitudes from roughly 160 kilometres to 2,000 kilometres, a definition used by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration and the European Space Agency in their orbital environment references. Within that broad band, a constellation designer chooses specific shells: groups of satellites sharing an altitude and an inclination, the angle between the orbital plane and the equator. Shell choice looks like geography. It functions as economics, because altitude and inclination together determine sunlight, drag, radiation exposure, coverage of the ground, and the number of satellites a complete service requires.

Altitude governs the coverage step by step. A satellite sees a circular patch of Earth whose radius grows with altitude, and its signals or optical links reach correspondingly farther. Higher shells therefore need fewer satellites for continuous coverage, because each node watches more ground and sees neighboring nodes across longer lines of sight. Higher shells also lengthen each orbital period, slow the apparent motion across the sky, and increase the distance that downlink signals travel, weakening them under the inverse square law. Lower shells invert every term: more satellites required, faster motion, shorter contacts with any one ground station, but stronger signals, lower latency by distance, and a natural cleansing effect described below.

The band from 500 to 1,000 kilometres attracts constellation designers for converging reasons. Below roughly 500 kilometres, atmospheric drag shortens orbital life and raises propellant spending. Above roughly 1,000 kilometres, the inner Van Allen belt raises radiation exposure for electronics and solar arrays, increasing upset rates and degradation. Between those bounds lies a compromise region where drag is manageable, radiation is elevated but engineerable, coverage per satellite is useful, and orbital periods near 95 to 105 minutes distribute satellites evenly as Earth rotates beneath the shell. The compromise explains why so many communications and proposed computing constellations cluster in the same few hundred kilometres of sky.

Inclination adds the second dimension. An equatorial shell serves equatorial populations and wastes nothing on polar coverage. A polar shell, inclined near 90 degrees, passes over the poles and, as Earth rotates, eventually over every longitude, giving global coverage at the cost of concentrating satellites where the orbital planes converge near the poles. Mid inclination shells near 53 degrees, the inclination flown by large communications constellations documented in Federal Communications Commission filings, concentrate capacity over the temperate latitudes where most customers live. For a data center constellation, inclination also shapes sunlight: certain Sun synchronous inclinations near 97 to 99 degrees keep the orbital plane at a fixed angle to the Sun, producing consistent illumination or, in a dawn dusk configuration, near continuous sunlight that eases the battery burden of eclipse.

Crowding disciplines every choice. The shells most attractive for physics are also the shells already filled with communications constellations, Earth observation satellites, debris from decades of launches, and the tracked catalog maintained by United States Space Command, which follows tens of thousands of objects larger than roughly 10 centimetres. Each added constellation raises conjunction rates for every operator in neighboring shells, and each operator must maneuver to avoid collisions, spending propellant budgeted for station keeping.

Shell selection is thus a single decision with five prices attached: satellite count, propellant life, radiation overhead, power consistency, and collision risk. No shell minimizes all five. A computing service, hungry for power and allergic to silent errors, weights the radiation and sunlight terms more heavily than a simple relay service would. Geography in orbit is a budget written in kilometres and degrees.

## Staying in Place: Station Keeping, Drag and Propellant

An orbit is not a shelf. A satellite placed at a chosen altitude and inclination begins drifting away from its assigned position immediately, pushed by small forces that never rest, and a constellation holds its pattern only by spending propellant for the entire life of every satellite. Station keeping is the name for that spending: the regular application of small thrusts that correct altitude, spacing, and orientation of the orbital pattern against decay and perturbation. When the propellant ends, the pattern ends with it, however healthy the computers remain. Design life in orbit is therefore usually a propellant statement before it is an electronics statement.

The currency of the work is delta-v, the total change in velocity a spacecraft can deliver with its propellant, measured in meters per second. A propellant load, an exhaust velocity, and a starting mass together fix the delta-v budget, and every maneuver draws against that single account. Correcting inclination spends delta-v at punishing rates, because turning an orbital plane means redirecting the full orbital velocity vector. The final deorbit maneuver must be reserved from the first day and never spent on operations.

Atmospheric drag is the largest routine withdrawal at low altitude. The atmosphere does not end at a clean boundary. It thins exponentially, and at 400 to 600 kilometres enough molecules remain that a satellite striking them at roughly 7.8 kilometres per second loses measurable energy on every orbit. The mechanism is simple momentum transfer: each collision removes a minute amount of orbital energy, the orbit sinks slightly, the satellite meets slightly denser air lower down, and the decay accelerates unless thrust restores the lost energy. Solar activity heats the upper atmosphere and makes it expand, raising density at a given altitude and multiplying drag, an effect the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration space weather services forecast because operators must plan propellant and reentry timing around it. In February 2022, a geomagnetic storm raised drag enough that dozens of newly launched Starlink satellites reentered despite functioning hardware, a loss SpaceX reported publicly and investigators attributed to the storm driven density increase at their low insertion altitude.

Designers answer drag in three ways, each with a price. Flying higher reduces density and drag steeply, at the cost of greater radiation exposure and longer signal paths. Building compact satellites reduces the force itself, at the cost of constraining solar array and radiator size, which computing payloads can rarely accept. Carrying more propellant, or more efficient electric propulsion using xenon or krypton, extends life, at the cost of launch mass and power drawn from the same ration the computers need. Electric thrusters documented by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration on Deep Space 1 in 1998 deliver far more delta-v per kilogram of propellant than chemical thrusters, but deliver it slowly, in thrust too gentle for urgent collision avoidance.

Perturbations beyond drag also draw on the account. Earth oblateness twists orbital planes at rates that depend on altitude and inclination, lunar and solar gravity tug unevenly, and solar radiation pressure pushes lightly on large arrays and radiators. A computing satellite, with its broad arrays and broader radiators, presents more area to every one of these forces than a compact satellite does.

The end arrives by arithmetic. A satellite launched with a five year propellant budget reaches a date after which it can no longer hold its slot, dodge debris, and still reserve its deorbit burn. The operator then retires a machine whose processors may still function, because position, not computation, was the scarce resource all along. Replacement launches are not failures of the plan. They are the plan, priced in delta-v from the beginning.

## Laser Links: A Network Fabric That Never Stands Still

A terrestrial data center rests on a fixed topology. Switches sit in racks, fibers run in trays, and a link that works at commissioning works in the same geometry years later. An orbital network has no such stability. Every node moves at roughly 17,500 miles per hour in low Earth orbit, neighbors ahead and behind hold a near constant separation, and neighbors in adjacent orbital planes drift continuously as the planes precess at different rates. The fabric must therefore be rebuilt in software many times each second even though no cable ever moves.

Optical inter-satellite links answer that problem with light rather than radio. A transmitting terminal generates a narrow beam, a fine steering mirror acquires the partner terminal, and a tracking loop holds the beam centered on a detector while both spacecraft vibrate and rotate. The mechanism proceeds in sequence. A coarse pointing assembly turns the telescope toward a predicted position derived from orbital ephemeris. A beacon or a widened beam sweeps a small uncertainty cone. The receiving terminal detects the sweep and returns its own beam. Both ends then narrow their beams, lock their tracking sensors, and begin data modulation on the same optical carrier.

### How fast is a laser link between two satellites?

Operational optical links between satellites transfer data at rates from 1 gigabit per second to more than 100 gigabits per second, depending on distance, telescope aperture, and transmit power. NASA reported in 2023 that its TBIRD payload delivered 200 gigabits per second from low Earth orbit, a record rate for a space optical downlink demonstration.

Higher rate alone does not make a network. A constellation needs routing across a mesh whose edge weights change with distance and pointing constraints. When two satellites in crossing planes meet, their relative velocity can exceed 15,000 miles per hour, the usable contact may last only minutes, and the link budget changes throughout the pass because range and off-axis angle change together. A routing protocol must therefore predict contacts in advance, schedule capacity on links that do not yet exist, and abandon routes before geometry breaks them. Delay tolerant networking, formalized by the Internet Engineering Task Force in RFC 4838 in 2007, was designed for exactly that class of problem, storing bundles at intermediate nodes until a suitable next hop appears.

Operational precedent exists at radio frequencies. The Iridium constellation, placed in service in 1998 with 66 satellites in polar orbit, maintains crosslinks between neighboring satellites so that a packet can travel through space and descend near its destination rather than near its origin. Each Iridium satellite links to neighbors in the same plane and in adjacent planes, forming a genuine mesh decades before optical terminals became compact enough for routine use. The architecture proved that handover, routing, and billing could function on moving nodes, and it also exposed the cost: crosslink hardware consumes power and mass on every satellite whether traffic is heavy or light.

A second precedent comes from relay rather than mesh design. The NASA Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System, first launched in 1983, places relay satellites in geostationary orbit to forward data between low orbit spacecraft and ground stations, extending contact far beyond a single ground pass. A relay tier can smooth intermittency, but it adds a hop, adds light time, and concentrates failure risk in a few expensive nodes.

The thermal thesis governs this layer as well. Every optical terminal draws power for its laser, amplifiers, and pointing actuators, and every watt becomes heat that radiators must reject. A designer who adds links to cut latency therefore also adds radiator area, and the fabric competes with the processors for the same thermal budget.

## The Way Down: Gateways, Weather and the Ground Bottleneck

Computing in orbit creates value only when results reach users on the ground, and that return path is the scarcest resource in the entire system. A satellite in low Earth orbit sees a given ground station for roughly 10 minutes per pass, with a handful of useful passes per day from a single site. The mechanism of scarcity is pure geometry. The station horizon limits contact to the arc where the satellite stands above a minimum elevation angle, outside that arc the Earth itself blocks the line of sight, and no increase in transmitter power changes the duration of the window.

Operators multiply gateways to multiply windows. A station in Svalbard, operated by Kongsberg Satellite Services, exploits polar orbits because nearly every revolution of a polar satellite passes near the pole, giving far more contacts per day than a mid-latitude site provides. Stations in places such as Punta Arenas, Mauritius, and Western Australia fill longitudinal gaps so that a satellite completing a 90 minute orbit finds a listening site within a few revolutions. Each added station, however, requires land, power, backhaul fiber, licensing in its national jurisdiction, and staff or remote operations, so the ground segment grows as a terrestrial infrastructure business attached to a space system.

Weather divides the two downlink technologies. Radio links in Ka band suffer rain attenuation, and the International Telecommunication Union rain models in Recommendation P.618 quantify fades that can exceed 20 decibels in heavy precipitation at tropical sites. Optical downlinks face a harder limit. Cloud is opaque at near infrared wavelengths, so a cloud covered station receives nothing at all rather than a degraded signal. The mitigation is site diversity. The European Space Agency optical ground station network concept, described in agency material in 2019, relies on geographically separated stations under different weather systems, with traffic routed to whichever site has clear sky at the scheduled pass.

Diversity works through a specific sequence. Forecast and local sky sensors rank stations by cloud probability. The constellation scheduler assigns each downlink to the best ranked station that the satellite will overfly. Data that misses its window stays in onboard storage, competes with newer data for the next window, and may be discarded under a priority policy if storage fills. Storage therefore acts as a buffer against weather, and buffer size becomes a design variable traded against mass and cost.

Radio backup remains necessary for command and safety even where optical links carry bulk data. Telemetry, tracking, and command traffic must get through in all weather because a spacecraft that cannot be commanded during an anomaly may be lost before skies clear. Operators therefore retain low rate radio links with near omnidirectional antennas for control, reserving high rate systems for payload data. The arrangement mirrors aviation practice, where critical control channels use the most robust rather than the fastest medium.

The bottleneck shapes the computing itself. Workloads whose output is far smaller than their input fit the downlink budget well, because a satellite can ingest sensor data directly from a neighboring instrument and transmit only a compact result. Workloads that must move large inputs up and large outputs down consume the scarcest resource twice. System architects therefore evaluate a proposed orbital service first by its data movement ratio, and only afterward by its processor requirements, because gateways and weather, not silicon, set the delivered throughput of the whole constellation.

## Latency Arithmetic: What Light Time Permits and Forbids

Latency in orbit begins with a constant that no engineering can negotiate. The speed of light in vacuum is 299,792 kilometers per second, a value fixed by definition of the meter since the General Conference on Weights and Measures adopted it in 1983. Light in glass fiber travels slower, at roughly two thirds of that speed, because the refractive index of silica is near 1.5. That difference gives free space links a genuine advantage over long terrestrial routes. A signal crossing 3,000 miles through vacuum arrives sooner than the same signal crossing 3,000 miles of fiber, and the advantage grows with distance.

Altitude then taxes every exchange. A satellite at 340 miles altitude, the approximate regime of the International Space Station as documented by NASA in 2022, adds at least 680 miles of vertical travel to a round trip between two ground users, before any horizontal distance counts. A geostationary satellite at 22,236 miles altitude adds far more. One hop to geostationary orbit and back takes about 240 milliseconds at light speed alone, a figure the International Telecommunication Union has long used in telephony planning, and no processor improvement can reduce it.

### Why can orbital computers not serve interactive applications?

Interactive applications require a response within a fraction of a second, and orbital round trips consume that budget before computation begins. Light time to orbit and back, added gateway processing, added routing hops, and added queuing together push response times past the threshold where typing, gaming, and live control feel immediate or remain stable.

The arithmetic compounds across a protocol. A modern web transaction is not one round trip but many. Name resolution, connection establishment, transport security negotiation, and request response exchanges each wait on the previous step under protocols standardized by the Internet Engineering Task Force, including RFC 9110 for HTTP semantics in 2022. Ten sequential round trips through a geostationary relay add seconds, not milliseconds. Protocol designers reduce chattiness with persistent connections and local caching, but a workload that is inherently conversational, such as remote desktop control or multiplayer simulation, cannot be restructured without becoming a different workload.

Low orbit reduces the penalty without removing it. A constellation at 340 to 550 miles can deliver round trips in the tens of milliseconds under favorable geometry, comparable to a long terrestrial fiber path. Favorable geometry, however, is temporary. The serving satellite changes every few minutes, routes lengthen and shorten as the mesh reconfigures, and jitter, the variation in delay, often harms interactive quality more than average delay does. Voice and control systems tolerate modest constant delay through buffering, but buffering itself adds delay, and variable delay forces larger buffers.

Batch workloads invert the problem. A training run or an image processing job that runs for hours cares about total throughput and completion time, not about the delay of any single packet. Its inputs can arrive in bursts during gateway passes, its outputs can leave the same way, and light time becomes a rounding error inside a long computation. The dividing line between fitting and failing workloads is therefore rarely processor speed. The line is the number of sequential round trips the workload demands per unit of useful work, multiplied by a light time that physics fixes and altitude sets.

Designers who ignore that multiplication repeat a familiar error. A demonstration that moves one request through orbit successfully proves reachability, not usability. Usability requires the full exchange pattern of the real application, measured across handovers, weather, and congested gateways, and judged against human patience thresholds that psychologists have measured in tens of milliseconds for direct manipulation tasks since Robert Miller published response time limits in 1968.

## Workloads That Fit Orbit and Workloads That Do Not

Sorting workloads for orbit is an exercise in matching data gravity, delay tolerance, and output size. The fitting class shares three traits. Inputs originate in space or tolerate delayed delivery, computation is dense relative to data movement, and outputs are compact or can wait. The failing class reverses each trait, demanding constant contact, rapid round trips, or movement of large data sets whose home is on the ground.

Earth observation preprocessing is the clearest fit. A synthetic aperture radar satellite generates raw data at rates that can exceed several gigabits per second during imaging, a burden documented for the European Space Agency Sentinel-1 mission, launched in 2014, whose ground segment was designed around large daily data volumes. Sending every raw scene down consumes scarce downlink minutes. Processing on board changes the ratio. The mechanism runs in stages. The instrument writes raw echoes to storage. A processor applies focusing and calibration to form an image. A detection stage flags ships, floods, or deforestation against reference data. Only the flagged chips and a compressed summary descend, while the full scene follows later or on request. Each stage discards volume that users rarely need, and the downlink carries answers rather than measurements.

Delay tolerant model training also fits under specific conditions. Training divides naturally into long computations punctuated by synchronization, and gradient updates can be compressed, quantized, and exchanged in batches during contact windows. A constellation can hold a replica of a model on several satellites, train each replica on locally collected data, and merge updates when links allow. The approach resembles federated learning, formalized by Google researchers H. Brendan McMahan and colleagues in a 2017 paper on communication efficient learning from decentralized data. The orbital variant accepts staleness that a data center would reject, trading a small loss in convergence speed for the ability to compute where the data originates.

Batch inference belongs to the same family. Classifying a backlog of images, transcribing recorded audio, or scoring archived sensor traces has no human waiting on each item. Jobs queue on board, processors work through the queue during eclipse and sunlight alike within the thermal limit, and results accumulate for the next gateway pass. Store and forward operation, the pattern that delay tolerant networking standards describe, becomes the normal mode rather than an exception.

The non-fitting class is equally distinct. Interactive databases fail because their consistency protocols require rapid agreement among replicas, and partitions between orbital and ground nodes last minutes rather than milliseconds. High frequency trading fails because its value decays in microseconds and its data originates in terrestrial exchanges. General purpose cloud hosting fails in a subtler way. Its tenants expect arbitrary software, constant connectivity, and rapid provisioning, while an orbital node offers fixed hardware, intermittent contact, and a thermal ceiling that throttles sustained load. Live video contribution can fit when a satellite is itself the camera platform, but live video distribution to mass audiences belongs on terrestrial content networks whose caches sit near viewers.

A useful test follows from these contrasts. A workload fits orbit when removing the network for 30 minutes merely delays its results, and fails when the same removal corrupts its state, breaks its promises to users, or destroys its economic value. That test, applied honestly, leaves a real but narrow catalogue: sensing, preprocessing, batch analysis, and delay tolerant training at the edge of the data source. The catalogue is valuable precisely because it is narrow, since each fitting workload exploits the one advantage orbit offers, proximity to instruments above the atmosphere, instead of competing with ground data centers on their strongest ground.

## Autonomy by Necessity: Running Machines Nobody Can Touch

No technician will open an orbital server, and for long stretches no operator will speak to it either. Light time in low orbit is short, measured in milliseconds, but contact is intermittent, and intermittency rather than distance creates the autonomy requirement. A satellite may pass over its operations center only a few times per day, and a constellation spread across many planes distributes contacts unevenly, leaving individual nodes unobserved for hours. Faults that a data center resolves with a page and a short walk must therefore be handled by the machine itself, correctly, the first time.

Fault detection, isolation, and recovery, known by the acronym FDIR in aerospace practice, provides the governing discipline. The European Space Agency standard ECSS-E-ST-70-11C, issued in 2008, sets requirements for spacecraft on-board control procedures that embody the same logic. The mechanism proceeds through four stages. Sensors and software monitors compare telemetry against expected ranges and models. A detection stage raises an event when a value departs from its envelope. An isolation stage identifies the smallest replaceable unit consistent with the evidence, distinguishing a sensor fault from a real thermal or power excursion. A recovery stage then acts, switching to a redundant unit, shedding load, restarting a process, or placing the spacecraft in a safe mode that preserves power and communication while awaiting ground instruction.

Each stage carries a design tension. Sensitive detection catches faults early but triggers false recoveries that waste redundancy and interrupt service. Conservative detection avoids false alarms but allows a failing battery cell or a stuck reaction wheel to progress toward loss of the spacecraft. Engineers tune thresholds against failure data from earlier missions, and they test the full chain in simulators that inject faults faster and in stranger combinations than flight experience provides.

Software autonomy extends beyond hardware faults. An orbital computer must schedule its own work around power and heat. It knows its orbit, predicts eclipse entry, forecasts which gateway passes will occur, and can plan accordingly. A well designed executive therefore queues heavy computation for periods with surplus solar power and adequate radiator capacity, defers deferrable jobs when a payload task takes priority, and checkpoints long jobs so that a safe mode entry does not erase hours of work. Checkpointing follows a simple discipline: write state to nonvolatile storage at intervals, verify each write, and keep the previous verified state until the new one is complete, so that any interruption returns the job to a known point rather than to corruption.

Ground operations economics push in the same direction. The Goddard Space Flight Center missions of the 1970s often staffed control rooms around the clock with large teams, a model that the National Aeronautics and Space Administration itself documented as a driver of operations cost in later cost reduction studies. A constellation of hundreds of nodes cannot carry hundreds of control teams. Operators therefore manage by exception. Healthy satellites report summaries, ground software correlates fleet wide patterns, and human attention concentrates on the few nodes whose telemetry departs from the fleet norm. Staffing scales with anomaly rate rather than with node count only when autonomy absorbs routine events without human help.

Autonomy also creates a distinct risk. A recovery action that is correct for one satellite can be harmful when a shared software fault triggers it across the fleet at the same time. Designers therefore stagger software uploads, canary new versions on a small subset of nodes, and retain a protected minimal mode that no routine update can overwrite. The machine that nobody can touch must be able to save itself, and must also be prevented from destroying itself beyond operator reach.

## Maintenance Without Hands: Failure, Redundancy and Replacement

Terrestrial maintenance assumes access. A failed drive is pulled, a faulty fan is swapped, and a technician with a screwdriver restores a rack in an afternoon. Orbital maintenance at constellation scale assumes the opposite. No repair visit will occur, the cost of rendezvous and servicing for an uncrewed satellite generally exceeds the value of the node, and the design must therefore treat every component failure as permanent for that unit. Reliability engineering shifts from repairability to survival probability over a fixed service life.

Redundancy is the first instrument. Critical units fly in pairs or triples, power controllers and radios and reaction wheels among them, so that a single failure degrades rather than ends the mission. The mathematics is unforgiving. If a unit has a 90 percent probability of surviving five years, a simplex string inherits that figure, while a fully redundant pair, under the independence assumptions set out in standard reliability texts such as Charles Ebeling An Introduction to Reliability and Maintainability Engineering in 1997, survives unless both fail. Independence, however, is an assumption rather than a fact. Units built in the same batch share manufacturing defects, units on the same spacecraft share heat and radiation, and redundancy fractions must therefore be spent across different failure domains rather than stacked inside one box.

Designers quantify the spend as a redundancy fraction, the share of mass and power devoted to spares and duplicate strings rather than to revenue producing computation. A constellation node might devote a substantial fraction of its mass budget to redundant avionics, extra battery capacity, and propellant for disposal and collision avoidance, before a single processor board is counted. That fraction competes directly with the radiator budget that the thermal thesis places at the center of capacity. Mass given to a spare radio is mass denied to a larger radiator, and the trade is settled at design time for the whole fleet, not adjusted later when failure data arrives.

Replacement is the second instrument, and it converts maintenance into logistics. A constellation operator plans a replenishment cadence, launching fresh satellites to replace units that have failed, exhausted propellant, or become obsolete, and deorbiting or parking the old units in disposal orbits under guidelines such as the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee mitigation guidelines, first issued in 2002. The fleet stays healthy as a population even though no individual member is ever repaired. Launch capacity, production rate, and licensing then become maintenance variables, and a production line halt becomes the orbital equivalent of a spare parts shortage.

The contrast with a crewed station clarifies the model. On the International Space Station, astronauts have replaced pumps, batteries, and handrails during spacewalks documented by NASA across the station assembly era, including the ammonia pump module replacement in 2010. Hands allow modular repair, extend service life far beyond initial design, and justify carrying spare units on orbit. None of those advantages scale to hundreds of uncrewed nodes spread across many planes, where a servicing mission to one node costs more than its replacement and cannot be repeated across the fleet.

End of life planning completes the discipline. Each node must reserve propellant and power to leave its operational orbit, passivate stored energy sources so that a dead satellite does not fragment, and reenter or reach a graveyard orbit within the period its license requires. A maintenance model built on replacement therefore also carries a disposal obligation for every unit launched. The true cost of an orbital computer includes its launch, its redundant fraction, its replacement, and its removal, and a business case that counts only the first understates the price of keeping capacity on orbit.

## When One Satellite Fails: Cascades Across a Constellation

A constellation fails as a system, not only as a collection of parts. Identical nodes share software, share procedures, and share traffic, so a fault that begins in one place can recruit the rest of the fleet into the failure. Understanding that recruitment matters more than cataloguing individual component faults, because the largest outages in distributed systems historically arise from interaction effects rather than from the initial defect, a pattern documented in post incident reviews across the cloud industry and analyzed in the study of [cascading failures in large cloud systems](/2026/11/14/how-cascading-failures-work-in-cloud-systems/) published for terrestrial operators.

Load redistribution is the first cascade mechanism. When a satellite fails or enters safe mode, its share of sensing, processing, and relay traffic does not vanish. Schedulers reassign the work to neighbors, neighbors draw more power and reject more heat, and a node that was operating near its thermal ceiling may throttle or fail under the added load. The sequence then repeats. Each additional failure concentrates the same total demand on fewer survivors, so the load per survivor rises in steps, and a fleet sized with thin margins can cross from one failure to total collapse without any new external cause. Terrestrial operators call the pattern a metastable failure, a term formalized by researchers including Nathan Bronson and colleagues in a 2021 paper on metastable failures in distributed systems, where a system remains in a degraded state because the recovery load itself sustains the overload.

Retry behaviour is the second mechanism. Ground gateways and peer satellites that receive no answer from a failed node retry their requests, often on timers set for ordinary transient faults. Retries multiply traffic precisely when capacity has fallen. A gateway that retries a downlink request, a peer that retries a routing update, and a client that retries an inference call can together generate several times the normal load aimed at the surviving nodes least able to carry it. The standard defenses are exponential backoff, retry budgets, and circuit breakers that stop traffic to a failing destination, patterns catalogued by the National Institute of Standards and Technology in cloud resilience guidance and long standard in terrestrial practice. Orbital systems must implement those defenses on board, because the congestion occurs in space where no operator can intervene during a contact gap.

Shared software faults form the third and most dangerous mechanism. A constellation runs one software build across hundreds of identical nodes, so a defect triggered by a rare date, a malformed input, or a specific orbital geometry can fire on many satellites within a single revolution. The 1990 AT and T long distance network collapse, investigated by the Federal Communications Commission, followed exactly that pattern on the ground, when a software fault in one switch triggered resets that propagated to neighboring switches carrying the same code. Orbit adds a cruel variation. A fleet wide fault can place many nodes in safe mode simultaneously, saturating the few gateway contacts available for recovery commands, so that restoration proceeds at the speed of the contact schedule rather than at the speed of diagnosis.

Defenses therefore operate at fleet level. Operators stagger software rollouts across orbital planes, hold a fraction of the fleet on the previous build as a control population, and design load shedding that discards low priority work when survivor load crosses a threshold. Schedulers reserve thermal and power headroom explicitly as cascade insurance, accepting lower nominal capacity in exchange for survival of a single node loss. That reservation returns the argument to radiators once more. Headroom is physical. A fleet that keeps spare thermal capacity can absorb a neighbor failure, and a fleet run at its limit cannot, whatever its software promises.

## Hardened Silicon Against Commercial Chips: The Hardware Fork

Every orbital computer begins with a fork between two kinds of silicon that obey the same physics and opposite economics. Radiation-hardened processors are built to survive ionizing radiation by design, through enlarged transistors, insulating substrates, and redundant logic that shrugs off a charged particle strike. Commercial processors are built to sell by the million, through the smallest transistors a fab can print and the highest clock rates a market will pay for. The gap between the paths is measured in generations. The BAE Systems RAD750, flown on the Mars rovers Curiosity and Perseverance and documented by NASA in mission hardware descriptions from 2011 and 2020, runs at clock rates near 200 megahertz, while commercial server parts from Intel and AMD in the same period ran above 3,000 megahertz across dozens of cores. Hardening buys certainty at the price of speed, because each protective feature consumes die area that a commercial designer would spend on arithmetic.

The mechanism behind the gap starts inside the transistor. A heavy ion crossing a memory cell deposits charge along its track, and that charge can flip a stored bit in a single event upset or latch a parasitic circuit open in a single event latchup. A hardened process counters the first failure by storing each logical bit in multiple physical cells and voting among them, and counters the second by isolating transistors on a silicon on insulator wafer so that a stray current path has nowhere to propagate. A commercial fab counters the same failures with volume instead, accepting that a small fraction of parts will fail and replacing them from a production run counted in millions.

The Spaceborne Computer experiment, run by Hewlett Packard Enterprise and NASA aboard the International Space Station from 2017 and reported by the partners in 2019, tested whether the second path could carry real workloads. The system flew unmodified commercial servers, protected not by hardened silicon but by software that watched for radiation induced errors, throttled power, and restarted failed processes. The reported result was that the machines completed their test period and returned usable performance despite the radiation environment of low Earth orbit, with software fault tolerance absorbing errors that hardware hardening would have prevented. The lesson is narrow and durable. Software can detect a flipped bit after the fact, compare the result against a redundant computation, discard the corrupted answer, and recompute, step by step, at the cost of wasted cycles.

Cost per computation decides which path a data center operator can afford, and the arithmetic favors commercial silicon by orders of magnitude whenever the workload tolerates delay and recomputation. A hardened flight processor costs tens of thousands of dollars per chip in low production volumes, a figure consistent with procurement reporting on radiation hardened parts summarized by the Government Accountability Office in reviews of space acquisition during the 2010s, while a commercial server processor of far greater throughput costs a few thousand dollars or less at volume. Batch inference, model training with checkpointing, and image processing all tolerate discarded and repeated work, because the computation can restart from a saved state without harm. Real time control of a spacecraft bus tolerates none of it. The fork therefore splits the payload rather than settling it. Flight critical functions stay on hardened parts, bulk computation migrates to commercial parts wrapped in software protection, and the radiator must still reject the heat of both, which is why faster cheap silicon does not relax the thermal ceiling and often tightens it.

## Mass Production and Launch: Building a Data Center Like a Fleet

A terrestrial data center is a building project, poured, wired, and commissioned once, while an orbital data center is a manufacturing project, repeated until the fleet reaches capacity. The distinction changes every cost and schedule assumption. A building tolerates custom work because a crew stands beside the structure for months, cutting and fitting on site. A fleet punishes custom work because each unit must leave a production line identical to the unit before it, sealed for a launch it will never rehearse, and operated by radio for its entire life. SpaceX demonstrated the production logic at scale with Starlink, reporting to the Federal Communications Commission in deployment filings from 2019 onward that flat packed satellites were stacked by the dozens inside a single fairing and released as a train, a packing method possible only because the spacecraft shared one design, one interface, and one test sequence. An orbital computing fleet inherits the same discipline or it inherits building costs at launch prices.

Launch cadence becomes the construction schedule, and the schedule behaves like a factory takt time rather than a ribbon cutting. A fleet adds capacity in the increments a rocket can lift, on dates set by range availability, weather, and vehicle turnaround. The mechanism runs in sequence. Finished units wait in a queue for a manifest slot, the slot fixes the mass and volume available, the launch places the units in an initial orbit, electric propulsion raises them to an operational orbit over weeks, checkout confirms power and thermal control, and only then does saleable capacity appear. Operators therefore design for graceful partial capacity, partitioning workloads so that a missing tranche reduces throughput instead of halting service, a partitioning problem terrestrial operators meet only during phased construction.

Unit cost curves reward the fleet model and punish hesitation. The first unit of any spacecraft carries the full design, qualification, and tooling cost, a pattern the NASA Cost Estimating Handbook, 2014 edition, formalizes through learning curve theory, under which each doubling of cumulative production cuts unit labor cost by a predictable percentage as workers repeat tasks and defects fall. A building captures almost none of that curve because the second hall differs from the first in soil, code, and utility connection. A fleet captures the curve fully if the design freezes early and changes are refused, because the hundredth radiator panel, battery pack, and computer tray come off the same jigs as the first. Computing hardware improves on a cycle of roughly two years, so a frozen fleet design locks in processors that age against ground competition while the fleet is still launching, and an unfrozen design restarts qualification and destroys the learning curve. Operators resolve the tension by freezing the bus, the structure, power, and thermal systems that dominate mass, while allowing the computer trays inside a standard slot to refresh between production blocks.

The fleet model also rewrites failure accounting. A building concentrates risk in one site, where a fire or flood can remove the whole asset, while a fleet spreads risk across hundreds of independent units, each small enough that its loss is an operating statistic rather than a catastrophe. The rational operator therefore orders spares as part of the original run, launches replacements on the same cadence as expansion units, and treats the line itself as infrastructure, closer to a chip fab that must never fully idle than to a construction site that finishes and disperses.

## The Ground Baseline: What Terrestrial Data Centers Still Do Better

The ground baseline is formidable, and any honest account of orbital computing starts by conceding how much a terrestrial facility does well. A ground data center draws power from a grid connection measured in tens or hundreds of megawatts, adds diesel or gas generation behind it, and rejects heat into air or water that carries the heat away and is replaced by more air or water at no launch cost. The mechanism of terrestrial cooling explains the advantage in one pass. A server converts electricity into heat, a fan or a liquid cold plate moves that heat into a coolant loop, the loop carries it to a chiller or a cooling tower, and the tower evaporates water or rejects heat to outside air, after which the cooled fluid returns to repeat the cycle. Engineers comparing facilities start from [how data centers work on the ground](/2026/10/07/how-data-centers-work/) because the grid fed, air and liquid cooled building sets the cost and density standard that any orbital design must meet on total cost per delivered computation, not on novelty.

Power density follows from that cooling chain. The Uptime Institute, in its annual data center survey reporting through the 2010s and 2020s, documented average rack densities climbing from single digit kilowatts toward and past 10 kilowatts per rack, with high performance computing halls designed far higher, because a liquid loop can move heat at rates no practical radiator field in orbit can match per unit of deployed mass. In orbit the same cold plate works perfectly well inside the spacecraft, and then the chain stops, because the final step, rejection to an environment that carries heat away, does not exist. The radiator is the whole answer, and its area grows with every watt.

Repair is the second ground advantage, and it compounds daily. A failed drive, fan, or power supply in a terrestrial hall is a ticket, a cart, and a technician, with mean repair times measured in hours in the operations literature summarized by Google in its site reliability engineering publications from 2016 onward, where the company described fleets of commodity servers kept useful by rapid part replacement rather than by component perfection. An orbital unit offers none of those options. A failed pump or a stuck deployable is permanent for that unit, so designers add redundant strings for every function whose loss would kill the spacecraft, and each redundant string adds mass, power draw, and heat that the radiator must reject. Reliability on the ground is a staffing decision, while reliability in orbit is a mass decision, and mass is the scarcest currency in the system.

Fibre density completes the baseline. A terrestrial campus sits on multiple diverse fibre paths, and adding bandwidth means leasing another wavelength or pulling another cable through an existing duct, at costs the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory documented in its United States Data Center Energy Usage Report, 2016, as a small share of facility budgets beside power and cooling. Inside the hall, switching fabrics connect tens of thousands of servers at latencies set by the speed of light across meters. An orbital fleet must move its data by radio or laser across kilometers to thousands of kilometers, sharing spectrum, pointing terminals, and accepting contact geometry dictated by orbital mechanics. For workloads built on tight coupling between processors, that interconnect gap alone settles the comparison before power or cooling enters it, and it is why the ground remains the default and orbit must argue for exceptions workload by workload.

## Debris and the Kessler Cascade: The Shared Sky Problem

Every object placed in orbit shares its volume with every other object there, and the sharing is not peaceful. Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais set out the governing analysis in the Journal of Geophysical Research in 1978, arguing that collisions between orbiting objects would generate fragments faster than atmospheric drag could remove them once object density passed a threshold, so that each collision raised the probability of the next. The mechanism is a cascade with simple steps. Two objects meet at relative speeds near 10 kilometers per second, the impact converts both into a cloud of fragments across a range of sizes, each fragment becomes an independent collision hazard on its own orbit, and the enlarged population raises the encounter rate for everything else in the shell.

Collision probability scales with object count in a way that punishes crowded shells twice. The chance that a given spacecraft meets debris grows with the number of tracked and untracked objects crossing its orbit, and the total number of collisions across a shell grows roughly with the square of the population, since each added object can strike any of the objects already present. NASA, through its Orbital Debris Program Office and its long running breakup and environment models including ORDEM 3.0, released in 2019, has maintained the catalogs and flux estimates operators use to shield and maneuver. A data center fleet adds large objects with large cross sectional area, because radiator panels present broad flat surfaces to the debris flux, and area is exactly the variable that converts flux into expected impacts per year.

End of life disposal is therefore part of the design rather than an afterthought, and the physics of disposal mirrors the physics of return. A satellite in low orbit disposes of itself by lowering its perigee with a final propulsive burn or by deploying a drag device that enlarges its area, after which atmospheric drag removes energy on each pass, the orbit circularizes lower and decays faster, heating rises as density increases, and the structure breaks up and ablates in the upper atmosphere. Controlled reentry targets the burn so that surviving debris falls into a remote ocean corridor, the same targeting discipline, at far lower energy and with no surviving payload, that governs returning hardware through the atmosphere in [sample return missions](/2026/10/19/how-sample-return-missions-work/), where a capsule must instead survive the heating that a disposal plan deliberately uses to destroy the spacecraft.

### What happens to a data center satellite at the end of its life?

A disposal burn lowers the orbit, drag then pulls the satellite into denser air over weeks or months, frictional heating breaks the structure apart, most material ablates high in the atmosphere, and any surviving fragments fall along a predicted corridor, ideally over remote ocean, ending the collision hazard the unit posed.

Operators plan that sequence from launch day because propellant reserved for disposal cannot serve station keeping, and a unit that exhausts its propellant early becomes an uncontrolled object for the remainder of its natural decay, which at higher altitudes can run to decades or centuries under the decay estimates published by the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordinating Committee in its mitigation guidelines, first issued in 2002 and revised since. If each unit disposes successfully with high probability, a fleet of hundreds still expects a residue of failures, and each failure is a large derelict in exactly the shell the operator most needs to keep clean. Debris discipline is thus a capacity cost, paid in propellant, shielding, tracking, and maneuver labor for the life of the fleet.

## Law Above Jurisdiction: Treaties, Licences and Liability

An orbital data center operates inside a legal structure built for flags and rockets, not for servers, and the structure assigns responsibility by launch rather than by location. The Outer Space Treaty, signed in 1967 and ratified by every major spacefaring state, declares in Article VI that states bear international responsibility for national activities in space, including activities by private companies, and must authorize and supervise them. Article VII adds that a state which launches or procures the launch of an object is internationally liable for damage caused by that object. The Liability Convention, adopted in 1972, then splits the standard by where harm occurs, imposing absolute liability for damage on the surface of the Earth or to aircraft in flight and fault based liability for damage in orbit. The mechanism matters to an operator in sequence. A company incorporates in one state, that state licenses the activity, a launch occurs from possibly a second state, the spacecraft is registered by one launching state under the Registration Convention of 1975, and liability follows the launching states regardless of where customers, data, or ground stations sit.

Licensing turns those principles into paperwork with technical teeth. A state regulator, in the United States principally the Federal Communications Commission for spectrum and commercial remote sensing style review and the Federal Aviation Administration for launch and reentry, conditions a licence on debris mitigation, disposal reliability, and financial responsibility. Spectrum is coordinated internationally under the ITU Radio Regulations, administered by the International Telecommunication Union, which allocate frequency bands, require filings for satellite networks, and govern interference rights between systems. An orbital computer that cannot lawfully transmit is a vault with no door, so the downlink and inter satellite links that define its usefulness exist only inside assignments and coordination agreements negotiated years before launch.

Liability for data, as opposed to liability for hardware, has no equivalent treaty, and the gap is where commercial disputes will live. A collision claim fits the Liability Convention, slowly and state to state, but a claim that processing was mishandled, disclosed, or lost fits no space instrument at all and falls back on contract, on the law of the licensing state, and on the law of the customer state, potentially at once. The unresolved question is which jurisdiction governs data that is received in one country, processed in orbit over several others, and returned to a fourth, a question sharpened by the data localization and government access rules analyzed under the [sovereign cloud](/2026/11/03/how-sovereign-cloud-works/) model, where physical location of hardware is used as the lever for legal control. Orbit removes the lever. The hardware sits in no state territory, since Article II of the Outer Space Treaty bars national appropriation of outer space, yet it remains under the jurisdiction and control of its state of registry under Article VIII, which preserves ownership and authority over the object itself.

That registry based jurisdiction produces answers that surprise customers. The licensing state can impose export controls on the hardware and software aboard, as the United States does through the International Traffic in Arms Regulations and the Export Administration Regulations, and it can compel operator cooperation under its own lawful access statutes, wherever the satellite happens to be overhead. The legal product an orbital operator can honestly sell is not lawlessness or immunity, but a defined and unusual jurisdictional profile, stable across geography, priced against the compliance burden of building equivalent capacity under several terrestrial regimes at once.

## The Complication: When the Ground Becomes the Harder Place

The strongest case against the thermal thesis does not dispute the radiator arithmetic at all, and that is what makes it serious. It argues that the binding constraint on computing capacity in practice is rarely a physical ceiling derived from first principles, but a queue, a permit, a water right, or a substation, and that terrestrial capacity has begun to fail on those mundane constraints on a schedule that has nothing to do with thermodynamics. In the United States, the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, in its interconnection queue analysis published in 2024, counted roughly 2,600 gigawatts of generation and storage capacity waiting in queues for grid connection studies and agreements, more than twice the installed capacity of the national grid, with typical projects completing only a fraction of entries after waits of several years. A data center that cannot obtain power on a commercial timescale has a capacity of zero regardless of how cheaply it could cool itself, and that zero arrives by administrative process rather than by physics.

Water and land tighten the same screw from other directions. The United States Environmental Protection Agency and state regulators in water stressed basins have faced sustained local opposition to evaporative cooling withdrawals, and reporting by state agencies in Arizona, including the Arizona Department of Water Resources in its 2023 groundwater decisions, showed large industrial water allocations being curtailed or denied on aquifer sustainability grounds. Permitting adds years in many jurisdictions through environmental review, zoning contests, and litigation risk that a developer must price before breaking ground. Launch cost per kilogram, by contrast, has fallen on an industrial learning curve, with the Falcon 9 demonstrating routine first stage reuse from 2017 onward under SpaceX reporting to the Federal Aviation Administration and in company mission records. If the terrestrial constraint worsens on a political schedule while the orbital constraint improves on a manufacturing schedule, the crossover for some workloads can arrive before the thermal arithmetic, read in isolation, would predict it.

The workload qualification does the real work in the argument, and delay tolerant batch work is its home ground. For such work the comparison is not radiator against cooling tower in the abstract, but a launched tranche of capacity on a known date against a terrestrial hall waiting on a substation, a water determination, and a permit defence. Step by step, the orbital path converts an uncertain multi year siting process into a manufacturing and launch process whose delays are measured in months and whose unit of expansion can be redirected among orbital shells, while the terrestrial path concentrates all delay in one jurisdiction whose decision cannot be appealed to physics.

The thesis survives that argument in narrowed form, and the narrowing should be stated plainly. Radiator area and mass still set the ceiling on how much computation a given launched mass can sustain, and no queue on the ground changes the Stefan-Boltzmann law or raises the temperature at which silicon can run. Capacity that cannot be permitted, powered, or watered is not capacity, so the practical contest is between a hard physical ceiling in orbit and a hard institutional ceiling on the ground, and either can bind first depending on place and decade scale planning. Thermal limits govern what orbit can do per kilogram, while siting limits increasingly govern what the ground can do per year, and a serious operator watches both ceilings rather than mistaking either one for the whole sky.

## Who Pays and What the Bill Includes: The Cost Basis Problem

Announced figures for orbital computing rarely state what they include, and the omission is not a detail, because the three cost bases of a space system differ so widely that a number drawn from one basis can mislead by an order of magnitude against another. Development cost covers design, qualification, software, ground systems, and regulatory work, paid once and heavily front loaded. Launch cost covers the vehicle, integration, range services, and insurance for ascent, paid per tranche. Operations cost covers ground stations, network capacity, flight operations staffing, licensing compliance, collision avoidance maneuvering, and replacement units, paid every year for the life of the fleet. The NASA Cost Estimating Handbook, 2014 edition, insists on separating these bases through a work breakdown structure precisely because programs that blend them cannot be compared, and the same discipline applied to commercial announcements dissolves most headline claims.

The payer changes with the basis, which is why the question of who pays belongs inside the cost analysis rather than after it. Development is typically paid by investors or by an anchor government customer under milestone contracts, following the pattern the Congressional Budget Office described in its 2021 analysis of federal space acquisition, where fixed price milestone payments shifted some overrun risk to contractors while leaving the government as the funder of first resort for capability that no market had yet priced. Operations is paid, ultimately, by customers through service pricing, which means the replacement rate enters the bill whether the marketing material names it or not. A unit with a five year design life in a fleet of hundreds produces a standing annual replacement obligation, and the step by step arithmetic is unforgiving. The fleet divides by its service life to yield the units owed each year, each owed unit multiplies by its manufactured and launched cost, and that product is an operating expense as regular as electricity in a terrestrial hall, except that it arrives in rocket sized increments.

Insurance prices the risks that the cost bases leave exposed, and its structure reveals what actuaries believe about the fleet. Launch insurance covers the ascent phase, on orbit insurance covers defined failures after separation, and liability insurance covers third party damage, with rates the Government Accountability Office has reported in space acquisition reviews as running to a substantial percentage of insured value for launch plus the first year in orbit, far above the property rates a terrestrial facility pays on replacement value. The mechanism of fleet insurance differs from single mission cover in one useful way. A fleet can self insure against the loss of individual units once the population is large enough for loss rates to be predictable, retaining the premium and absorbing routine attrition as an operating cost, while still buying cover against correlated events such as a launch vehicle failure that destroys a full tranche or a debris event that damages many units in one shell.

Utilization is the quiet variable that decides whether any of the bases can be recovered. The bill, stated whole, is development amortized across a fleet that may be redesigned, launch at market or transfer prices, operations staffed around the clock, insurance bought or honestly reserved, replacements launched on schedule, and disposal performed at the end, divided by computations actually sold. Any figure that omits one of those lines is not wrong as arithmetic. It is incomplete as a basis, and the reader who asks which basis a number uses will learn more from the answer than from the number itself.

## Computing in Space Before the Data Center: A Working History

Computing entered orbit as an instrument of flight rather than as a product in its own right, and that origin explains the shape of every later system. The Apollo Guidance Computer, built by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Instrumentation Laboratory and flown from 1968, operated at 0.043 megahertz with about 4 kilobytes of random access memory, figures documented by NASA historian David Mindell in Digital Apollo in 2008. Its mechanism was austere by design. Core rope memory held fixed programs in woven wires that could not be corrupted by a power interruption, while a small erasable store held variables for navigation and rendezvous calculations. The machine did not process data for a customer. It kept a crewed vehicle alive and on course, and its limited throughput was acceptable because the task list was closed in advance and every instruction had been rehearsed on the ground.

The Voyager spacecraft, launched in 1977, extended the same logic across decades and billions of kilometers. Jet Propulsion Laboratory documentation describes three interconnected computer systems handling command, attitude articulation, and flight data formatting, each built for redundancy rather than speed. Because light travel time grew to more than 20 hours in one direction, real time control from Earth became impossible, and the mechanism that replaced it was autonomy bounded by stored sequences. Instructions were uplinked weeks ahead, executed on board, and confirmed long after execution. Computing power mattered less than predictable behavior under radiation, thermal cycling, and a power supply that declined year by year as the radioisotope generators decayed. A slower processor that never surprised its operators outperformed a faster one that might.

Space Shuttle and International Space Station computing widened the workload without changing its purpose. NASA technical histories record that the shuttle flew five general purpose computers running in parallel, four voting on the same primary software and one holding separate backup software written by a different contractor. The voting mechanism turned computing into a safety system: disagreement among machines triggered procedures, not degraded service for a paying user. On the station, laptops and control processors managed life support, attitude, communications, and experiment scheduling, while scientific instruments typically recorded data to storage and returned it through scheduled contacts. That store and forward pattern was the dominant data mechanism for orbital science. Instruments on missions such as the Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, accumulated observations between contacts and transmitted them in bursts when a relay satellite or ground station was in view, a rhythm dictated by orbital geometry rather than by demand.

Three continuities run through this history and set the baseline against which any orbital data center must be judged. First, electrical power was always allocated by mission priority, with heaters, transmitters, and propulsion competing against processors for the same watts. Second, heat left the spacecraft only by radiation or by conduction to a radiating surface, so sustained computation competed with every other heat source for rejection capacity. Third, maintenance meant remote commanding or crew intervention on a unique vehicle, never the routine swapping of failed units that defines terrestrial facilities. The European Space Agency Columbus module, attached to the station in 2008, illustrates the pattern in miniature: its data management system served experiments housed in the same pressurized volume, under crew supervision, with power and cooling drawn from station systems sized for the whole complex. Computing in space remained mission support embedded in a vehicle, sized by the vehicle, and retired with the vehicle. A service sold to outside customers, with capacity that strangers could rent by the hour, required a different economic and thermal proposition altogether, and history supplies no precedent for it.

## Partial Trials and Demonstrators: What Has Actually Flown

Announced capability and flown capability diverge sharply in orbital computing, and the demonstrators that have reached orbit mark the boundary between the two. The clearest long running example is the Spaceborne Computer program developed by Hewlett Packard Enterprise with NASA. The first Spaceborne Computer launched to the International Space Station in 2017 and operated for 615 days, according to Hewlett Packard Enterprise project reports published in 2019. Its significance lay in what it deliberately did not change. The hardware was commercial off the shelf server equipment, hardened primarily through software that monitored radiation induced errors, throttled performance when conditions demanded, and restarted components without crew assistance. The mechanism under test was fault tolerance by supervision rather than by radiation hardened fabrication.

Spaceborne Computer-2, installed on the station in 2021, extended the trial from survival toward usefulness. Hewlett Packard Enterprise and Microsoft Azure Space documentation from 2021 and 2022 describes experiments in which station generated data, including crew health related imagery and plant growth observations, were processed on board rather than downlinked in raw form. The mechanism was edge filtering close to the sensor: algorithms discarded unusable frames and extracted features in orbit, so the limited downlink carried answers instead of bulk. That distinction matters for judging later claims. The demonstrator proved that selective processing in orbit can reduce transmission load for specific station workloads. It did not operate as a general purpose cloud region, accept arbitrary external customers, set its own power and cooling budget independent of the station, or reject its own heat to space through radiators it carried for that purpose. The station supplied the structure, the power bus, the thermal loop, and the crew.

Smaller satellite demonstrators explored the same filtering idea at far lower scale. The PhiSat-1 mission, launched by the European Space Agency in 2020 with a Movidius Myriad 2 vision processing unit from Intel, used an on board neural network to identify cloud covered Earth observation images and discard them before transmission. European Space Agency mission summaries report that roughly 30 percent of acquired images were cloud obscured and therefore candidates for on board rejection, saving downlink capacity without any increase in ground infrastructure. The mechanism was narrow, fixed function inference on a single instrument stream, executed on a processor drawing a few watts. Similar CubeSat class experiments by university and commercial teams placed machine learning accelerators beside imagers for ship detection, wildfire spotting, and spectrum monitoring, each time with a defined input, a small model, and a power envelope measured in watts rather than kilowatts.

The gap between such trials and a commercial orbital computing service is structural rather than incremental. A demonstrator rack borrows power, cooling, shielding, communications, and repair capacity from a host vehicle whose costs sit in another budget. A commercial service must carry all of those functions itself, price them, insure them, and replace failed hardware without a crew. Scale compounds the difference. Moving from a few watts of inference beside one camera to sustained kilowatts of general computation multiplies radiator area, solar array area, battery mass for eclipse periods, and radiation mitigation overhead in direct proportion to the added load. None of the flown demonstrators removes that proportionality, and none was designed to test it at commercial scale. What has actually flown establishes three narrower conclusions: commercial processors can be kept alive in orbit by software supervision, on board filtering can save scarce downlink for defined sensor workloads, and small accelerators can run useful inference within a few watts. Each conclusion is real, each is bounded, and each leaves the central question of self supporting, heat rejecting, customer facing capacity exactly where the physics placed it.

## Reading an Orbital Computing Announcement Critically

An announcement for an orbital computing venture compresses engineering, finance, and licensing into a few confident figures, and a critical reader separates those layers before accepting any of them. The first separation concerns performance. Processor figures in announcements almost always quote nameplate throughput, the peak operations a chip can sustain under ideal laboratory conditions with unlimited power and cooling. Orbital capacity obeys a different quantity: the watts a spacecraft can generate and then radiate continuously, hour after hour, including eclipse periods when batteries must supply the load. A reader therefore converts every performance claim into a power claim. If a proposed facility advertises accelerator counts, the implied electrical load follows from the rated draw of those accelerators plus conversion losses, memory, networking, and control systems. If the announcement omits total sustained watts, radiator area, and array area, the missing numbers are the story, because the Stefan-Boltzmann relationship leaves no alternative route for the heat those watts become.

The second separation concerns money, and specifically which cost basis a quoted figure uses. Announcements variously report the cost of the computing payload alone, the cost of a complete spacecraft bus with power and thermal systems, the cost including launch, or a projected cost per unit of computation at an assumed utilization rate. These bases can differ by an order of magnitude for the same hardware. A careful reading asks whether launch mass is stated, whether replacement of failed units is priced, whether ground stations and operations staff appear anywhere in the accounting, and whether revenue assumes continuous full utilization that thermal and eclipse constraints may not permit. The United States Government Accountability Office, in its 2021 assessment of large constellation management, noted that projected business cases for orbital systems routinely depend on utilization and replacement assumptions that remain untested at scale, a caution that applies with full force to computing ventures whose product is utilization itself.

The third separation concerns milestones. A spectrum filing, an experimental licence, or a coordination submission records permission to proceed along a regulatory path; none of these records the existence of funded, manufactured hardware. The Federal Communications Commission licensing database distinguishes experimental authorizations from operational authority, and conflating the two inflates a paper system into an imminent service. A reader checks which milestones represent metal: contracts for radiation tolerant components with long lead times, booked launch capacity with stated mass, ground station agreements, and insurance placement. Venture announcements often sequence these items in the order most flattering to fundraising rather than in the order of technical risk, placing a partnership memorandum beside a launch date as if the two carried equal weight.

A practical test sequence, applied in order, keeps the reading honest. The reader first restates the claimed service in plain terms, naming the customer, the workload, and the delivery point for results. The reader then computes the sustained power the claimed hardware implies and asks where the corresponding radiator and array areas appear in any published configuration. Next, the reader identifies the cost basis behind every monetary figure and lists the excluded items explicitly. After that, the reader sorts each milestone into permission, partnership, procurement, or flown hardware, and weights only the last two as evidence of progress. Finally, the reader asks what happens on failure: how a dead unit is isolated, how capacity degrades, and who bears the loss. An announcement that survives these questions with specific numbers deserves serious attention. An announcement that answers them with adjectives has disclosed its own evidentiary status, whatever its processor figures promise.

## Design Choices That Decide Whether a System Works

Every orbital computing design is a negotiation among constraints that cannot be relaxed by software, procurement skill, or optimism, and the decisive choices are made where those constraints intersect. Power generation fixes the input, heat rejection fixes the sustainable output, radiation fixes the trust that can be placed in any single computation, and orbital geometry fixes when the system can be reached and how much of its product can be returned. The table below settles how each governing constraint sets its limit, what consequence follows for design, and which workload effect the designer cannot negotiate away.

| Governing constraint | What sets the limit | Consequence for design | Workload effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electrical power | Solar array area and eclipse storage mass | Arrays and batteries rival processors in mass | Capacity follows sunlight geometry |
| Heat rejection | Radiator area under the Stefan-Boltzmann law | Radiators dominate the deployed structure | Sustained load is capped by radiated watts |
| Radiation | Altitude, shielding mass and error correction | Commercial chips need software fault tolerance | Silent errors must be priced into results |
| Latency | Light travel time and contact windows | Interactive service is excluded by geometry | Batch and delay tolerant work fits |
| Downlink bandwidth | Gateway count, spectrum and cloud cover | Data is processed in orbit or not returned | Bulky raw data stays in orbit |
| Debris risk | Object density in the chosen shell | Disposal and shielding consume mass budget | Constellation size carries a shared cost |

Two rows repay closer attention because they govern all the others in practice. Consider heat rejection first. A radiator rejects energy in proportion to its area and to the fourth power of its absolute temperature, so a designer seeking more rejection has only two levers: build more area or run hotter. Running hotter degrades processor efficiency and shortens component life, while building more area adds mass, deployment mechanisms, micrometeoroid exposure, and attitude control burden. The International Space Station illustrates the resulting proportions at crewed scale: NASA documentation describes photovoltaic radiator and heat rejection systems whose deployed area rivals the solar arrays themselves, serving a total electrical load modest by terrestrial data center standards. An orbital computing platform that doubles its sustained processing load must roughly double its radiating area at the same operating temperature, and that area, not the processor count, becomes the visible structure of the spacecraft and the dominant term in its launch mass.

Electrical power interacts with heat rejection through eclipse geometry in a way that surprises designers trained on terrestrial facilities. A spacecraft in low Earth orbit spends a substantial fraction of each orbit in shadow, during which batteries supply the full load while also accepting the mass penalty of charge and discharge cycling thousands of times per year. The designer therefore sizes three systems against one another simultaneously: arrays large enough to run the load and recharge the batteries during the sunlit fraction, batteries massive enough to bridge the longest eclipse at the permitted depth of discharge, and radiators large enough to reject the combined heat when the system runs at full load in sunlight. Reducing any one element forces a reduction in sustained computation rather than a mere loss of efficiency. The workload effect follows directly. Capacity follows sunlight geometry means that honest service definitions must specify average sustained throughput across whole orbits and seasons, not peak throughput in favorable illumination, and workloads that tolerate throttling and checkpointing fit the platform while workloads priced on constant peak capacity do not.

## Environmental Accounting on Both Sides of the Atmosphere

Environmental comparison between orbital and terrestrial computing fails most often at the boundary of the account, because each side of the comparison tends to count the burdens it can measure and omit the ones it cannot. The orbital ledger begins with launch. Kerosene and liquid hydrogen launch vehicles emit carbon dioxide, water vapor, black carbon, and alumina particles at altitudes where their effects differ from surface emissions, and the altitude of injection matters as much as the mass emitted. A 2022 study by Eloise Marais and colleagues at University College London, published in Earths Future, modeled growth in space industry emissions and found that black carbon emitted in the stratosphere produces a warming effect per unit mass far larger than the same emission at the surface, because stratospheric particles persist and absorb sunlight without the washout that removes surface particles. Any honest orbital account must therefore weight launch emissions by altitude and composition rather than by propellant tonnage alone.

The orbital ledger continues after the mission ends. Spacecraft and upper stages reentering the atmosphere vaporize at high temperature, depositing metals including aluminum, copper, and lithium into the upper atmosphere. A 2023 study by Daniel Murphy and colleagues at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, measured particles in the stratosphere and found that about 10 percent of sulfuric acid particles sampled contained metals consistent with spacecraft reentry, including niobium and hafnium that have few natural sources at that altitude. The long term atmospheric consequences of sustained metal deposition remain incompletely characterized, which is itself an accounting fact: a burden whose mechanism is identified but whose magnitude is unresolved belongs in the ledger as an open liability, not as a zero. Debris generation adds a further orbital cost with no terrestrial equivalent, since a failed computing platform in a crowded shell imposes collision avoidance and risk burdens on every other operator in that shell for as long as its orbit decays.

The terrestrial ledger is better measured but equally incomplete in the opposite direction. The International Energy Agency, in its 2024 analysis of electricity and data centers, estimated that data centers consumed about 415 terawatt hours in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of global electricity demand, with demand projected to grow substantially under artificial intelligence workloads. Water use divides analysts further. Evaporative cooling consumes water at the facility, while electricity generation consumes water upstream, and studies differ on whether both belong in a single figure. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, in its 2024 United States data center energy usage report, presented energy figures with explicit treatment of these boundary choices, illustrating how the same facility yields different totals under different stated conventions.

No verdict follows from placing these ledgers side by side without a stated boundary, a stated functional unit, and a stated time horizon. A comparison per computation must decide whether launch emissions are amortized over nameplate capacity or delivered capacity, whether reentry deposition is counted at disposal or discounted as uncertain, whether terrestrial electricity is priced at average or marginal grid intensity, and whether the orbital system displaces terrestrial construction or merely supplements it. Each choice is defensible in isolation and decisive in combination. The disciplined conclusion is procedural rather than numerical: any environmental claim for orbital computing that does not publish its boundary, its treatment of stratospheric emissions and reentry metals, and its terrestrial counterfactual has not yet made a comparison at all. It has presented half a ledger and invited the reader to mistake the missing half for an advantage.

## The Practical Significance for Computing on the Ground

The orbital case repays study on the ground precisely because its constraints are unforgiving versions of constraints that terrestrial operators also face, softened by infrastructure that hides them. A ground operator who works through the orbital arithmetic gains a sharper account of a familiar facility. Every watt delivered to a terrestrial server also becomes heat that must leave the building, and the terrestrial advantage is only that air and water carry heat away more cheaply than radiation from a panel in vacuum. When you size a ground facility by its cooling rejection capacity rather than by its floor space or its server count, procurement decisions change: accelerator purchases that outrun the heat rejection plant buy idle silicon, exactly as an orbital design that outruns its radiators buys mass that cannot be used. The mechanism is identical on both sides of the atmosphere, and the orbital version merely removes the option of pretending otherwise.

For a researcher, the transferable lesson concerns workload placement under hard resource ceilings. Orbital systems reward computation that reduces data at the source, tolerates delay, checkpoints cleanly, and degrades gracefully when power or contact windows contract. If you evaluate a ground workload against those same properties, a sorting emerges that ordinary cost accounting often misses. Batch analytics with compact outputs, model training runs that checkpoint frequently, and preprocessing pipelines that discard bulk early all resemble the workloads orbit can serve, and they are also the workloads most resilient to terrestrial power interruptions, price spikes, and capacity rationing. Interactive workloads with tight latency expectations sit at the opposite pole in both environments, dependent on proximity and continuous availability that geometry grants on the ground and denies in orbit. The orbital case therefore functions as a stress test for assumptions about which computing must be instantaneous and which merely defaults to instantaneity out of habit.

For a planner, the significance lies in accounting discipline. Orbital proposals fail visibly when nameplate figures substitute for sustained figures, when cost bases shift between comparisons, and when regulatory milestones masquerade as hardware. Ground programmes fail the same way more quietly, because abundant infrastructure delays the reckoning. You can apply the orbital reading method directly: restate any capacity claim as sustained throughput under the binding constraint, name the excluded costs, and sort milestones into permission, procurement, and operating evidence. Where a decision carries safety, financial, or regulatory consequences for an organization, that evaluation belongs with qualified professionals in engineering, finance, and law rather than with promotional material from any vendor, orbital or terrestrial. A planner who keeps a [reference notebook](https://vaultbook.net) for such evaluations, recording the constraint behind each claim beside the claim itself, builds an audit trail that survives staff changes and vendor revisions. A planner who checks figures against a [reference tool](https://reportmedic.org) before a decision meeting likewise separates verified numbers from assembled impressions while the separation can still change the outcome.

The deepest ground level significance is cultural. Terrestrial computing grew up inside subsidies that rarely appear on its own ledger: rivers and aquifers for cooling, grids built by others, land priced without its alternative uses, and an atmosphere that accepts waste heat without invoice. Orbit offers no such subsidies, so every cost appears in the design mass and the launch manifest, impossible to externalize and embarrassing to omit. Studying the orbital case trains an operator, researcher, or planner to ask where the hidden subsidy sits in a ground proposal and what the proposal costs when the subsidy is priced. That habit, carried back to terrestrial decisions about siting, cooling, utilization, and replacement cycles, is worth more than any single forecast about how much computing will eventually leave the ground.

## What Orbit Changes and What It Leaves Alone

Orbit changes the setting of computation without changing its arithmetic. Above the atmosphere, sunlight arrives unfiltered and uninterrupted by weather, distance imposes delay that no engineering can repeal, and a machine that fails stays failed until a replacement completes a journey measured in months. Those conditions reward small, patient, self sufficient computing placed beside the sensors that feed it, and they punish scale pursued for its own sake. What orbit leaves alone is more consequential. Energy conservation holds without exception: every joule spent on computation becomes heat, and heat in vacuum leaves only by radiation from a surface whose area and temperature set a hard ceiling on sustained work. Economics holds as well. Mass delivered to orbit carries a price per kilogram that multiplies every design indulgence, so a system that needs more power, more shielding, and more structure to support each added processor faces rising cost per unit of delivered computing rather than the falling cost that terrestrial construction often delivers.

The constraint table set out in the design section remains the shortest honest summary of the whole subject, because each of its rows names a limit that no later engineering revision has repealed. The single point worth carrying away is a habit of measurement. Capacity in computing is the throughput a system can sustain under its binding constraint, indefinitely, with failures included, and not the peak a component can reach in a demonstration. Judged by that standard, the radiator is not an accessory to the orbital computer but its defining component, the surface on which the entire business case is written in area and mass. Ground systems obey the same standard under kinder disguises, and the reader who learns to ask for radiated watts, sustained watts, and delivered results before admiring processor counts has acquired the essential skill the orbital case teaches. The structural implication follows without ornament. Computing will expand into orbit exactly as far as heat rejection, power geometry, and launch mass permit, in the niches where proximity to data outweighs those penalties, and no further, because the ceiling is set by physics that marketing cannot amend and capital cannot repeal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is an orbital data center and how does it differ from a ground data center?
An orbital data center is a satellite or group of satellites that stores and processes data using computers carried in Earth orbit rather than servers installed in a building. The computing hardware draws electricity from solar arrays, sends results to Earth by radio or laser links, and rejects waste heat into space by radiation alone. A ground facility can draw power from a grid, circulate air or water for cooling, and receive replacement parts within hours. An orbital facility must carry or generate everything it uses, survive launch vibration, and operate for years without physical access. NASA describes satellites as self contained systems built around a power source, a communications system, and a payload, and an orbital data center simply treats general purpose computing as that payload.

### Q: Why is removing heat from computers harder in space than on the ground?
Heat removal is harder in space because a vacuum provides no air and no water to carry warmth away from hot components. On the ground, fans move air across heat sinks and chilled water loops carry heat out of a building. In orbit, conduction can move heat only inside the spacecraft itself, so every watt used by a processor must eventually leave the satellite as infrared radiation from an external surface. The Stefan Boltzmann law, published by Josef Stefan in 1879 and derived by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1884, shows that radiated power depends on surface area and on temperature raised to the fourth power. A cool surface therefore radiates weakly, which forces designers to use large panels or run electronics hotter than ground practice allows.

### Q: How do satellites power computers while passing through eclipse?
A satellite in low Earth orbit passes behind Earth roughly every 90 minutes and spends about 35 minutes of each circuit in shadow, so solar arrays alone cannot supply continuous power. Engineers pair the arrays with rechargeable batteries, usually lithium ion cells, that charge during sunlight and discharge during eclipse. The power system sizes both parts together, since a larger computer load demands a larger array to run the payload and refill the battery before the next shadow period. The European Space Agency notes that eclipse sizing drives battery mass on most Earth observation missions. Repeated deep discharge shortens battery life, so operators limit how much stored energy each eclipse may use. Higher orbits face longer but less frequent eclipses, which changes the balance between array area and battery capacity.

### Q: What does space radiation do to computer memory and stored data?
Space radiation can flip individual bits inside memory cells when a charged particle passes through a chip and deposits enough charge to change a stored value. Engineers call such an event a single event upset. The flipped bit may corrupt a number, alter an instruction, or change a pointer, and most upsets cause no permanent damage to the device itself. Heavier particles can also latch a circuit into a high current state or destroy a transistor outright. A 2005 study by Normand and colleagues at Boeing documented upset rates measured on aircraft and spacecraft electronics across several altitude regimes. Designers answer with error correcting memory that detects and repairs single bit errors, with multiple copies of critical data, and with periodic scrubbing that rewrites memory before errors accumulate beyond correction.

### Q: Why do satellites use laser links to exchange data with each other?
Laser links, called optical inter satellite links, move data between spacecraft without sending it down to a ground station and back up again. A focused beam spreads far less than a radio signal, so a laser terminal can deliver higher data rates with less power and a smaller antenna for a given link distance. The narrow beam also makes interception harder and avoids congested radio spectrum and much of its licensing burden. The European Space Agency demonstrated a 1,800 kilometre optical link between the Sentinel 1A and Alphasat spacecraft in 2014, transferring imagery at 1.8 gigabits per second. Laser terminals demand extremely precise pointing, since the beam must track a moving target across thousands of kilometres, and that pointing machinery adds mass, cost, and mechanical complexity to each satellite.

### Q: How much latency does processing data in orbit add to a request?
Latency in orbit comes mainly from the distance signals travel at the speed of light, plus time spent routing and processing. A ground station link to a satellite 550 kilometres overhead adds only a few milliseconds of travel time, but a request routed across several satellites and down at a distant station accumulates more. Geostationary satellites sit 35,786 kilometres above the equator, and a round trip to that altitude adds about 240 milliseconds before any processing begins, a figure long used in International Telecommunication Union link budgets. Low orbit systems therefore suit delay tolerant work such as batch processing, model training, and image analysis, while interactive services sensitive to delay generally remain closer to users on the ground.

### Q: What kinds of computing work are best suited to orbit?
Work best suited to orbit begins with data already collected in space, because processing sensor output on board avoids sending every raw measurement to Earth. Earth observation satellites generate far more imagery than their downlinks can carry, so filtering cloud covered images and flagging floods or fires on the satellite saves scarce bandwidth. A 2020 European Space Agency mission, PhiSat 1, used an on board artificial intelligence chip to discard cloudy images before transmission. Delay tolerant batch jobs, backup storage, and scientific analysis of space based measurements also fit orbital constraints well. Work needing frequent human intervention, rapid hardware upgrades, or very low latency fits poorly, because access is impossible and communication windows remain limited by orbital geometry and ground station availability.

### Q: How are dead satellites and failed computing nodes removed from orbit?
Removal begins with passivation, where operators vent remaining fuel and discharge batteries so a dead satellite cannot explode and create fragments. United States government orbital debris mitigation standard practices call for limiting the time a spacecraft remains in low Earth orbit after its mission ends, with 25 years long used as the benchmark and five years adopted by the Federal Communications Commission in 2022 for many licensed satellites. Atmospheric drag then lowers the orbit until the object burns up during reentry, a process that takes months at low altitudes and centuries higher up. For large objects in crowded orbits, dedicated removal spacecraft can rendezvous, capture the target, and drag it toward the atmosphere, an approach the European Space Agency contracted for its ClearSpace 1 mission.

### Q: Who owns data that is processed or stored on a satellite in orbit?
Ownership of data processed in orbit generally follows the contracts and national laws that governed the data before launch, because no separate body of space property law replaces them. Article VIII of the Outer Space Treaty of 1967 provides that the state of registry retains jurisdiction and control over a space object and its personnel, and ownership of the object itself is unaffected by its presence in space. That rule settles authority over the satellite rather than over information held inside it. Copyright, trade secret, and personal data obligations therefore travel with the data under the law of the relevant country, while the satellite operator gains only the rights granted by its service agreement. Disputes turn on registration, contract terms, and which national regulator licensed the mission.

### Q: What does the Kessler syndrome mean for computing satellite constellations?
The Kessler syndrome describes a cascade in which collisions between orbiting objects create fragments that strike other objects and generate still more debris, raising collision risk across an entire altitude band. Donald Kessler and Burton Cour Palais described the mechanism in a 1978 paper in the Journal of Geophysical Research, using models of the satellite population and its breakup rate. For computing constellations the implication is structural, since a design built on hundreds or thousands of similar satellites concentrates many valuable objects in a narrow shell of orbits. A single breakup can force repeated avoidance maneuvers, consume fuel, interrupt service, and shorten fleet life. Operators answer with shielding for small particles, automated collision avoidance, rapid disposal of failed units, and spacing between orbital planes, although none of those measures removes debris already present.

### Q: How should claims about orbital computing capacity be checked against evidence?
Capacity claims deserve checking against the physical budgets behind them rather than against headline processor counts alone. A credible claim states the electrical power available after eclipse losses, the radiator area that sets the sustainable heat load, the radiation tolerance of the chosen chips, and the downlink rate that limits how much processed data reaches Earth. Reviewers can compare those figures with published mission data from agencies such as NASA and the European Space Agency, whose technical reports list array output, battery capacity, and thermal limits for comparable spacecraft. Peak benchmark scores measured for seconds reveal little, because a satellite must sustain its workload within a fixed power and cooling envelope for years. A claim that omits mass, launch configuration, or replacement rate leaves out costs that determine whether the stated capacity can exist at all.

### Q: Why do processors flown in space usually lag behind processors used on the ground?
Processors flown in space lag ground models because qualifying a chip for orbit takes years of testing, and radiation hardened designs sacrifice speed and efficiency for survivability. A hardened processor uses larger transistors, redundant circuits, and conservative clock rates so that particle strikes cause fewer failures, and each of those choices lowers performance per watt. NASA has long relied on the RAD750, a radiation hardened processor derived from a 1990s PowerPC design, precisely because its behavior in radiation is thoroughly characterized. Ground chips advance on a commercial cycle of roughly two years, while a space mission may freeze its design a decade before launch and then fly the same hardware for another decade. Newer missions increasingly fly commercial processors with software protection, accepting more errors in exchange for far greater computing performance.

### Q: How is an orbital computing constellation maintained when individual satellites cannot be repaired?
A constellation maintains service by treating each satellite as replaceable rather than repairable, so maintenance happens at fleet level instead of on any single spacecraft. Operators launch spare satellites, shift workloads away from failing units, and let ground software route traffic around nodes with degraded batteries, stuck mechanisms, or corrupted storage. Designers support that model with modular software updates sent from the ground, redundant subsystems inside each satellite, and graceful degradation that keeps a partly damaged unit useful at reduced capacity. The Iridium constellation, completed in its second generation by 2019 according to Iridium Communications, replaced its satellites wholesale rather than servicing them in place. End of life units are commanded into disposal orbits or toward reentry, and fresh replacements restore total capacity, which makes fleet planning and launch cadence part of routine operations.

### Q: What is a radiator on a satellite and how large must it be for computing loads?
A radiator is a flat external panel that sheds internal heat to space as infrared radiation, and it is the only route by which a satellite can reject the energy its computers consume. Panel size follows directly from the heat load, because each square metre radiates a limited amount that depends on its temperature and surface coating. A panel near 300 kelvin with high emissivity radiates roughly 400 watts per square metre under ideal conditions, a relationship set by the Stefan Boltzmann constant. A computing payload drawing 10,000 watts therefore needs at least 25 square metres of effective radiating surface, before allowances for sunlight striking the panel, heat from other subsystems, and degradation. NASA thermal control handbooks treat radiator sizing as a primary constraint on payload power for crewed and robotic spacecraft alike.

### Q: How does launch cost shape the design of computers sent into orbit?
Launch cost shapes orbital computer design because every kilogram sent to orbit carries a price, and mass spent on structure, shielding, batteries, and radiators competes with mass available for processors. High cost per kilogram rewards designs that deliver more computation per watt and per kilogram, since power and cooling hardware multiply the mass behind each chip. It also discourages heavy redundancy duplicated in metal, pushing designers toward redundancy in software and across a fleet instead. Published prices compiled by the Federal Aviation Administration have long placed launch costs in the range of thousands of dollars per kilogram to low Earth orbit, even as reusable rockets lowered advertised prices. Falling launch costs relax those pressures and allow larger, less exotic hardware, but they do not remove the power and heat limits that govern a satellite after separation.

### Q: Does orbital computing save water or energy compared with ground data centers?
Orbital computing avoids the large volumes of water that many ground facilities use for evaporative cooling, since a satellite rejects heat by radiation and consumes no water at all in operation. Energy comparisons are less favorable, because a satellite must generate every watt from its own arrays, carry batteries for eclipse periods, and accept conversion losses at each stage. Manufacturing and launching the spacecraft also consume energy and materials that a ground building does not require per server. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centers consumed about 415 terawatt hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of global demand, which frames the scale any orbital alternative must match. Orbital systems therefore shift resource use away from local water and land toward launch activity, scarce hardware, and limited orbital capacity.

### Q: What does the Outer Space Treaty say about computing infrastructure in orbit?
The Outer Space Treaty of 1967, signed by the United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union, sets the legal frame within which orbital computing infrastructure operates. Article I declares space free for exploration and use by all states, while Article II bars national appropriation of outer space, so no country can claim sovereignty over an orbital position itself. Article VI makes states responsible for national activities in space, including those of private companies, and Article VII attaches liability for damage caused by launched objects. Article VIII preserves the jurisdiction of the state that registered a space object. The treaty never mentions computers or data, a silence scholars at institutions such as the International Institute of Space Law have noted for decades, so computing activity is governed by applying those general duties to licensed operators.

### Q: How does data travel between an orbital data center and users on Earth?
Data reaches Earth through radio links to ground stations, through optical downlinks where terminals and clear skies allow them, or by relay through other satellites that hold a better line of sight. A low orbit satellite passes over a given station for only a few minutes per pass, so operators either store results until contact or use networks of stations spread across several continents. Relay systems such as the European Data Relay System, operational since 2016 according to the European Space Agency, pass data through geostationary satellites and extend contact time greatly. Downlink capacity remains far smaller than the storage and processing capacity a satellite can carry, which is why orbital systems favor work whose output is much smaller than its input, such as summaries, alerts, and filtered imagery.

### Q: What happens when two computing satellites collide in orbit?
A collision at orbital speed releases enormous energy, because objects in low Earth orbit travel near 28,000 kilometres per hour and even a small satellite carries the destructive force of a large explosion. The impact typically destroys both spacecraft, ends their computing service at once, and converts much of their mass into a cloud of tracked and untracked fragments. The 2009 collision between Iridium 33 and the defunct Cosmos 2251 satellite, documented by NASA orbital debris assessments, produced more than 2,300 trackable fragments and forced operators across that altitude band to maneuver more often. Liability for damage in space rests on fault under the Liability Convention of 1972, while damage on the ground carries absolute liability for the launching state. Operators also lose the invested hardware with no prospect of salvage or physical inspection.

### Q: How long does an orbital data center satellite remain useful before replacement?
Useful life for an orbital computing satellite usually falls between five and 15 years, set less by processor failure than by battery wear, fuel exhaustion, radiation damage, and orbital decay. Batteries lose capacity with every eclipse cycle, and a low orbit satellite endures roughly 5,500 charge cycles per year, so power margins shrink steadily even when the computers still function. Solar arrays also degrade under radiation and micrometeoroid impacts, commonly losing a few percent of output each year in figures reported in NASA photovoltaic performance studies. Station keeping fuel limits how long a satellite can hold its assigned orbit and avoid neighbors. Operators therefore plan replacement launches before the fleet reaches those limits, because a satellite that cannot hold position, power its payload, or dispose of itself safely has ended its service regardless of chip condition.
