Real couples called Romeo and Juliet are the subject of this article: not couples in plays or films, but documented men and women whose own families, faiths, castes or wars divided them, and onto whose lives strangers pressed the names of Shakespeare's lovers. The pressing is the story. When a newspaper, a documentary or a book reaches for the play's names, it lends a real couple a ready-made plot, a pre-assembled sympathy and an ending the audience already knows, and the loan always has terms. The verdict argued here, and tested case by case in the survey that closes the article, is that the name honours a couple only under three conditions: when the bereaved or the couple themselves keep and ratify it, when the printed record bears the weight the name puts on it, and when the borrower declines to import the play's ending into a life that has not finished, or never followed, that plot. Where those terms hold, as they largely hold in Sarajevo, the naming is an act of witness. Where they fail, as they fail in the mountains of Appalachia, the naming is an appropriation dressed as a compliment.

The stakes are not literary. The couples in this survey were shot on a bridge, murdered on a village road, stoned in a bazaar, hunted through mountains, arrested in their bedroom, or married under police guard while protesters shouted outside the hall. Some died. Some are alive. Some had the names fastened to them by a correspondent's dispatch; some had them fastened by a book's subtitle; some declined every symbolic role offered to them and asked only to be left alone. A survey that treats these namings as a single phenomenon, graded by evidence rather than by sentiment, is the honest way to honour the dead and to leave the living their own stories. What follows takes the cases in the order of their documentary strength: first the naming that the bereaved themselves ratified, then the naming the record cannot support, then the namings applied to couples in mortal danger in India and Afghanistan, and finally the couples who survived their naming and broke the plot they had been assigned.

The method throughout is grading rather than celebration. Every naming is tested against the record that survives it: a dispatch's date, a court file, a cemetery register, a mother's objection, a father's correction. Where the record supports the name, the article says so and says why; where it does not, the article prosecutes the borrowing with the borrower's own evidence. The play is not on trial in these pages. Its text, quoted at each stage from the scenes the borrowers themselves use, is the standard against which the borrowings are measured, and it acquits itself with ease: the Chorus announces a pair of star-cross'd lovers, but the Friar, the first man inside the fiction to draft the couple into a public argument, is the tragedy's own warning about the practice, delivered four centuries before the first wire dispatch.

## Why do people call real couples Romeo and Juliet?

The play itself performs the operation first, which is why the operation feels natural. In the Prologue, before any character has spoken, a Chorus announces the whole shape: "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, lines 5 to 6). The lovers are introduced as a pair, a unit with a label attached, before they are introduced as persons. Readers who want the phrase's full history can consult [what the words star-crossed lovers really mean](/2022/03/25/star-crossed-lovers-meaning-explained/); what matters here is the grammar of the thing. The Prologue does not say that two young people will meet and die. It files them under a category, a pair of star-cross'd lovers, and the category is doing work that journalism would later find indispensable: it tells a stranger, in four words, what kind of story this is and how it will end. The same speech promises "the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, naught could remove" (Prologue, lines 8 to 9). The children's end is the price the category charges, and it is printed on the label from the start.

The adjective does quiet work of its own. Star-cross'd assigns the catastrophe to the heavens, to a configuration no human arranged and no human could have appealed. Borrowers inherit the absolution along with the adjective: if the stars crossed the lovers, then no living defendant stands in the dock, not an army that broke a corridor agreement, not a council that decreed a killing, not a police force that summoned a bridegroom until he broke. The survey's fidelity term exists largely to reverse this absolution, to return each death to the hands the record can name.

At the play's close, the Prince performs the naming ceremony that every later borrower imitates. Standing over the bodies in the Capulet tomb, he converts two corpses into a titled narrative: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (Act 5, Scene 3). The sentence is the first headline ever written over this material, and it has the headline's exact virtues and vices: it is memorable, it is portable, it assigns the woe to the lovers rather than to the city that produced it, and it can be repeated by people who know nothing else of the case. When Kurt Schork named two dead Sarajevans in a Reuters dispatch in May 1993, he was doing what Escalus does, with the same tools and under the same pressure: a public overwhelmed by a conflict it could not parse needed a story with names on it, and the names were already loaded.

### What does the Prince's closing speech do to the lovers?

It turns them into a story with a title. The Prince stops describing two dead children and announces a tale, "this of Juliet and her Romeo," whose woe hearers can carry away and retell. The conversion of persons into an emblem is the play's own final act, bequeathed to every later borrower of the names.

The second mechanism the borrowers inherit is the Friar's. Laurence marries the lovers for a public reason he states to himself: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (Act 2, Scene 3). The Friar drafts a private attachment into public service, hoping the bond will reconcile the houses, and the play is unsparing about the result: the scheme is the tragedy's engine of catastrophe. Journalism repeats the Friar's move whenever it renames a couple across a divide. The renamed pair is drafted, without consultation, into an argument about the divide itself: that love crosses it, that the divide is murderous, that reconciliation is imaginable. Sometimes the argument is honourable. It is never the couple's own. The lovers in the play did not marry to unite Verona; they married to escape Verona's jurisdiction, and they hid the marriage from both houses. The public meaning was the Friar's addition, and it is the addition that the modern naming preserves while discarding the lovers' actual intention, which was privacy.

Juliet herself supplies the template's shortest form. Meeting Romeo at her father's feast and learning his name from the Nurse, she compresses the whole machine into a single line: "My only love sprung from my only hate" (Act 1, Scene 5). Love and hate are figured as the world's only two forces, each generating the other, and the line's balance is the template's balance: two houses, two children, nothing else admitted. Public borrowings of the lovers' names preserve that balance and discard everything that complicates it, the Nurse's pragmatism, Mercutio's mockery of love's posturing, the Prince's failed policing, the apothecary's poverty. What survives the borrowing is the shape alone. And the play ends by monumentalising the shape: the reconciled fathers promise statues of pure gold over the bodies, Verona's first act of public commemoration, completing the story's conversion into civic property inside the fiction before any real city attempts it. Sarajevo's plaque, Pikeville's marker and the classroom's pairing are all, structurally, the statues in gold, scaled to their budgets.

The third inheritance is structural: the two-house template. The play's feud has no stated origin, a silence the text maintains from the Prologue to the tomb; readers can examine [the Capulet and Montague feud and why its cause is never given](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) in the series' own explainer. A feud without a cause is the most portable of all narrative machines, because any divide can be poured into it: Serb and Bosniak, Tajik and Hazara, Hindu and Muslim, Hatfield and McCoy, Jew and Arab, Black and white. The template asks only for two camps, a young pair, and a border. It does not ask what the camps are fighting about, whether their power is equal, or whether the couple's families actually oppose the match. Those are the questions the template exists to skip, and they are precisely the questions on which the ethics of each naming turn. A comparison that cannot register the difference between a siege and a property dispute, between families who welcomed a match and families who ordered a killing, is not a comparison at all. It is a stencil.

Notice, too, the label's grammar: it is always plural and always fused. Nobody is ever called a Romeo or a Juliet alone in these borrowings; the unit of meaning is the pair, hyphenated by and, and the fusion performs the borrower's real work, which is to dissolve two biographies into one emblem with one fate. A person named singly could still surprise the audience. A pair named jointly cannot, because the audience has already read their ending in the title. That is why the label falls so readily on the young and the dead, whose capacity to surprise is spent, and why living couples strain against it: Zakia and Mohammad Ali, Morel Malka and Mahmoud Mansour, keep producing singular, unfused facts, a conversion, a court case, a satisfied interview, that the emblem cannot metabolise.

Other literary couples were available, and the market's choice among them is diagnostic. Antony and Cleopatra carry empire and middle age; Tristan and Isolde carry adultery and a potion's excuse; neither pair dies young, defenceless and newly married, and neither is set in every schoolroom in the English-speaking world. The schoolroom is the distribution network: a play read at fourteen by millions is the one referent a correspondent can assume in readers from Sarajevo to Sydney, and the assumption is what makes the names worth borrowing. Ubiquity, not aptness, is the names' commercial property, and a borrowing driven by ubiquity will always prefer the recognisable outline to the accurate one. That preference, repeated across a century of cases, is the phenomenon this survey grades.

What the borrower buys with the names, then, is a complete emotional instrument: instant legibility for distant readers, sympathy pre-assembled by four centuries of rehearsal, and an ending whose approach gives every detail a borrowed significance. The place of this play in [the long history of the idea of romantic love](/2029/11/30/play-and-idea-of-romantic-love/) explains why the instrument is always within reach: the lovers have become the language's default notation for love against the world. But an instrument that supplies the ending in advance is dangerous in proportion to its power. Applied to the dead, it decides what their deaths meant before the facts have been established. Applied to the living, it writes a tomb into a story still being lived. Applied carelessly, it flatters the applier, who gets to feel the tragedy's feelings at no cost. The cases that follow are graded on how each borrower handled those three dangers.

## Admira Ismić and Boško Brkić: the lives behind the Sarajevo name

Begin with the lives, because the label's defenders and critics alike tend to begin with the bridge. Admira Ismić and Boško Brkić were Sarajevans, born in 1968, who met as teenagers at a New Year's Eve party, he fifteen and she sixteen in the usual telling, and who stayed together for nine years. The courtship was ordinary in the way the city itself was ordinary before the siege: schooldays, a graduation photograph in 1985 that the wire services would later carry around the world, and letters. When Boško performed his compulsory military service, Admira wrote to him, and one of her letters survives in quotation in the documentary record: "My dear love, Sarajevo at night is the most beautiful thing in the world." The sentence is worth pausing over, because it is the only voice in this entire survey that belongs, unmediated, to one of the named. It is not a sentence about defiance or divides. It is a sentence about a city at night, written by a young woman who expected to grow old in it.

Their families were not the Capulets and the Montagues, and the record is emphatic on the point. Boško's mother Radmila, known as Rada, had raised her sons without a strict framework of religion or nationality. Admira's parents, Zijo and Nedreta Ismić, welcomed Boško; the two households were on good terms, and the relationship was never a secret kept from either family. One grandmother doubted that a mixed marriage could last, and her doubt is recorded; against it stands the documented conduct of both sets of parents, who treated the couple as a couple. Whatever divided Admira and Boško from a shared life, it was not their families. It was the siege of their city, which began in April 1992 and which sorted Sarajevans into categories that their own biographies had never observed. Boško was a Bosnian Serb; Admira was a Bosniak. The categories that would kill them were administrative facts of the war long before they were facts of their household.

The city they grew up in made their pairing unremarkable by design. Sarajevo's mixed marriages were a commonplace of its civic life, woven through its schools, offices and apartment blocks, and the documentary record of the siege years preserves the world's surprise at a fact Sarajevans considered beneath remark: that a Serb and a Bosniak might marry was news only to audiences abroad. During the siege, mixed-marriage ceremonies at the city hall drew international camera crews, a spectacle the couples involved endured as one more indignity of being watched by people who found their ordinariness exotic. Admira and Boško's nine years unfolded inside that ordinary fabric. Their story became exportable at exactly the moment the fabric tore, and the tearing, not the couple, was the news.

### Did Admira and Boško's families oppose their relationship?

No. Boško's mother raised him without a strict national framework and welcomed Admira; asked whether the war could separate the couple, Admira answered, "Dear Rada, only bullets can." Admira's parents received Boško warmly across nine years. One grandmother doubted a mixed marriage could last. The match was approved, ordinary and unhidden.

When the war came, Boško's parents left Sarajevo for Serbia, and his mother begged him to go with them. He refused to leave Admira. That refusal is the biographical centre of the story, and it deserves to be stated without decoration: a young man who was free to walk out of a besieged city chose to stay in it because the woman he loved could not safely leave, and the two of them then endured a year of siege together, travelling between their parents' homes under shelling, planning the wedding and the life that the city's collapse kept postponing. In May 1993 they arranged an escape. Money was gathered; a passage was negotiated; the crossing of the Vrbanja bridge, from the besieged centre into Serb-held Grbavica, was to be covered by an arrangement between the two armies under which fire would be held while they crossed. Most of the elements of the play are missing from this plan. There was no secret marriage, no feuding household, no potion and no tomb. There was a negotiated corridor, two armies' assurances, and a couple walking toward a border in daylight, at five in the afternoon, carrying their lives in the open.

The bridge itself requires a sentence of geography, because the emblem flattened it into scenery. The Vrbanja bridge crosses the Miljacka river at the centre of Sarajevo, on the seam between the besieged city and the Serb-held district of Grbavica across the water; by May 1993 the span and its approaches were a front line, watched from both banks, and the short walk across it was the distance between the couple's life and the territory where Boško's parents waited. Front lines in that city ran through streets its residents had commuted across all their lives, which is the siege's specific obscenity and the context the naming, for all its faults, forced distant readers to imagine.

The year between the siege's beginning and the crossing is documented in outline: the couple moved between their two family homes as the front lines hardened, endured the shelling and the shortages with the rest of the centre, and watched the categories printed on a dissolving state's documents acquire the power to decide who might pass a checkpoint. Escape was a market, priced in hard currency and promises, and the arrangement for the Vrbanja crossing was assembled from both: money gathered, assurances passed through intermediaries, a date and an hour fixed. The plan's existence is itself evidence against the play's template. Romeo and Juliet act on impulse and die by haste and mischance. Admira and Boško planned for months, paid for safe conduct, and were killed under a negotiated truce.

On 19 May 1993, on the majority and documentary record, the crossing failed. Some retellings give 18 May, and the division is noted here because this article's standard is that a reader should know where the record divides; the established date in the reporting and in the series' own audit is 19 May. Boško Brkić was shot first and died at once. Admira Ismić, wounded, crawled to him, embraced him, and died beside him. Who fired has never been established: each side blamed the other, and no investigation has produced a name. The photographer Mark Milstein made the image of the two bodies locked together on the bridge, in a no man's land that neither army would enter under fire to clear. Their bodies lay there for days while the armies disputed responsibility; the accounts of the recovery divide between four days and eight. Everything in the preceding paragraph is the factual skeleton. Everything that the world did with that skeleton is the subject of the next section, and the two must not be confused: the couple's own story ends on the bridge, in an embrace that was not a pose but a dying woman's last act of locomotion, performed on wounded limbs, toward a man already dead. The emblem came later, and other people made it.

One discipline governs everything written about this couple, and the survey states it plainly: almost all of the personal record survives through the testimony of two families, gathered by journalists and by Zaritsky's cameras. Admira and Boško left the letters, the photograph albums and the memories their parents and siblings chose to share; they left no account of themselves as an emblem, because they never were one to themselves. The world knows the couple almost entirely through the bereaved, which is a further reason the bereaved's verdict on the name carries the weight this survey gives it. The evidence and the authority arrive from the same source, and any borrower who discounts the second while retailing the first is engaged in extraction, not reporting.

The world's visual memory of the couple is a diptych of two photographs, and the survey notes the construction because it recurs across the cases. On one side stands the graduation portrait of 1985, two teenagers in the ordinary dress of their city and decade, smiling at a school camera. On the other stands Milstein's frame from the bridge. Journalism pairs the two relentlessly, before and after, innocence and atrocity, and the pairing is a true statement about Sarajevo and a constructed one about the couple, who lived nine years, a courtship, a war and an escape plan in the unphotographed interval. The interval is where biographies happen. Emblems live at the ends of it, which is why the lives, and not the emblem, have been given the longer sections of this article.

## How did Sarajevo and the world judge the name?

The name travelled on a wire. Kurt Schork, the Reuters correspondent who had been told what Milstein's photograph showed, wrote the dispatch published on 23 May 1993 that gave the couple the play's names: the Romeo and Juliet of Sarajevo. The dispatch's own emphasis, preserved in the record, was accusatory rather than decorative; Schork wrote of two people exposed on a bridge under the eyes of both armies' soldiers, betrayed by an arrangement both sides had made. The play's names were the vehicle, but the cargo was an indictment of the watchers, the armies and by extension the distant governments that were describing the siege as an ancient quarrel beyond remedy. Within a year the naming had been institutionalised: the documentary Romeo and Juliet in Sarajevo, directed by John Zaritsky and released in May 1994, carried the names to audiences who had never read wire copy, and it did the name the honour of investigating beneath it, interviewing the families and the soldiers who had held the bridge in their sights, without ever establishing who fired. In the same month, Bob Herbert's column in the New York Times, headed "Romeo & Juliet In Bosnia," fixed the frame for American readers. The name was, by then, the siege's most exported emblem, alongside the burned library and the cellist in the ruins.

The naming proved durable enough to anchor retrospectives. When CNN returned to the story in 2012, in Nic Robertson's report on what the couple's fate might offer a still-divided Bosnia, the play's names stood as the story's settled title two decades after the dispatch that coined them, and the report's hope, that the emblem might yet instruct the peace, was precisely the hope Zijo Ismić had refused on camera in 1994. The photograph meanwhile did the slower work: reproduced in exhibitions, textbooks and anniversary coverage, Milstein's image became the siege's most portable artefact, the embrace standing in for four years of shelling that no single frame could hold. Portability is the name's gift and its distortion in one property. The world can carry the embrace in a pocket. It cannot carry the siege.

Zaritsky's documentary deserves separate credit in the audit, because it is the one major act of naming that audited itself. The film carries the play's names in its title and then spends its length doing what the names discourage: reconstructing the crossing minute by minute, interviewing the families in their grief and the soldiers in their positions, testing the truce story against the men who were supposed to honour it, and ending without the answer its audience most wanted, the name of the shooter. A borrowing that submits itself to cross-examination in public is a different instrument from a borrowing that merely repeats, and the difference is visible in the film's afterlife: it is the version of the story the families themselves continued to cooperate with, the record against which later retellings, this article's included, are checked.

The family's verdict on the name is double, and the doubleness is the most instructive document in this survey. On one side stands ratification by conduct. After the war, in 1996, on the initiative and at the wish of Admira's parents, the couple's remains were transferred to Sarajevo's Lav cemetery and buried together in a single grave, the resting place they still share. When Schork was killed on assignment in Sierra Leone in 2000, part of his ashes was interred beside them: the namer lies with the named, at the families' sufferance, which is as formal an acceptance as a journalistic act can receive. The city, in time, added its own instruments. The Sarajevo band Zabranjeno Pušenje recorded the song "Boško i Admira," carrying the story under the couple's own names rather than the play's, and in July 2023 the Centar Municipality announced, on the initiative of its head Srđan Mandić and with the Ismić family's agreement, that a memorial plaque would mark the place near the Miljacka bank where they died. A name imposed by a foreign correspondent was adopted, amended and kept by the bereaved and their city. That adoption is the strongest defence the naming will ever have, and it is a defence no critic can argue away.

Schork's own relation to the name deserves its sentence, because he is the only namer in this survey who lies beside the named. He had reported the siege through its worst years; he treated the play's names not as decoration but as the largest megaphone a wire correspondent possessed, and he pointed it at the men with rifles and at the governments declining to move them. When part of his ashes was brought to the Lav grave in 2000, the families were not honouring a headline. They were burying a witness among his evidence, and the burial is the naming's certificate, signed by the only signatories whose signatures count.

### Why did Boško Brkić's mother resist the comparison to the play?

Because the play's premise was false in her son's case. In the tragedy the houses feud and the love is forbidden; in Sarajevo the families respected each other and the love was welcomed by both households. Radmila Brkić accepted the couple as a symbol of peace while correcting the comparison's facts, as the person best placed to correct them.

Within Bosnia, the naming acquired a second career that the international coverage rarely followed. The couple became the siege's shared dead, commemorated across the lines the war had drawn: the grave at Lav receives visitors of every community, the story is retold in the city's schools and anniversary programmes as evidence of the Sarajevo that the siege tried to unmake, and the 2023 plaque proceeded with municipal formality rather than controversy. That domestic career matters to the grade, because it distinguishes a name a city uses to remember itself from a name an industry uses to sell a valley. Sarajevo's use is funerary and civic; it points at the couple and says they were ours. The feud country's use is commercial; it points at the couple and says they were a story. Both sentences borrow the same names, and the survey's three terms are what separate the borrowing that mourns from the borrowing that markets.

On the other side of the family's verdict stand two recorded dissents, and an honest survey must quote them both. The first is Radmila Brkić's objection to the analogy itself, set out in the direct-answer block above: the comparison misdescribes the marriage's social facts, whatever its emotional truth. The second is the refusal of Admira's father. Zijo Ismić, interviewed for the documentary, declined the redemptive moral that the world kept drawing from his daughter's death: in his words, the deaths were "the proof that love cannot conquer all, love cannot win over people who don't believe in love." That sentence is the anti-emblem, spoken from inside the emblem's own household. It denies that the story teaches hope; it insists that what the bridge proved was the strength of the men with rifles, not the strength of the couple. A naming that can survive its subjects' father's correction, and be kept by his family anyway, has passed a test that no editor's enthusiasm could simulate. The Sarajevo naming passed it. The name is wrong about the families and right about the divide, wrong about the plot and right about the loss, and the people entitled to discard it chose instead to keep it with its errors noted. That is what ratification means, and no other case in this survey achieves it in full.

## Johnse Hatfield and Roseanna McCoy: the couple the newspapers built

If Sarajevo is the survey's best case, the Tug Valley is its worst, and the distance between the two cases measures the label's whole moral range. Johnse Hatfield and Roseanna McCoy were real people, children of the two families whose feud along the Kentucky and West Virginia border became American folklore. They met in 1880, at an election-day gathering in Pike County, Kentucky; he was eighteen, she was twenty-one, the daughter of Randolph McCoy. A courtship followed, brief and consequential: Roseanna went to live for a time among the Hatfields, then returned to her own family pregnant. What the record supports is a short liaison between two young people from hostile camps, its abandonment under family pressure, and a child. What the newspapers built on it was the play, complete with two houses, a forbidden love and a romantic cause for a quarter-century of violence.

### Did Johnse Hatfield marry Roseanna McCoy?

No. He married her cousin. After Roseanna returned to her family pregnant, Johnse Hatfield married Nancy McCoy in May 1881, the daughter of Asa Harmon McCoy, the feud's first killed man. The marriage failed; Nancy later left him for Frank Phillips, the deputy whose raids captured Johnse. The documented sequence inverts the legend at every point.

The casting's career can be dated, which is what makes it a case study rather than a mystery. When John Spears reported the feud for the New York Sun in 1888, in the first substantial newspaper account, the Roseanna story appeared in a form measurably closer to the record. The full Romeo-and-Juliet treatment hardened in the twentieth century: Jean Thomas retold the tale with romantic shaping in Big Sandy in 1940, and Virgil Carrington Jones's 1948 history of the feud gave the affair a chapter titled "The Mountain Romeo and Juliet." The Encyclopaedia Britannica's modern summary states the mechanism without euphemism: the feud legend was embellished by the brief affair, and newspapers turned it into a Romeo-and-Juliet romance. The play's names were not a reporter's shorthand for a documented courtship. They were an editorial invention, layered onto real people by writers who needed the feud to have a heart, and the invention then fed back into the feud's own commemoration until the invention was mistaken for its cause. The years 1888 and 1889, when the feud's trials and reprisals made it a national sensation, were the legend's true forge: metropolitan readers met the valley first as a melodrama, with assigned heroes and a thwarted bride, and the casting set before the facts could harden against it. Once a national audience has been given lovers, no subsequent archive stands much chance; the archive is read, when it is read at all, as the lovers' background.

The violence the romance was asked to explain has its own documented spine, and it needs no love affair to be intelligible. In 1878 a dispute over a hog's ownership put Randolph McCoy and Floyd Hatfield in a Kentucky courtroom, which found for Hatfield. In 1882 Johnse's uncle Ellison Hatfield was stabbed and shot at an election-day gathering by three of Roseanna's brothers, who were then taken from custody by Hatfields, tied to pawpaw bushes and shot in reprisal. On New Year's night 1888, Hatfield raiders burned Randolph McCoy's house, killing his son Calvin and his daughter Alifair and beating his wife Sarah nearly to death. Between those poles lie posses, a governor's extradition quarrel between two states and the hanging of Ellison Mounts in 1890. Land records, court files and the timber market explain the sequence. The romance explains the sequence away.

Against the legend, set the lives. Roseanna McCoy's story after 1881 is not a romance but an attrition. The child, Sarah Elizabeth, called Sally, was born in 1881 and died at about eight months, of measles and pneumonia, in the care of Roseanna and her aunt Betty McCoy. The historian Leonard Roberts, annotating the feud's traditions, recorded that the baby's birth, age and death are not proved, a caution the legend has never observed; Kentucky nonetheless set a state historical marker, number 2176, dedicated in June 2005, at the grave of Sally McCoy's mother, and the marker tells the infant's story as settled fact. The two registers, the scholar's doubt and the state's bronze, coexist on the same hillside, and the distance between them is exactly the distance this article exists to measure. Roseanna herself died in 1889, still in her twenties, buried in Dils Cemetery in Pikeville; local memory says her heart broke, while some family records give illness. Johnse Hatfield married twice more after Nancy, served prison time for his part in the feud's violence, and died in 1922, a man whose legend had been running away from his biography for forty years.

Nancy McCoy's own arc is the record's quiet rebuke to the legend that displaced her. Married to Johnse in 1881, she left the marriage, and in time married Frank Phillips, the special deputy whose raids into West Virginia seized Johnse and other Hatfields for the Kentucky courts: the wife of the feud's most wanted fugitive became the wife of the man who captured him. She died in 1901, aged thirty-six. No feud history gives her a romantic chapter, although her two marriages cross the divide more completely than the affair the newspapers canonised, and her single biography contains the feud's whole machinery of loyalty, desertion and law.

Roseanna herself is the legend's most invented figure for a reason the survey should name: she left no words. No letter of hers survives, no interview, no testimony in her own voice; everything the tradition knows of her feelings was supplied by the men and the memorialists around her. Admira Ismić left a letter that corrects her emblem; Roseanna left a silence that invited hers. The contrast is the fidelity term's human form. A figure who cannot answer will be made to say whatever the borrower's plot requires, and the plot required a heroine who loved constantly, grieved picturesquely and died of the grief. The record's Roseanna, a daughter returned in disgrace, a mother bereaved within the year, a woman dead before thirty in a poor county, was less useful and more true.

The feud's scholars are unambiguous about what the romance is for. Altina Waller's Feud, published in 1988 and still the standard scholarly history, locates the violence in land, timber and the economic transformation of the valley: a hog trial, disputed timber rights, the killing of Ellison Hatfield, the execution-style murder of three McCoy brothers, the New Year's night attack on Randolph McCoy's house in 1888 that killed two more of his children and nearly killed his wife. Against that catalogue, the romance performs a specific laundering. It converts a story about property, courts and economic displacement into a story about passion, which is to say into a story that reflects on nobody's interests and implicates nobody's ancestors. Passion is a cause that cannot be sued, zoned or compensated. The descendants' own settlement of the matter came in the valley's time, not the legend's: on 14 June 2003, in Pikeville, more than sixty Hatfield and McCoy descendants signed a formal truce during the families' reunion festival, an event organised in part by Reo Hatfield and broadcast to the nation as a gesture of unity. "You don't have to fight forever," Reo Hatfield said, a plainer epitaph than the play's, and one the record earned. The casting of Johnse and Roseanna, by the survey's terms, fails on fidelity outright: the bereaved never ratified it, the subjects never sought it, and the record contradicts its central claims. It survives because it sells, in a 1949 Hollywood film called Roseanna McCoy, in the 2012 History miniseries that set the pair at the feud's emotional centre, and in the tourism of two states. The memory economy built on the feud runs on the romance as its principal asset: trails, festivals and gift-shop genealogies all presuppose the star-crossed pair, because a property dispute with a body count is a harder souvenir than a broken heart. Waller's history has been in print for decades, in the same bookshops, at a fraction of the traffic. That ratio, between the corrected record and the romance that outsells it, is the feud naming's balance sheet, and it balances only because the couple at its centre cannot invoice anyone for the use of their youth, their child or their graves. A name that persists against the evidence, for revenue, is the naming at its most Friar-like, the couple drafted into a public use they never consented to serve.

## Manoj and Babli, Rizwanur and Priyanka: the ballad frame in India

The Indian cases sit at a different grade of the survey, and honesty requires the grade to be stated before the stories. In Indian public life the Romeo-and-Juliet frame most often travels without the English names attached: it travels as a shape, the shape of the ballad, the young pair against the caste, the family and the state, with the couple's names fused into a single compound, Manoj-Babli, by which courts, commissions and newspapers still cite the precedent. Scholarly treatment adopted the ballad form explicitly. Vinay Lal, a historian at the University of California, Los Angeles, wrote of the Kolkata case within weeks of its climax under the title "The Love Ballad of Rehman and Todi," and his essay opens by placing such stories in an honour economy as old as the epics. The survey therefore grades the Indian namings at the commentary tier: the frame is documented in the scholarly and journalistic record, the play's names circulate in the surrounding discourse of forbidden love, but the strongest Indian evidence is for the ballad shape rather than for the naming itself, and a survey that claimed more would be inventing evidence of the kind it exists to prosecute.

Manoj Banwala kept an electronics repair shop in Kaithal, in Haryana. Babli was a schoolgirl from Karora village, in the same district. They belonged to the same clan grouping, the Banwala gotra of the Jat community, and under the customary law enforced by the khap panchayats, the unelected caste councils of the region, persons of the same gotra are siblings, and a marriage between them is no marriage at all. The couple married regardless, at a temple in Chandigarh, on 7 April 2007. The khap annulled the marriage by decree, ordered a social boycott of Manoj's family, and the couple sought the law's protection: a court directed the police to protect them. The protection failed, in circumstances the family has always disputed; the official account holds that the escort was withdrawn on the couple's own instruction, and the family alleges it was withdrawn against the court's orders. On 15 June 2007, travelling by bus, they were abducted near Pipli by Babli's relatives. Their bodies were recovered from a canal roughly a week later.

The legal ground had shifted a year before the couple married. In 2006 the Supreme Court of India, ruling in Lata Singh's case, had held that marriages across caste are lawful, that adults who marry by choice commit no offence, and that officials must protect such couples against exactly the threats the councils issued; Lal's essay cites the judgment as the law the Karora decree set itself against. The khap's authority was customary, extra-constitutional and, within its jurisdiction, absolute, which is why the court-ordered escort mattered so much and why its failure is the case's darkest document: the state's paper protection dissolved at the precise point where custom's enforcement began.

### What happened to the men convicted of killing Manoj and Babli?

The prosecution, driven by Manoj's mother Chanderpati, produced the first death sentences in an Indian honour-killing case. In March 2010 a Karnal court sentenced five killers to death, gave the khap leader Gangaraj a life sentence for conspiracy and the driver seven years. In March 2011 the Punjab and Haryana High Court commuted the death sentences to life.

The second Indian case inverts the first. Rizwanur Rahman, a Muslim of thirty, taught computer graphics at an institute in Kolkata; Priyanka Todi, a Hindu of twenty-three, was his student, and the daughter of Ashok Todi, the industrialist behind the Lux hosiery empire. They married in secret under the Special Marriage Act in August 2007, and on the last day of that month they moved into his family's small flat in Tilzala and wrote jointly to the police, asking for protection against her father's interference. The state's answer was pressure, not protection. The couple were summoned repeatedly to police headquarters; a case of abduction was pursued against Rahman although the marriage was registered and his wife was an adult living with him by choice; intermediaries later described payments made to separate the pair. On 21 September 2007 Rahman was found dead beside the railway tracks at Patipukur, his hands folded across his chest. The city's police commissioner pronounced suicide within hours. The Central Bureau of Investigation, called in after public protest, concluded that Rahman had taken his own life and charged members of the Todi family and others with abetting the suicide. His family has never accepted the conclusion. Priyanka Todi, after his death, returned to her father's house, and wrote to her mother-in-law that she would stay with her father and not come back. The prosecution the case generated ground through the courts for years without reaching the concluded verdict the family still seeks.

The death detonated in Kolkata's public life before it travelled anywhere. The city's writers, students and opposition politicians treated the case as an indictment of a police force at a rich family's disposal; there were marches, candlelight vigils and a sustained press campaign, and it was that pressure, rather than routine procedure, that moved the investigation from the city police to the Central Bureau of Investigation. The episode matters to the survey because it shows a public refusing the ballad's consolations in real time: Kolkata's argument was never that a romance had been thwarted, but that a citizen had been failed by the institutions paid to protect him, and the argument was conducted in the language of rights rather than of fate.

The international telling arrived quickly, and its shape is instructive. Sengupta's account in the New York Times, filed within weeks of the death, framed the marriage as a test of the new India's promises: a Muslim instructor and an industrialist's daughter, a registered marriage, a police force acting as one family's instrument. That framing indicted the state rather than any feuding house, and to that extent it served the record. But a parable's neatness costs the least parabolic facts their place, and the detail most often thinned in retellings of the Kolkata case is the bride's return to her father's house and her letter declining to come back, an act the two-house template cannot process and the ballad would rather not carry.

Set the two cases side by side and the ballad shape shows its seams. In Haryana, both families were of one community, and the lethal authority was a council enforcing a kinship rule, against which the couple had a court's written order that failed them. In Kolkata, there were not two houses at all: there was one family's wealth, deployed through police stations and intermediaries against a bridegroom of modest means, and the bride survived to return to that family's house. The play's symmetry, two households alike in dignity, describes neither. What describes both is a structure Lal named in his essay's framing: an economy of honour in which a daughter's choice is treated as a theft from her male kin, and the couple's own wishes count for nothing against the ledger. That structure is real, cross-regional and murderous, and the ballad frame honours its victims when it keeps the structure in view. It betrays them when it lets the romance glow and the structure blur, when the khap's decree and the industrialist's police access are dissolved into the generic pathos of young love. A third, briefer case marks the frame's limit from the family's side, as Radmila Brkić marked it in Sarajevo. Ankit Saxena, a Hindu wedding photographer of twenty-three, was killed in a Delhi street on 1 February 2018 by the family of his girlfriend Shehzadi, whose father, mother and uncle a Delhi court convicted of the murder years later. His father Yashpal Saxena then made a public plea that his son's killing not be converted into communal ammunition, refusing, in grief, the political frame that strangers were preparing for the case. The couple had been neighbours since childhood and had planned a court marriage within weeks, around his birthday; the attack was carried out in a public street, in daylight, and the father spoke afterward of bystanders who filmed rather than intervened. Every element of the documented case invited conversion into communal propaganda, which is what made the father's refusal an act of authorship: he claimed the meaning of his son's death before the market in meanings could take delivery of it. The plea is the Indian counterpart of Zijo Ismić's sentence: the bereaved asserting ownership of a death's meaning against everyone who would spend it.

The case's wake reached India's law reformers. The Manoj-Babli prosecution became the standing exhibit in a national argument about whether custom's interference with marriage required its own statute, and in 2012 the Law Commission of India delivered a report devoted to preventing interference with the freedom of matrimonial alliances, proposing legislation aimed at the councils and the intimidation they organised. No equivalent reckoning has been extracted from the legend industries of the Tug Valley, which is one measure of the difference between a naming culture that argues with its frames and one that merely sells them.

## Zakia and Mohammad Ali: named in a book's title, and alive

The Afghan case is the survey's hardest, because here the namer knew exactly what the naming might cost and said so in print. Zakia and Mohammad Ali grew up on adjoining potato farms outside Bamiyan, in Afghanistan's central highlands: she a Tajik and a Sunni, he a Hazara and a Shia, childhood friends separated at puberty by custom, who courted first at a distance and then, dangerously, in her family's garden. Her family refused the match and assigned her to a cousin. The couple married without permission in 2013, and in early 2014 Zakia fled the violence of her father's house to the women's shelter in Bamiyan, from which the two eloped into hiding. Her family pursued them and vowed to kill her; the case moved through courts in Bamiyan and Kabul; the couple survived in mountain caves and in a succession of small Kabul apartments, kept alive by money sent from abroad. The man who arranged much of that money, and who wrote the book, was Rod Nordland of the New York Times, whose reporting had made the couple famous in 2014 and who recorded, in the book itself, the obligation that followed: having exposed them, he felt bound to help them reach safety. The book appeared in 2016 with the couple's story under the subtitle Afghanistan's Romeo and Juliet, its epigraph drawn from the garden scene of the play, Act 2, Scene 2, the scene in which Juliet asks what a name is worth. Nordland died in 2025. The book closes with the couple alive, married and still in hiding, and no later account of equal authority establishes what became of them.

The geometry of the divide deserves the precision the template usually dissolves. Bamiyan is a province of Afghanistan's Hazara heartland, and Ali's people were its majority; Zakia's Tajik family were the local minority, and the sectarian line between Sunni and Shia in that valley ran through a landscape where the couple's courtship had been conducted in sight of both households. None of this maps onto two Verona houses. It maps onto the demography of a province, the economics of bride-price and cousin-marriage, and a legal order in which a daughter's disobedience was a family injury before it was a public question. The play's names flatten all of that into a feud, and the flattening is the subtitle's unacknowledged cost, set against the protection its fame demonstrably bought.

The courtship the book reconstructs was conducted under a surveillance the lovers understood precisely. Separated at puberty by custom, the pair communicated across the boundary drawn through their adjoining farms, by glances, by intermediaries, by telephone when telephones could be risked. When Zakia's family assigned her to a cousin and beat her for refusing, she used the one door her world left open, the women's shelter in Bamiyan, a refuge that protected women partly by confining them, and the elopement from it was planned in full knowledge of the pursuit it would trigger. The courts that heard the case, in Bamiyan and then in Kabul, treated the marriage as a problem of family honour to be negotiated back inside the family, and each proceeding returned the couple to danger. A financed attempt to flee across the northern border collapsed, and the pair went back into the mountains. It is a record of institutions performing their scripts around two people who declined every script offered to them, including, in the end, the play's.

### Are Zakia and Mohammad Ali still alive?

On the documented record, yes, at the precision the record allows: Nordland's book, published in 2016 after years of close contact, closes with the couple alive, married and in hiding, and no reporting of comparable authority establishes a later fate. The ending the borrowed names predict remains, in fact, unwritten.

Judge the naming by the survey's terms and it splits down the middle. On fidelity, it is strong: Nordland's account is granular, sourced and self-implicating, a record that names its own author's role in the danger it describes. On the borrowed-ending term, it fails by design: the subtitle assigns two living people, hunted by armed relatives, the names of literature's most famous dead lovers, and the assignment is not innocent, because everyone who repeats it knows how the play ends. The defence is practical, and Nordland's book makes it without flinching: the fame the names bought was itself a form of protection and of income, the shield that raised the money that paid for the caves and the apartments. The naming may have kept the named alive. That defence is real, and it does not cancel the debt; it refinances it. A title that trades on an expected corpse owes its subjects a conspicuous accounting of the trade, and to his credit the book attempts the accounting, which is more than the Hatfield legend ever attempted. The survey grades the Afghan naming as the label at its most defensible and its most dangerous at once: a loan taken out in the subjects' names, by a lender who stayed to service it.

The book's own construction confesses the entanglement it records. Reviewers noted its frame structure, the couple's flight interleaved with the war's history and with the reporter's deepening implication in his subjects' survival; the subtitle's borrowing is therefore not an editor's afterthought but the book's admitted premise, argued with its author inside the frame. That self-exposure is the strongest available mitigation, and the survey accepts it as such, while noting the limit no mitigation removes: the couple could not consent to the subtitle in any sense a publisher would recognise, because consent given under pursuit, to the man financing the hiding places, is consent of a degraded kind. The Afghan naming thus stands as the case where every term of the loan was strained, disclosed and serviced at once.

The same country's record supplies the control case, and it is the most quietly devastating entry in this survey. On 15 August 2010, in the bazaar of Mullah Qulli village, in the Dasht-e-Archi district of Kunduz province, a Taliban council stoned two people to death before a crowd: a young woman, given in the wire reports as Siddiqa, nineteen or twenty years old, and a man, Khayyam, twenty-five or twenty-eight; the transliterations of both names vary between reports, and the division is recorded here because it is part of the evidence. He was married. She was engaged to another man. She had refused that match; his family had refused him permission to take her as a second wife; they eloped, and they were lured back by a promise that the families had agreed to the marriage, and the promise was the trap. Amnesty International recorded their reported last words, "We love each other no matter what happens," and noted that the killing was the first confirmed stoning in Afghanistan since the fall of the Taliban in 2001. In the primary reporting of the stoning, by the wire services, the BBC, Al Jazeera, Amnesty and the New York Times, the play's names do not appear. The survey records that absence as data. A stoning filmed by its perpetrators, carried out as public policy by an armed movement, offered the label nothing it could use: no ambiguity to soften, no reconciliation to hope for, no photograph the West could grieve over prettily. Where the story could not be made to flatter its distant audience, the names stayed in the drawer. There is no cleaner proof that the naming serves the namer's needs before the named.

The perpetrators filmed the stoning, and the recording's circulation is part of the record: a killing staged as instruction, addressed to the population it governed. That audience was not invited to grieve; it was invited to obey or to look away. The play's names belong to the economy of grief, and grief was not the commodity on offer in the Mullah Qulli bazaar. Set the two Afghan cases side by side, as the survey's table does, and the label's true jurisdiction comes into focus: one couple named, financed and left alive in the record; one couple unnamed, filmed and left dead in it. The names operate on the conscience of distant readers. Where no such conscience was being addressed, the names were not spent.

## The couples who lived: Morel and Mahmoud, Richard and Mildred

The label's deepest structural assumption is the ending, and the surest test of it is a couple who refused to supply one. On 17 August 2014, in the Israeli city of Rishon Lezion, Mahmoud Mansour, a Muslim Arab of twenty-six from Jaffa, and Morel Malka, twenty-three, born Jewish and converted to Islam, held their wedding reception. They were already legally married; the reception was the celebration, with some six hundred guests expected. The anti-assimilation organisation Lehava obtained and published the couple's invitation and called a protest against the marriage. A court permitted the protest at a distance of two hundred metres; hundreds of police guarded the hall; counter-demonstrators arrived with flowers; arrests were made outside while the dancing went ahead inside. The families, as in Sarajevo, declined to play their assigned parts: the groom's parents and the bride's mother supported the couple, while the bride's father boycotted the celebration and called it a sad event. Israel's president, Reuven Rivlin, posted the couple the traditional wedding blessings and condemned the campaign against them. Mansour told a television interviewer that the protest had failed to ruin the wedding. Within days, the Jerusalem Post Magazine was framing Jewish and Arab couples under the headline "When 'Romeo and Juliet' are Reuven and Jamila," a piece occasioned by this wedding and written about another couple, which is the frame in its purest form: the names detached from any particular pair, floating free as a headline in search of subjects.

The legal backdrop sharpens the case's irony. Israel registers marriages through religious authorities, and a couple across the Jewish and Muslim line must therefore satisfy one tradition's gatekeepers, which is the path Malka's conversion had already taken before the reception was planned: in law, the divide the protesters defended had been crossed months earlier, in an office, with forms. The protest was thus never about the marriage's validity. It was about the spectacle of the crossing, the six hundred guests and the dancing, and it sought to tax the spectacle with shame. The state's answer, a measured distance and a wall of police, conceded the tax and refused its collection. Between the protesters' emblem of contamination and the headline writers' emblem of hope, the registrable facts of the case are almost defiantly dull, and their dullness is the survey's point.

The Rishon Lezion case deserves the survey's attention precisely because nothing tragic occurred. A wedding absorbed a protest, a court managed a distance, a president blessed a marriage, a husband reported satisfaction to the cameras. The borrowed plot has no chapter for any of that. The play's names, applied to a living couple at the start of a marriage, smuggle in the expectation of Act 5, and the expectation is a slander on a probable future of ordinary years. Malka had also crossed the divide in her own person, by conversion, which the two-house template cannot represent at all: the template needs its lovers frozen on opposite banks, and a bride who has already crossed is a fact it must quietly delete. The naming, in this case, was done around the couple rather than to them, by protesters who needed them as an emblem of a threat and by headline writers who needed them as an emblem of hope, two drafts of the Friar's letter written over a wedding invitation. The couple's own contribution to the record is a sentence about a party going well. It outweighs both drafts.

The affair's mechanics show the frame's modern supply chain. The couple's private invitation was obtained and circulated by the organisation campaigning against the marriage, converting a guest list into a mobilisation document; the state answered with a court order measuring the permissible distance of protest at two hundred metres and with hundreds of police; and the couple's counter-move was to proceed exactly as planned, at full size, the dancing uncurtailed. Every other actor in the sequence treated the wedding as a text about the nation. The bride and groom treated it as a wedding. Between those two readings stands the ethics of this entire survey in miniature, and the couple's reading has the prior claim, because the day was theirs.

The American case closes the section and supplies the survey's most complete refusal. Richard Loving, a white bricklayer, and Mildred Jeter, a woman of Black and Native American ancestry, grew up in the same corner of Caroline County, Virginia, married in Washington in June 1958, and were arrested in their own bedroom that July on an anonymous tip, for the felony of their marriage under Virginia's Racial Integrity Act. In January 1959 they pleaded guilty and accepted a suspended sentence on condition that they leave Virginia for twenty-five years. They raised their children in exile in the capital of the country whose constitution they would eventually be cited for vindicating, and when Mildred Loving wrote to the Attorney General, Robert Kennedy, asking for help, the letter set in motion the litigation that ended, on 12 June 1967, with the Supreme Court's unanimous ruling in Loving v. Virginia, striking down the marriage bans of the sixteen states that still kept them. Richard's own message to the court, relayed by his lawyer Bernard Cohen, was one sentence: tell the court I love my wife, and it is just unfair that I cannot live with her in Virginia. He was killed by a drunk driver in 1975, in a crash that cost Mildred an eye. She died in 2008. Neither ever remarried.

One late act complicates the refusal, and the complication strengthens it. In 2007, on the fortieth anniversary of the ruling, Mildred Loving issued a public statement supporting the freedom to marry for same-sex couples, declaring her belief that all Americans should be free to marry the person they love in the state they call home. The statement was hers: chosen, timed and worded by a woman who had declined four decades of other people's podiums. It demonstrates the distinction the survey's first term exists to protect. The Lovings never objected to meaning something. They objected to being meant, on other people's schedules and in other people's arguments, and Mildred's late statement shows what their consent looked like on the single occasion it was publicly given.

The exile years fill out what the refusal cost. The Lovings raised their children in Washington, in a city neither had chosen, while their families and their land sat an hour's drive away across a state line they were forbidden to cross together; they slipped back into Virginia for visits at risk of arrest, and Richard worked, and Mildred kept house and, in time, acted. Her letter to the Attorney General came after years of exile, prompted by the sight of the civil rights movement on television, and it asked for nothing grander than the right to go home. The couple who would remake American marriage law experienced their own case, for its first five years, as a homesickness with a police file. Emblems are weightless. Exile is not, and the survey weighs the naming by the second measure.

Against that record, set the couple's relation to symbolism, which is the point on which their survey entry turns. The Lovings declined the roles. Documentaries and appreciations record a couple who insisted on discretion and privacy throughout the years of their fame, who did not attend their own hearing at the Supreme Court, and whose stated ambition was never to represent anything: it was to live in Caroline County, in their own house, among their own people. The play's names, in their case, live mostly where emblems are manufactured, in the classroom: school study materials pair the Lovings with the play as a real-life instance of its pattern, a pedagogical coupling made by teachers rather than by the press of their day, and graded here as such. It is the gentlest naming in the survey, and even it involves drafting two determinedly private people into a curriculum. Their case proves the survey's rule by exception: the one couple whose stance toward symbolism is fully documented refused the symbolism, and the naming proceeded anyway, because the naming was never theirs to refuse. The emblem career continued after their deaths on the strength of the archive Mildred had consented to leave: the interviews she gave on her own terms became the spine of the 2011 documentary The Loving Story, and the couple's quiet domesticity, bricklaying, children, exile and homesickness, carried the 2016 feature film that took their surname for its title. Even respectful cinema drafts the unwilling, and the survey notes the fact without indicting the films, because the distinction the case teaches is not between good borrowers and bad ones. It is between borrowing a precedent, which the republic was entitled to do, and borrowing a pair of lives, which required a consent the record shows was withheld for forty years and given, once, in 2007, for a cause the giver chose herself. Mildred Loving's sentence for the condition she lived under needs no play to dignify it. She was, she said, a Virginian. The play's machinery adds nothing to that sentence except a tomb the Lovings declined to occupy.

The case's legal afterlife adds one more layer to the grading. Loving v. Virginia became the template citation for every later American argument that marriage is a civil right the state may not fence by ancestry, and the couple's surname, by accident of genealogy, became the most quoted pun in constitutional law. None of that afterlife was theirs. It belongs to the lawyers, the courts and the movements that borrowed the precedent, and the borrowing of a precedent differs from the borrowing of a name in the one way that matters here: a precedent is designed to be used by strangers, while a marriage is not. The Lovings gave the republic a rule. The emblem-making that followed was the republic's own work, done over their documented preference for quiet, and the survey's grade records the preference alongside the fame.

## What the critics say a borrowed name does

The survey's verdict rests on a disagreement among four named judges, and the disagreement is genuine. Barbie Zelizer, a media scholar at the University of Pennsylvania and a former journalist, studied the Sarajevo photograph's class of images in About to Die, published in 2010, her account of how news pictures of people at the edge of death move their publics. Such images, Zelizer argues, work in a subjunctive voice, privileging the "as if" over the "as is"; they reach their viewers through contingency, imagination and emotion, and their power lies precisely in what they leave unfinished, in the viewer's need to complete a scene whose outcome the frame withholds. On Zelizer's account, the naming of the Sarajevo pair was not a garnish on the photograph but part of its machinery: the play's names told distant viewers how to feel about two bodies on a bridge, and the feeling produced a public that the facts of the siege, reported daily and unabsorbed, had failed to produce. The naming worked. Her evidence for what such images do in the world includes the uses publics make of them, and Sarajevo's own afterlife, the documentary, the shared grave, the plaque and [the headline career of the star-crossed formula](/2030/01/04/star-crossed-lovers-in-headlines/), is the record of a naming that its city metabolised into memory rather than discarding as a foreign error.

Susan Sontag, in Regarding the Pain of Others, published in 2003, judged the same transaction from the spectator's side and found it wanting. Photographs of distant suffering, she argued, let the viewer purchase innocence with feeling: "Our sympathy proclaims our innocence as well as our impotence," and the sympathy that costs nothing changes nothing. The siege of Sarajevo lasted nearly four years after Schork's dispatch; the world's Romeo and Juliet were mourned by publics whose governments declined to lift it. The Hatfield legend, on Sontag's account, is the same economy in folk dress: audiences consumed a feud as a passion play for a century, and the consumption was the point, the valley's land and timber and dead children serving as scenery for a feeling bought cheap. Sontag's warning is not that spectators feel too much. It is that the feeling certifies the feeler, and the certification is the product, and the subjects are the packaging. The book is also a self-correction: Sontag revisited the sterner scepticism of her earlier writing on photography, granting that images of atrocity can obligate as well as anaesthetise, and the revised position is the harder one for the naming's defenders to answer, since it prices sympathy strictly by its fruits and asks what a purchaser of the feeling ever did with it.

Altina Waller's scholarship, already met in the feud's section, supplies the survey's standard of fact: whatever a naming does for its audience, it must not falsify its subjects, and the Hatfield casting falsifies its subjects in every particular that can be checked. Douglas Lanier, in Shakespeare and Modern Popular Culture, published in 2002, pushes from the opposite direction: the traffic between the play and popular use, which he calls Shakespop, is not a fall from an original purity but a negotiation, in which communities take the material and make it mean what they need. On Lanier's account, even the Hatfield legend is a community's way of metabolising a shameful history into a bearable story, and the Sarajevo naming is a city's appropriation of the world's attention for its own mourning.

Lanier's negotiation thesis earns its keep on the two appropriations that bent the name home. In Sarajevo, the borrowed names were answered by a song that discarded them: "Boško i Admira" carries the story in the couple's own names, in their own language, for their own city's memory, which is appropriation running in reverse, the named community repossessing the material. In the Tug Valley, the same process arrived a century late and wearing the descendants' own clothes, in a truce ceremony that used the reunion industry the legend built and redirected it toward an ending the legend never offered. Lanier is right that the traffic is two-way. The survey's terms exist to police the direction of travel and to bill the freight to the right party.

The four judges agree on one premise, and the agreement is worth isolating before their verdicts are distributed. All four treat the naming as an act with effects in the world, never as a compliment that evaporates on utterance: Zelizer's images mobilise publics, Sontag's sympathy certifies spectators, Waller's romance launders a material history, Lanier's appropriations renegotiate a community's memory of itself. Nobody in the serious literature defends the naming as harmless ornament, and nobody serious proposes abolishing it, because the Sarajevo record shows what the naming can carry when its terms are met. The scholarly argument is entirely about the terms, which is why the adjudication that follows can afford to be specific.

Adjudicated, the four positions resolve into the survey's three terms, and each judge wins the ground proper to the evidence. Zelizer wins on mechanism and on Sarajevo: the naming did the public work she describes, and the city's ratification is the fact her theory predicts and Sontag's cannot easily explain. Sontag wins on spectatorship and on the feud: where the bereaved do not ratify and the subjects cannot answer, the naming's benefits accrue to its consumers, and its costs, falsified biographies and a laundered history, are billed to the dead. Waller wins on fidelity as an absolute floor: no public benefit licenses a false record, and a naming that cannot survive the archive has no defence in the feelings it produces. Lanier wins the caution that the traffic is two-way, that the named and their communities sometimes seize the name and bend it home, as Sarajevo did and as the McCoy descendants' truce finally did to the feud. The composite verdict is a loan agreement, and the next section states its terms.

## The verdict: three terms for borrowing the lovers' names

The first term is consent, or its nearest available substitute. Where the couple live, their own account of their story governs, and a naming they have not sought must justify itself against their stated wishes, as the Lovings' stated wish for privacy condemns even the gentlest classroom pairing. Where the couple are dead, consent devolves to the bereaved, and the Sarajevo case shows both its power and its limit: the families kept the name, shared a grave under it and a plaque beside it, while the groom's mother corrected its premise and the bride's father denied its moral. Consent of that kind is not a signature. It is a negotiation conducted across decades, and the naming is legitimate only while it remains answerable to it.

The second term is fidelity. The record, at the precision the record supports, is the floor: dates where dates are established, divisions where sources divide, the shooter unnamed where no investigation named one, the baby's existence doubted where the historian doubted it. A borrower who improves the record, who marries Johnse to Roseanna in print or moves the bridge deaths to a neater date, has stopped borrowing the play and started borrowing against the dead.

The third term is the open ending. The play's plot is a loan with a corpse in it, and the borrower who takes the names takes, in the same parcel, an expectation that must be explicitly refused wherever the subjects live. Zakia and Mohammad Ali are owed their unwritten ending. Morel Malka and Mahmoud Mansour are owed their ordinary one. The label may point at the danger a living couple faces; the moment it begins to savour that danger as a plot, it has become the thing Sontag described, and the couple have become its material.

Held under those three terms, the naming remains what it was in Schork's hands at its best: a way of making strangers grievable to strangers, of refusing the arithmetic by which distant deaths become statistics. Released from them, it is what it was in the feud's legend: a romance printed over a robbery, admiring itself.

One further obligation falls on the borrowers who come after, the teachers, editors and essayists who repeat a naming they did not coin. Repetition is not innocence. Every reprinting of the Hatfield romance restates a falsified marriage as fact; every classroom pairing of the Lovings restates a symbolism its subjects declined; every anniversary invocation of Sarajevo's lovers borrows authority from a family's grief and owes that family the accuracy of the record and the honesty of the grade. The survey's table is built for exactly that point of repetition, the moment a name is about to be borrowed again and the terms can still be attached to the loan before it closes.

Applied at a desk, the terms reduce to three questions an editor can ask before the names go into a headline or a subtitle. Who, among the living or the bereaved, has been asked, and what did they answer? Which fact in the story, as drafted, has been improved toward the plot, and can it survive the archive unforgiven? And does the piece, read cold, leave its subjects an ending of their own, or has it quietly moved them into the tomb for the reader's convenience? A naming that passes those questions is rare enough to be newsworthy in itself. A naming that fails them is the commonest artefact in the modern press, and the survey's eight rows are offered as the beginnings of its catalogue. The survey that follows grades every case in this article on those terms, and it is offered as an instrument rather than a monument. A researcher assembling the clippings, verdicts and family statements behind a survey of this kind keeps them where they can be re-examined and audited, in a private archive such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), because the whole value of the exercise is that its evidence can be checked. The names will be borrowed again, by the next correspondent at the next bridge. The terms stand on record.

## The InsightCrunch real-couples survey

The table grades every naming treated in this article on the three terms argued in the verdict: ratification by the subjects or the bereaved, fidelity to the documented record, and refusal of the borrowed ending where the subjects lived. A naming's grade is a finding about the naming, never about the couple. The survey includes one negative entry, the Kunduz stoning, because a survey of a label's use that omits the label's documented absence would flatter the label's reach. Grades use the evidence standards of the sections behind them: dates are stated where the reporting establishes them, divisions are recorded where the reporting divides, and no couple appears whose story rests on a single unverifiable account. Each row's naming source is graded by the class of its documentation, wire and documentary record first, institutional adoption second, commentary and pedagogy last, so that a reader weighing any row can see the strength of the evidence at a glance and discount the grade accordingly. Couples whose supposed naming rests only on unsourced listicles and aggregator slideshows were excluded from the table altogether; the survey would rather record eight defensible rows than twenty decorative ones, and the exclusion rule is itself one of its findings about how carelessly the label usually circulates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who were Admira Ismić and Boško Brkić?

They were a Sarajevo couple, both born in 1968, who had been together since their schooldays and who died on 19 May 1993 while attempting to escape the besieged city across the Vrbanja bridge, under an arrangement between the armies that fire would be held. He, a Bosnian Serb, was shot first and died at once; she, a Bosniak, was wounded, crawled to him and died embracing him. The photographer Mark Milstein's image of their bodies, and Kurt Schork's Reuters dispatch of 23 May 1993, carried their story to the world under the play's names. The shooter was never established. Both were twenty-five years old, Sarajevans of the same generation, and their pairing had been welcomed by both families across nine years before the war made it legible to strangers as a symbol of the city's divisions and its hopes.

### Q: How did Admira Ismić and Boško Brkić meet?

The accounts agree that they met as teenagers at a New Year's Eve party in Sarajevo, he fifteen and she sixteen in the usual telling, and that the relationship begun there continued without interruption for nine years, through schooldays, a graduation photograph in 1985, and the letters Admira wrote while Boško performed his compulsory military service. Their meeting matters to the survey because it was unremarkable: no ball, no feud's truce-day, no instant recognition across a crowded room. Two Sarajevo teenagers met at a party, as Sarajevo teenagers did, and the ordinariness is the fact the famous framing most easily loses. The pair's graduation photograph of 1985, two students in the dress of their city and decade, later became the world's portrait of them, carried by the wire services beside the bridge image until the two frames fused into a single before-and-after emblem neither teenager had posed for.

### Q: Where are Admira and Boško buried?

They lie together in Sarajevo's Lav cemetery, in a single grave. Their first burial was at Lukavica, and in 1996, on the initiative and at the wish of Admira's parents, their remains were transferred to Lav and buried side by side, where the grave remains a place of commemoration. When the correspondent Kurt Schork, whose dispatch had given the couple the play's names, was killed on assignment in Sierra Leone in 2000, part of his ashes was interred beside them. A memorial plaque near the Miljacka bank, where they died, was announced by the Centar Municipality in July 2023, with the Ismić family's agreement. The grave and the plaque together form the material half of the ratification the survey records: the name survives in Sarajevo on ground the family chose, in a form the family accepted.

### Q: What survives in Admira Ismić's own words?

Very little, and what survives is treasured accordingly. Her letter to Boško during his military service opens with an endearment and a sentence about the city: "My dear love, Sarajevo at night is the most beautiful thing in the world." Her answer to his mother, who asked whether the war could separate the couple, is preserved in the documentary record in reported speech: "Dear Rada, only bullets can." Both fragments predate the emblem entirely. Neither mentions defiance of family, because her family was not defied; both are the words of a young woman describing her attachments plainly, in a city she loved, before strangers required her to symbolise anything. The fragments survive because the documentary gathered them from her family, which is the pattern of the whole Sarajevo record: the emblem's raw material was supplied, and its use supervised, by the bereaved.

### Q: How did Roseanna McCoy's life end?

Alone, young, and inside her own family's country rather than the legend's. After her return from the Hatfields and the death of her infant daughter, Roseanna McCoy lived out the feud years in Pike County, Kentucky, and died in 1889, still in her twenties, buried in Dils Cemetery in Pikeville. Local memory attributes her death to a broken heart; some family records give illness, and the survey carries both registers because the record carries both. She never married Johnse Hatfield, who had married her cousin Nancy in 1881 and married twice more after that marriage failed. The legend required a heroine constant unto death; the record gives a woman worn down by grief and poverty while the men of both families continued a feud about land and timber. The feud's violence ran on without her, through the posses and the trials of the following years, indifferent to the romance that posterity would claim as its cause.

### Q: Who was Sarah Elizabeth McCoy?

Sarah Elizabeth, called Sally, was the daughter born to Roseanna McCoy in 1881, after Roseanna's return from the Hatfields, and the infant at the centre of the feud's most contested footnote. The tradition holds that she lived with her mother and her great-aunt Betty McCoy and died at about eight months, of measles and pneumonia. The historian Leonard Roberts, annotating the tradition, recorded that the child's birth, age and death are not proved. Kentucky's historical marker number 2176, erected at Sally McCoy's mother's grave in 2005, tells the infant's story as settled. The survey records the marker and the doubt together, because the feud's literature has always printed the first and buried the second. The pairing of the two texts, bronze certainty on the hillside and scholarly caution in the footnote, is the feud's whole historiography in miniature.

### Q: When did the Hatfield and McCoy families formally end the feud?

On 14 June 2003, in Pikeville, Kentucky, during the families' reunion festival, when more than sixty Hatfield and McCoy descendants signed a formal truce. The festivals had begun in 2000, organised by cousins from both families, among them Reo Hatfield, and the signing was broadcast nationally as a gesture of unity in a country still arguing about its divisions after the attacks of 2001. Reo Hatfield's words at the signing were plainer than any the legend supplied: "You don't have to fight forever." The truce is the feud's genuine ending, made by the descendants themselves, a century after the newspapers had begun insisting that only a pair of dead lovers could end it. The signing came during the fourth of the reunion festivals, which had begun in 2000 as a gathering of cousins and grown into the valley's principal act of self-revision.

### Q: Why were Manoj and Babli killed?

Because a caste council declared their marriage impossible and their families enforced the declaration. Manoj Banwala and Babli, of Karora village in Haryana, belonged to the same gotra, the Banwala clan grouping, and under the customary rule the khap panchayat enforced, persons of one gotra are siblings, so their temple marriage of 7 April 2007 was annulled by decree and a boycott ordered against his family. A court ordered police protection for the couple; the protection failed, in circumstances their families dispute to this day. On 15 June 2007 Babli's relatives abducted the pair from a bus near Pipli, and their bodies were recovered from a canal about a week later.

### Q: What did Manoj's mother do after the murders?

Chanderpati Banwala refused to let the killing be absorbed as custom. She pressed the complaint, pursued the prosecution through intimidation and delay, and carried the case to the verdicts of March 2010, when a Karnal court imposed the first death sentences in an Indian honour-killing prosecution: five of the killers condemned, the khap leader Gangaraj given life for conspiracy, the driver seven years. The Punjab and Haryana High Court commuted the death sentences to life imprisonment in March 2011. Her persistence is the documented reason the case became a precedent rather than a village statistic, and it is why the fused citation Manoj-Babli, preserved in the legal record, carries her labour inside it. Later honour-killing prosecutions and judgments cite the case by the couple's fused names, a form of survival that neither the khap nor the killers intended when they set out to erase the marriage from the record.

### Q: What happened to Rizwanur Rahman?

Rizwanur Rahman, a Kolkata computer-graphics teacher who had married his former student Priyanka Todi in secret under the Special Marriage Act in August 2007, was found dead beside the railway tracks at Patipukur on 21 September 2007, three weeks after the couple had asked the police for protection against her father's interference. The police commissioner called the death a clear suicide within hours. The Central Bureau of Investigation, brought in after public protest, likewise concluded suicide, and charged members of the Todi family and others with abetting it. His family has maintained, through years of proceedings, that he was murdered. The survey records the conclusion and the dissent together.

### Q: What separates the Rizwanur case from the play's pattern?

The symmetry is missing, and the play is a symmetry machine. Verona divides its guilt between two households alike in dignity; Kolkata's case had one household of industrial wealth and police access, and one bridegroom of modest means whose family could protect him from nothing. There was no second house to feud, no mutual raiding, no shared blame, and no reconciliation over a tomb. The bride, moreover, survived and returned to her father's house, writing to her mother-in-law that she would stay with her father and not come back, an act the plot has no place for and the ballad frame quietly omits. What the case shares with the play is only the premise: a marriage treated by a parent as a catastrophe to be undone at any price. No reconciliation scene exists anywhere in its record: the play's closing tableau, in which the fathers clasp hands over the bodies and promise statues, has no counterpart in Kolkata, where no apology is documented and the proceedings simply continued.

### Q: Who are Zakia and Mohammad Ali?

They are an Afghan couple from the villages outside Bamiyan, childhood friends from adjoining potato farms, separated at puberty by custom, who married without her family's permission: she a Tajik and a Sunni, he a Hazara and a Shia. Her family assigned her to a cousin and pursued the couple after their elopement from the Bamiyan women's shelter in 2014, vowing to kill her. They survived in caves and rented rooms on money sent from abroad. The New York Times reporter Rod Nordland, whose coverage had exposed them, documented their case in his 2016 book The Lovers, and the record closes, at the grade this survey can certify, with the couple alive, married and in hiding. Their families farmed adjoining land in the villages outside Bamiyan named in Nordland's dramatis personae, Kham-e-Kalak and Surkh Dar, a few fields and one communal boundary apart.

### Q: Why did Rod Nordland call his book Afghanistan's Romeo and Juliet?

Because the subtitle was the instrument that bought the couple safety, and Nordland's book is candid about the transaction. His reporting for the New York Times in 2014 made Zakia and Mohammad Ali internationally famous under the sign of the play's lovers, and the fame raised the money, the attention and the diplomatic awkwardness that kept two hunted people fed and hidden. He recorded his own obligation in the book: having exposed them, he felt bound to help them. The epigraph he chose is from the garden scene, the passage in which Juliet asks what a name is and what it costs to surrender one, a choice that shows the borrowing was self-conscious. The survey grades the naming dangerous and defended at once, by a borrower who stayed to service the debt.

### Q: What did Siddiqa and Khayyam's case show about the label?

It showed the label's selectivity, by the label's absence. When the Taliban stoned Siddiqa and Khayyam to death in a Kunduz bazaar on 15 August 2010, before a crowd, after the pair had been lured back by a false promise that their families had agreed to the marriage, the primary reporting by the wire services, the BBC, Al Jazeera, Amnesty International and the New York Times recorded the facts without reaching for the play's names, although the pattern, a refused match, an elopement and a killing, matched the template point for point. Amnesty preserved their reported last words, "We love each other no matter what happens." A story that offered its distant audience no redemptive angle received no literary frame. The absence is the survey's control case, and it disciplines every claim made for the label elsewhere. A label's ethics become visible only where its use is a choice, and Kunduz is the entry in the record where the choice was available and declined.

### Q: What happened at Morel Malka and Mahmoud Mansour's wedding?

The wedding survived its protest, which is the point of the entry. The couple, he a Muslim Arab from Jaffa and she a convert to Islam born Jewish, were already legally married when they held their reception in Rishon Lezion on 17 August 2014, with some six hundred guests. The anti-assimilation group Lehava published their invitation and called a demonstration; a court held the protesters two hundred metres away; hundreds of police guarded the hall; counter-demonstrators brought flowers; the bride's father boycotted while her mother and the groom's parents attended; President Reuven Rivlin posted the couple his blessings. Mansour told television interviewers that the protest had failed to ruin the wedding. The borrowed plot, which requires the tomb, had no use for any of it. Lehava campaigns against interfaith relationships as a standing programme, so the protest was an application of policy to a wedding rather than a response to anything in the couple's conduct beyond marrying in public and publishing the day.

### Q: What did Susan Sontag argue about sympathy for distant suffering?

In Regarding the Pain of Others, published in 2003, Sontag argued that the sympathy aroused by images of distant suffering certifies the spectator rather than assisting the sufferer. "Our sympathy proclaims our innocence as well as our impotence," she wrote: the feeling establishes that the feeler is not an accomplice, and its work ends there. Applied to the survey, her argument prices the Romeo and Juliet naming from the audience's side. The publics that wept for Sarajevo's named lovers did not lift the siege that killed them, and the audiences that consumed the Hatfield feud as a romance for a century were purchasing a feeling about passion, not knowledge about the valley. Sontag's test asks what a naming does for its subjects, and treats every benefit to its consumers as suspect until answered. She wrote, moreover, from knowledge rather than from a desk: in the summer of 1993 she had gone to besieged Sarajevo and directed a production of Waiting for Godot there, an act of witness her later argument about the limits of witness never disowned.

### Q: What does Barbie Zelizer's study of news images explain about the Sarajevo photograph?

Barbie Zelizer, a media scholar at the University of Pennsylvania, argued in About to Die, published in 2010, that news images of people about to die operate in a subjunctive voice, privileging the "as if" over the "as is," and that they move publics through contingency, imagination and emotion, obliging viewers to complete scenes whose outcomes the frame withholds. Mark Milstein's photograph of the couple on the Vrbanja bridge is the class of image her theory describes: two bodies in an embrace, in a space no army would enter, at a moment the viewer is made to finish. The play's names, on her account, were part of the image's machinery, instructing distant publics in how to feel about deaths they would otherwise have filed under a siege's statistics. Sarajevo's later ratification of the naming, the documentary, the shared grave and the plaque, is the strongest evidence her side of the argument possesses. Her account also explains the names' function in the image's career: a picture in the subjunctive voice requires words that tell the viewer how to complete it, and the play's names were the completion the world's editors supplied.

### Q: When is it fair to call a real couple Romeo and Juliet?

When the naming passes the three terms this survey applies. First, consent or its devolved form: the couple themselves, if living, have not refused the emblem, or the bereaved, if the couple are dead, have kept and ratified the name, as Sarajevo's families did in the shared grave and the agreed plaque, corrections and all. Second, fidelity: the printed record bears the weight, with dates where dates are established, divisions where sources divide, and no improvement of the story toward the plot, the improvement that married Johnse Hatfield to Roseanna McCoy in a century of retellings. Third, the open ending: the borrower explicitly refuses the play's catastrophe wherever the subjects live, and reports a guarded wedding or an unwritten fate without savouring the danger. A naming that meets all three is witness. A naming that fails any one is borrowing against the dead or the hunted, and should be returned. The terms are cumulative rather than alternative: a naming strong on consent and weak on the record still fails, and a scrupulous naming imposed on living people who refused it fails with it.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch real-couples survey record, and how does it grade each naming?

The survey records eight namings across five countries, from Sarajevo in 1993 to Rishon Lezion in 2014, together with the label's documented absence in the Kunduz stoning of 2010. For each case it fixes the facts the naming must answer to: who applied the name, what divide it claimed to describe, what the documented record shows, and what befell the couple. It then grades the naming, not the couple, on three terms: ratification by the subjects or the bereaved, fidelity to the record, and refusal of the borrowed ending for the living. Sarajevo's naming is graded ratified, with its premise corrected by the groom's mother. The Hatfield casting is graded a press manufacture that fails fidelity. The Indian cases are graded at the commentary tier, where the ballad frame rather than the naming itself is documented. The Afghan naming is graded self-implicating, its ending refused. The Israeli case is graded a frame applied around a couple who declined its plot by living, and the Lovings are graded a symbolism the subjects themselves refused.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch real-couples survey to argue that the label honours only where the bereaved keep it and the record bears it?

Begin from the survey's two poles, because they isolate the variables. Sarajevo and the Tug Valley share the full template, two camps, a young pair, an early grave, yet the namings grade oppositely: in Sarajevo the bereaved kept the name, amended its premise in public and buried the namer's ashes beside the named, while in Appalachia the casting was imposed by newspapers, contradicted by the record on marriage, constancy and cause, and never ratified by anyone it described. The difference cannot be sentiment, since both stories are piteous; it can only be consent and evidence. Then test the middle cases against the same two measures: Nordland's subtitle, defended by its author's recorded obligation and its subjects' survival, and the Kunduz absence, which shows the label withheld exactly where consent was impossible and the record unbearable. Close on the burden of proof the survey establishes: a borrower of the names must show the bereaved keeping the name and the archive bearing it, and where either showing fails, the honour is being paid by the named to the namer, and the essay should say so in those words.
