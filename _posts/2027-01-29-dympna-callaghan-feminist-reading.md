## The Verdict First: Callaghan Puts a Date on Romantic Love

Dympna Callaghan's feminist-materialist reading of Romeo and Juliet begins from a refusal. Where the play's admirers, including many of its feminist admirers, treat the lovers' passion as a fixed star by which Verona's arrangements can be judged and found wanting, Callaghan's essay "The Ideology of Romantic Love: The Case of Romeo and Juliet" asks when the star was hung, who hung it, and what work its light performs. Her answer, argued first in The Weyward Sisters: Shakespeare and Feminist Politics in 1994 and carried into classrooms by reprinting in R. S. White's Contemporary Critical Essays in 2001, is that the love the tragedy celebrates is not nature protesting against society. It is a historically specific formation of desire, assembled in the decades around the play's first performances, serviceable to an emerging order of companionate marriage and emergent capitalism, and installed so successfully that audiences ever since have mistaken the artifact for the human constant it was built to replace.

![Dympna Callaghan and the feminist-materialist reading of the play - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-115.webp)

The verdict defended in this article is that Callaghan's reading reveals more about Romeo and Juliet than any rival feminist account of her generation, and that its central claim survives its critics. She is right that the play does ideological work: it teaches a culture to want a particular kind of love, to want it inside marriage, and to feel that wanting as the most private and least historical thing about a person. She is right, further, that feminist criticism pays a price when it exempts that wanting from its own analysis, canonizing Juliet's desire as heroine's truth rather than asking what produced the desire's shape. Where her reading reaches its limit, and the limit matters, is at the tomb: an account of love as manufactured consent has to explain why the manufactured article mourns its makers' world so persuasively, and why the drama's causal weight falls so consistently on the fathers' machinery rather than on the lovers' feeling. Callaghan's essay does not wholly fail that test, but it passes it at a discount, and the discount is the space her critics, Coppelia Kahn from one side and Paul A. Kottman from another, have honorably occupied.

Nothing in the reading requires Shakespeare to have been a propagandist, and Callaghan's materialism is precise on this point. Ideology, in her usage, is not a message an author inserts into a text the way a leaflet is slipped into a book. It is the medium a text swims in and helps to circulate: the set of arrangements that a culture experiences not as arrangements at all but as the way things feel. The play need not argue for companionate marriage to work for it. It works for it by making chosen, mutual, consuming desire appear as the one authentic relation in a world of bargains, and by arranging its catastrophe so that the bargains, and never the desire, carry the blame. A feeling can be sincere in every breast that holds it and still have a history, a function, and a price. Callaghan's subject is the history, the function, and the price.

That subject explains the essay's longevity. Readings of the play divide, roughly, into those that explain the feud and those that explain the love. The feud has yielded to history readily: it is about fathers, honour, masculine codes, and the traffic in daughters, and a shelf of criticism, Kahn's landmark "Coming of Age in Verona" at its head, has mapped that machinery. The love has resisted history, protected by the intuition that whatever else changes, this does not. Callaghan's essay is the strongest sustained attack on that protection. It takes the one element of the tragedy that criticism had agreed to treat as given, and it treats it as made.

## Who Is Dympna Callaghan, and What Does Feminist Materialism Ask?

Callaghan's career gives the essay its authority and its angle. Her first book, Woman and Gender in Renaissance Tragedy (1989), already worked the seam between literary form and the social production of gender, reading the period's tragedies as sites where femininity was manufactured for a stage that contained no women. The Weyward Sisters (1994), written with Lorraine Helms and Jyotsna Singh, announced a programme in its subtitle, Shakespeare and Feminist Politics, and in its title's theft of the witches' name for the enterprise: feminist criticism as a sisterhood of the displaced, working the margins of a canon built at the centre. The Romeo and Juliet essay was the volume's longest and most consequential single reading. Shakespeare Without Women followed in 2000, extending the argument from texts to the institution that produced them, and in 2003 Callaghan edited the play itself for the Bedford Texts and Contexts series, an editorial act this article examines below, since an edition is an argument that has learned to keep quiet. Around these fixed points stands the editorial infrastructure of a field: A Feminist Companion to Shakespeare (2000), which she edited for Blackwell, gathered the movement's dispersed work into a single syllabus-ready volume and fixed, among much else, the terms on which her own essay would be taught.

Feminist materialism, the method named in those books, needs defining at the outset because the article's whole case runs on the definition. It descends from the British cultural materialism associated with Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield and their landmark collection Political Shakespeare (1985), crossed with a feminist insistence that gender is not a theme inside social arrangements but one of the load-bearing arrangements themselves. Its premises can be stated plainly. First, texts are material products: written by identifiable workers, in identifiable institutions, for identifiable markets, and their meanings are effects of those conditions as much as they are of any intention. Second, desire is not exempt from the first premise. What a culture's members want, and how wanting feels from inside, are themselves produced, and the production leaves records: sermons, conduct books, marriage liturgies, statutes, and plays. Third, the critic's task is not to rescue a text's radicalism or convict it of complicity but to specify what work it did, for whom, at whose expense. A feminist materialist does not ask whether Juliet is a feminist. She asks what kind of subject the figure of Juliet taught its first audiences to imagine a desiring girl to be, and what that imagining cost and bought.

### What does Callaghan mean by calling a feeling an ideology?

An ideology, in the usage Callaghan inherits from the materialist tradition of Louis Althusser, is a lived relation to the world that presents itself as no relation at all: not a doctrine believed but a reality inhabited. To call romantic love an ideology is therefore not to call it false, a trick played on the gullible by the powerful.

It is to say that a particular organization of desire, historically assembled, succeeds precisely by being experienced as spontaneous, universal, and uncaused. The stronger the feeling of inevitability, the better the ideology is working. This is why paraphrase of Callaghan as claiming the lovers do not really love, or that Shakespeare peddled propaganda, misses her argument entirely. Her claim operates one level down, at the formation of the desiring subject: the play participates in producing the kind of person for whom this love, in this form, aimed at this object, feels like fate.

That formation has a datable address in her account, and the date is what separates her from every purely celebratory reading. She locates the lovers' desire within what she calls, in the essay's most quoted sentence, a certain formation of desiring subjectivity attendant upon Protestant and especially Puritan ideologies of marriage and the family, a formation required by, or at the least highly conducive to, the emergent economic order of capitalism. Every element of that sentence is load-bearing. The desire is a formation, not a datum. It attends upon ideologies of marriage and the family that are specifically Protestant, sharpened by Puritan divinity's relocation of holiness into the household. And it answers a need: an economy increasingly organized around the mobile, contracting individual requires subjects who experience their deepest attachments as personal choices, freely made and binding without appeal. Companionate marriage, marriage chosen for love and sustained by mutual affection, is that requirement wearing its kindest face, and the tragedy, on Callaghan's account, is one of the kilns in which the requirement was fired.

The claim is large, and its size is deliberate. An essay that merely observed that Elizabethan marriage was arranged would add nothing to what the play's own first act states outright. Callaghan's intervention is to reverse the polarity of the standard contrast. Criticism habitually sets the lovers' chosen love against the arranged marriage market as freedom against system. Callaghan argues that the chosen love is also system: newer, softer, and destined to win, but a system, with its own compulsions and its own casualties. The feud's Verona is the old order of marriage as alliance between houses. The orchard is the new order announcing itself in the old order's garden. The tragedy is the sound of the two orders colliding over a girl's body, and the audience's tears are the new order's recruiting instrument.

The legal architecture under the collision deserves its precision, since the play's plot is impossible without it. In the Catholic Europe the fiction inhabits, the Council of Trent's decree Tametsi (1563) had made the presence of a priest and witnesses a condition of a valid marriage, a reform aimed exactly at clandestine unions. England, having broken with Rome, never enacted the decree, and English law continued to hold that a couple's exchange of consent in the present tense made an indissoluble marriage, priest or no priest, parents or no parents, a position that stood until Lord Hardwicke's Marriage Act reformed it in 1753. The lovers' ceremony in the Friar's cell is therefore not a romantic irregularity but a valid marriage under the law of the country where the play was written and first watched, which is why the threatened Paris wedding would be bigamy and why Juliet's resistance has a sacramental spine. Callaghan's history makes the anomaly do analytical work. The new doctrine of chosen marriage grew up inside the old law's most consequential gap, and the play, which needs the gap for its plot, also advertises the doctrine that would eventually persuade Parliament to close it. Formation and statute are chapters of one story.

## The Essay's Architecture: What "The Ideology of Romantic Love" Actually Argues

Because the essay is more often cited than read, its architecture deserves reconstruction before judgment. It proceeds in three movements. The first examines the history of marriage in the century before the play, arguing that the period saw a contest between marriage as an arrangement of property and alliance, governed by parents and kin, and an emergent ideal in which the couple's mutual affection was becoming marriage's stated ground. The second movement reads the play inside that contest, showing how its structure of sympathy conscripts the audience for the emergent side: the lovers are the only figures in Verona whose wants are presented as self-evidently legitimate, and every obstacle to them, the feud, the father's will, the Friar's schemes, the Nurse's counsel, is presented as a violation of something prior to society. The third movement follows the play's afterlife, its canonization as the West's normative love story, and argues that the afterlife is not an accident of reception but the ideology completing its work across centuries: a culture that has absorbed the play's formation of desire points to the play as proof that the formation is nature.

The first movement leans, as any such argument of its decade leaned, on Lawrence Stone's The Family, Sex and Marriage in England 1500-1800 (1977), with its thesis that the period saw the rise of affective individualism and of the companionate marriage among the propertied classes. Stone gave Callaghan the historical chassis: a datable transition in what marriage was for, from lineage strategy to personal fulfilment, gathering force through the very decades in which the play was written. The essay's dependence on Stone is at once its strength and its exposed flank, since Stone's chronology was contested by historians from the moment it appeared, and the contest matters enough to this article's verdict to earn its own section below. What should be registered here is the use Callaghan makes of the chassis. She does not need Stone to be right in every particular. She needs only the documented contest: sermons and conduct books of the period arguing, in real time, about whether affection should found a marriage, whether children might refuse a parent's choice, whether a wife's desire was a danger or a duty. The contest is in the archive whatever its outcome, and the play takes a side in it.

The lived texture beneath Stone's curves is worth pausing on, because the essay's history is sometimes mistaken for abstraction. The church courts of the period handled a constant traffic in marriage litigation: suits to enforce spousals contracted in fields and alehouses, annulment pleas, defamation actions over sexual honour, the whole docket that Martin Ingram's Church Courts, Sex and Marriage in England (1987) reconstructed from the act books. The records show a culture in which marriage formation was public business, contested before officials, witnessed by neighbours, and negotiated in the idiom of property and reputation, generations of couples learning what a promise was worth by watching what the court would enforce. This is the world the play's clandestine ceremony both uses and hides from, and its documentation matters to Callaghan's argument for a simple reason: the formation of desire she describes was not a vapour of ideas. It had courtrooms, dockets, fees, and penalties, and the lovers' flight from every one of those venues into a friar's cell is the play's own map of the territory between the old dispensation and the new feeling that claimed to need no venue at all.

The second movement contains the essay's most durable insight, and it is a point about the audience rather than about the characters. The play, Callaghan observes, is engineered so that its spectators never seriously entertain the legitimacy of any claim against the lovers. Capulet's authority, Paris's suit, the Nurse's pragmatism, even the Prince's law are all given their lines, and all are structurally discredited in advance by the feud's opening brawl, which poisons every public claim the city makes. The audience's consent is manufactured by the dramaturgy before it is ratified by the poetry. When Juliet, alone at the centre of the play's most famous speech, asks the night to come and bring her Romeo, the house does not weigh her desire against her father's arrangements. The house aches for the arrangements to fail. That ache, Callaghan argues, is the ideology working in real time, in the theatre, on bodies: the experience of siding with desire against every institution is precisely the experience the emergent order requires its subjects to rehearse until siding feels like instinct.

The third movement, on the afterlife, is where the essay's title earns its abstraction. The play became, across the following centuries, the culture's central exhibit for the belief that romantic love is a transhistorical constant, the same in Verona, in London, in every suburb that has named a balcony. School curricula, Valentine commerce, film adaptations, and the sheer proverbial force of the names built a feedback loop: the formation of desire the play helped to install turns and cites the play as its evidence. Callaghan's name for this loop is ideology in its purest form, a representation that has erased its own manufacture and presents its product as its proof. The essay's polemical edge is aimed at criticism's participation in the loop. Every reading that begins by conceding the love's transcendence and then debates only the feud's meaning has already surrendered the ground the essay exists to contest.

## Companionate Marriage: The History Callaghan Sets Under the Play

The materialist method stands or falls on its history, so the history needs stating in its own right, as Callaghan assembles it, before the play is allowed back on stage. The old order she describes is documented in the play's own opening negotiations. Marriage among the propertied was an instrument of lineage: it moved land, sealed alliances, and settled feuds, and its proper decision-makers were fathers, guardians, and kin networks, not the couple. The church's doctrine contained a famous fault line. Canon law made the couple's present consent the essence of the sacrament, so that two young people exchanging vows in private were, in strict theology, married; yet custom, property, and pulpit all insisted that consent without parental knowledge was theft. The play lives in that fault line. The lovers' secret ceremony is valid, binding, and catastrophic, all three at once, and Callaghan's history explains why a 1590s audience did not need the contradiction explained to them.

Against that order the period's divines were building, in print, the ideology of the companionate household. The marriage service of the Book of Common Prayer named among the causes for which matrimony was ordained the mutual society, help, and comfort that the one ought to have of the other, a formula that placed companionship inside the sacrament's official purposes beside procreation and the avoidance of fornication. Puritan conduct writers took the formula and built a literature on it. William Gouge's Of Domestical Duties (1622), the period's most systematic treatment, preached the marriage bond as a companionship of souls under a husband's government: affection became a duty, coldness a sin, and the household a little commonwealth in which desire had an assigned, sanctified channel. William Perkins and a shelf of colleagues supplied the same doctrine in shorter compass. The point for Callaghan's argument is not that these writers liberated anyone. Gouge's companionate wife remains a subject in her husband's household. The point is that desire was being re-housed: relocated from the extramarital spaces where the older culture had tolerated or romanticized it, the adulterous gardens of courtly tradition, into the married bed, where it could be made to do reproductive, economic, and devotional work.

This relocation is the deep context of Callaghan's central claim. An economy moving toward wage labour, geographical mobility, and the contracting individual needed marriages that could be formed by the couple's choice and held together by the couple's feeling, since the older enforcement machinery of kin, land, and parish was weakening for the classes the new economy was making. Feeling had to become reliable enough to found households on. Cultures do not produce such reliability by statute. They produce it by rehearsal: by stories in which chosen love is the noblest thing in the world and every alternative is a cage. The tragedy, on this account, is a rehearsal of genius, all the more effective because its chosen lovers die: the feeling is certified as absolute precisely by its destruction, and the audience leaves the theatre pledged to a formation of desire whose historical novelty has been laundered, by grief, into eternity.

### Did anyone in the 1590s choose a spouse for love?

Some did, and the honest answer protects Callaghan's argument from the caricature that she invented a loveless past. Parish and court records preserve couples who married against family wishes, clandestine unions prosecuted and confessed, and a steady traffic in the church courts' marriage litigation.

What the records do not preserve is the ideal: the public doctrine that love ought to found a marriage and that a match without it is a kind of falsehood. That doctrine was being assembled in Callaghan's period, in pulpits and in plays, and assembly is the event her essay dates.

The class dimension of the history matters and the essay keeps it in view. Companionate doctrine addressed itself first to the godly and the propertied, the strata whose marriages carried settlements and whose households modeled order for the rest. The play's Verona is a city of great houses, and its love story is an elite artifact: the Capulet heir, sole surviving child and carrier of the line, is the most valuable marriage property in her city, which is why her desire, framed as private feeling, is simultaneously a public event of the first magnitude. Callaghan's reading never lets the reader forget that the freedom the lovers claim is claimed in the idiom of the class that was inventing freedom as a personal possession. Juliet's rebellion speaks the language of the orchard, but it is mortgaged to the language of the counting house, and the essay's account of the final statues, examined below, collects the debt.

Historians have fought over Stone's timetable for two generations, and the fight qualifies the essay's frame without dissolving its subject. Alan Macfarlane's Marriage and Love in England (1986) argued that individualistic, affection-based marriage ran centuries deeper in English society than Stone allowed, and Ralph Houlbrooke's work on the family counselled gradualism against Stone's stage theory. The full adjudication of that quarrel belongs to a later section of this article. What survives any timetable is the narrower fact Callaghan's reading actually needs: that in the decades around the play, the justification of marriage by the couple's love was a position being argued into cultural dominance by named writers in named books, against named alternatives. An ideology does not have to be new to be installed. It has to be installed, and installation leaves the kind of evidence the play's second act is full of.

## Desire Built to Order: Juliet's Longing as a Historical Formation

The essay's boldest move is also its most easily misunderstood. Callaghan does not deny Juliet's desire, minimize it, or expose it as feigned. She asks what shape it has, and shows that the shape is specific. Juliet wants, with an urgency the play presents as absolute, a very particular thing: not escape from Verona, not a lover outside the forms, not even, in the end, Romeo at the price of the forms. She wants a husband. Her desire runs, from its first articulation, toward the sacrament. Alone in the orchard, overheard, she does not propose flight or a pastoral exile from the city's categories. She proposes a contract: "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2.143-144). The most famous declaration of spontaneous passion in English drama is, in its decisive clause, an application for a wedding, specifying the institution, the messenger, and the deadline. Callaghan's reading seizes on the specification. Spontaneity that arrives so precisely formatted is not the opposite of the social order. It is the social order's newest product, desire that has learned to want what the emerging world needs it to want.

The orchard scene rewards her attention at every level. Juliet's great figure for her feeling is drawn from the economy she is supposedly escaping: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133-135). The image is routinely praised as the antithesis of the marriage market's arithmetic, and in one register it is: a gift economy miraculously exempt from depletion. In Callaghan's register the miracle is the tell. The speech imagines desire as a form of wealth that multiplies by circulation, an inexhaustible capital of feeling, and it does so in the metaphors of bounty, giving, and having that belong to the world of goods. The lovers' private language is not a refuge from Verona's economy. It is Verona's economy transfigured into poetry, which is what ideologies do when they are winning: they colonize the idiom of their own transcendence.

Her wedding-night invocation receives the same treatment, and here the feminist stakes sharpen. "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1) opens the most candid expression of female desire in the period's drama, a bride commanding the night to bring her husband to her bed. Generations of feminist criticism have rightly honoured the speech as evidence that Shakespeare could write a girl's wanting from inside. Callaghan honours it differently: she asks what the wanting is for, within the fiction and within the culture. Inside the fiction, the desire is strictly marital, sanctified by the Friar's ceremony that morning, and its object is the consummation that will make the clandestine marriage indefeasible. Inside the culture, the speech performs a relocation that Puritan divinity was simultaneously preaching: female desire, which the older moral tradition figured as the gate of hell, is represented as innocent, ardent, and holy on condition that it burn in the marriage bed. The condition is the ideology. Juliet's candour is real, and it is licensed, and the licence has terms.

### Why does Juliet's desire not count as rebellion in this reading?

Because rebellion is measured by its object, and the object of Juliet's desire is the institution her father also serves, reached by a route he did not authorize. She defies Capulet's choice of groom, his timing, and his authority to dispose of her, and the defiance is genuine and costs her everything.

But she never defies marriage itself, the sacramental form, or the household order marriage founds. She wants the new marriage, the chosen and companionate one, against the old marriage of alliance. In Callaghan's ledger that makes her not the system's enemy but its herald, and her death its founding sacrifice.

The contrast with Rosaline is instructive and the essay draws it. Romeo's first beloved has vowed chastity and remains offstage, a refusal the play treats as a dead end, the sterile alternative of desire declined. Juliet is the fertile alternative of desire routed: into vows, into a bed, into the reproductive future the feud interrupts. Between the nun and the bride the play offers its heroines exactly two positions, and both are assignments within the same economy of female sexuality that the period's divines were administering. A feminist criticism that celebrates Juliet's choice without noticing the menu's brevity has, for Callaghan, mistaken the newest assignment for freedom. The menu is the ideology's quietest victory in the play, the place where its work is least visible because it is done before the choosing starts.

Romeo's desire submits to the same analysis, and the essay's treatment of him corrects a limitation of purely gynocentric readings. His longing arrives pre-formatted by Petrarch: the oxymorons of the first act, the worship of an absent mistress, the whole borrowed liturgy that the play itself mocks through Mercutio. The sonnet the lovers share at the feast (1.5.93-106), in which his pilgrim's script is taken up, corrected, and completed by her answering lines, is usually read as the moment borrowed language becomes mutual truth. Callaghan reads the same moment as the formation completing its circuit: two subjects discovering that their most intimate improvisation was a set form all along, fourteen lines of inherited architecture that they experience as the freest speech of their lives. Form experienced as freedom is the ideology's signature effect, and the play writes the signature in the most beautiful ink it has.

## The Marriage the Lovers Want Is the Institution's Own

Follow the ceremony and the reading deepens. Friar Laurence agrees to marry the lovers for a reason he states as policy: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91-92). The sacrament is, in his calculation, an instrument of civic engineering, a marriage deployed against a feud. The lovers come to him for love's sake; he marries them for the city's. Neither party is cynical, and that is Callaghan's point about how ideology actually operates: not as conspiracy but as the happy convergence of private feeling and public function, each supplying the other's alibi. The new marriage ideology needed couples who married for love and thereby stabilized the order their love seemed to defy. The Friar, the play's one agent who sees both registers at once, blesses the convergence in terms that give the game away to anyone reading with Callaghan's suspicion: the alliance he hopes for is the old marriage politics of houses, pursued by the new marriage politics of hearts.

His warning at the ceremony itself, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9), is usually read as the voice of moderation against youthful excess. In the materialist register it is something more precise: the institution's antibodies announcing themselves. The church that is about to absorb this desire into marriage simultaneously instructs the desiring couple that their feeling, unmoderated, is a fire that "in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6.10-11). Desire is welcomed on the condition that it learn measure, duration, and form: the whole curriculum of the companionate household compressed into a wedding-day homily. The lovers do not learn it, and the play kills them, and the audience's judgment, carefully prepared, acquits the curriculum and convicts the circumstances. Callaghan's reading asks the forbidden question: acquitted by whom, and in whose interest?

The ending is her closing exhibit, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence the play offers her. The lovers die; the fathers reconcile over the corpses; and the reconciliation is immediately denominated in the currency of the marriage market. Montague promises a statue of Juliet "in pure gold," and Capulet matches him, "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie" (5.3.298-304). The children who could not be given to each other in marriage are exchanged at last as monuments, each house honouring the other's dead at a stated price, the feud's economy of reciprocal obligation completing its final transaction over the bodies of the couple who tried to exit it. A fuller study of that market's bookkeeping stands in the companion account of [Patriarchy and the Marriage Market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/), whose ledger of tenders, settlements, and statues traces the same arithmetic from the other side. For Callaghan the statues do double work. Within the fiction they are grief priced as gold. Within the culture they are the ideology monumentalizing its martyrs: the love that died for the new marriage is cast in metal and set up in the city square, where it can recruit forever and threaten nothing, being dead.

Set beside this reading the play's famous alternative marriages and the pattern locks. Paris, the County, represents the old order at its most presentable: wealthy, well-born, courteous, and willing in the first act to wait two years for a bride not yet fourteen. Capulet's first answer to his suit is the play's most quoted piece of parental moderation: "My child is yet a stranger in the world" (1.2.8), and "My will to her consent is but a part" (1.2.17). Critics often treat the second Capulet, the tyrant of 3.5 who tells his daughter she shall "Graze where you will" but "shall not house with me" if she refuses (3.5.189-190), as a contradiction or a collapse. Callaghan's history dissolves the puzzle. The consent Capulet offers in 1.2 is the emergent doctrine already installed in the old father's mouth, the companionate concession that a daughter's heart must be got, and it coexists with the alliance system because it was designed to: consent modernizes the transaction without disturbing the property. When the transaction is threatened after Tybalt's death, the concession is withdrawn in an afternoon, revealing the terms on which it had been granted. The ideology, in the father's own practice, is a courtesy extended to feeling exactly as far as feeling ratifies the settlement.

## A Heroine Without Women: Shakespeare Without Women and the Boy Juliet

Six years after the essay, Callaghan published the book that supplies its missing foundation. Shakespeare Without Women: Representing Gender and Race on the Renaissance Stage (2000) argues a thesis its title states without flinching: Shakespeare's theatre contained no women, and the female figures it presented were not, in any sense a modern audience assumes, representations of women by women or even, straightforwardly, of women at all. Every Juliet of the 1590s was a boy apprentice in a playing company, performing a script written by a man, in a commercial institution owned by men, for a culture that barred women from the stage by custom. Callaghan's chapters pursue the implications without sentimentality: the exclusion of the female body in Twelfth Night, the castrato-haunted question of the impersonated female voice, the racial impersonations of Othello, and a closing inquiry into audiences that refuses, pointedly, to invent the responses of the women who did attend, since the record of what they thought does not exist and fabricating it repeats the period's own habit of speaking for them.

For the Romeo and Juliet argument the book changes the status of the essay's central figure. If Juliet's desire is a formation, it is a formation twice removed from any woman's experience of desiring: authored by a male playwright and embodied by a male performer, in a medium whose celebrated heroines are, materially, male constructions wearing femininity as a theatrical property. The liberal-feminist celebration of Juliet as a voice of female subjectivity must therefore answer a question it prefers to skip: whose subjectivity, materially, is being celebrated? Callaghan's answer is that the subjectivity on offer is a cultural production in the strictest sense, a model of girlhood manufactured by an all-male institution and consumed by a mixed audience as knowledge about girls. That the model is sympathetic, inward, and eloquent does not exempt it from the analysis. It completes the analysis, since the ideology of romantic love required exactly such a heroine: desiring enough to found the new marriage on feeling, scripted enough never to desire outside the form that feeling was assigned.

### What difference does a boy actor make to Juliet's subjectivity?

It relocates the subjectivity from expression to manufacture. A heroine played by a woman might be claimed, however contestably, as a collaboration between text and female experience. A heroine played by a boy in a company that excluded women is a representation produced entirely inside the institution that also produced the period's marriage doctrine.

For Callaghan the two productions are one: the stage that could imagine Juliet's desire in such inward detail, and the pulpit that was teaching wives to find their fulfilment in chosen marriage, were departments of a single culture equipping its members with the feelings the new order ran on.

The book also disciplines the essay's method in a way that strengthens both. The younger essay could be charged, and was charged, with treating the play as a transparent instrument of social forces, its poetry a mere delivery system. The book's attention to the material theatre, to voices, bodies, costumes, and the economics of the playing companies, shows the same critic insisting that representations are made of stuff and labour, and that the making constrains what can be meant. Juliet's subjectivity, on this fuller account, is not a vapor rising from the marriage market. It is a crafted object, built by a playwright of genius and a boy of talent out of the period's available materials, Petrarchan lyric, conduct-book psychology, sacramental theology, and it is precisely because the object is crafted rather than natural that its ideology can be specified. Genius is not an alibi in this method. It is a description of the workshop's skill.

There is a further consequence for feminist criticism that Callaghan draws with characteristic refusal of comfort. If the stage's women are male constructions, then the tradition of women readers and performers reclaiming Juliet, from Anna Jameson's Victorian portrait to the present stage, is a tradition of women working on a figure men made, and its emancipatory claims need restating in those terms. The earlier tradition of women reading the heroines, Jameson's landmark among them, receives its due elsewhere in this series; Callaghan's relation to it is one of inheritance under audit. She takes from that tradition its founding insight, that the heroines are where the culture thinks hardest about women, and declines its founding consolation, that the thinking was somehow women's own. The consolation, she argues, is one more product the culture sold its daughters.

## The Kahn Confrontation: Idealized Love, Ideologized Love

The debate that defines Callaghan's place in the field is her confrontation with Coppelia Kahn, and it must be staged at full strength on both sides, because each critic lands a blow the other cannot parry. Kahn's "Coming of Age in Verona," published in Modern Language Studies in 1977 and canonized by its reprinting in The Woman's Part in 1980, reads the tragedy as the working of Verona's patriarchy upon its children: a masculinity manufactured by the feud's codes, daughters disposed of as property, and the lovers destroyed as the system's scapegoats, their love the one relation in the play not organized by domination. The series' study of [Coppelia Kahn: Coming of Age in Verona](/2026/12/18/coppelia-kahn-coming-of-age-verona/) gives that essay its own full hearing. What concerns this article is the charge and counter-charge between the two landmarks, since between them they map the fork at which feminist readings of the play still divide.

Callaghan's charge against the Kahn tradition, stated at the strength she states it, is that it idealizes the lovers' love. On her account, Kahn's essay, for all its demystification of the fathers, leaves the children's desire in a state of nature: repressed by patriarchy, distorted by it, killed by it, but never produced by it, a pre-social good that the social order merely mishandles. Jonathan Goldberg pressed the same objection in the form Paul A. Kottman later summarized for the profession: Kahn, like Janet Adelman, idealizes the love, treating as natural and oppositional a desire that the play has itself ideologized, formatted by Petrarch, by sacrament, and by the emerging doctrine of companionate marriage. The charge is not that Kahn is soft on Verona. It is that her analysis stops at the garden gate: everything inside the wall is feeling, everything outside is system, and the wall is exactly where ideology does its best work, since a desire experienced as the self's innermost truth is the one artifact its owners will never inspect.

Janet Adelman stands as the confrontation's third party, and her presence clarifies what the idealization charge was really about. Adelman's reading of the play, in Suffocating Mothers (1992), treats the lovers' bond as a fantasy of merging in which the threatening maternal body is at last escaped, the couple sealing themselves against a world of fathers and nurses into a union she presents as the play's psychic achievement. Goldberg's objection, as Kottman reported it, named Kahn and Adelman in one breath, and the pairing is just: in both accounts the love functions as the psyche's or the children's truth against a corrupt social surface, and in both the surface is analyzed with a rigour the truth is spared. Callaghan's essay is the refusal of that asymmetry stated as a method. Whatever in the play is shown to be made, the love must be shown to be made of, and the materials, sacrament, sonnet, conduct book, and market, are not less material for being beautiful.

Kahn's side of the confrontation answers with the dramatic facts, and they are formidable. Whatever the love's ideological pedigree, the play's killing is done, scene by scene, by the fathers' machinery and by nothing else: a brawl the old men sustain, a duel their code compels, a forced marriage their authority imposes, a tomb their city fills. The lovers' feeling, by contrast, harms no one in the fiction except by existing where it is forbidden; it builds the play's only honest speech, its only reciprocal relation, its only promises that are kept. A reading that prices the love as the system's product must explain why the system in the play behaves as the love's executioner rather than its sponsor, and why the text's own distribution of sympathy, blame, and poetic wealth so consistently acquits the desire and indicts the arrangements. If the ideology manufactured the love to serve the order, the play is a strange advertisement, since the order it supposedly serves comes off as a charnel house and the product dies unconsummated as a social fact: no household founded, no children, no alliance, nothing but statues. Ideology that fails this completely at the level of plot has some explaining to do at the level of theory.

### Where does Kahn's Verona end and Callaghan's begin?

At the garden wall of the orchard scene. Kahn's analysis governs everything outside it: the fathers, the feud, the market, the codes that manufacture sons and price daughters. Callaghan's begins at the moment criticism steps inside and accepts the lovers' own account of what they feel. For Kahn the orchard is the one place Verona's writ does not run.

For Callaghan it is where the writ runs deepest, in the couple's own handwriting: the proposal of marriage, the economics of bounty, the desire aimed at a wedding. The two Veronas are the same city surveyed from opposite sides of that wall.

The adjudication this article defends grants each critic her strongest ground and prices the remainder. Callaghan wins the theory of the desire: the orchard evidence is hers, and it is decisive against any reading that treats the love as an unformatted natural force. Juliet does propose marriage; the sonnet is a set form; the bounty speech thinks in wealth; the wedding night is desired as a sacrament's completion. A criticism that cannot see the formatting has not finished its homework. Kahn wins the theory of the catastrophe: the play's causal chain runs through the feud and the fathers at every link, and a functional account of the ideology must treat the love's destruction not as the system's plan but as its accident, the point where two of the order's own formations, alliance marriage and companionate desire, collide inside one household. Properly stated, then, the two readings are not rivals for the same territory but accounts of different strata that a complete feminist reading must hold together: Kahn explains what the play's Verona does to its children, Callaghan explains what the play's culture was teaching its audiences to want. The tragedy lives in the interference between the strata. The same culture that priced daughters like settlements was learning to want marriages founded on love, and it learned the wanting partly from this play, whose lovers are destroyed by the pricing. Ideology here is not a puppet-master. It is a historical weather system, and the play is both its product and its most beautiful instrument for changing the climate.

That verdict assigns Callaghan the deeper originality. Kahn's Verona was, by 1994, the field's common property; the feud as patriarchy's machine was a truth the profession had absorbed. Callaghan's contribution was to rotate the instrument ninety degrees, from the play's represented world to its representing work, and to show that the rotation convicts the audience along with the fathers. It is the more uncomfortable reading, and discomfort, in a materialist, is a diagnostic sign: the readings that flatter their readers' self-image are the ones ideology has already processed.

## Kottman's Objection: Love as the Struggle for Freedom

The most philosophically serious contest of Callaghan's reading arrived in 2012, when Paul A. Kottman published "Defying the Stars: Tragic Love as the Struggle for Freedom in Romeo and Juliet," an argument he later folded into Love As Human Freedom (2017). Kottman reads the tragedy through Hegel: the lovers' bond is a struggle for mutual recognition, each seeking to find a self in the other that no inherited form, family, city, or church, can confer or accommodate. On this account the love is not a social product at all in the reductive sense. It is the claim of subjective freedom against every objective order, and its destruction is the measure of the orders' blindness, not of the claim's falsity. Kottman names the opposing camp precisely: readings for which the idealization of love in the play is an ideological construct, patriarchal, heterosexist, homosocial, and hence social through and through. Callaghan's essay is that camp's founding document for this play, and Kottman treats it with the respect of direct engagement, conceding the historicity of love's forms while denying that historicity exhausts the love's meaning.

The objection has real force and this article gives it its weight. If desire's every form is assigned by its culture, then the category of freedom does no work anywhere, and the tragedy's peculiar ache, the sense that something with an unconditional claim was refused by conditional arrangements, becomes an effect the theory must explain away rather than explain. Kottman's Romeo, hearing of Juliet's supposed death and crying "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), is staking exactly the unconditional claim: a self that will not be administered, even by fate's paperwork. A materialism that hears only ideology in that cry has confused the conditions of a feeling's formation with the feeling's verdict on its conditions. Formations can turn on their formers. The history of every emancipation is the history of assigned feelings exceeding their assignment.

Yet the adjudication cannot rest with Kottman either, for his reading purchases the love's freedom at the price Callaghan taught the field to notice: it reinstates, in philosophical dress, the very exemption of desire from history that the essay exists to contest. Hegel's lovers struggle for a recognition whose grammar Kottman finds in the play's reciprocities, the shared sonnet, the exchanged vows, the mutual education of the aubade. But the grammar has a bibliography. The reciprocity is conducted in Petrarchan forms, ratified by a sacrament, aimed at a household; its every instrument is period equipment. To call the struggle timeless is to do again what the ideology requires, mistaking the best-documented formation of desire in English literature for desire as such. The verdict this article reaches is that the two accounts describe the same phenomenon at different depths, and that depth order matters. Callaghan's is the prior question: before asking what the lovers' freedom means, ask what made this shape of longing available, creditable, and fatal. Kottman's is the necessary second question: granted the shape was made, what does the play do with it, and the honest answer is that it makes of it a claim that judges its makers. A reading that keeps both questions in that order has paid Callaghan's toll and kept Kottman's insight, and the toll, not the insight, is what the profession was refusing to pay before 1994.

The toll is visible even inside Kottman's own evidence, which is why the exchange repays close attention. His book's most original exhibits are the lovers' repeated farewells: the partings at dawn, the negotiated leavetakings, the aubade in which Juliet first wills the nightingale and then, reversing, hurries her husband toward exile and life. For Kottman the farewells prove the love's freedom, since the bond is sustained by mutual independence rather than possession. Observe, though, the form the freedom takes. The dawn parting is the alba, among the oldest set forms in European lyric, a genre with rules as strict as the sonnet's; the lovers' most autonomous act is performed inside a thousand-year-old script for how lovers part at daybreak. Callaghan's ghost could not ask for a better exhibit. The same pattern repeats in the exchange's scholarly wake: when later critics, Jonas Kellermann among them, praised Kottman's essay as ground-breaking and built on its account of mutual recognition, aligning it with Laurie Maguire's reading of the lovers' reciprocity, the debate's centre of gravity had still shifted onto Callaghan's ground. The question was no longer whether the love was exempt from analysis but what kind of claim a historically formed desire could stake, which is the question her essay taught the field to ask.

There is a second contest, quieter but closer to the essay's machinery, from within materialist feminism itself. Catherine Belsey's work on the subject of tragedy and Kathleen McLuskie's "The Patriarchal Bard" (1985) share Callaghan's suspicion of recuperated heroines, yet both resist any account in which a text's ideological function is read straight off its content, without the mediations of performance, genre, and a readership that never receives a play in the passive posture functionalism imagines. McLuskie's insistence that feminist criticism attend to the conditions of production and to the play in performance, rather than mining it for positions, is a friendly amendment with teeth: it accepts the essay's premises and questions its confidence that the work the play did can be deduced from the work the play says. Callaghan's later practice, the stage-centred argument of Shakespeare Without Women and the documentary pluralism of her edition, reads in part as the amendment absorbed. The essay's functionalism is at its most vulnerable exactly where these allies press: not on whether the play formed desire, but on how directly any critic can invoice the formation.

## The Historians' Objection: Was Companionate Marriage New?

Every link in Callaghan's chain is only as strong as Stone's chronology, and the chronology is the chain's most attacked link. Stone's The Family, Sex and Marriage argued that affective individualism, the valuing of personal feeling as the ground of domestic life, rose among England's propertied classes across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, displacing the kin-dominated, arrangement-first family of the earlier period. The thesis organized a generation of literary argument, Callaghan's included. It also drew sustained fire. Alan Macfarlane's Marriage and Love in England: Modes of Reproduction 1300-1840 (1986) contended that the individualistic, love-choosing pattern Stone dated late was visible in English records centuries earlier, rooted in a property system that had long made marriage a couple's enterprise. Ralph Houlbrooke's studies of the early modern family counselled a picture of slow, uneven change in which parental authority and personal affection had coexisted for generations, neither simply succeeding the other. If Macfarlane is right in the strong form, the formation Callaghan dates to the decades around 1600 was not a formation but a fixture, and her essay's historical engine loses its fuel.

The adjudication defended here distinguishes the essay's two historical claims, which its critics habitually merge. The strong claim is developmental: that companionate desire as marriage's foundation was new in the period, an emergent formation answering an emergent economy. That claim is hostage to the historians' quarrel and, on the present state of the evidence, must be discounted: the records show affectionate, chosen marriages too early and too widely for novelty in the strong sense. The weaker claim is ideological in the strict sense: that in the decades around the play, a concerted cultural campaign, in pulpits, conduct books, liturgy, and theatre, was elevating chosen, affectionate, sacramental marriage into the publicly authoritative account of what marriage is, and demoting the alliance account to a vulgarity practised by stage fathers. That claim survives Macfarlane intact, because a doctrine's dominance is an event in the history of representations even when the practice it blesses is old. People may have married for love for centuries; a culture that teaches its daughters, from the stage, that love is marriage's only honest foundation has still changed, and the teaching is Callaghan's subject. Her essay occasionally writes as if it needs the strong claim. Its argument only ever spends the weak one, and priced at the weak claim it remains solvent.

This is the point at which the reading's Marxist relatives clarify its identity, since the relation between economic formation and intimate feeling is the oldest problem in that tradition. The broader family of [Marxist Readings of Verona's Class Conflict](/2027/02/12/marxist-readings-romeo-and-juliet/) approaches the play through property, labour, and the feud's economics; Callaghan's feminism shares their premise that feeling has a material history while refusing their habit of treating gender as a secondary theatre of the real war. Her desiring subject is formed at the intersection of the two systems, capital and patriarchy, and the essay's refusal to subordinate either is precisely its feminism. Where the class-first readings sometimes leave Juliet as an asset in her father's portfolio, Callaghan restores her as the portfolio's most sophisticated product: an asset that has learned to want its own transfer, on terms the new market prefers.

## Callaghan the Editor: What the Bedford Edition Does with the Essay

In 2003 Callaghan edited Romeo and Juliet: Texts and Contexts for the Bedford Shakespeare series, and the edition is the essay's argument translated into pedagogy. The play text she reprints is David Bevington's, a mainstream modern edition, unemended by doctrine. Around it she sets six clusters of primary documents: materials on the social relationships among men, honour, and violence; on views of love and the Petrarchan paradigm; on spiritual life; on the family in Elizabethan society; on astrology, medicine, and death; and, as a coda of the intimate archive, letters of the Bagot family and passages from the diary of Lady Anne Clifford, documents in which early modern people negotiate love, marriage, and bereavement in their own unliterary words. The design is the materialist method made visible. The play is not glossed into an interpretation. It is re-shelved inside its documentary neighbourhood, and the student is trusted to feel the neighbourhood's pressure on the text.

The selection of documents deserves a closer look, because it is where the essay's evidence ethic becomes visible. The Bagot letters and Lady Anne Clifford's diary are not the period's official voice but its private paperwork: a family's negotiations over marriages and money, a great lady's record of a marriage endured as a decades-long property dispute. Setting those documents beside the tragedy teaches a student to ask Callaghan's question in its most concrete form: what did the arrangements feel like from inside, to the people whose desires the arrangements processed? Clifford, who fought her husband and the crown for her inheritance and recorded her married misery in prose of iron control, is the anti-Juliet the archive supplies: a woman whose story shows what the companionate doctrine's failures looked like when the wife survived them. The edition's argument is made by the shelving, and the shelving is the essay's method with its sleeves rolled up.

The edition matters to this article's verdict for two reasons. First, it answers the charge that the essay's method reduces the play to a thesis. Given the whole apparatus of an edition to command, Callaghan does not print her thesis as the play's meaning; she prints the archive and lets the play argue with it, which is the practice of a critic who trusts her method more than her conclusions. Second, the edition's contents enact the essay's historical claim in miniature. A student who reads the Petrarchan documents beside the feast sonnet, the conduct literature beside Capulet's two voices, and Lady Anne Clifford's marital sorrows beside Juliet's, has been taught Callaghan's lesson without being told it: that the love in the play has neighbours, dates, and documents, that it did not fall from the sky of human nature. The classroom is where ideologies are either reproduced or inspected, and the edition is built for inspection.

Whether the play is, in the end, a feminist text is a question the edition conspicuously declines to settle by apparatus, leaving it where a long-running debate in this series, [Is Romeo and Juliet a Feminist Play?](/2031/04/18/is-romeo-and-juliet-feminist/), continues to argue it. Callaghan's own answer, across the essay and the book, is that the question is malformed. A text manufactured by an all-male institution, inside a culture assigning women to silence, can be many things: a superb instrument for studying how femininity and desire were constructed, a site where the construction shows its seams, an occasion for readers to refuse the assignment. What it cannot be is a woman's testimony or a programme of liberation, and feminist criticism that requires it to be either has mistaken the object of its own study. The refusal is the bleakest sentence in her work and the most characteristic: she will not let the canon console the people it excluded.

## Does the Ideological Reading Dissolve the Love? The Limit, Faced Directly

The brief for any honest account of Callaghan must end at the objection her readers feel in their stomachs: that a reading this suspicious leaves nothing standing, that if the love is ideology the tears are a fraud and the play a confidence trick played across four centuries. Callaghan's own texts supply the materials of the answer, and the answer has three parts.

First, ideology in her sense is not the opposite of the real. A formation of desire is still desire; the girl in the orchard wants what she wants with her whole being, and the historical account of how that wanting was assembled does not make the wanting counterfeit, any more than the history of a language makes its poems insincere. What the account dissolves is not the love but the love's alibi: its claim to be uncaused, universal, and therefore beyond judgment. Second, the reading restores to the play a strangeness that familiarity has worn smooth. A tragedy in which two children are destroyed by a feeling their culture was just learning to manufacture, and are then cast in gold by the men who priced them, is not a smaller object than the eternal love story. It is a larger and darker one, whose darkness includes its audience. Third, the reading explains the very fact the celebrants cite against it, the love's felt absoluteness. If the formation had not been installed with genius, it would not feel like nature. The ache is the evidence, and only a weak materialism would be embarrassed by its own best exhibit.

### What survives of the love story after the ideological reading?

Everything except its innocence. The poetry's beauty is untouched, since the argument was never that the verse is bad but that its beauty is an instrument with a history. The lovers' courage survives, and even grows: they pursue the newest feeling in their world at the price of their lives, which is a recognizably human grandeur.

Whether the feeling was born in their hearts or installed there, its pursuit at that price stands as the play's human weight. What does not survive is the use of the play as proof that love needs no history, the balcony-shaped certificate of timelessness. Callaghan's reader leaves the theatre moved and disarmed of exactly one thing, the alibi.

The reading's consequences in the theatre deserve recording beside its consequences in print, since a materialism that cannot reach the stage has missed its object's home. Performance criticism after McLuskie had already insisted that a play's meanings are remade nightly under material conditions; Callaghan's framework handed directors and their critics a specific question to ask of any production: what is this staging doing with the formation? A production that costumes the feud in modern wealth and plays the orchard as a realm outside economics has reproduced the ideology in its purest form, whatever its programme notes claim. A production that keeps the market visible inside the love scenes, the servants counting, the fathers negotiating in earshot of the garden, the statues prefigured in the set's gold, has staged the essay without naming it. The framework's portability is a test of its truth, and on the evidence of the play's stage life the test is passed more often by accident than by design, which is itself the most Callaghanian result imaginable: the ideology survives its exposure, because exposure was never the same as exemption, a sentence that belongs over the door of every seminar room where her essay is taught.

The residual weakness should be recorded with the same plainness. The essay's machinery is better at explaining the love's form than its force: why this play, and not the period's hundred other romances, installed the formation so durably. Genius, theatrical circumstance, the accident of Shakespeare, enter her account as givens rather than as explanations, and a materialism that cannot price its object's singularity will always leave a remainder that formalists and philosophers, Levin in an earlier generation and Kottman in this one, will claim as their territory. The claim should be conceded at the border and refused at the centre. The remainder is real, and it is small: a difference in degree of execution, not in kind of meaning. Many plays preached the new marriage. One play made the preaching unforgettable by giving the doctrine two children, a garden, and a tomb, and the difference, though it is the difference between a footnote and a monument, does not rescue the doctrine's product from its history.

## What Callaghan's Reading Reveals: The Verdict Weighed

Weighed whole, Callaghan's feminist materialism reveals the play's best-kept secret: that Romeo and Juliet is not a love story with a social setting but a social argument with a love story for its engine. The setting is the argument's evidence. A daughter priced by her house, a suitor vetted like a merger, a friar calculating alliances, a nurse brokering between the market and the bedchamber, fathers settling accounts in gold over two corpses: the play's Verona is a complete diagram of marriage as an institution in transition, and the lovers are the transition's test case, the first couple in English drama asked to found a marriage on nothing but the feeling the new doctrine said was foundation enough. They fail the test because the old institution still holds the weapons, and the play's four-century career is the new institution's long victory parade, in which the test case is exhibited as proof that the doctrine was always true. Callaghan's reading is the first to read the parade as part of the play.

Her reading also reveals, by contrast, the protective arrangement under which the love had previously been studied. Criticism had historicized everything in the tragedy except its centre: the feud got a sociology, the fathers a psychology, the Prince a politics, and the love a pass, on the ground that feeling is where history stops. The exemption was never argued; it was inherited, and inheritances of that kind are ideology's favourite vehicle. By refusing the exemption, Callaghan did for desire what the previous generation of feminists, Kahn at their head, had done for the feud: she made it an object with a provenance, a function, and a cost. The field's subsequent practice is her victory's measure. No serious account of the play treats the love as self-explanatory, and the accounts that resist her conclusion, Kottman's included, resist it on ground she staked out, the question of what the formation does with the freedom it simulates or supplies.

The verdict on the reading is therefore that it stands, discounted at two points and sovereign at its centre. Discounted, first, where its historical engine runs on Stone's strong chronology: the formation's novelty must be softened from invention to installation, the moment a culture's doctrine about love changed faster than its practice. Discounted, second, where its functionalism underprices the play's grief, the remainder of force that no assignment of function quite invoices. Sovereign at the centre: in its demonstration that the lovers' desire arrives formatted, aims at the institution, speaks the market's metaphors in the garden of its supposed escape, and is mourned by a machinery that prices the mourning. A feminist criticism that cannot see the formatting will keep mistaking the newest assignment for liberation. A criticism that can see nothing else will keep missing why the assignment broke hearts. Callaghan saw the formatting first and saw it whole, and the seeing permanently raised the price of innocence in readings of this play. That is what a landmark is.

For the reader arriving at the play through her work, the practical legacy can be stated as a discipline of three questions, applicable to any scene. What institution is speaking in this feeling? What would the feeling cost, and whom would it pay, if the whole city felt it? And what in the scene cannot be invoiced to any function, the remainder where the play's singular force resides? The first two questions are Callaghan's, and they will dismantle a century of balcony sentiment in an afternoon. The third is her critics' gift back to her, and it keeps the dismantling honest. A criticism that asks all three has inherited the essay correctly: not as a verdict to repeat but as an instrument to operate, on the play first and on its own responses always.

## The Formation in the Language: Petrarch's Script and the Sonnet at the Feast

A materialist reading earns its keep at the level of the line, and the play's first act supplies Callaghan's evidence in its most quotable form. Romeo enters the tragedy already desiring in a borrowed idiom. His love for Rosaline is conducted entirely in the period's off-the-shelf erotic vocabulary, the oxymorons of the Petrarchan tradition: "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1.176), bright smoke, cold fire, sick health, the whole liturgy of a desire that has never needed an actual woman to sustain it, which is fortunate, since its object has vowed chastity and never appears. The play is explicit about the mechanism. Romeo's feeling precedes its object and survives the object's instant replacement, which marks it, in the terms this article has been building, as a formation in search of an assignee. Petrarchism was the period's installed software for elite desire: a set of postures, figures, and frustrations that taught young men of a certain class how longing was supposed to feel and sound. Romeo runs the software flawlessly, and the play's comedy at his expense, Mercutio's mockery, Benvolio's diagnosis, is the sound of his friends recognizing the programme.

The feast scene then performs the formation's upgrade, and Callaghan's reading of it deserves the prominence her essay gives the moment. Romeo meets Juliet and the borrowed idiom changes hands. The lovers' first exchange is a complete sonnet, fourteen lines shared between them (1.5.93-106), built on the pilgrim conceit: his lips are pilgrims, her hand a shrine, the kiss a prayer answered. Criticism has generally treated the sonnet as the tragedy's certificate of authenticity, the moment scripted feeling becomes mutual truth because two voices complete one form. Callaghan's question cuts the celebration at the joint. Why should the completion of a set form certify freedom from forms? The sonnet is the most rule-bound structure in the period's lyric repertoire, and the lovers experience its constraints, the alternating quatrains, the negotiated kiss at the couplet, as the freest speech of their lives. Form experienced as freedom is, in her analysis, the ideology's signature effect, and the play signs it in the scene every audience remembers as love's spontaneous birth.

Juliet's part in the sonnet complicates the picture in a way the reading handles with care. She is no passive shrine. She corrects the conceit's theology ("You do wrong your hand too much," 1.5.96), returns its figures with interest, and grants the kiss by out-arguing the pilgrim in his own liturgy. Edward Snow's landmark essay "Language and Sexual Difference in Romeo and Juliet" (1985) built a celebrated case on such evidence: the lovers' language differentiates as it matures, Juliet's speech shedding convention faster and further than Romeo's, until her desire speaks in a register the tradition had not scripted for girls. Snow's evidence is real, and Callaghan's framework absorbs rather than refutes it. A formation is not a straitjacket; it is a repertoire, and virtuosity inside a repertoire is exactly what a well-installed formation produces. Juliet's brilliance at revising the script testifies to her maker's genius and her own dramatic life. It does not testify that the script was absent. The distinction, between performing a formation superbly and standing outside it, is the distinction on which the essay's quarrel with celebratory feminism finally rests.

The formatting begins before any lover speaks. The Prologue delivers the entire story in advance, in sonnet form, and names its product with a marketer's precision: "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue.6). The audience is told what it will feel before it feels it, told that the deaths are the meaning and the love the instrument, and the telling is itself cast in the period's prestige lyric structure, so that the tragedy's frame performs the same operation as its scenes: an assigned response, elegantly formatted, experienced on delivery as discovery. Callaghan's reading is at its most persuasive on such structural courtesies. A play that trusted its love to announce itself would not need to pre-narrate the announcement in the form the announcement will take. The Chorus is the formation's advance billing, and the house, weeping on schedule two hours later at a catastrophe it was promised in the first minute, is the billing's proof of work.

The orchard scene extends the lesson into the lovers' most private register. Romeo, overhearing, reaches for the old liturgy and is stopped by its object: Juliet, learning she has been overheard, conducts an audit of the forms themselves, refusing the oath by the inconstant moon (2.2.109-111) and demanding that the exchange be denominated in the one currency she trusts, which turns out to be the marriage contract examined earlier in this article. Even her refusals, that is, are refusals of one period form in favour of another period form, the sacramental bond over the lyric oath. The play's deepest privacy is transacted entirely in public instruments: sonnet, oath, contract, sacrament. Callaghan's reading simply declines to be surprised. A culture's most intimate feelings arrive in its most durable forms because the forms are how the feelings were taught, and the tragedy, read with her patience, becomes legible as the record of a teaching so successful that its pupils experience the lesson as their souls.

## The Case in the Scenes: A Walking Tour of the Formation

The reading's evidence is not confined to the famous speeches. Walked scene by scene, the play shows the formation at work in its minor transactions, and the tour strengthens Callaghan's case precisely because the exhibits are the ones celebratory criticism steps over. Begin with the play's first act of salesmanship. Lady Capulet, sent to prepare her daughter for the Paris match, presents the bridegroom as a text: "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face, / And find delight writ there with beauty's pen" (1.3.82-83), and, in the speech's notorious culmination, "This precious book of love, this unbound lover, / To beautify him, only lacks a cover" (1.3.88-89). The metaphor is doing economic work under its wit. The daughter is positioned as a reader, the suitor as a commodity in print, and the marriage as the binding that completes the volume: Paris, unbound, lacks only the cover that Juliet is to supply. A mother is teaching a thirteen-year-old to consume a husband as literature at the very moment a playwright is teaching an audience to consume a daughter's desire as poetry. Callaghan's method was built for such rhymes between the play's world and the play's work. The market inside the fiction and the market that sold the fiction speak one language, and the language is print, the period's newest machinery for distributing formations.

The Nurse supplies the formation's vernacular. Her register is the body priced in memory: the weaning, the fall, the husband's joke about falling backward, a whole economics of appetite and use that treats Juliet's growing as a crop's progress toward harvest. Criticism divides her into comic relief and secret ally, but in the materialist ledger she is the household's broker, the servant whose labour mediates between the market above stairs and the bedchamber where the goods are kept. Her counsel in the crisis of 3.5, that Juliet should marry the County since Romeo is banished and as good as dead, is usually read as betrayal, and Juliet receives it as one. Read structurally it is the old order's voice speaking its plainest prose inside the new order's stronghold: a marriage is a marriage, the asset must not sit idle, sentiment is a luxury the market does not price. The formation's triumph in the play is measurable by the fact that the audience recoils from this arithmetic exactly as the new doctrine taught it to. Her mistress's daughter has learned the lesson better than the broker: Juliet would rather counterfeit death than be re-entered in the ledger, and the recoil the house feels at the Nurse's counsel is the companionate conscience, installed and functioning.

The fourth act then shows the formation's crisis management, which is the church's. Juliet, cornered between a living husband and a scheduled wedding, is given by Friar Laurence a technology: a distilled liquor that will counterfeit death for two and forty hours (4.1.93-95). The materialist notes what the celebrant passes over. The desire the sacrament licensed can be preserved, at the extremity, only by a simulation of the tomb administered by the sacrament's own officer: the institution rescues its product by pretending to bury it. Her soliloquy over the vial (4.3) is the formation's interior at maximum pressure, a girl rehearsing the vault's horrors, the bones of her ancestors, Tybalt's festering corpse, madness, and resolving on the draught anyway, because the alternative is the ledger. Whatever else the speech is, and it is many great things, it is not the voice of a desire outside the social order. It is the voice of a subject choosing which of the order's instruments, bigamy or counterfeit death, will kill her less completely. Callaghan's reading does not shrink the speech. It explains why the choice set is so short.

Even the lovers' deaths obey the pattern the tour has traced. Romeo dies by a purchased poison, procured from an apothecary whose poverty is the play's most explicit piece of economics: "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1.75), answered by Romeo's verdict on the whole system, "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will" (5.1.76). Money, the market's plainest instrument, delivers the tragedy's final agent, in a transaction the play itself annotates as the world's law overriding the soul's. Juliet dies by the dagger, the feud's own weapon, kept at her husband's side. The instruments are the two orders' signatures: gold and steel, the counting house and the brawl, dividing the corpses between them as neatly as the fathers will divide the statues. A reading that treats the love as the play's only reality has no use for this symmetry. Callaghan's reading is built on it.

## The Afterlife as Evidence: Canon, Classroom, and the Balcony Trade

The third movement of Callaghan's essay, its account of the play's afterlife, is sometimes treated as a coda. It is closer to a proof, since the afterlife is where the ideology's work becomes measurable in institutions rather than inferable in texts. Consider the stage history's most instructive episode. David Garrick's adaptation, first performed in 1748 and dominant for a century, rewrote the ending so that Juliet wakes before Romeo dies, granting the lovers a final exchange in the tomb. The alteration is usually filed as eighteenth-century sentimentality. In Callaghan's register it is the formation maintaining itself: an audience formed by the doctrine of companionate love required the doctrine's martyrs to ratify each other with dying words, required the marriage to be witnessed as mutual to the last breath, and the theatre supplied the requirement. The text was corrected until it matched the feeling the culture had learned from the text. Ideology rarely leaves a clearer fingerprint than an improvement.

The schoolroom extended the work the theatre began. The tragedy's installation as the set text of adolescence, the play through which generations of students meet Shakespeare and, not coincidentally, meet the authorized account of what young love is, belongs to the same history. A curriculum does not merely transmit the formation; it schedules the encounter at the age of maximum susceptibility and certifies the feeling as literature's central exhibit, so that the student's own first longings arrive pre-interpreted by the Capulet orchard. Juliet's age, a datum the play states and the culture prefers to forget, is the installation's pressure point: the doctrine requires its heroine to be a girl whose desire is innocent because sacramental, and the classroom's discomfort, when the arithmetic is done aloud, measures the distance between the doctrine and the document. Callaghan's essay thrives on such measurements. Each is a place where the formation's seams show.

Verona itself supplies the afterlife's most literal exhibit. The house marketed as Juliet's, with a balcony assembled in a twentieth-century restoration for visitors who needed the play's most famous stage property to exist, receives pilgrims who leave letters addressed to the heroine, letters a local secretariat, the Juliet Club, has answered since the 1970s in her name. The phenomenon is easy to patronize and better read as data. A fictional girl, authored by a man, played by a boy, dead at thirteen in a feud not her own, has become the confidante of the world's unhappy lovers, consulted as an authority on the feeling her play was accused by Callaghan of manufacturing. The balcony trade is the ideology's tourism division: the formation, fully installed, sells tickets to its own birthplace and answers its own correspondence. Against this background the essay's polemic reads less as an attack on the play than as the only account that predicted the balcony.

Film carried the formation to its largest audiences, and the two landmark adaptations mark its range. Franco Zeffirelli's film of 1968 cast actual teenagers and sold the lovers' innocence to a generation primed to read the feud as its parents' world; Baz Luhrmann's film of 1996 dressed the same feeling in the iconography of youth marketing and demonstrated that the formation survives any costume, since its product was never Verona but the conviction that this desire is the viewer's own. Callaghan's framework handles both without strain, which is the mark of a theory with genuine reach. Celebratory criticism must treat each era's Romeo and Juliet as a rediscovery of the eternal. The materialist treats the succession as a maintenance schedule, and notes which parts of the play each era files down: the heroine's age blurred, the marriage plot's economics muted, the statues forgotten, the orchard eternal.

## The Field She Built: The Feminist Infrastructure Around the Essay

An essay's influence runs through arguments; a career's influence runs through institutions, and Callaghan built hers with unusual deliberation. A Feminist Companion to Shakespeare, which she edited for Blackwell in 2000, gathered the movement's history and its methods into a single teaching volume, opening with a history of feminist Shakespeare criticism that runs from the early women readers Juliet Fleming surveyed in "The Ladies' Shakespeare" to the materialist present. The Companion's function in the field is easy to underestimate precisely because it succeeded: it made feminist reading a syllabus category with a genealogy, a canon of its own, and a set of settled questions, among which the question of the heroines' manufacture, her question, stands near the head. When she later edited The Impact of Feminism in English Renaissance Studies, the stocktaking genre itself testified to the installation: a movement that began by picketing the canon had acquired Festschriften and balance sheets.

The infrastructure matters to the weighing of the essay because it clarifies what kind of victory Callaghan was playing for. The liberal-feminist criticism she opposed sought admission: heroines admitted as subjects, women readers admitted as authorities, the play admitted as an ally. Callaghan's programme sought jurisdiction. Her companion, her casebook selections, and her Bedford edition all work to relocate the authority to describe the play from the play's admirers to its documents, and to make the audit of desire a standard instrument in the kit every student is handed. A field is changed less by the essays it cannot answer than by the textbooks it cannot avoid, and on that metric her two decades of editing did as much as the 1994 essay to fix the terms on which Romeo and Juliet is taught. The essay argues that the love has a history. The infrastructure ensures that the argument is assigned.

Placed in that setting, her much-noted refusal of consolation appears in its proper light. Feminist criticism had two comforts available in its dealings with this play: the comfort of the heroine, Juliet as the canon's gift to its daughters, and the comfort of the author, Shakespeare as the male writer who uniquely understood women. Callaghan's work declines both, on the same evidence. The heroine is a manufacture, and the manufacturer's understanding is itself a product of the institution that excluded women from the stage and assigned them, in marriage doctrine, to companionship under government. What the refusal buys is the only ground from which the play can be criticized without sentimentality: outside the formation, in the archive, where the documents are older than the feelings they taught. Critics who find the ground cold have generally not proposed a warmer one that is not inside the formation's own weather.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch Callaghan summary collect, and what single pattern unites it?

It collects the load-bearing claims of Dympna Callaghan's feminist-materialist reading in the order her essay builds them, from the claim that romantic love is an ideology with a history, through the evidence of Juliet's marriage-bound desire and the fathers' golden statues, to the refusal of feminist consolation in Shakespeare Without Women. The single pattern uniting the entries is manufacture: each row shows a feeling, a heroine, or a sympathy being produced by identifiable institutions, sermons, theatres, and markets, and then misrecognized by its audience as nature. The table is built for testing, since every claim is printed beside the evidence it rests on and the use it makes of the play.

### Q: Where was "The Ideology of Romantic Love" first published, and when?

The essay first appeared in The Weyward Sisters: Shakespeare and Feminist Politics, the 1994 volume Callaghan wrote with Lorraine Helms and Jyotsna Singh for Blackwell, where it occupied pages 59 to 101 as the book's longest single reading. Its argument reached its widest classroom audience through reprinting in Romeo and Juliet: Contemporary Critical Essays, the New Casebooks volume edited by R. S. White for Palgrave in 2001, where it stands at pages 85 to 115 among the play's landmark modern readings. The double life matters to its influence: the 1994 text addressed a profession mid-argument about feminist method, while the 2001 reprint installed the argument as a set text for students meeting the play's criticism for the first time.

### Q: What history of marriage does Callaghan's argument depend on?

It depends on the documented contest, in the century before the play, between marriage as an alliance of houses arranged by kin and marriage as a companionship founded on the couple's affection. The alliance system is visible in the play's own negotiations, where Paris sues to Capulet and Juliet's consent is treated as a courtesy inside a settlement. The companionate alternative was being preached in the period's pulpits and conduct books, which relocated desire's legitimate home into the married household and made mutual affection a duty. Callaghan's essay reads the tragedy as a product of the collision: the lovers claim the new foundation while the old system's officers still control the doors, the law, and the tomb.

### Q: Who was Lawrence Stone, and what did his family history give the essay?

Lawrence Stone was the historian whose The Family, Sex and Marriage in England 1500-1800, published in 1977, argued that affective individualism and the companionate marriage rose among the propertied classes across the early modern period. His work gave Callaghan's essay its historical chassis: a datable transition in what marriage was understood to be for, from lineage strategy toward personal fulfilment, unfolding in the very decades when the play was written and first canonized. The chassis carried the essay's central move, which was to set the lovers' desire inside that transition rather than outside history altogether. Later historians contested Stone's timetable, and the essay's strongest claims must be discounted accordingly, but the documented argument about marriage's foundations is independent of any single chronology.

### Q: What do Protestant marriage manuals contribute to Callaghan's evidence?

They contribute the doctrine in its own words, from the culture that produced the play. The Book of Common Prayer's marriage service named mutual society, help, and comfort among matrimony's ordained causes, placing companionship inside the sacrament's official purposes. William Gouge's Of Domestical Duties in 1622 built the period's most systematic account of the godly household, in which affection between husband and wife is preached as a duty and coldness treated as a fault, while the husband's government of the house stands unquestioned. For Callaghan these texts prove that desire was being deliberately re-housed: praised, sanctified, and assigned to the married bed by named writers with a programme. Juliet's longing, aimed at a wedding from its first speech, is the doctrine wearing poetry.

### Q: Why does the lovers' wish to marry matter more to Callaghan than their defiance?

Because the wish defines the desire's object, and the object is the institution itself. The lovers defy a father, a feud, a timetable, and a rival suitor, and the defiance is genuine; but what they defy them for is a wedding, a household, and a sacrament, the very forms the emerging order needed couples to want. Juliet's orchard proposal specifies marriage, a messenger, and a deadline with a lawyer's precision. In Callaghan's ledger this makes the desire not the system's opposite but its herald: the new marriage of chosen affection announcing itself against the old marriage of alliance, on ground the new order was already winning in sermons and conduct books. Rebellion aimed at the altar is an application with better poetry.

### Q: How does Juliet's "bounty" speech function in the materialist reading?

The speech functions as the ideology's signature inside the lovers' most private language. Juliet figures her feeling as wealth that grows by being given: her bounty is boundless as the sea, her love as deep, and the more she gives the more she has. Admirers read the image as the marriage market's antithesis, a gift economy exempt from depletion and price. Callaghan reads the same lines as the market transfigured rather than escaped: desire imagined as inexhaustible capital, multiplying through circulation, in the metaphors of bounty, giving, and having that Verona's counting houses supplied. That the lovers' freest speech thinks in the economy's idiom is, for the materialist, the proof that the formation reaches the innermost chamber.

### Q: What does the golden statue ending show about the uses of the lovers' deaths?

It shows the fathers converting the catastrophe back into the currency the lovers tried to leave. Montague promises Juliet a statue in pure gold; Capulet answers that Romeo's shall lie as rich beside his lady. Within the fiction this is grief, but grief denominated as reciprocal expenditure, each house honouring the other's child at a stated price, the alliance the living marriage would have sealed is concluded over corpses. For Callaghan the statues also do cultural work beyond Verona: the dead lovers are monumentalized as the patron saints of the new marriage doctrine, safe to worship because dead, recruiting audiences to the formation that their living counterparts were killed for attempting too early.

### Q: What does Shakespeare Without Women claim about the original Juliet?

It claims that the original Juliet was not a woman and was not, in the assumed sense, a representation of one. Callaghan's 2000 book argues that Shakespeare's stage contained no women at all: every female figure was a script written by a man and embodied by a boy apprentice in an all-male commercial company, in a culture that excluded women from playing by custom. Her chapters examine what such exclusion meant, from the absent female body in Twelfth Night to the impersonated female voice, and refuse to invent the responses of women in the audience, since the record does not preserve them. Applied to the tragedy, the argument relocates Juliet's celebrated subjectivity from female expression to male manufacture.

### Q: Why does Callaghan refuse to treat Juliet as a woman's voice?

Because the material record gives her no woman to attribute the voice to. The words are a male playwright's, the body a boy performer's, the institution a company of men playing to a culture that barred women from its stage. Treating the result as women's testimony mistakes a representation for a witness. Callaghan's refusal is not a denial that the figure is inward, eloquent, or sympathetic; it is an insistence that those qualities are achievements of construction, and that construction is the proper object of feminist analysis. The point disciplines her whole method: criticism should study how femininity and desire were manufactured for consumption, not consume the manufacture as knowledge about the women it excluded.

### Q: What separates Callaghan's feminism from the celebration of Juliet as a heroine?

The separation is her refusal to exempt desire from the analysis she applies to everything else. Celebratory readings honour Juliet's wanting as the self's truth breaking through a repressive order, and build a heroine on the honour. Callaghan asks what produced the wanting's shape, notes that it runs from its first articulation toward marriage, sacrament, and household, and concludes that the desire is itself one of the order's formations, newer than the feud's arrangements but not outside history. The heroine is not diminished by the question; she is relocated, from nature's witness against the system to the system's most sophisticated artifact. Feminism, on her account, begins where the audit of its own sympathies begins.

### Q: What does Paul A. Kottman object to in the ideology reading?

Kottman objects that it cannot account for the love's claim to freedom. In "Defying the Stars," published in 2012 and extended in Love As Human Freedom in 2017, he reads the lovers' bond through Hegel as a struggle for mutual recognition, a bid for a self that no inherited form, family, city, or church, can confer. Grouping Callaghan with the critics for whom the play's idealized love is an ideological construct, social through and through, he answers that a desire wholly assigned by its culture could not stake the unconditional claim Romeo stakes in defying the stars. The objection has force against any materialism that confuses a feeling's formation with its verdict on its conditions.

### Q: What do historians dispute in the companionate-marriage timeline the essay inherits?

They dispute its novelty and its slope. Alan Macfarlane's Marriage and Love in England in 1986 argued that individualistic, affection-based marriage ran centuries deeper in English society than Lawrence Stone's rising curve allowed, rooted in older property arrangements that made marriage a couple's enterprise. Ralph Houlbrooke's work counselled gradualism: parental authority and personal affection coexisting for generations rather than succeeding each other in stages. The dispute costs Callaghan's essay its strong claim, that companionate desire was invented in the period. It leaves standing the weaker and sufficient claim, that the decades around the play saw chosen, sacramental, affectionate marriage elevated into public doctrine by pulpits, manuals, and stages, which is an event in the history of representations whatever the practice's age.

### Q: What did Callaghan put around the play in her Texts and Contexts edition?

She put the archive. The 2003 Bedford edition reprints David Bevington's text of the play and surrounds it with six clusters of primary documents: materials on men's social relationships, honour, and violence; on views of love and the Petrarchan paradigm; on spiritual life; on the family in Elizabethan society; on astrology, medicine, and death; and intimate records including the Bagot family's letters and passages from Lady Anne Clifford's diary. The arrangement is her materialist method as pedagogy. Rather than glossing the play into her thesis, the edition re-shelves it inside its documentary neighbourhood and trusts students to feel the neighbourhood's pressure on every famous speech.

### Q: How does the essay treat the feud: as the love's enemy or as its frame?

It treats the feud as the frame that lets the love be misread as pure opposition. Within the fiction the feud is plainly the enemy: its brawls, duels, and loyalties perform the play's killings. But Callaghan's attention is on what the frame does for the audience. Because the feud poisons every public claim in Verona in advance, spectators never weigh the lovers' desire against any legitimate rival; consent to the love is manufactured by the dramaturgy before the poetry ratifies it. The feud is thus the ideology's necessary villain, the old order whose brutality certifies the new desire as innocence. Remove the frame and the desire would have to argue its case; the frame excuses it from arguing.

### Q: Why does the play's popularity across the centuries matter to Callaghan's argument?

Because the popularity is the ideology completing its work. The tragedy became the culture's standing proof that romantic love is a transhistorical constant, cited in curricula, commerce, and adaptation as evidence that this feeling needs no history. Callaghan reads the loop as the formation's final victory: a representation that helped install a particular organization of desire serves as the exhibit that the organization is nature. Each generation's certainty that the lovers are simply universal is, on her account, a measurement of the installation's success rather than a refutation of it. The essay's polemic against criticism follows directly: readings that begin by conceding the love's transcendence have surrendered the ground before the argument starts.

### Q: Does Callaghan argue that Shakespeare intended to manufacture an ideology?

No, and the distinction is load-bearing in her method. Ideology, in the materialist usage, is a medium rather than a message: the set of arrangements a culture inhabits as reality, which texts circulate by working within them, not by conspiring to insert them. The play need not argue for companionate marriage to work for it; it works by making chosen, mutual, consuming desire appear as the one authentic relation in a world of bargains, and by arranging its catastrophe so that the bargains carry the blame. A playwright of genius inside a formation will produce its most effective instruments without intending any of them. Intention is the wrong address for the question, and Callaghan's refusal to send it there is what makes her account an analysis rather than an accusation.

### Q: What happens to Juliet's subjectivity in a materialist account?

It becomes an effect to be explained rather than a testimony to be believed. The materialist does not deny the figure's inwardness, her soliloquies' unmatched registration of a desiring consciousness. She asks what the inwardness is made of: Petrarchan lyric forms, conduct-book psychology, sacramental theology, and the labour of a playwright and a boy performer in an all-male company. Subjectivity, so described, is a cultural production with a workshop and a date, and its celebrated depth is a measure of the workshop's skill rather than an exemption from history. What changes for the reader is the direction of admiration: from the heroine as a window onto girlhood to the heroine as the period's most sophisticated machine for imagining it.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Callaghan summary to argue that the play's love has a history?

By building the case from the summary's middle rows outward. Start with the play's own formatting evidence: Juliet's proposal of marriage in the orchard, her bounty speech's economics, the sacrament the lovers seek within a day of meeting. Set those beside the summary's documentary rows, the Prayer Book's purposes of matrimony and the conduct books' companionate doctrine, to show the desire's shape matching a datable cultural programme. Then close with the statues, where the fathers price the love in gold, and the play's later career as the standing proof of love's timelessness. The summary supplies the pattern; the essay's task is to make a reader see manufacture where the tradition taught nature.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence in the play for Callaghan's reading?

The strongest evidence is the orchard scene's proposal, where the most famous declaration of spontaneous passion in English drama takes the form of a marriage application. Juliet, alone and overheard, does not propose flight, exile, or a life outside Verona's forms. She specifies the institution, the intermediary, and the deadline: if Romeo's bent of love is honourable, his purpose marriage, he is to send word tomorrow by her messenger. Spontaneity that arrives so precisely formatted is difficult to read as the social order's opposite. Around that single exhibit the other evidence arranges itself: the shared sonnet's set form, the bounty speech's economics, the Friar's alliance calculation, and the golden statues in which the fathers finally price the feeling.
