Rent, in the analysis this article examines, is income obtained because someone owns a condition that others need and cannot freely reproduce, and it is paid for access to that condition rather than for anything the owner has produced. Profit, by contrast, is income arising where capital organises production, employs wage labour, and appropriates part of the new value that labour creates. Holding those two definitions apart is the entire starting point, because a debate that blurs them at the outset cannot be settled later by any quantity of evidence. A reader who has met the claim that a new feudalism has arrived has usually met it as a mood before meeting it as an argument, a sense that access charges, tolls, and permissions now stand where buying and selling once stood. Moods deserve respect as symptoms. They do not deserve surrender as conclusions.

![Stone gateway, toll road and market stalls arranged for an essay on rent and profit, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Two different questions hide inside the popular formulation, and separating them does more work than any verdict offered prematurely. One question concerns distribution: has the share of income flowing to owners of monopolised conditions grown relative to the share flowing to capitals that organise production? That question is empirical, gradual, and entirely capable of being answered yes without anything dramatic following. The other question concerns the mode of production itself: have the relations through which goods and services get produced, and through which labour is engaged, been replaced by different relations altogether? That question is categorical. A mode of production is not a flavour of income. It names who works, under whose direction, producing what, and by what compulsion the whole arrangement reproduces itself from one period to the next. Confusing a shift in the first register with a transformation in the second is the characteristic error this article exists to correct, and the correction has a name that will recur throughout: rent presupposes production.

The stakes of the distinction reach beyond taxonomy. If the mode of production has changed, then categories built for capitalism, surplus value among them, lose their grip, and political strategy built on those categories loses its object. If instead the distribution of income has shifted while production relations persist, then the older categories retain their grip precisely where it matters, and the interesting questions concern why owners of certain conditions have strengthened their claim on a surplus they did not create. Between those two diagnoses lies a third, quieter possibility, that a distinctive phase has developed inside an unchanged mode, marked by the dominance of asset ownership in the pattern of incomes. Much of what follows is an attempt to give each of these three positions its strongest form before judging any of them, because a debate conducted against weakened versions of its participants teaches nobody anything.

Nothing in the opening requires the reader to accept the framework in advance. The framework will be stated plainly, its central categories defined where they are used, and its difficulties admitted where they genuinely lie, including one admission that advocates of the tradition sometimes avoid: the category most needed for the present argument, monopoly rent, is the category its founder developed least. Honesty about that thinness is not a concession wrung from the analysis under pressure. It belongs inside the analysis, since an instrument's known weakness is part of what a careful user knows about the instrument. The verdict offered later, that the mode of production has not changed, will also carry its defeater openly, the statement of what evidence would reverse it. A judgement that cannot say what would overturn it is not a judgement at all, only a loyalty.

## Rent and the shape of the question

Rent enters the framework as a portion of something already produced, claimed by an owner whose contribution is permission. The landlord who lets a field, the holder of a mineral deposit, the owner of a right that others must license before they may proceed, each stands in the same analytical posture: none of them, in that capacity, sets labour to work or organises a production process. Each controls a condition without which production, or distribution, or access to a market, cannot occur, and each charges for lifting the barrier that ownership erects. The charge is real income, often very large income, and its reality is not in dispute anywhere in this article. What is in dispute is its source. The tradition's answer is uncompromising: the source is surplus value created elsewhere, in production, by labour, and transferred to the owner through the price mechanism or through contract. Rent is therefore a distribution category before it is anything else.

Profit occupies a different place in the same architecture. Where capital advances money for means of production and for labour power, sets a process in motion, and sells the resulting commodities, the excess of proceeds over advances contains surplus value in its primary form. Competition then redistributes that surplus among capitals, among lenders, among merchants, and among owners of monopolised conditions, so that the income any particular recipient pockets rarely announces its origin honestly. A landowner's rent cheque, a lender's interest payment, a merchant's margin, and an industrialist's profit all arrive as money income of apparently similar standing. The theory's work is to trace each flow back through the redistribution to the single spring from which, on this account, they all ultimately draw. That tracing is what makes the rent debate more than a quarrel about vocabulary. To call an income rent is to make a claim about where value originates. To call it profit is to make the opposite claim. The words are compressed theories.

### Is rent replacing profit?

rent's share of income has risen, but within Marx's framework rent is a claim on surplus value produced elsewhere by labour, so it cannot replace profit without eliminating the source it draws on. The change is in distribution rather than in the mode of production.

That compressed answer, which serves as this article's snippet and its thesis in miniature, deserves unpacking rather than mere repetition. A share can rise for many reasons: because the conditions being monopolised have grown more central to economic life, because legal protections around certain rights have lengthened or widened, because scarcity in some location or resource has intensified, or because productive capitals themselves prefer paying for access over bearing the risks of ownership. Each mechanism raises the rent share while leaving the underlying generation of surplus exactly where it was, in workplaces where labour is employed and commodities are produced. The rising share is therefore evidence about bargaining strength and institutional design before it is evidence about the system's identity. Treating it as automatic proof of systemic transformation skips every analytical step that would make the conclusion earned.

There is also a limit built into the arithmetic, and the limit is logical rather than statistical. Rent draws on surplus value. Profit, in the framework's usage, names surplus value in the hands of the capitals that organised its production, before or during its redistribution. Were rent to absorb the whole surplus, production organised for profit would cease to reward its organisers, and with that cessation the flow of new surplus would itself falter, since under the prevailing relations it is the prospect of profit that sets production in motion. A rentier order that consumed its host would be consuming the condition of its own income. This is why the strong version of the replacement thesis faces an internal difficulty that no accumulation of examples can dissolve: the examples all show rent growing alongside production, parasitic upon it in the strict analytical sense, flourishing precisely where production flourishes enough to feed it.

None of this denies that something significant has changed in the pattern of incomes over the span of decades. Owners of certain kinds of assets have done strikingly well. Access charges have multiplied across domains where outright purchase once prevailed. Control over gateways, standards, rights, and locations has become a prized corporate asset, sometimes prized above the productive apparatus itself. A serious analysis registers all of that. The question is what category the change belongs to, and categories are not decorations placed on facts after the facts are gathered. They determine which comparisons are apt, which mechanisms are sought, and which remedies appear sensible. Misclassify a distributional shift as a change of mode, and the analyst goes hunting for new laws of motion in a system still running on the old ones, while neglecting the levers, legal and institutional, through which the distributional shift was actually accomplished and could actually be contested.

The plan of the article follows from that framing. First the rent categories themselves need careful statement, differential, absolute, and monopoly, because the debate constantly trades on their confusion. Then the test for whether one mode has yielded to another needs stating, so that the three positions can be measured against a standard fixed in advance rather than against intuitions shifting from paragraph to paragraph. The positions themselves come next, each attributed and each given in its own strongest terms. Only after that groundwork does the decisive argument arrive, the claim this article calls rent presupposes production, followed by the two disputes it reorganises, the argument over access-based platforms and the argument over financial expansion. The feudal analogy gets its own slow examination, dimension by dimension, because analogies persuade by atmosphere unless they are forced to specify their points of comparison. Finally the underdeveloped state of monopoly rent in the founding texts gets the frank treatment it requires, and the evidence that would settle the matter, or reverse this article's verdict, is stated as plainly as the verdict itself.

Readers arriving from the dispute about platforms will find that dispute addressed on its own ground later, and the fuller treatment of platform organisation itself lives in the companion analysis of [platform capitalism marxist analysis](/2020/11/15/platform-capitalism-marxist-analysis/), to which this article links rather than duplicating its work. The same discipline applies to finance, whose dedicated treatment sits at [financialisation marxist theory](/2020/12/01/financialisation-marxist-theory/). What the present article contributes is not another survey of either terrain but the single argument that disciplines both: a claim about what rent is, where it comes from, and what therefore cannot be true of an economy in which rent looms large. Arguments that organise other arguments are rare enough to be worth isolating, and this one has been hiding in plain sight inside a debate that prefers its dramas louder.

## Rent in the third volume: differential, absolute, and monopoly forms

The treatment of rent in the third volume of Capital begins from agriculture and mining, where the monopolised condition is land, but its logic extends to any condition that is limited, non-reproducible at will, and owned. Differential rent arises from differences among sites or deposits. Where capitals operate on conditions of unequal fertility, unequal location, or unequal richness, the regulating price of the product is set by conditions on the least favoured site in use, and operators on better sites pocket a surplus profit that competition among tenants then transfers to the owner as rent. A second differential form follows from unequal returns to successive investments of capital on the same site. In neither form does the owner create the advantage. Nature, geography, and prior investment create it, and ownership converts it into income by standing at the gate.

Absolute rent has a different root. It arises from ownership as such, from the bare power of owners as a class to withhold their condition from production unless a payment is made, even on the least favoured site where no differential advantage exists. Its classical explanation in the founding text turns on a particular composition of agricultural capital and on the barrier that landed property poses to the free equalisation of profit rates, details that later readers have disputed and revised. What survives every revision is the structural idea: a monopoly of a necessary condition, held by a class distinct from the capitals that produce, can compel a share of surplus value simply by refusing access. Permission itself becomes a revenue stream. That idea, more than any agricultural detail, is what later debates about access charges inherit.

### What is differential rent in plain terms?

differential rent is the extra income an owner can claim because one site, deposit, or location outperforms another while both sell at a shared market price. The advantage belongs to the condition rather than to effort, so competition among users bids the surplus away from producers and into the owner's hands as rent.

Monopoly rent, the third form, arises where a unique advantage permits a price above what either differential or absolute mechanisms explain. The founding text illustrates it with a vineyard producing wine of exceptional quality from soil that cannot be replicated, where the price is governed by the eagerness and means of wealthy purchasers rather than by costs of production. The category is suggestive and brief. It occupies little space beside the elaborate machinery built for the differential forms, and it arrives without the systematic development that would let later readers apply it confidently to new cases. That brevity matters enormously for the present debate, because the incomes most often described as rent in discussions of intellectual property, of access to controlled infrastructures, and of scarce positional advantages resemble monopoly rent far more than they resemble the rent of corn land. The tradition reaches for its thinnest tool precisely where the load is heaviest, a point developed at length later in this article.

A common confusion treats rent as any payment that seems excessive, unearned, or detached from effort. The framework is stricter and more useful than the moralised usage. Rent is defined by the relation, not by desert: income flowing to ownership of a monopolised condition, drawn from surplus value produced elsewhere. By that test some apparently modest incomes are rent and some enormous incomes are not, since an enormous profit earned by organising production remains profit however much it offends. Conversely, the smallholder who lets a single parcel and lives quietly on the proceeds receives rent in the full analytical sense. Keeping the definition relational protects the analysis from becoming a vocabulary of complaint, and it also protects the debate's strong position from cheap rebuttal, because that position, properly stated, is a relational claim about where income originates, not a complaint that some incomes are too large.

### How does absolute rent differ from a charge for a service?

absolute rent is payment extracted by ownership alone, made because the owner can withhold a necessary condition until terms are met. A service charge pays for labour actually performed for the payer. The distinction turns on whether withholding, rather than working, is what the payment overcomes, and that test separates permission income from productive earnings.

Ground rent in its agricultural home also teaches a lesson about capitalisation that echoes through every later asset debate. Because rent is a regular income, it can be treated as the yield on a sum of money, and the condition that yields it acquires a price as though it were capital, although no labour produced the soil or the location. The price of the condition is thus an irrational expression in the theory's own terms, a capitalised claim on future surplus rather than a store of value created by work. Later asset markets generalise this logic far beyond land: any dependable stream of access income can be priced, traded, and borrowed against, so that claims on surplus acquire the appearance of wealth in themselves. The appearance is powerful and the theory insists it remains an appearance. Behind every capitalised rent stands a flow that must be fed, period after period, by production happening somewhere, under relations the rent recipient does not organise and may never see.

The historical depth of rent inside capitalism deserves emphasis, because popular versions of the debate sometimes imply that rent is an intruder from an earlier order, a feudal residue swelling inside a system that once ran purely on profit. The founding analysis treats landed property differently: capitalism finds landed property in its path, transforms it, subordinates it to the requirements of accumulation, and preserves it as a distinct revenue claim within the capitalist distribution of surplus value. Rent is therefore not external to the mode. It is one of the mode's standing forms of distribution, present from the beginning, varying in weight across periods and sectors. That standing presence is the background against which claims of novelty must prove themselves. Showing that rent exists, even that it grows, cannot by itself announce a new order, since rent growing and shrinking within capitalism is among the oldest stories the framework tells.

## The mode of production criterion: what would have to change

Before any position in the debate can be assessed, the standard of assessment needs fixing in words that all sides can be held to. A mode of production, in the usage this tradition inherits, is characterised by its dominant relations of production: the way labour is engaged, the way the means of production are owned, the form in which the surplus is extracted, and the compulsion that reproduces the whole arrangement. Under capitalism the signature relations are familiar. Labour power is sold by workers who are free in the double sense the founding texts emphasise, free from personal bondage and free from ownership of the conditions of their own work. Production is organised by capitals competing with one another. The surplus is extracted through the wage relation itself, quietly, in production, rather than levied openly by political or customary right. Accumulation, the reinvestment of surplus in expanded production, is not a preference of individual capitalists but a compulsion competition imposes on each of them.

Measured against that standard, a change in the mode of production is a demanding event. It would require that wage labour cease to be the dominant way production is staffed, or that production for exchange under competitive compulsion cease to organise economic life, or that surplus extraction migrate to a mechanism resting on direct coercion or customary obligation rather than on the labour market and the production process. Shifts in which revenues grow fastest, in which industries dominate stock markets, in which professions carry prestige, or in which legal forms proliferate do not meet the standard, however dramatic they feel to live through. The standard is deliberately stern. Its sternness is what gives the concept its analytical bite, since a category that changes identity whenever distribution shifts would announce a new mode every generation and explain nothing about any of them.

### What test decides whether a mode of production has changed?

the decisive test asks whether the dominant relations of production have been replaced: how labour is engaged, how the surplus is extracted, and what compulsion reproduces the system. Altered income shares, larger asset values, or new industries fail that test by themselves, because each can unfold inside unchanged production relations.

The criterion also clarifies what kind of evidence could ever satisfy it. Evidence about income shares, however striking, speaks to distribution. Evidence about production relations speaks to the mode: who hires whom, under what contracts, producing commodities for which markets, subject to what discipline when revenues fall short. A claimant arguing that the mode has changed therefore owes a particular kind of showing, namely that an expanding mass of activity has escaped the familiar pattern of goods made for sale by hired hands altogether, proceeding instead under a different relation that has grown dominant rather than remaining a fringe. Marginal exceptions have always existed, in every mode, without disturbing the classification. Peasant plots persisted deep into industrial capitalism; household production persists still; neither fact reclassifies the system, because classification follows the dominant relation that organises the social product as a whole and subordinates the exceptions to its logic.

There is a subtler point about levels of abstraction that strong versions of the debate sometimes blur. The mode of production is the most abstract characterisation of a social order, and between it and the texture of daily economic life lie several intermediate levels: the phase or stage a mode passes through, the institutional configuration a phase adopts in a given place, the policy regime layered over that configuration. Change at each lower level is real change, worthy of study and often of political contest. The error lies in promoting change at a lower level to the highest level without the evidence the promotion requires. A phase in which asset ownership dominates income distribution is a perfectly coherent object of analysis. Calling it a new mode adds drama and subtracts precision, and the subtraction is not innocent, because strategies addressed to a new mode will neglect the wage relation, the production process, and the competitive compulsions that continue, on the phase reading, to govern outcomes underneath the distributional surface.

The full statement of the category, with its history in the tradition, belongs to the companion piece on the [mode of production explained](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/), and readers who want the underlying architecture of value and price will find it in the treatment of [marx on money and the value form](/2010/08/15/marx-on-money-and-the-value-form/). This article borrows both results without re-deriving them, in order to spend its length where its own contribution lies: on the confrontation between the three positions, the internal objection that disciplines the strongest of them, and the honest reckoning with the framework's least developed category. With the criterion fixed and the rent forms distinguished, the positions can now be stated, each in the voice of its most serious proponents, before any of them is judged.

## The strong position: a new feudalism of access

The first position holds that the change is categorical. Its most widely read statement is Yanis Varoufakis, Technofeudalism: What Killed Capitalism (2023), which argues that owners of the infrastructures through which exchange, search, and social life now pass no longer compete to sell commodities in markets they must win, but instead levy charges on activity occurring inside enclosures they govern. Cédric Durand, Techno-féodalisme: Critique de l'économie numérique (2020), reaches a kindred conclusion from a different route, emphasising the capture of territory-like control over digital space and the extraction of a rent grounded in that control. In both accounts the analogy with feudalism is not decorative. It is the thesis: a order of lords and dependents, organised around access to a controlled domain, has displaced an order of markets and competitors.

Given in its own terms, the position has genuine force, and this article will not pretend otherwise. Its proponents observe that the commanding firms of the age do not primarily distinguish themselves by producing goods more cheaply than rivals. They distinguish themselves by owning the junction points: the marketplace through which other sellers must pass, the standard others must meet, the right others must license, the audience others must rent. A producer who once needed a factory and a sales force may now need, above all, permission, and permission arrives with a percentage attached. On this telling, other capitalists have been demoted to something like vassals, paying tribute for reach, while the owners of the junctions behave less like competitors than like sovereigns of private territories, setting rules, adjudicating disputes, and taxing movement within their borders.

### What does the strong position claim has changed?

the strong position claims the dominant relation itself has changed: owners of controlled infrastructures extract rent from users and from other capitalists, markets give way to governed enclosures, and accumulation through commodity production no longer organises the system, which therefore deserves a new name rather than a capitalist one.

The position also draws strength from a real feature of the landscape that purely celebratory accounts of markets prefer to ignore: exit is often fictitious. Where a junction concentrates an entire field of buyers, or an entire stock of accumulated reputation, or an entire archive of work, the formal freedom to leave conceals a practical compulsion to stay and pay. Proponents compare that compulsion to the dependence of tenants on a lord's land, and the comparison lands emotionally because dependence is genuinely felt by sellers, creators, and firms whose livelihoods hang on decisions taken inside a governance structure they cannot appeal. A theory of the present that cannot register that felt dependence will persuade nobody who experiences it, and the strong position's popularity owes much to its willingness to name dependence plainly rather than dissolving it into the language of partnership and opportunity.

There is a further strand in the strong case that deserves separation from the feudal label, because it would survive the label's rejection. The strand concerns the direction of investment and ambition. Where the surest fortunes attach to controlling junctions rather than to improving production, talent and capital flow toward junction capture, toward the acquisition of rights, positions, and gateways whose yield depends on others needing passage. The system's centre of gravity, on this account, shifts from making to owning, from the workshop to the tollbooth, and the shift deforms everything downstream: innovation bends toward enclosure, competition bends toward acquisition of chokepoints, and politics bends toward the defence of the legal architecture that keeps tolls collectable. Stated that way, the strong position is a claim about incentives and gravity, and much of it can be granted by readers who will ultimately reject its headline.

What the headline adds, beyond the gravity claim, is a judgement about kind. Varoufakis writes as though profit itself has been dethroned, as though the engine Marx described, capital advancing through production to emerge enlarged, has stalled and been replaced by a rent engine answerable to different laws. Durand similarly treats the capture of digital territory as a mutation in the social order rather than a redistribution within one. That judgement of kind is where the debate properly joins, because everything else, the dependence, the tolls, the gravity toward ownership, can be and is acknowledged across all three positions. The disagreement is not over whether tolls exist. The disagreement is over what tolls mean for the identity of the system that contains them, and meaning here is not a matter of taste. It is settled, if it can be settled at all, by asking where the surplus that pays the tolls is produced, and whether the toll-takers could persist for a single period if production under the old relations stopped feeding them.

## The second position: capitalism functioning as capitalism does

The second position answers that nothing in the toll economy surprises the framework, because monopoly rent is an ordinary outcome of capitalist competition rather than its negation. Competition does not tend toward a serene equality of producers. It tends toward concentration, toward the erection of barriers, toward the conversion of temporary advantages into durable property. A firm that wins an advantage seeks to make the advantage permanent, through law, through standards, through control of complements, through sheer scale, and the income of a permanent advantage is rent. On this reading the tollbooth is not a foreign body inside capitalism. It is one of capitalism's recurring products, regenerated in each era from materials the era provides, land and minerals in one period, rights and junctions in another.

David Harvey, The Limits to Capital (1982), supplies much of the theoretical spine for this reading, since that work treats rent and the built environment as internal moments of accumulation rather than as survivals from a prior order, showing how landed property and monopoly claims are continuously remade by capital itself. The broader classical tradition behind the second position adds a historical observation: ownership of scarce conditions has yielded rent in every phase of the system's development, through chartered monopolies, through urban land, through mineral rights, through spectrum and franchise, and nobody concluded from those yields that the mode had changed, because the wage relation and the compulsion to accumulate continued to organise production beneath them. The feudal analogy, on this reading, obscures more than it reveals, and its central obscurity concerns coercion. Feudal extraction rested on extra-economic coercion, on the direct political and legal subordination of producers who were tied to the land and owed dues by customary and enforceable obligation. The dependent seller inside a digital enclosure is exploited, squeezed, and sometimes ruined, but the dependence runs through contracts, markets, and property law of a distinctly capitalist kind, and the producer remains formally free, mobile in law, and engaged through wage labour or commodity sale rather than through bondage.

### Why do defenders of the continuity reading reject the feudal label?

because feudal extraction rested on direct coercion and tied producers, while dependence inside controlled marketplaces operates through contract, property, and competition among formally free participants. A label that erases that difference, they argue, mistakes the feeling of dependence for the mechanism producing it, and misdirects both analysis and remedy.

The continuity reading also presses a point about the toll-takers themselves that the strong position tends to soften. The owners of junctions behave, in their internal organisation, exactly like capitals. They employ wage labour on a vast scale. They invest, they compete ferociously for position, they fear displacement, they accumulate. Their revenue may arrive substantially as rent drawn from other capitals and from users, yet their own operations remain capitalist enterprises subject to capitalist discipline, and their valuations rise and fall with expectations about future surplus in ways no feudal lord's demesne ever did. A lord did not face a quarterly reckoning against rival lords for the allegiance of mobile tenants in a unified market. The junction owner does. That exposure to competition, however imperfect and however rigged, belongs to the anatomy of capital, not to the anatomy of feudal tenure.

There is, finally, a methodological caution embedded in the second position that this article endorses independently of its verdict. Every generation of capitalist development has produced announcements that the system has ceased to be itself: the age of trusts, the age of managerial corporations, the age of finance, each in turn was declared a successor order by serious observers. Some of those announcements identified real phase changes and mislabelled them as mode changes. The recurrence of the error is itself evidence, not that change never happens, but that the threshold for declaring a mode change should be set high and guarded by a criterion fixed in advance. The criterion fixed earlier in this article, replacement of the dominant production relations, is the guard the second position invokes, and under it the toll economy, for all its genuine novelty of form, presents as a redistribution of claims within relations that persist.

That said, the continuity reading has a weakness its holders should admit, and the middle position exists because of it. Saying that rent is an ordinary product of competition can slide into saying that nothing much has happened, that the pattern of incomes is a detail beneath theoretical notice. Yet the pattern of incomes shapes politics, shapes the lived experience of dependence, shapes which fractions of the owning classes hold power over the rest. An analysis that wins the argument about the mode and loses interest in distribution has answered the largest question and abandoned the most consequential ones. The third position begins exactly there, in the space the second position's complacency leaves open.

## The middle position: a rentier phase within an unchanged mode

The third position holds that a distinctive phase has emerged in which asset ownership dominates the pattern of incomes without the mode of production changing underneath it. Its fullest statement is Brett Christophers, Rentier Capitalism: Who Owns the Economy, and Who Pays for It? (2020), which catalogues the domains in which income increasingly rewards the ownership of scarce assets, land, resources, rights, infrastructures, and positions, rather than the organisation of production. Mariana Mazzucato, The Value of Everything: Making and Taking in the Global Economy (2018), supplies a complementary strand, arguing that the boundary between value creation and value extraction has been blurred in public language until extraction routinely masquerades as creation, with consequences for policy and for the legitimacy of large incomes. Neither writer concludes that capitalism has been superseded. Both conclude that its distributional centre has shifted toward owners, and that the shift is the politically decisive fact of the period.

The middle position is sometimes misread as a compromise, a splitting of the difference between drama and denial. It is not a compromise. It is a distinct claim with its own object: distribution rather than production relations, phase rather than mode. Where the strong position says the system's identity has changed, and the second position says its behaviour remains familiar, the middle position says the identity persists while the weight of its parts has been rearranged, and that the rearrangement, not the identity, is what demands explanation and response. That is a stronger and more precise assertion than either neighbour in one respect: it specifies the level at which change is claimed, and therefore specifies what evidence bears on it, namely evidence about the composition of incomes, the legal construction of asset scarcity, and the political defence of ownership claims.

### What exactly does the middle position assert?

the middle position asserts that asset ownership has come to dominate income distribution, so rents claim a growing share of the social surplus, while wage labour, commodity production, and competitive accumulation continue to organise production itself. Change is located in the phase and in distribution, deliberately not in the mode of production.

Consider what the middle reading explains well. It explains why the language of rent feels apt to so many observers without requiring the feudal machinery to fit. It explains why conflicts over housing, over rights, over access, and over the pricing of necessities carry such political charge: those are the points where households meet the rent claim directly, as a deduction from wages for permission to live, work, and participate. It explains why productive investment can appear sluggish beside asset appreciation, since a system rewarding ownership over organisation will rationally starve organisation. And it explains why the owners of assets form a recognisable political constituency with shared interests across otherwise unrelated industries, because the rentier relation, unlike the productive one, is the same relation in every domain: own the condition, charge for access, defend the scarcity.

The middle position also carries a distinctive implication for remedy that separates it from both neighbours. If a new mode had arrived, remedies addressed to capitalist relations would be obsolete. If nothing had changed, no remedy beyond the traditional repertoire would be needed. If instead a phase has tilted distribution toward owners through identifiable legal and institutional constructions, then those constructions become the terrain of contest: the scope and duration of exclusive rights, the treatment of land and location value, the governance of junctions, the public provision of alternatives that break scarcity. The phase reading thus converts an abstract debate about the system's name into a concrete agenda about its rules, which is one reason it has attracted policy audiences that the other two positions, for opposite reasons, leave cold.

Its vulnerability lies at the boundary it draws. A phase is defined against a mode, and the middle position owes an account of how far distributional change could proceed before the quantitative shift would become qualitative, before a system in which owners of conditions dominate so completely that production itself is reorganised around their requirements would deserve a new classification. Christophers and the writers near him do not always supply that threshold, and critics from the strong camp exploit the gap, asking how much rent dominance the phase reading can absorb before it becomes a polite name for the transformation it denies. The question is fair, and this article's verdict section answers it with the defeater the brief for the debate requires: the boundary gives way once toll revenue is demonstrated to flow, on a scale at once substantial and still expanding, from pursuits conducted with no hired hands and no goods made for sale anywhere in sight. Until that showing is made, the phase reading holds, and holds more comfortably than its rivals.

## The three positions grid

The three positions grid is the findable artifact of this article: the debate's rival claims set out in rows, against columns that interrogate every claim in turn: what precisely is alleged to have altered, where does the money ultimately begin, what proof would the allegation accept, and where does the allegation hurt? A grid cannot settle an argument, but it can prevent an argument from being won by atmosphere, since a position that looks commanding in prose sometimes reveals, in tabular form, that its evidence column and its claim column do not match. Readers preparing for examinations, and readers preparing to argue in any forum, will get more from the grid by completing its cells from memory than by admiring it completed.

Reading the grid vertically teaches as much as reading it across. The first column shows that only one position claims a change of kind; the other two divide over significance, not identity. The second column shows the real fault line of the whole debate, since every disagreement about names reduces, under pressure, to a disagreement about where surplus originates and whether access income can be primary rather than derivative. The third column shows an asymmetry that partisans of the strong position rarely confront: the proof their claim requires is of a specific and demanding kind, proof concerning how making itself is organised, while the proof usually offered concerns the size of incomes, which belongs to a different column of a different argument. The fourth column, finally, is the grid's act of fairness. Each position carries a difficulty, including the one this article defends, and a reader who cannot state the difficulty of a favoured position does not yet hold that position as an argument, only as an allegiance.

### How should the grid be used in an examination answer?

use the grid to allocate the argument before writing: one movement stating each position and its claim about kind, one movement testing the surplus-origin column against the framework, and one movement concluding with the threshold evidence that would change the verdict. Answers structured that way display judgement rather than summary, which examiners reward.

The grid also exposes a quiet agreement among the three positions that the shouting of the debate tends to hide. All three accept that access income has grown in weight. All three accept that control over junctions, rights, and scarce conditions confers power disproportionate to any productive contribution. All three accept that the legal construction of scarcity is politically contestable. They differ over classification and over origin, and those differences matter, but the shared ground matters too, because it identifies the terrain on which practical argument can proceed without waiting for the theoretical dispute to close. A housing campaign, a rights reform, a governance rule for junctions, none of these needs the feudal question settled before it can be argued on the merits. Theory clarifies stakes. It should not be used as a waiting room where action sits until philosophers agree.

## Rent presupposes production: the decisive point

The argument on which this article stakes its verdict can be stated in a single sentence, and the rest of the section exists to show why that sentence carries so much weight. Take the supersession camp's own borrowed theory seriously and its thesis dissolves: a toll, on that theory, is a portion of value that workers elsewhere have already brought into being, so a world where tolls had wholly ousted profit would be a world with nothing left to divide, and the claim eats itself. The objection is internal. It does not import standards from a rival theory, does not dispute the phenomena the strong position describes, and does not depend on any measurement of income shares. It simply follows the strong position's own category back to its definition and asks what reality would resemble had the category swallowed the system whole. The answer is a world that cannot reproduce itself for a single period.

Unfold the logic slowly. Surplus value, on the framework's account, originates in production, where labour employed by capital creates more value than the value of its own labour power. That surplus is then contested and divided: industrial and commercial profit, interest, taxes, and rent are all, in the final analysis, portions of it or claims against it. Rent's portion is secured by ownership of a monopolised condition. Now imagine the rent portion growing, decade by decade, exactly as the debate's participants describe. Nothing in the framework forbids the growth; the framework predicts contests over distribution and expects their outcomes to shift with law, scarcity, and organisation. But imagine the growth completed, rent triumphant, profit extinguished as the organising income of production. Who, in that completed world, sets production in motion, and from what motive, and with what funds? The rent recipients receive a surplus they do not produce. The producers, ex hypothesi, no longer produce a surplus appropriable as profit. The flow that rent distributes has its spring in the very relation the thesis declares superseded. A distribution form cannot outlive, let alone replace, the production relation that feeds it.

### What makes replacement of profit by rent a contradiction?

because replacement would sever rent from its only source. Rent distributes surplus value created in production and first appropriated through the profit relation, so a completed triumph of rent would leave production unrewarded, halt the generation of new surplus, and strand rent itself as a claim against an empty fund.

The point generalises into a test that can be applied to any candidate successor income form, and applying it is a habit worth acquiring beyond this debate. Whenever a writer announces that some revenue, rent, interest, fees, data income, attention income, has become the new engine of the system, the framework-trained reader asks a single question: is that revenue a primary appropriation of newly produced surplus, or a secondary claim transferred out of surplus produced under the wage relation elsewhere? If the answer is secondary, the announcement confuses the loudest claimant with the source, rather as one might confuse the distribution of a harvest among landlord, tithe, and tax with the growing of the grain. The confusion is rhetorically powerful because claimants are visible, concentrated, and wealthy, while production is dispersed, ordinary, and easy to overlook. Whole industries of commentary live inside that visibility gap.

A possible rejoinder deserves its moment. A defender of the strong position might reply that the framework's premise is precisely what is being abandoned: perhaps access income is not a claim on surplus produced elsewhere, because the activity inside enclosures, the posting, the searching, the transacting, the socialising, itself produces the value that access charges capture. That rejoinder at least engages the argument at the right joint, since it contests the origin of the surplus rather than its distribution. But notice what the rejoinder concedes by its shape. It abandons the rent category in the act of defending the rent thesis, because income drawn from value produced by the very activity being charged for is not rent in the framework's sense at all; it would need analysis as a form of surplus extraction within a production relation, which is to say, within something recognisably continuous with capitalism's own logic of employing, organising, and appropriating. The rejoinder thus faces a fork: either access income is rent, in which case it presupposes production elsewhere and cannot found a new mode, or it is a direct extraction from value-producing activity, in which case the feudal vocabulary is wrong and the debate becomes a familiar one about new forms of the wage relation and of surplus appropriation. Both branches return the argument to capitalism's terrain.

There is a second rejoinder, quieter and more empirical, which holds that the incoherence proof shows only that a pure rentier economy is impossible, while the strong position needs only a predominantly rentier one. The distinction is real and the concession should be made cleanly: the decisive point refutes the completed form of the thesis, the claim that profit has been replaced and the mode superseded. It does not refute, and this article does not pretend it refutes, claims about predominance in distribution, which belong to the middle position's terrain and are assessed there on evidence. What the incoherence proof accomplishes is a discipline on language with consequences for thought. Predominance of a claim is not replacement of a source. A phase dominated by rentiers remains a phase of the mode whose production feeds them. Writers who use replacement language for predominance facts borrow the prestige of a mode-change thesis while supplying only phase-change evidence, and the borrowing should be called in whenever it occurs, in this debate and in the neighbouring ones to which the argument now turns.

## The same point applied: access platforms and the value dispute

The dispute over large access-governing infrastructures, marketplaces, audiences, and junctions through which producers must pass, has generated its own literature about where value comes from in such arrangements, and the rent presupposes production claim reorganises that literature with unusual economy. One strand of writing treats the junction owner's percentage as a tax on other capitals, a deduction from profits earned by sellers who did the producing and the selling, transferred upward because the junction controls the meeting place. Another strand treats the users of social and search infrastructures as themselves producers, whose activity generates the material that access charges and advertising revenues monetise. A third strand treats the junction as a productive enterprise in its own right, employing engineers and organisers whose labour builds and maintains a genuine service. The framework does not force a choice among these strands by slogan. It forces an accounting: for each revenue stream, identify whether it is produced surplus, transferred surplus, or rent on a monopolised condition, and expect most real cases to blend all three in proportions that argument, not labelling, must establish.

The fuller anatomy of the platform form, its labour processes and its business models, is the subject of the companion article on platform capitalism in this series, and the present discussion deliberately borrows its results rather than rebuilding them. What matters here is narrower: the classification of the junction percentage. Where a marketplace charges sellers for access to a concentrated body of buyers, the charge behaves analytically as monopoly rent, payment for a condition, the assembled audience and the trust apparatus around it, that sellers cannot reproduce for themselves at comparable cost. Where the same enterprise sells logistics, computation, or payment services, it sells commodities produced by its own wage labour, and the revenue is profit of enterprise in the ordinary sense. Where it monetises the attention or the traces of its users, difficult questions about production arise that the tradition is still digesting. An analysis that calls the whole revenue rent, or the whole revenue profit, has declined to do the accounting, and the decline is usually motivated: the rent label serves a mode-change narrative, the profit label serves a nothing-new narrative, and the blended reality serves neither.

### Are junction owners landlords in the analytical sense?

in one precise respect, yes: where their income is payment for access to a condition they monopolise, an assembled audience, a standard, or a gateway, the income is rent by the framework's relational test. In other respects, no, because the same owners also organise production with wage labour like any capital, which no landlord qua landlord does.

Applying the decisive point here yields a verdict that will disappoint partisans of both loud camps. Against the mode-change camp: the junction percentage, insofar as it is rent, presupposes sellers producing commodities, advertisers earning profits, and users embedded in a wage economy whose incomes fund the whole circuit, so the junction's dominance evidences a redistribution of surplus toward condition-owners, exactly the middle position's claim. Against the nothing-new camp: the scale on which a single condition, access to the assembled market itself, has been enclosed and charged for is a genuine development in the architecture of distribution, and the dependence it creates for smaller capitals is a real shift in the balance of power among capitals, not a detail. The framework, handled carefully, thus refuses both the funeral oration for capitalism and the assurance that the present is merely the past with faster machines. It identifies a phase in which the gate has become more valuable than many of the goods passing through it, while insisting that gates are fed by goods.

## The same point applied again: financial expansion and rent-like income

A parallel dispute surrounds the growth of finance, where incomes from lending, trading, fees, and asset appreciation are often described either as a new mode of extraction that has emancipated itself from production, or as a froth that sober analysis should ignore in favour of the real economy underneath. The rent presupposes production claim cuts into this dispute at the same joint. Interest, in the framework, is a portion of surplus value transferred to the lender, a division of the gross profit between the functioning capital that organises production and the money capital that advances funds. Fees for financial services divide similarly between payment for labour actually performed and rent-like charges for occupying a licensed or positional gateway. Appreciation of assets, where it is not matched by growth in the surplus those assets claim, is a repricing of claims, a redistribution among claimants and across time, which can enrich its holders enormously while adding nothing to the social product. The dedicated treatment of these mechanisms lives in the series article devoted to financialisation and Marxist theory; the organising point here is that every one of these income forms, traced back, terminates in value created by hired hands, or in a quarrel over how tomorrow's such value will be shared.

That tracing explains a pattern that purely financial narratives struggle with: the periodic violent reconciliation of claims with the surplus available to honour them. When claims on future income multiply beyond what production can service, the excess does not get quietly validated by ingenuity. It gets destroyed, in defaults, collapses, and forced revaluations, and the destruction falls unevenly on holders according to their distance from the spring of surplus. A mode of production genuinely superseded by rent and finance would not exhibit that subordination; its claims would be self-validating, drawn on access and circulation themselves. The recurring subordination of financial claims to productive reality is therefore not a footnote to the rent debate. It is among the strongest standing evidence for this article's verdict, because it shows the hierarchy of the system asserting itself under stress: distribution forms flourish in the upswing and are audited by production in the downswing, and an audit implies an auditor with priority.

### Does financial income escape the rent presupposes production claim?

no. Interest divides surplus produced by functioning capital, fees split between payment for services and charges for positional gateways, and asset appreciation reprices claims on future surplus. Each form, traced through its chain of transfers, terminates in production under the wage relation, which is why crises periodically force claims back into line with it.

There is a temptation, having wielded the organising point twice, to wield it everywhere and call the work done. Resist it. The claim disciplines arguments about the system's identity; it does not measure the weight of rent in any period, explain why particular scarcities were legally constructed, or judge the political salience of distributional change. Those tasks require the empirical and institutional work surveyed in the middle position's literature and in the distributional evidence gathered at [labour share evidence](/2021/03/01/labour-share-evidence/). A reader who leaves this article wielding one sentence against every phenomenon has acquired a slogan where an argument was offered. The sentence earns its keep only when it is used to sort claims, primary from derivative, mode from phase, origin from distribution, and then set down while the sorted material is examined on its own terms.

## The feudal analogy examined dimension by dimension

Analogies in social theory persuade by selecting one resemblance and letting atmosphere supply the rest, so the remedy is to force the comparison onto stated dimensions and score it on each. The brief for this debate names the dimensions on which feudal extraction and capitalist rent must be compared: whether extraction rests on coercion, whether producers are tied, and whether accumulation drives the system. Take them in turn, and state the feudal term of the comparison honestly, since feudalism itself was a varied order across places and centuries, and the analogy deserves the strongest version of its own reference point. Under feudal relations in their classical form, the direct producers held customary access to their means of subsistence, above all land, and the surplus was extracted from them by lords wielding political, legal, and military power in their own persons. Extraction was therefore extra-economic in the precise sense that it did not occur through a market transaction between equals in law; it occurred through obligation backed by force, owed by persons whose status bound them to place and lord.

On the coercion dimension, the analogy fails. The dependent producer inside a capitalist enclosure, however squeezed, is not bound by personal status, owes no customary dues, and confronts the owner of the condition through contract and market rather than through jurisdictional lordship. The coercion present is the impersonal coercion of needing a livelihood within a property order, which is capitalism's own characteristic compulsion, analytically distinct from feudal bondage and shared by every wage worker who never touches a junction or pays an access charge. Collapsing the two coercions into one word erases the distinction the founding tradition laboured to draw between modes, the distinction on which the entire concept of a mode of production rests. An analogy that requires erasing its own framework's founding distinction is not an insight within that framework. It is a departure from it wearing its vocabulary.

### On which dimensions does the feudal analogy hold?

on none of the three dimensions the comparison itself specifies. Extraction under access capitalism rests on market and property compulsion rather than direct coercion, producers remain legally mobile rather than tied, and accumulation through reinvestment drives owners and producers alike, whereas feudal extraction served consumption and status within a comparatively static order.

On the tied-producer dimension, the analogy fails again, though the failure needs care because practical immobility is real. Producers accumulate reputations, archives, customer relations, and skills specific to a junction, and walking away can mean abandoning the capital of a working life. That is a serious dependence and the middle position is right to centre it. But dependence through accumulated investment differs in kind from bondage through status. The tied producer's immobility is economic and revisable, sometimes at grievous cost; the bound tenant's immobility was legal and hereditary, enforced as a matter of personal condition. Moreover the junction owner actively courts mobility in one direction, competing to attract producers from rival junctions, a behaviour without feudal analogue, since lords did not bid against one another in an open market for tenants free in law to choose. The dimension therefore scores against the analogy even while the phenomenon that suggested it, costly exit, is fully acknowledged.

On the accumulation dimension, the failure is clearest of all. Feudal extraction, for all its local variation, characteristically funded consumption, display, warfare, and the reproduction of status, within an order whose productive forces developed slowly and whose lords faced no systematic compulsion to revolutionise production on pain of extinction. Access owners face exactly that compulsion. They reinvest at extraordinary rates, they fear technological displacement, they are disciplined by rivals and by capital markets, and their rents are perpetually threatened by the same innovative ferment that created their positions. A class compelled to accumulate or perish is a capitalist class whatever the form of its revenue, and a system whose commanding heights obey the accumulation compulsion is capitalism whatever metaphors its surface suggests. Measured along the very axes the comparison itself selects, likeness gives way to opposition at every step, which is the most complete defeat an analogy can suffer: not exaggeration, not discourtesy, but inversion on each point where resemblance was advertised.

What survives the analogy's collapse is the experience that motivated it, and intellectual honesty requires naming the survival plainly. Dependence on owners of conditions, the payment of tribute-like shares for access, the sense of working land one will never own while another party grows rich on the gate: these experiences are real, they are politically potent, and they genuinely resemble the emotional texture of tenancy more than the emotional texture of market exchange among equals. A Marxism that cannot explain why the feudal metaphor feels true will lose every audience that feels it. The explanation is available inside the framework: rent has always been the form of capitalist income that most resembles pre-capitalist extraction, because it is payment to mere ownership, unaccompanied by any organising function, and as rent's weight grows, the system's surface legitimately acquires an archaic colouring. The colouring is a fact about distribution. The metaphor errs only when it promotes colouring into constitution.

## Monopoly rent: the least developed category in the framework

Every honest treatment of this debate must pause over an embarrassment. The category that the present argument most needs, monopoly rent, is the category the founding texts developed least, and the imbalance is not marginal. Differential rent receives a long, systematic exposition in the third volume, with forms distinguished, mechanisms traced, and objections entertained. Absolute rent receives a contentious but extended defence. Monopoly rent appears briefly, almost in passing, illustrated by the exceptional vineyard whose wine commands a price set by the wealth and desire of its purchasers, and then the text moves on. The founder cannot be blamed for failing to anticipate the weight later placed on the category; the cases that now crowd under it, exclusive rights over inventions and expression, control of gateways and standards, positional advantages in networks of users, belong to configurations of property that his period knew only in embryo. But the consequence for the tradition is real: at the exact point where the modern debate needs its sharpest instrument, the inheritance is a sketch.

Saying so matters for more than scholarly candour. A tradition that presents its rent theory as complete will apply the sketch with false confidence, forcing cases into a category whose boundaries were never drawn, and will mistake the forcing for analysis. A tradition that admits the sketch must do constructive work: it must ask what monopoly rent would have to be, given the architecture of the whole, if the category is to bear the load now assigned to it. That constructive work has occupied serious writers, and its outlines can be stated. Monopoly rent, developed properly, would name income arising where a unique or legally exclusive condition permits its owner to sustain a price above the value that differential and absolute mechanisms would explain, the excess being a transfer of surplus value from purchasers, from other capitals, or from the revenues of users, secured by the exclusivity itself rather than by any productivity of the condition. The vineyard generalises: wherever desire or necessity meets a barrier that only ownership can lift, and the barrier cannot be circumvented by producing an equivalent condition, a monopoly rent can form.

### Why is the thinness of monopoly rent a problem for the tradition?

because the cases the category must now cover, exclusive rights, gateways, standards, and positional advantages, dominate the modern rent debate, while the founding exposition is brief and unsystematic. Applying an underdeveloped category as though it were finished produces confident classifications that the underlying theory never actually earned or tested.

The underdevelopment cuts in two directions, and fairness requires registering both. Against complacent defenders of the framework, it means that easy assurances about rent's ordinariness rest partly on a category nobody has fully built; the second position in this debate borrows strength from a theory whose relevant chapter is a fragment. Against the strong position, however, the thinness is equally awkward, because the claim that a rent order has superseded capitalism leans on the same category, generalised far beyond its textual warrant, and a supersession thesis built on the framework's weakest plank inherits the weakness. Both camps, in other words, have an interest in the constructive work neither has completed, and the debate would improve if each admitted that its favourite conclusion now outruns the category meant to support it. The middle position suffers least from the gap, since its claims are distributional and can be investigated empirically while the theory is repaired, one more reason this article's sympathies settle where they do.

There is a methodological lesson here that extends well beyond rent. Founding texts in any tradition are uneven: some categories are machined to fine tolerances, others are left as rough castings, and the distribution of finish rarely matches the distribution of later need. Mature use of a tradition consists partly in knowing its internal map of finish, in being able to say which tools are calibrated and which are approximate, and in discounting conclusions drawn with approximate tools accordingly. Immature use treats the corpus as uniformly authoritative, quoting the sketch and the system in the same tone of certainty. The rent debate, conducted at its usual volume, is full of that immaturity on all sides. This article's insistence on the underdeveloped category is therefore not a decorative confession. It is a calibration instruction: hold the verdict about the mode firmly, because it rests on the framework's most developed architecture, the account of surplus value and its distribution; hold particular classifications of particular income streams more loosely, because they rest on the sketch.

### What would a developed theory of monopoly rent need to explain?

a developed account would need to specify how exclusivity sustains prices above value, whose surplus the excess transfers, how competition among owners of rival conditions limits the transfer, and when an advantage decays into an ordinary differential return. Those four questions mark the distance between the founding sketch and a working theory.

Consider, as a discipline, how the four questions discipline a single familiar type of case without naming any holder or asset. An exclusive right over a technique permits its owner to license the technique to producers. The price sustained above value appears as a royalty stream exceeding any return the right's creation cost would justify. Whose surplus pays? Partly the licensees' profits, partly, where licensees can pass costs forward, the revenues of purchasers, which trace back in turn to wages and profits across the economy. What limits the transfer? Rival techniques, the expiry and contestability of the right, the possibility of designing around the barrier, and the political vulnerability of exclusivity that comes to be seen as taxing necessities. When does the advantage decay? When the technique diffuses into general practice and the barrier's height falls toward the differential advantage of early mastery. A case worked through those four questions has been analysed. A case merely labelled monopoly rent has been filed. The debate needs fewer filings and more workings, and the admission that the founding text supplies questions rather than a finished apparatus is the beginning of that improvement.

## Intellectual property and the rent form

Few sub-questions in the debate attract as much heat as whether intellectual property is a form of rent, and the heat usually comes from arguing about the label before fixing the test. Apply the relational test developed in this article. An inflow counts as rent, under the test, when title to some fenced scarcity is its whole warrant and the money itself was created by hands other than the recipient's. An exclusive right over an invention, a text, a design, or a mark is a legally constructed monopolised condition: the underlying knowledge, once created, could be used by everyone without being used up, and it is the law that manufactures the scarcity from which the income flows. Royalties and licence fees paid for permission to use what could otherwise be freely copied fit the rent form closely, indeed almost purely, since the barrier lifted by payment is entirely artificial and the owner's ongoing contribution, qua owner, is nil. By that test, a large and growing family of incomes in knowledge-intensive production qualifies as rent under the strictest reading available, and the phase camp's emphasis on legally constructed scarcity finds here its clearest illustration.

Qualifications keep the classification honest. The creation of the knowledge itself is production, often highly skilled production, and the wages and profits earned in creating it are not rent. Enterprises typically bundle the two: they employ creators whose labour produces new knowledge, and they hold rights whose ownership yields rent, and their accounts merge the streams into a single margin that no label applied to the whole firm can describe. There are also defensive and strategic uses of exclusive rights, held to protect a productive operation rather than to levy tolls on others, where the rent element, while present in the right's exclusionary power, is not the point of the holding. And the social bargain embedded in such rights, a temporary monopoly granted to elicit disclosure and investment, means the rent is, by design, a policy instrument, created by statute and alterable by statute, which distinguishes it sharply from the natural scarcity underlying differential rent on land. That distinction is politically momentous: what statute constructs, statute can reconstruct, and the debate over the proper scope of exclusive rights is a debate over how much rent society chooses to manufacture.

### Is every income connected with knowledge a rent?

no. Creating knowledge is production, and the earnings of that labour and of the capital organising it are wages and profit. Rent enters with the exclusive right itself, when ownership of a legally scarce permission yields income drawn from surplus produced by those who pay for access to the knowledge.

The knowledge case also sharpens the decisive point rather than escaping it. Suppose the rent interpretation is granted in full: licence income is rent, drawn from the surplus of licensees and purchasers. Then the entire glittering edifice of rights income presupposes an economy of producers employing wage labour and generating the surplus from which licences are paid, which is precisely the structure the mode-change thesis declares superseded. The more completely the rent reading wins at the level of the individual stream, the more completely it loses at the level of the system, because each stream it captures is a confession of dependence on production elsewhere. This recursive quality, where the strong position's local victories assemble into its global defeat, is the signature of the internal-incoherence pattern this article instantiates, and noticing it converts the debate from a contest of examples, which the strong position can always prolong, into an examination of structure, which it cannot prolong indefinitely.

Housing offers a second clarifying case, approached here by type rather than by any named market or measure. Where dwellings in a constrained location are let by owners who did not produce the location's desirability, the payment divides into a return on the building, which depreciates and must be maintained like any capital, and a rent on the location itself, which the surrounding society collectively produced and the owner privately appropriates. The persistence of that second element across every phase of capitalist development is one of the continuities the second position rightly stresses. Its growth in weight, as location-bound scarcity tightens around employment centres, is one of the distributional facts the middle position rightly stresses. Both readings coexist because they answer different questions, and the framework, unlike the shouting debate, has room for both answers at once, provided each keeps to its own level: continuity of form, change of weight.

## Evidence, direction, and the defeater stated plainly

A debate conducted under an extreme decay risk, where any appeal to passing magnitudes would date and rot the analysis, must be careful about what kind of evidence it invokes. This article therefore works with direction and duration rather than with endpoints and snapshots. The direction of the rent share over the span of decades is upward in the judgement of all three positions, a rare unanimity that can be taken as the debate's shared factual floor. The duration matters because a movement sustained across decades, through varied policy regimes and varied technological waves, cannot be dismissed as a conjunctural blip, while a movement visible only in a single episode would deserve exactly that dismissal. The distributional evidence assembled in the companion study of labour's share of income bears directly on this floor, since a rising claim by owners of conditions appears, from the other side of the ledger, in the pressure on the share of income reaching labour and in the squeeze on productive margins that neither labour nor productive capital captures. Readers who want the measurement controversies in full should go there; the present argument needs only the directional floor, and it is a strength of the argument, not an evasion, that it needs so little. A verdict resting on the framework's architecture and on directions all sides concede is harder to overturn than one resting on a contested snapshot.

Against that floor, the verdict of this article can be stated with its defeater attached, in the form the series requires. The judgement runs as follows. Hired labour still staffs the making of the social product; the surplus thrown up by that making still feeds every claimant, toll keeper and organising capital alike; and the toll's enlargement is therefore a redistribution inside a capitalist phase, captured best by the middle account and kept honest by the continuity camp's refusal to rename the system. The defeater is equally plain. That judgement would be defeated by evidence that a substantial and growing share of total income derives from access charges levied on activity that is not itself organised as commodity production with wage labour, since that is the empirical form the strong claim would have to take if it were true. Note what the defeater demands: not larger rents, not richer rentiers, not more pervasive access charges on production that remains capitalist underneath, but access income arising from activity outside the wage-labour commodity form altogether, at a scale capable of bearing the word dominant. Nothing in the debate's literature, on this article's reading, yet makes that showing, and the burden of making it rests squarely on the position that asserts a change of kind.

### What evidence would settle the rent debate?

evidence about the organisation of production rather than about the size of incomes: a demonstration that access charges increasingly fall on activity not organised as commodity production staffed by wage labour. Income shares alone cannot settle the question, because every position in the debate already agrees that rent's share has risen.

The defeater also protects the verdict from the charge of unfalsifiability that defenders of the strong position sometimes level at continuity arguments. A continuity claim that no evidence could disturb would indeed be dogma. This one names its disturbing evidence precisely, and in doing so reveals the shape of a research programme: trace the largest access-income streams to the activities on which they are levied, classify those activities by their production relations, and measure the drift of the classification over time. Should that programme one day return the strong position's answer, this article's verdict falls, and falls by its own stated standard, which is how arguments in a serious tradition are supposed to behave. Until then, the programme's preliminary indications run the other way: the great access charges of the age fall overwhelmingly on sellers, producers, advertisers, and users whose incomes arise inside the wage economy, which is dependence documented, not supersession.

There is a companion discipline for readers assessing new contributions to the debate as they appear. Ask of each new claim three sorting questions. Does it concern the origin of surplus or only its distribution? Does it describe activity inside the commodity and wage form or outside it? Does its evidence measure incomes, which all positions concede, or relations, which alone can decide between them? Contributions that survive the sorting with relational evidence deserve to move the verdict. Contributions that offer income evidence for relational conclusions, however vivid their examples and however righteous their tone, should be filed under the middle position's growing dossier, where most of them, properly read, actually belong. The debate will continue to generate heat. The sorting questions are offered as a way of extracting light from it at a steady rate.

## Misreadings corrected: five confusions that keep the debate circling

The first misreading treats rent and profit as the same thing, two names for the enrichment of owners. The framework separates them by origin and by function: profit arises where capital organises production and appropriates newly created surplus; rent arises where ownership of a condition levies a share of surplus created elsewhere. The separation is not pedantry, because remedies and predictions differ by category. Profit responds to the conditions of production and competition among producers; rent responds to the construction and defence of scarcity. An analyst who merges them will prescribe competition policy for a scarcity problem and scarcity reform for a competition problem, and will be surprised by failure in both directions.

The second misreading infers the end of capitalism from a rising rent share. The inference skips the criterion: a mode changes when its dominant production relations are replaced, and a share, however large, is a fact about distribution. Shares have swung widely within the mode's history without disturbing its identity, and the burden of showing that a swing has become a replacement lies on whoever asserts the replacement, in the relational currency the defeater specifies.

### Does a rising rent share by itself prove the system has changed?

no. A share is a distributional fact, and distributional facts, however dramatic, leave the identity of the mode untouched until they are joined to evidence that the dominant production relations themselves have been replaced. The inference from share to system skips every step that would make it an argument.

The third misreading supposes the feudal analogy is merely rhetorical, a flourish its proponents would disown if pressed. They would not. Varoufakis and Durand intend the claim literally, as a thesis about the successor order to capitalism, and treating it as metaphor understates both its ambition and its vulnerability. Literal claims can be tested against dimensions, as this article has tested them; metaphors can only be applauded or deplored. Doing the proponents the courtesy of literal reading is what allows the debate to be settled rather than performed.

The fourth misreading credits the founding rent theory with a completeness it does not possess, particularly for monopoly cases. The correction has been made at length above and needs only its consequence drawn here: arguments on all sides that lean on monopoly rent should carry a visible discount, and the constructive development of that category is among the most useful theoretical work the tradition could now undertake, more useful than another round of verdicts pronounced with the unfinished tool.

The fifth misreading casts the middle position as a timid compromise between two braver claims. The middle position is a distinct assertion about levels: distribution has shifted decisively, production relations have not, and the politically operative facts live at the level that shifted. Compromises split differences along a single scale. The middle position refuses the single scale, insisting that the debate's loudest question, what to name the system, is less consequential than its quieter question, which constructions of scarcity to contest. That is not timidity. It is a reordering of priorities that the other positions, each fixated on the name, are free to dispute but not to dismiss as splitting.

## Rentier capitalism as a term: uses and limits

The phrase rentier capitalism, carried into wide circulation by Christophers's work, deserves a section of its own because terms steer thought long after arguments are forgotten. Its strength is descriptive economy: it gathers land, resources, rights, infrastructures, and platforms under one relation, ownership of scarce conditions yielding income, and thereby reveals a constituency and a logic that sector-by-sector analysis misses. A policymaker who sees only separate problems, in housing, in energy, in knowledge rights, in digital gateways, confronts a hydra; the rentier lens shows the shared anatomy, that in each domain a constructed or natural scarcity is owned, access is charged, and the charge is defended politically as the natural reward of ownership. Shared anatomy suggests shared remedies, or at least remedies that can learn from one another across domains, and that suggestion is the term's genuine contribution to practical thought.

Its limit is the mirror of its strength. Gathering unlike scarcities under one relation can flatten differences that matter for both analysis and remedy. Natural scarcity, as with a mineral deposit, cannot be abolished, only its yield socialised or taxed. Constructed scarcity, as with an exclusive right, can be redesigned at its statutory root. Positional scarcity, as with a location or a gateway, sits between, partly natural and partly built. A politics that treats all three identically will misjudge each. The term also risks a moralised slide in which rentier becomes a synonym for villain and producer a synonym for hero, a slide the framework forbids, since the categories describe relations rather than characters, and since the same enterprise, and often the same household through its pension claims, stands on both sides of the line at once. Used with those cautions, the term names the phase this article's verdict endorses. Used without them, it becomes another atmosphere word, and the debate has enough of those.

### Who counts as a rentier in the strict sense?

a rentier, strictly, is anyone receiving income by virtue of owning a monopolised condition, in proportion to that ownership rather than to any productive activity performed. The definition is relational and unglamorous: it captures quiet holders of small assets alongside the great junction owners, and it judges the income stream, never the person's worth.

Beneath the terminology sits a question about class that the debate raises without always facing. If income from ownership of conditions grows while income from organising production stagnates in prestige and reward, the owning classes themselves recompose: fractions whose wealth is positional and rights-based gain weight against fractions whose wealth is operational, and the state's policy, which is never neutral among fractions, tilts toward the defence of asset values and the stability of access charges. Much of the political texture attributed vaguely to a new feudalism, the sense of a society governed for holders rather than for makers, is explicable at this level without any change of mode, as a shift in the internal balance of the propertied. Class analysis, the tradition's home ground, thus recovers from the phase concept a rich agenda that the mode-change concept actually obscures, since a new mode would require new classes, while the observable reality is old classes rearranging their weights and their alliances.

## Competition, monopoly, and why rent keeps regenerating

To understand why rent keeps reappearing in new costumes, follow competition itself rather than its official portrait. The official portrait shows many producers, none able to set prices, each driven to efficiency by rivals, with monopoly as an occasional pathology cured by entry. The framework's portrait is dynamic and less comforting: competition is a war in which each combatant seeks, rationally, to escape competition, to reach a position rivals cannot assail, and the weapons of escape are exactly the materials of rent. Scale that newcomers cannot match, a location that cannot be duplicated, a right that law forbids others to use, a standard that everyone must meet and one party owns, a stock of accumulated users whose presence is itself the attraction: each is a moat, and the income of a moat, once dug, is rent or carries a rent component, because it is payment extracted by a barrier rather than by the ongoing superiority of a product. Winners do not merely earn more than losers. Winners convert their winnings into barriers, and barriers convert future competition into future tribute.

This regenerative account does explanatory work across the debate. It explains why the forms of rent mutate with each technological and legal era while the relation persists, since each era supplies new materials from which moats can be dug. It explains why anti-monopoly action, however vigorous, treats symptoms in a system whose competitive process manufactures the condition it polices, a paradox the tradition has always noted about reforms that leave the underlying relations intact. And it explains the emotional rhythm of the debate itself, in which each generation rediscovers rent with a sense of scandalised novelty, because each generation's moats are built from materials its parents did not have, and the novelty of the material disguises the familiarity of the relation. The analyst's task is to hold material and relation apart: marvel at the material if marvel is due, but classify by the relation.

### Is monopoly an accident within competition or one of its products?

a product, reliably. Rivalry drives each firm to hunt for shelter from rivalry itself, some edge competitors cannot copy, and any edge that hardens into permanence starts paying like a toll rather than like earnings from making. Calling that outcome an accident misreads the engine and sends reformers chasing interruptions instead of tendencies.

A corollary concerns the fate of rent under competitive pressure from other owners. Rents are not guaranteed by ownership alone; owners of rival conditions compete, and their competition can compress the rent each can levy, just as competition among capitals compresses profit rates toward an average. Where two gateways contest the same traffic, where two rights cover substitutable techniques, where two locations vie for the same tenants, the tribute falls, and in the limit a condition that anyone can replicate yields no rent at all, however useful it remains. Rent therefore measures not usefulness but scarcity under ownership, a point with a clarifying consequence for popular argument: the indispensability of a condition does not entitle its owner to rent in the analysis, nor does it explain the rent's size. Air is indispensable and yields nothing, being unowned and unscarce in the relevant sense. A gateway of modest social utility can yield enormously, being scarce, owned, and placed across a road everyone must travel. Indignation that useless things earn much and useful things earn little is as old as the value paradoxes, and the framework dissolves it the same way: price and income track relations of scarcity and property, not moral or functional worth.

The distinction between creating a condition and capturing one deserves its own emphasis, because the ethics and the economics of rent often turn on it. Some owners built the condition they charge for: they assembled the audience, drained the marsh, developed the technique, laid the infrastructure. Others captured a condition built by predecessors, by the public, or by the unowned growth of a surrounding society, and charge for what they found. The framework's category covers both without distinction, since rent is defined by the ongoing relation of ownership to surplus, not by the biography of the asset. But analysis that stops at the category misses a politically live difference. Claims to reward for building are at least arguable in the language of desert that public debate uses; claims arising from capture must be defended in the colder language of property rights alone, and they are correspondingly more vulnerable when scarcity is contested. The middle position's literature is at its best when it separates building from capturing case by case, and at its weakest when the single word rentier is allowed to blur a distinction its own politics needs.

## The state, the law, and the manufacture of scarcity

No rent exists without a property order, and no property order exists without a state that defines, registers, and enforces it. That truism becomes analytically sharp in the rent debate because so many of the conditions now yielding rent are creatures of law in a direct and revisable sense. An exclusive right over knowledge exists because a statute grants it, for a term a statute fixes, with exceptions a statute enumerates. A licence to operate in a restricted field yields rent because entry is rationed by permission. A location yields its rent partly from nature and partly from public decisions about infrastructure, zoning, and permitted density that concentrate desirability here rather than there. Even the gateway positions of digital infrastructures rest on legal foundations: property in the infrastructure itself, contract law binding users to terms, and the liability and regulatory settlements within which enclosure operates. The state is therefore not an umpire standing outside the rent economy, occasionally intervening. The state is one of its authors, continuously, in the fine print of the rules that decide which scarcities exist and who may own them.

This authorship reframes the politics of the debate in a way all three positions can use. For the strong position, state authorship is an embarrassment, since a feudal order would not need its rents legislated into being by a capitalist state pursuing recognisably capitalist ends; the dependence of access income on statute is evidence that the phenomenon lives inside the old order's legal house. For the second position, state authorship is familiar ground, since the tradition has always treated property law as the juridical expression of production relations rather than as a neutral framework above them. For the middle position, state authorship is the practical opening: scarcities that are manufactured can be remanufactured, terms shortened, exceptions widened, public alternatives built, and the yield of unavoidable scarcities taxed or socialised, all without awaiting a transformation of the mode. The phase reading thus emerges, once again, as the position that converts analysis into agenda with the least metaphysical overhead.

### Why does the legal construction of scarcity matter politically?

because scarcity made by statute can be remade by statute. Where rent rests on exclusive rights, rationed permissions, or public decisions concentrating desirability, the size and destination of the rent are policy choices wearing the costume of natural facts, and recognising the costume is the first step toward contesting the choice.

There is a deeper theoretical point about the state and rent that the founding tradition supplies and the debate underuses. In pre-capitalist orders, the functions of economic extraction and political rule were fused: the lord who took the surplus was the same authority who judged, taxed, and coerced, and rent and tax were correspondingly hard to distinguish. Capitalism separates the functions, at least in form: surplus is extracted economically, through the wage relation and the market, while the state stands apart as guarantor of the order within which extraction proceeds. The modern rentier, however grand, receives income through that separated, economic channel, as a private property holder under general law, not as a jurisdictional lord fusing rule and revenue in his person. The separation is not a technicality. It is one of the defining marks of the mode, and its persistence is further evidence, of a structural kind, that the mode persists. Where commentators sense feudalism in the private governance exercised by great gateways, in their rule-setting and adjudication over users, they are noticing a real partial refusion of functions at the level of the firm, and the observation deserves the careful treatment the companion platform analysis gives it. But a refusion inside enterprises that remain capitals, employing wage labour and accumulating under competition, is a deformation within the mode's separation of functions, not the restoration of the order in which that separation never existed.

Fiscal policy enters here as well, because taxation is the state's standing instrument for redirecting rent without abolishing the underlying property. A tax falling on pure rent, on the yield of a condition in fixed supply, has the elegant property, recognised across otherwise hostile schools of economics, of collecting revenue without discouraging the condition's availability, since the condition cannot be withdrawn in response. Land value taxation built an entire reform tradition on that property. The framework's own view is more reserved about what fiscal instruments can achieve within unchanged relations, expecting captured rent to be contested, avoided, and partly shifted, yet even the reserved view concedes the analytical point: rent, precisely because it is a transfer rather than a product, is the income form most available for social redirection without productive damage. The rent debate's practical stakes concentrate here, in the question of who pockets the transfer, and that question is distributional and political through and through, which is the middle position's home ground once more.

## Land, housing, and the oldest rent of all

The debate's centre of gravity sits among new scarcities, but its oldest case remains its most instructive, because land rent displays the mechanism with the fewest disguises. A plot's yield to its owner divides, on analysis, into elements that centuries of argument have learned to separate. There is the return on capital sunk into the plot, drainage, fencing, buildings, improvements, which behaves like profit on that capital and decays as the improvements decay. There is differential rent, arising because plots differ in fertility and in distance from the markets and centres where life concentrates, so that equal capitals earn unequal surpluses which ownership then harvests. And there is the element arising from the sheer growth of the surrounding society, the increment of value that population, infrastructure, and collective activity confer on a location whose owner contributed nothing to the increment. That unearned increment, as an older reform vocabulary called it, is rent in its purest visible form, and its visibility is why land has always been the teaching case: nobody supposes the owner manufactured the city's growth, so nobody can pretend the increment rewards production.

Housing translates the teaching case into the idiom of daily life. A household's payment for shelter divides into the cost of the structure, its maintenance, and the return on the capital embodied in it, together with a location rent that can dwarf the rest where scarcity of permitted, serviced, well-placed land is severe. The household experiences the total as a single burden, and political argument that fails to divide it will misdiagnose the burden's causes: building more structures addresses the first elements, while the location element responds to the supply of permitted land, to public investment that creates new desirable locations, and to the fiscal treatment of the increment. The framework's contribution is the division itself, a refusal to let a single price conceal two different relations, one to production and one to ownership. That refusal, practised consistently, is most of what rent analysis is.

### What part of a housing payment is rent in the strict sense?

the part attributable to location rather than to the structure: the yield of a position whose desirability the surrounding society produced and the owner merely holds. Money that remunerates the structure itself, its repairs, and the funds sunk into raising it sits in an altogether separate pigeonhole, no matter how seamlessly the tenant's single monthly bill blends the ingredients.

Agriculture, the founding case, should not be abandoned too quickly either, because it preserves a phenomenon the newer cases sometimes hide: the effect of rent on production itself. High rents on the best conditions do not merely redistribute; they determine which marginal conditions enter cultivation at all, since production on the worst land in use must cover its costs including the rent its ownership can command, and the price of the product adjusts accordingly. Rent, in the classical phrase the tradition inherits, is price-determining in this setting rather than price-determined, a reversal of the usual direction that reminds analysts that distribution and production interpenetrate. The general lesson travels: access charges on a necessary gateway enter the cost structure of everything downstream, shaping what gets produced, at what price, and by whom, so that even where rent is derivative in origin it is causal in effect. Derivative does not mean unimportant, a distinction the strong position's critics occasionally forget in their satisfaction at having established the derivation.

There is also a temporal dimension to land's lesson. Ground rents, once established, capitalise into land prices, and land prices then confront each new generation of producers and households as a cost as hard as any natural fact, although the entire structure is a claim on future surplus resting on law and custom. The hardness of capitalised claims explains much of the felt permanence of rentier power, and also its fragility under crisis, when expected future surplus contracts and the capitalised values built upon it deflate together. An order whose commanding wealth consists substantially of capitalised rent claims is an order whose wealth is, in a precise sense, anticipatory: it is the present price of expected future transfers. That anticipatory character, shared by land values and by the valuations of gateway enterprises alike, is another thread binding the oldest rent to the newest, and another reason the framework treats both under one category while the debate's headlines treat them as different worlds.

## Surplus value: the spring beneath the streams

Because every argument in this article returns to surplus value, the notion deserves a slow restatement pitched at a reader meeting it here for the first time, with the fuller exposition available in the series hub at [marxism and modern capitalism](/2020/11/01/marxism-and-modern-capitalism/) for those who want the whole architecture. Labour power, the capacity to work, is bought by capital at its value, which is set, like the value of any commodity, by the labour required to produce it: in this case the labour embodied in the food, shelter, training, and rest that reproduce the worker. The working day, however, is longer than the portion of it whose product merely replaces that value. In the remaining hours the worker produces value for which no equivalent is paid, and that unpaid portion is surplus value, the spring from which every stream this article has discussed is ultimately fed. Profit is surplus value viewed against the whole capital advanced. Interest is a contracted share of it. Commercial profit is a share earned by the capital that realises it. Rent is the share levied by the owner of a condition. The forms quarrel over the division; the spring flows from production or it does not flow at all.

Stating the architecture so baldly exposes where a genuine refutation of this article would have to aim. It would not aim at the size of rents, the novelty of gateways, or the arrogance of owners, all of which the architecture accommodates. It would aim at the spring: it would show value arising, systematically and at scale, without labour, in processes whose revenues are therefore primary rather than transferred. Automation debates raise exactly that challenge in another register, and the tradition's answers there, that machines transfer value but do not create it, that a fully automated process would produce use-values without producing value in the social sense the category requires, belong to that neighbouring argument. For the rent debate the point is narrower. The strong position does not generally claim that value arises without labour inside its enclosures; it claims that access charges on labour-organised activity constitute a new mode. That claim the architecture answers directly, since charges on activity are distributions of the activity's product. A challenger who wishes to defeat the architecture must change the claim, and changing the claim dissolves the feudal thesis into a different debate, which is the fork identified earlier in this article and the reason the verdict stands.

### Where does the surplus that pays rent originate?

in production, where hired hands remain at their stations well past the hour at which they have already repaid, in fresh value, the full price of their own keep. The unpaid excess is surplus value, and rent, interest, and profit are rival claims upon its division, which is why no claimant among them can be the source of the others.

The architecture also explains a feature of distributional politics that purely descriptive accounts leave mysterious: the ferocity of contests over shares among parties who agree about nothing else. If the streams were independent springs, each claimant's gain would leave the others untouched, and distributional conflict would be a series of separate negotiations. Because the streams divide one spring, every gain in rent is, other things equal, a pressure on profit, on wages, or on the prices paid by purchasers, and every claimant knows it. The politics of rent is therefore zero-sum in structure even when its rhetoric is win-win, and the zero-sum structure is why distributional questions, which the mode-change debate treats as secondary, carry such charge in practice. The middle position's instinct to centre distribution is, on the architecture's own showing, an instinct toward the place where the system's daily conflicts actually occur.

## Historical phases: rent inside capitalism across its development

A phase claim earns credibility by showing that the mode has had phases before, distinguishable by their distributional centres and institutional forms, without anyone needing to rename the mode at each turn. The history obliges generously. An early phase centred on merchant capital and chartered monopoly, where rents from licensed trade and colonial plunder bulked enormous beside the profits of a still-small manufacturing sector. A competitive industrial phase followed, in which the manufacturer's profit occupied the ideological centre and rent appeared, to the classical economists who theorised that phase, as the reactionary income of a landlord class obstructing progress, a framing that survives in the moralised tone rent still attracts. A phase of organised monopoly and imperial expansion then redistributed weight again, toward finance and toward rents on resources and territories, and theorists of that phase debated, in terms strikingly parallel to the present debate, whether the system had become something else. A postwar phase of mass production and strong labour organisation compressed rents and profits alike into a settlement that felt permanent to its inhabitants and was not. The phase now under discussion, with its asset-centred distribution, is the newest entry in a series whose members differ dramatically in texture while sharing the relations underneath.

The parallels with earlier phase debates are instructive down to the level of rhetoric. The theorists of the monopoly phase announced, as some now announce, that competition had been abolished and a new order installed; their critics replied, as critics now reply, that concentration was competition's own product and that wage labour and accumulation persisted beneath the new forms. Posterity's judgement is available in that case and worth importing: the monopoly phase was real as a phase, its analysts of distribution were largely right, its announcers of supersession were wrong, and the system that emerged from it was recognisably the same mode in a further configuration. History does not repeat its verdicts by necessity, and the parallel proves nothing by itself. But it sets the prior: claims of supersession in this family have a track record, and the track record justifies demanding the specific relational evidence the defeater names before believing the newest announcement.

### What do earlier phases teach about phase claims?

earlier phases teach that distributional centres shift dramatically within an unchanged mode, that each shift tempts observers to announce a successor system, and that posterity has so far sustained the phase readings over the supersession readings. The lesson is a prior, not a proof: supersession claims inherit a burden set by their predecessors' failures.

Phase thinking also disciplines the opposite complacency. If phases are real and consequential, then an analysis content to repeat that the mode persists has finished only the first page of its work. Phases differ in which classes and fractions hold power, in which institutions mediate conflict, in what the state protects and what it permits to be contested, and in the lived texture of dependence and security for the population. The rentier phase, if the middle position is right, has a characteristic politics: asset holders as the constituency whose confidence governments court, housing and access costs as the friction points where the phase is felt, and the defence of asset values as a constraint silently binding policy even where no rentier lobby speaks. Describing that politics accurately matters more for most practical purposes than the metaphysical question of the mode's name, and the description is available only to analysts who take phases seriously as objects, neither inflating them into modes nor dissolving them into an undifferentiated capitalism that never changes.

There is a historiographical caution to add. Phases are visible clearly only in retrospect, when their boundaries can be drawn against what followed, and contemporaries disputing a phase claim are therefore arguing about an object whose outline is still forming. Humility about boundaries is appropriate: the rentier phase may prove, to later eyes, a sub-phase of a longer financialised configuration, or the early shape of a configuration centred on something else entirely. The verdict of this article does not depend on the phase's final outline. It depends on the level distinction, mode against phase, which survives any redrawing of the phase's borders, because wherever the borders fall, the relations inside them remain those of wage labour, commodity production, and accumulation until the defeater's evidence appears.

## The strongest objection, stated at full strength and answered

Fairness to the debate requires that the objection to this article's verdict be stated once more, in its most formidable form, without the softening that controversy usually applies to opposing views. The objection runs as follows. The proprietors of the conduits of modern commerce, so the objection runs, have ceased to earn by making and begun to earn by metering. The infrastructures are not one gateway among many but the environment in which markets themselves occur, so that the distinction between competing within a market and owning the market has collapsed in the owner's favour. Other capitalists, however large, are reduced to paying tribute for reach, for visibility, for permission to meet their own customers, and they organise their strategies around the owner's rules rather than around production. Users, meanwhile, are not customers in any robust sense but inhabitants, whose activity, attention, and social relations are the raw material the owner harvests. A system whose dominant firms do not primarily compete in commodity markets but levy access charges on those who do, whose subjects relate to the commanding heights as inhabitants rather than as buyers, is not the capitalism the founding analysis described, whatever continuities of wage labour persist in its lower storeys. Names should follow the commanding relation, and the commanding relation is now the levy.

That is the objection at strength, and parts of it are true. That the line separating participation in a market from proprietorship of the market itself has crumbled is a genuine and consequential development. The inhabitant relation captures something genuine about dependence inside environments that are simultaneously workplace, marketplace, and social space. The tribute description fits the experience of producers whose margins are set by a percentage they did not negotiate. An answer that denied any of this would forfeit credibility, and this article's answer denies none of it. The answer begins instead with a concession and a distinction. The concession: the concentration of control over the conditions of access is real, it is a significant change in the organisation of distribution and of power among capitals, and the tradition's category for the income it yields, monopoly rent, is its least developed, so the framework's grip on the phenomenon is genuinely weaker than its grip on the factory system that formed it. The distinction: significance is not supersession, and the commanding relation must be identified by origin as well as by visibility. The levy is commanding in the sense that everyone must pass it. It is not commanding in the sense that it generates the wealth it levies upon, and a mode of production is classified by how wealth is generated and labour organised, not by which checkpoint is hardest to avoid.

### What is the strongest form of the objection to the continuity verdict?

at full strength it runs thus: the firms astride exchange itself have stopped winning by making things and instead tax everyone who must pass, demoting fellow capitals to payers of tribute and turning users into residents of private territory, so command, and therefore classification, belongs to the tax rather than to any market.

Apply, finally, the test of the counterfactual, which often settles what definitions leave open. Remove, in imagination, the gateways and their owners, while leaving production, wage labour, and commodity exchange intact: the economy is wounded, disorganised, in places monopolised anew within a season, but recognisably continuous, and its wealth-generating processes proceed. Now remove production under the wage relation while leaving the gateways standing: the levies have nothing to levy upon within a single cycle, the audiences have no incomes to spend, the sellers have nothing to sell, and the entire apparatus of access is a tollbooth on a road to an abandoned town. The asymmetry of the counterfactual is the asymmetry of the system. One element is the condition of the other's existence in a way that does not run in reverse, and classification follows that asymmetry in every other case the tradition handles. The gateway is a power over capitalism's circulation and distribution, raised in some cases to a pitch that justifies anger, reform, and the phase vocabulary of the middle position. It is not a successor to the mode whose product it taxes.

The objection's residue, after the answer, is a research agenda rather than a refutation. If the inhabitant relation deepens until the bustle inside enclosures ceases to be the making of saleable goods by hired hands and becomes something else entirely, arranged on unfamiliar principles and yielding the toll from its own substance, then the defeater's condition approaches satisfaction and the verdict must be revisited. Watching for that development is a legitimate theoretical task, and the strong position's writers, whatever their verdict's fate, have performed a service by specifying the direction from which a genuine transformation would come. Debates in a living tradition often end this way: the losing thesis survives as the winning thesis's early-warning instrument, and both sides are improved by the arrangement.

## Labour's standpoint: what the rent shift feels like from below

The debate is usually conducted among analysts of capital, but its distributional facts are lived by labour, and the lived version clarifies several points the analytical version leaves abstract. A wage, from the standpoint of the household receiving it, is not yet income available for life; it is a fund from which a series of access payments must first be made. Shelter claims its location rent before anything else is bought. Transport to work claims its charges. The tools of participation in modern working life, communication, connectivity, credentials, insurance against catastrophe, each present themselves increasingly as subscriptions, licences, and permissions rather than as possessions owned outright. The household therefore meets the rentier phase not as a thesis but as a sequence of tolls standing between the wage and the life the wage is supposed to fund, and the political emotion of the phase, the sense of working hard while standing still, is the emotion of watching the tolls rise faster than the fund from which they are paid.

That standpoint exposes a limitation in how the labour share is sometimes discussed, as though the share of national income reaching labour settled the question of labour's condition. Two households with identical wage shares can face very different toll structures, depending on how much of the necessities of life have been converted from owned or publicly provided goods into rented access. The conversion itself, from possession to permission across domain after domain, is one of the phase's characteristic movements, and it has a double effect the framework is well placed to name. Economically, it transforms lumpy purchases into perpetual claims on future wages, stabilising the revenue of condition-owners at the cost of the household's flexibility. Politically, it multiplies the points at which the household experiences dependence on owners, so that the rent relation, once confined to the tenancy of land and housing, becomes a daily texture. Analysts puzzled by the feudal metaphor's emotional reach should look here: a life passed through a succession of permissions feels feudal from inside, whatever the production relations underneath, and feelings of that consistency are data about distribution even when they mislead about classification.

### Why does the shift toward access payments matter for households beyond the wage share?

two pay packets of equal size can confront utterly different gauntlets of permissions before a life can be lived, depending on how many essentials arrive as continuing access rather than as possessions or public provision. Each conversion steadies a proprietor's inflow, adds a fresh point of household dependence, and feeds the sensation of running hard merely to stay in place.

From the same standpoint, the organisation of work inside the rent-yielding sectors deserves attention, because it tests the mode question at ground level. The gateways and infrastructures are built, maintained, and operated by wage labour, employed under recognisably capitalist discipline, subject to hiring, dismissal, performance management, and the compulsion to produce more than the wage costs. The creators whose work fills the enclosures divide between wage labourers of the owning enterprises, independent commodity producers selling through the gate, and a penumbra of aspirants working speculatively in hope of access to an audience, a category the tradition would analyse as a reserve and a petty-commodity fringe rather than as a new class of serfs. At no point in the descent from commanding heights to ground level does the observer encounter a production relation that is not already in the framework's repertoire. The descent is worth performing in every such debate: announce a new mode at the level of headlines, then walk down through its workplaces asking who hires, who works, who is dismissed, and who appropriates, and see whether the answers ever leave the old repertoire. In the present case they do not, and the walk is more persuasive than any single definition.

There is a final labour-side consideration, concerning strategy, where the classification dispute has practical teeth. If the mode had changed to a rent order, the classical levers addressed to the wage relation, organisation at the point of production, contest over the working day and the wage, would lose their object, and strategy would migrate entirely to the points of access: rent strikes, access commons, public alternatives to gateways. If the mode persists within a rentier phase, both terrains matter, and their relative weight is an empirical question about where surplus and where dependence concentrate. The middle position, again, is the one that keeps both terrains in view, treating access struggles as the phase's distinctive front while refusing to declare the older front closed. Movements that have acted on the supersession thesis risk discovering, expensively, that the wage relation did not receive the news of its abolition.

## Crisis and rent: how the hierarchy reveals itself under stress

Systems disclose their hierarchies when they are stressed, because stress forces the question of which claims get honoured and which get written down. The rent debate can therefore be tested against the behaviour of rent claims in crises, and the test is illuminating. In expansions, capitalised rent claims swell together: land values, rights valuations, and gateway valuations rise on expectations of future surplus, and the owners of conditions appear, even to themselves, as the economy's sovereigns. In contractions, the sequence reverses with a regularity that no feudal order ever exhibited. Expected surplus shrinks; the transfers that feed rent shrink with it; capitalised values built on the transfers deflate, sometimes catastrophically; and the owners of conditions discover that their claims were options on a productive future rather than possessions of a productive present. Lords in a genuine rent order of the feudal type did not watch their incomes evaporate because commodity production faltered in distant markets; their extraction rested on direct command over producers and land, and it persisted through commercial crises that ruined merchants. The modern rentier's exposure to the productive cycle is the signature of a dependent, not a sovereign, and dependence is the opposite of the strong position's thesis.

The crisis test also refines the middle position. Phases are not merely distributional arrangements; they are arrangements with characteristic fragilities. A phase in which claims on future surplus have been capitalised thickly, across land, rights, and gateways, is a phase in which the deflation of those claims transmits shocks widely, into household wealth, into pension provision, into the balance sheets of institutions that lent against the capitalised values. The politics of such a phase therefore includes a standing temptation for the state to defend asset values as a matter of systemic stability, which entrenches the rentier distribution at exactly the moments when its foundations are weakest. Observers who sense that the system is rigged for holders are often sensing this crisis asymmetry: gains capitalised privately in the upswing, foundations defended publicly in the downswing. The framework names the mechanism without needing any feudal vocabulary, as the socialisation of the risks of fictitious claims combined with the privatisation of their yields, and naming it precisely is worth more, analytically and politically, than the atmospheric alternative.

### What do crises reveal about the standing of rent claims?

crises reveal that rent claims are options on future surplus rather than sovereign possessions: when expected production contracts, the transfers feeding rent contract, capitalised values deflate, and owners of conditions discover a dependence on the productive economy that a genuinely feudal extraction, resting on direct command, never exhibited.

A related stress test concerns the behaviour of access owners themselves in hard times. A sovereign extractor raises the levy when times worsen, since the levy is the point. Access owners in a capitalist phase do the opposite as often as not: they cut charges, subsidise participation, and spend reserves to keep producers and users inside the enclosure, because the enclosure's value is the flow of activity it hosts, and the flow is production and exchange conducted by others. That behaviour is unintelligible on the feudal reading, where the lord's interest is the dues and the tenants are a fixed endowment. It is perfectly intelligible on the framework's reading, where the owner's rent is a share of a surplus generated by the hosted activity, and killing the activity to raise the share is the classic error of the parasite that starves its host. Sophisticated owners know the Laffer-like geometry of their own extraction, and their knowledge is a standing confession of the dependence this article's decisive point asserts.

## Ground rent and monopoly rent: keeping the categories separate

One of the debate's persistent muddles is the habit of reaching for the word rent whenever any of its forms appears, as though differential, absolute, and monopoly rent were interchangeable labels for a single phenomenon. They are not, and the muddle has consequences, because each form has a different mechanism, a different limit, and a different politics. Unequal holdings generate the first mechanism: whenever rival producers work plots of differing quality under one market price, the superior plots throw off an excess that proprietors harvest, its size set by the gap separating each plot from the poorest in cultivation. The second mechanism needs no inequality at all: proprietors acting as a class can simply refuse to release their holdings until a payment is made, and the payment's ceiling is whatever that collective refusal can withstand against capital's pressure to level all returns. Monopoly rent is anchored in uniqueness, in a condition that has no comparable rival at all, and its size is disciplined principally by the depth of purchasers' desire and means, which makes it the least predictable and, in the founding text, the least theorised of the three.

Keeping the forms separate improves the modern debate at several points at once. Much of what is called platform rent, for instance, decomposes under pressure: part differential, since gateways differ in the quality of the audiences they assemble and the best can levy what the marginal gateway cannot; part absolute, since access to the assembled market as such is withheld until terms are met; part monopoly, where a particular standard, archive, or network position is genuinely without substitute. The decomposition is not scholastic decoration. Each part responds to different remedies: differential elements shrink as rival conditions improve, absolute elements respond to the terms on which withholding is permitted, monopoly elements respond to interoperability, expiry, and the deliberate construction of substitutes. A politics addressed to rent in general will blunder among these; a politics addressed to the forms can aim.

### How do monopoly rent and ground rent differ in mechanism?

payments for soil split two ways: a part tracking how much better one parcel performs than the worst in use, and a part extracted simply because proprietors can refuse entry. The singular holding obeys neither logic; its charge floats on longing and purchasing power, and the founding text left that third mechanism barely sketched beside the elaborated pair.

The separation also clarifies a recurring dispute about whether high incomes in knowledge industries are rent or a return to scarce skill and enterprise. Scarce skill earns a wage or a salary, however elevated, and the elevation of rare talent's pay is a phenomenon of the labour market, analysable in terms the tradition supplies for the value of complex labour power and for the bargaining of the scarce. Enterprise earns profit where it organises production. Rent begins only where ownership of a condition, separable from the person's labour and from the enterprise's organisation, levies its charge. A celebrated creator may receive all three streams braided together: a salary for ongoing work, a profit share as part-owner of an organising enterprise, and a rent from rights in past work that others must license. Public argument that calls the whole braid by any single name will misjudge the person's position and the policy that touches it, since the three streams respond to three different levers and carry three different justifications, or lack of them.

There is a historical irony in the categories' fortunes worth savouring. Ground rent, the form the founding texts developed most fully, has receded from the centre of economic life as agriculture's weight has shrunk, although its urban descendant, location rent, has grown to take its place in the household budget and the politics of cities. Monopoly rent, the form developed least, has expanded into the debate's centre as rights, standards, and gateways have multiplied. The tradition's map and the territory have thus drifted in opposite directions, fullness where the phenomenon thins, thinness where it thickens. Traditions survive such drift by constructive work, and the work here is specifiable: a proper theory of the singular holding under modern conditions, one that explains the birth of such tolls, their ceilings, their conversion into tradable wealth, and their slow erosion as substitutes multiply and protections lapse. Until that work is done, and this article has tried to mark its outlines honestly, every confident deployment of the category, by any camp, should be read with the discount the sketch deserves.

## The world market: rent across borders without a world landlord

Rent's logic does not stop at frontiers, and the international dimension adds a twist that tests the categories usefully. Conditions monopolised in one place levy charges on production organised in another: rights held under one jurisdiction are licensed to producers under many; resources extracted under one set of ownership arrangements feed industries scattered across continents; gateways assembled in one market govern sellers in all of them. The surplus transferred as rent therefore crosses borders inside prices, royalties, and fees, and the geography of rent receipt separates from the geography of production more completely than in any earlier phase. A region can specialise, in effect, in owning conditions while other regions specialise in producing under them, and the distributional politics of the phase acquires a map: contention between populations whose incomes rest on production and populations whose asset wealth rests on claims against production elsewhere.

The twist is that no world landlord exists to complete the feudal picture at the global scale, and the absence is diagnostic. Feudal extraction required a hierarchy of personal lordship ramifying across the whole territory of the order. Global rent flows instead through contracts, treaties, and legal harmonisation among states that remain the enforcers of the underlying property, which is to say through the juridical machinery of a capitalist world market. The flows are real, the asymmetries are real, and the resentment they generate in producing regions is a material political force. But the form of the whole, a market of capitals and states bound by law rather than a pyramid of lords bound by homage, is the form of the mode the strong position declares superseded, operating at its largest scale. Scale, once again, is mistaken for kind when the headlines are written, and the correction is the same correction this article has applied at every level: ask after the relations, not after the size of the flows that the relations carry.

### Does the international separation of owning and producing change the analysis?

the separation widens the geography of the phase without altering its logic. Rent crosses borders inside prices and fees, regions specialise in owning or in producing, and the resulting asymmetries are politically potent, yet enforcement still runs through states, contracts, and a world market rather than through any hierarchy of personal lordship.

A further international consideration concerns resources, where differential and absolute rent in their most classical forms still govern enormous flows. The owner of a rich deposit earns a differential rent set by the gap between its extraction costs and those of the marginal deposit in use, and where ownership is concentrated, in a state or in a small set of holders, the absolute element adds its levy. Resource rents have built sovereign funds, distorted exchange rates, and financed states independently of taxing their own populations, with political consequences, the so-called resource curse among them, that an entire literature examines. For the present debate the resource case is a standing reminder that colossal rent flows, capable of dominating whole national economies, have coexisted with capitalism for its entire history without announcing its supersession. If rent dominance at the scale of a resource state does not change the mode, rent dominance in the newer sectors must prove its case by the relations it creates, not by the size of the flows it commands, and the relations, inspected closely, remain the familiar ones of wage labour and accumulation.

## Rent and innovation: the incentive argument examined

The standard defence of rent-yielding rights is dynamic: without the prospect of rent, the condition would not be created. Nobody would develop the technique, assemble the infrastructure, or produce the work if imitators could appropriate the result the moment it appeared, so a temporary rent is the price society pays for a continuing flow of creation. The argument deserves respect in its proper domain and suspicion outside it, and the framework supplies the tools for telling the domains apart. Where creation is costly, imitation cheap, and the creator's return would otherwise collapse toward zero, a bounded exclusivity can indeed call forth production that would not occur, and the resulting rent is the deliberate instrument of a policy that has weighed the transfer against the creation. Where, by contrast, the condition would exist without the rent, because it arises from network effects, from public investment, from the growth of a surrounding society, or from a position won in a standards contest, the incentive defence fails on its own terms: the rent rewards capture rather than creation, and extending or enlarging it purchases no additional creation at all.

Between those poles lies the contested middle where most real rights live, and the honest analytical procedure is decomposition once more. Ask of any rent stream what portion answers to creation that the rent genuinely elicited, what portion answers to position that would have been occupied regardless, and what portion answers to extensions, widenings, and enforcements added after the creation was complete, which elicit nothing except lobbying. The third portion is pure transfer with a lobbying cost attached, and its existence across the rights landscape is the least disputed empirical claim in the entire debate, conceded in substance by defenders of the system whenever they distinguish use from abuse. A debate that began from the decomposition would spend less time on whether rent is good or bad in general, a question without content, and more time on the proportions, which is where policy actually lives.

### When does the incentive defence of rent succeed?

the defence succeeds where creation is costly, imitation is cheap, and exclusivity genuinely elicits production that would otherwise not occur. It fails where the condition arises from network effects, public investment, or social growth, since there the rent rewards capture of a position rather than creation of the thing accessed.

The framework adds a longer-run observation that the incentive debate, fixated on individual creations, tends to miss. A system that rewards capture increasingly will, over time, redirect its most ambitious energies from creation to capture, since rational actors follow the gradient of reward. The redirection is self-reinforcing: as more talent congregates around the acquisition and defence of positions, the returns to position rise relative to the returns to production, the political weight of position-holders grows, and the rules are rewritten further in position's favour. Theorists of the middle position describe this spiral when they speak of a rentier phase consolidating itself, and the description is compatible with everything this article has argued, because a spiral within distribution is still a spiral within the mode. Naming the spiral matters practically: the point of intervention that the analysis recommends is the gradient itself, the relative reward of capture against creation, and instruments that flatten the gradient, shorter terms, narrower scope, stronger public alternatives, taxation of pure transfer, address the phase's engine rather than its exhaust.

There is also an asymmetry in how the two errors of policy distribute their costs, and mature judgement should weigh it. Grant too little exclusivity and some creations are forgone, a cost that is real but speculative, since the forgone creations are invisible. Grant too much and the transfer is certain, compounding, and politically entrenched, since every rent granted creates a constituency for its own perpetuation and extension. The asymmetry explains why rights regimes drift toward excess over time in the absence of countervailing organisation, and why the drift is a distributional ratchet rather than a series of independent mistakes. The tradition's suspicion of rent finds here a precise, non-moralised foundation: rent is the income form that manufactures its own political defence most reliably, because its recipients' interest is concentrated, permanent, and attached to a legal artefact that legislatures control.

## Subscriptions, access, and the changing form of ownership

A quieter transformation runs beneath the debate's headlines and may, in the long run, matter as much as the gateways: the conversion of ownership into access in the consumption of ordinary goods. Where households once bought objects outright, a hammer, a record, a licence to use a tool in perpetuity, they increasingly rent the object's services by the period, retaining nothing when payment stops. The conversion is often convenient and sometimes genuinely economical, since access models can spread costs and update provision continuously. Its analytical significance lies in the direction of the property relation it generalises. The consumer of access stands to the provider as a tenant stands to a landlord, in a continuing relation of permission rather than in the completed independence of a purchase, and the provider's income takes on the stability and the character of a rent on the installed base of the dependent.

Multiply that relation across the domains of daily life and a pattern emerges whose significance the phase camp has done more than any rival to underline. Income streams across the economy become more rent-like in form: periodic, access-based, terminable for non-payment, and detached from any transfer of an asset to the payer. The form matters beyond bookkeeping because it changes the distribution of risk and of power. The owner of the accessed stock bears no risk of the household's changing needs; the household bears the risk of losing, at a stroke, the accumulated fabric of its working life, its archives, its tools, its audience, if payments lapse or terms change. Dependence of that texture, generalised, is the experiential basis on which the feudal metaphor feeds, and the analysis of this article can honour the experience while classifying it correctly: a proliferation of rent-form relations inside consumption, layered over production relations that remain capitalist, is a phase phenomenon of exactly the kind the middle position describes.

### Is the spread of access over ownership itself a change of mode?

no, though it is a genuine change in the texture of economic life. Access relations multiply rent-form dependence in consumption and stabilise owners' incomes, while the production of the accessed goods and services continues under wage labour and accumulation, which keeps the phenomenon at the level of phase and distribution.

A distinction within the access economy prevents overgeneralisation. Some access payments buy services continuously produced by labour, the maintenance of a tool, the updating of a provision, the hosting of an archive on machinery that wears and must be tended, and those payments divide between wages, profit on the operating capital, and depreciation, with rent entering only where the provider's position is itself monopolised. Other access payments buy almost pure permission, where the marginal cost of admitting one more user approaches nothing and the payment is levied by the barrier alone. The two kinds feel identical on a household budget and are opposites in analysis, and the habit of decomposing them, practised until it becomes reflex, is the single most transferable skill this article hopes to teach. Headline aggregates that sum the two kinds into one vast access economy, and then pronounce on the system from the sum, commit the aggregation error the framework exists to prevent.

## The classical inheritance: rent before Marx and what he changed

The rent debate did not begin with the framework this article applies, and a compressed genealogy sharpens what is distinctive in that framework's contribution. The classical political economists made rent a central category earlier and more anxiously than they made profit one. In the physiocratic inheritance, land was the sole source of the net product, and rent was its form, a doctrine that made the landlord the pivot of the economy and taxation of rent the obvious fiscal programme. The classical system that displaced physiocracy, above all in David Ricardo, Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817), rebuilt rent as a differential payment arising from the unequal quality and scarcity of land, determined at the margin of cultivation and innocent, in Ricardo's famous insistence, of any role in setting prices: rent was price-determined, not price-determining, the effect of high prices rather than their cause. Ricardo's rent was also a distributional villain in a specific historical drama, since rising rents on scarce land squeezed the profits that funded accumulation, aligning the interests of industrial capital against the landed interest in one of the classical system's most politically charged oppositions.

Marx inherited that machinery and altered its foundations in three ways that still govern the debate. First, he relocated rent inside the theory of surplus value, making it a derivative share of a surplus produced by labour, where Ricardo had treated the shares of the three great classes with a less unified account of their common source. The relocation is the origin of this article's decisive point: only because rent is, in the rebuilt theory, a portion of a single surplus with a single spring can the incoherence of a pure rent order be demonstrated rather than merely asserted. Second, he added absolute rent to the differential scheme, grounding a payment on even the worst land in the class monopoly of landed property, a category Ricardo's assumptions excluded and later economists have contested and reformulated ever since. Third, he historicised the whole arrangement, treating landed property not as a natural fixture but as a form capitalism finds, transforms, and subordinates, so that rent's persistence is a fact about the mode's way of digesting older property rather than evidence of an older mode surviving intact. Each alteration matters for the present argument: the first supplies the decisive point, the second supplies the analysis of withholding that access debates reuse, the third supplies the answer to the feudal analogy's historical form.

### What did the framework change in the classical theory of rent?

three moves stand out. Rival revenues were gathered into a single fund with a single birthplace in unpaid labour time. A payment on even the meanest plot was explained through proprietors' collective refusal to release their holdings. And landed property itself was historicised, shown being remade and subordinated by the newer order instead of surviving as its untouched predecessor.

The genealogy also warns against a flattering error to which traditions are prone: supposing that because a category is old and well built, its application to new material is automatic. Ricardo's differential scheme transferred to urban location with genuine success, because the underlying structure, unequal conditions competing under a common price, recurred faithfully. The transfer to knowledge rights and gateways is less faithful, since the conditions there are reproducible, excludable by law, and often valuable in proportion to the crowd they gather rather than to any fixed quality of the condition itself. Each transfer must therefore be argued, not assumed, and the argument will sometimes conclude that the category fits only partially, with a remainder that only the still-missing systematic treatment of singular holdings could absorb. Genealogy teaches respect for the tools and suspicion of their effortless application in the same lesson.

A second warning from the genealogy concerns the moral temperature of rent talk. From the physiocrats' landlord-pivot through Ricardo's anti-landlord drama to the land reform movements that taxed or threatened the unearned increment, rent has always attracted a moral charge that profit, for all the attacks on it, attracts in a different register. Rent looks like getting something for nothing in a way that even the critic of profit concedes involves organisation, risk, and activity. The moral charge is understandable and analytically dangerous, because it invites the slide from a relational category to a verdict on persons, and from there to a politics of resentment that the framework's structural analysis does not support and its remedies do not need. The owners of conditions are not a conspiracy and not a separate species of human motivation; they are capitals and households behaving as the gradient of reward instructs, and the remedy for a perverse gradient is to change the gradient, not to anathematise the gradient's followers. Keeping the analysis cold is what allows its conclusions to stay hot where heat is earned, on the structure of scarcity and the distribution of the surplus.

## Grading the three positions: a worked assessment

Students meeting this debate in an examination, and readers meeting it in controversy, need the same thing: a worked example of the positions being graded against the framework rather than merely summarised. Take the decisive claim, rent presupposes production, and run each position past it in turn, watching what the claim does to each. The strong position meets the claim head-on and is wounded at its centre, because its thesis requires rent to be capable of primacy, and the claim shows primacy to be incoherent for a category defined as a share of a surplus produced elsewhere. The strong position can escape only by redefining its central income as something other than rent, at which point its feudal vocabulary loses its referent and the thesis must be restated in other terms. A position that cannot state its thesis in its own chosen vocabulary after the decisive claim is applied has failed the grading, however many phenomena it has vividly described.

The second position meets the claim comfortably, since the claim is a corollary of the continuity it asserts: rent as a standing distribution form presupposes the production whose surplus it shares, and nothing in the toll economy disturbs that architecture. Its grade is nevertheless not the highest available, because the grading rewards not only survival but illumination, and the continuity position illuminates the phase's distinctive distributional facts weakly, tending to file them under the general heading of monopoly and move on. A reader who adopted only the second position would be right about the mode and under-equipped about the period, which is a real deficiency in an answer and in an analysis alike.

### How does the decisive claim grade the three positions?

one camp is disqualified by its own dictionary, since a slice of another's product cannot headline a successor order. A second camp passes the test it helped design yet is marked down for shrugging at the slicing's transformation. The third camp collects the highest grade, its whole thesis being the residue the test preserves: shares revolutionised, sources untouched.

The middle position meets the claim not merely comfortably but gratefully, because the claim draws the very line the position exists to draw. If rent presupposes production, then rent's growth is necessarily a fact about the division of a product generated under persisting relations, which is the phase thesis stated from the other side. The decisive claim thus functions as the middle position's theoretical foundation, converting its empirical observations about asset-centred distribution into a structured account with the mode question settled underneath it. Its remaining weakness, recorded earlier, is the unspecified threshold at which distributional dominance would become qualitative change, and the grading should record that the defeater stated in this article supplies the missing threshold on the position's behalf: the line is crossed once access revenue is demonstrated to spring from pursuits lying beyond the wage-staffed production of goods for sale. With that supplement, the middle position earns the verdict this article delivers, not as a compromise but as the only position whose claims all survive contact with the framework's architecture.

The worked assessment generalises into a study habit worth stating explicitly, because it transfers to every debate in this cluster of the series. When a framework contains a claim of the presupposition type, a claim that one category depends for its existence on another, that claim should be found early and applied to every position before the evidence is weighed, since positions that fail the presupposition test cannot be rescued by evidence, and positions that pass it divide the evidence among themselves. Hours of controversy are wasted, in print and in examination rooms, weighing evidence for conclusions that a two-minute presupposition check had already excluded. The rent debate is the cluster's purest instance of the pattern, which is why the series places its organising weight here, and why the platform and financialisation disputes, each with its own literature, both become shorter once this article's point is in hand.

## Rent, interest, and merchant profit: three derivative shares distinguished

Rent does not divide the surplus alone, and placing it among its fellow derivative shares sharpens its outline by contrast. Interest is the share claimed by the owner of money capital, paid for the temporary use of funds rather than for access to a condition of production. Its basis is the loan, its magnitude is disciplined by the supply of and demand for loanable funds together with the general level of profit from which it is carved, and its recipient, like the rent recipient, performs no productive function in the act of receiving. Yet the two shares differ in a way that matters. Money is fungible and mobile, reproducible in effect through the credit system, and its owner monopolises nothing unique; the interest relation therefore lacks the element of a specific, non-reproducible condition that defines rent. Where a lender does control a unique gateway to funds, where access to credit itself is rationed by position, a rent element enters the interest charge, and the decomposition habit applies once more: the payment divides into interest proper and a rent on the gateway, each answering to different analysis and different remedy.

Merchant profit, the share earned by capital specialised in buying and selling, offers a different contrast. Commercial capital performs a function, the realisation of commodities, the conversion of produced value into money, and its profit is the payment the system makes, out of surplus value, for a circulation function that production capital would otherwise have to perform itself at greater cost. Realising goods adds nothing to their value, strictly speaking, yet it is unmistakably work, an exertion, which is more than can be said for the toll keeper in the act of collecting. The line between the merchant's share and a gateway owner's rent can blur in practice, since a marketplace both realises goods and controls access to buyers, but the analytical line survives the blur and does its usual work: to the extent the income pays for realisation services actually performed, it is commercial profit; to the extent it pays for permission to reach a monopolised audience, it is rent. Enterprises straddling the line will naturally describe the whole income in whichever vocabulary flatters it more, and the analyst's independence consists in declining both flatteries.

### How does rent differ from interest in the framework?

a lender hands over funds for a season and takes a cut of the borrower's gains; funds being interchangeable, and credit able to multiply them, no unique thing is being monopolised. A toll keeper, by contrast, guards one particular passage that cannot be conjured into existence by rivals, and charges for that passage specifically.

The threefold division also illuminates the architecture of a modern balance sheet, where a single enterprise may simultaneously pay rent on its locations and rights, pay interest on its borrowings, earn profit on its production, and earn commercial margins on its distribution, while receiving rent from sublet conditions and interest from its own lending. The categories are not kinds of firm or kinds of person; they are kinds of flow, and a firm is a knot where many flows cross. Much confused writing about the rent debate dissolves once that sentence is absorbed, because claims that some enterprise is a rentier, full stop, usually mean that rent flows dominate its knot, a quantitative claim requiring decomposition of its accounts rather than a classification of its soul. The framework's discipline is accounting discipline before it is anything else: follow each flow to its source in the division of surplus, and let the proportions, once established, carry whatever verdict about dominance the evidence supports.

There is a further, subtler distinction between rent and the other shares concerning time. Profit is earned in a production cycle and realised in a sale; interest accrues across a loan's term; rent characteristically capitalises, converting an expected stream into a present price for the condition itself, so that the condition trades as though it were a capital sum. Capitalisation gives rent its peculiar social weight, because the capitalised price enters balance sheets, collateral, and household wealth, and a whole structure of credit and expectation builds itself on the anticipated transfers. When the transfers disappoint, the disappointment propagates through everything built on their capitalised value, which is the crisis mechanism examined earlier. No other derivative share capitalises into the price of a thing in quite the same way, since money and merchandise do not need to pretend to be capital in order to circulate, while a rent-yielding condition must acquire a fictitious capital price before it can be traded as wealth. The pretence is socially real in its effects and analytically transparent in its basis, and holding both truths at once is the framework's characteristic posture toward the entire rent phenomenon.

## Nature, scarcity, and rent beyond the economy of things made

Rent's oldest material basis is nature: the unequal gifts of soil, climate, mineral endowment, and position that no labour produced. Extending the analysis to nature's newer scarcities tests whether the category travels, and the test is worth performing carefully because the politics of environmental scarcity will dominate distributional argument for the foreseeable future. Where a right to emit, to extract, to fish, to build, or to use a spectrum is created by collective decision and allocated to owners, the right behaves exactly as the framework's analysis of absolute rent predicts: a monopolised condition, manufactured by law, yields a transfer to its holders from the surplus generated by those who must purchase access. Where the underlying scarcity is natural and the right merely rations it, a differential element joins the absolute one, since better deposits, better sites, and better bands yield more than marginal ones under a common price. The decomposition is by now familiar, and its familiarity is the point: the category travels because the relation recurs, a barrier owned, a payment levied, a surplus divided.

The environmental case adds one genuinely new wrinkle. Many natural conditions are not merely scarce but depleting or degrading, so that the rent extracted from them is partly a conversion of a stock of natural wealth into a flow of income, a liquidation wearing the costume of a yield. The framework's categories, built for reproducible production, handle the costume adequately, since the payment remains a transfer claim, but they flag a question the transfer analysis alone cannot answer: what portion of the apparent surplus of an extractive economy is not surplus at all but the consumption of an inheritance, whose replacement cost no account records? That question belongs to the neighbouring debates about value and nature, and it is flagged here rather than answered, in keeping with this article's discipline of linking where its competence ends. What can be said within the present argument is that the feudal analogy gains no ground in the environmental terrain either: extraction rights are created, traded, and revoked by states and markets, their holders are capitals under accumulation compulsion, and the labour that works the deposits is wage labour, so the mode's signature is present on every document.

### Do manufactured rights over natural scarcity create rent in the strict sense?

yes. A legally created right to emit, extract, or use a scarce natural condition is a monopolised barrier whose holders levy a transfer from those who need access, combining an absolute element from the withholding power with differential elements where the underlying conditions differ in quality, exactly as the framework's scheme predicts.

The natural case also returns the argument, by a long route, to the question of what a society should do with rents it cannot abolish because the underlying scarcity is real. Where scarcity is natural, the choice is never between rent and no rent; it is between rent pocketed by owners, rent collected publicly through taxation or auction of the rights, and rent dissipated in queues, congestion, and competitive waste as claimants scramble for unpriced access. That three-way choice is the practical core of every resource and environmental settlement, and the framework's contribution is to strip the first option of its usual disguise, in which the owner's pocketing of a socially created or naturally given scarcity presents itself as the natural order rather than as one distributional choice among three. The middle position's politics, generalised, is the insistence that the choice be made consciously and in public, domain by domain, with the decomposition of each rent stream as the evidence on the table.

## Culture, attention, and the limits of the rent metaphor

The rent vocabulary has spread, in popular commentary, to territories where its analytical licence needs checking, and attention is the territory where the checking matters most. To say that a gateway extracts rent from attention can mean something precise: that the owner of a monopolised audience levies charges on those who wish to reach it, which is monopoly rent on a condition in the strict sense, with advertisers' budgets, drawn from their profits, as the transferred surplus. Or it can mean something loose: that users' looking and posting is itself labour producing value which the owner appropriates, a claim belonging to a different theory, about the extension of the labour concept to unpaid activity, which must be argued on its own terms and gains nothing by borrowing rent's name. The two meanings are often braided in a single paragraph, producing an impression of theoretical richness that dissolves on inspection into two separate arguments, one sound and modest, the other bold and undefended. The discipline this article has practised throughout, decomposing streams and testing categories by their definitions, dissolves the braid and leaves each argument to stand at its proper height.

The bold argument deserves a fair statement before its demotion. If the activity of users generates the material that draws the audience, and the audience is what advertisers pay to reach, then users stand in a productive relation to the enterprise's revenue while receiving no wage, a configuration that strains the wage-labour frame and has led serious writers to speak of a new form of exploitation or of value production outside employment. The framework's defenders reply that the revenue remains, in its economic substance, a transfer: advertisers pay from profits earned by selling commodities produced by wage labour elsewhere, and the gateway's service, the assembly and sale of attention, is a realisation and circulation function whose costs and margins divide surplus rather than create it. On that reply, users are less like unpaid workers and more like the crowd whose gathering makes a market square valuable to stallholders, a condition of realisation rather than a source of value. The dispute is genuine and unresolved within the tradition, and this article takes no side beyond noting that neither side's resolution rescues the feudal thesis: the first makes enclosure income a form of surplus extraction within a production relation, the second makes it a toll drawing on producing that happens somewhere else, and the fork closes, as it has closed at every application in this article, around the strong position.

### Is attention rent a precise claim or a metaphor?

it is precise only where it names charges levied on advertisers and sellers for access to a monopolised audience, a transfer of surplus earned elsewhere. Where it names users' activity itself as value production, it is a different and bolder theory wearing rent's vocabulary, and it must be argued separately rather than assumed.

Cultural production more broadly offers a gentler application of the same discipline. The incomes of celebrated works divide, under decomposition, into the wages and profits of their ongoing exploitation, the returns to the enterprises that distribute them, and a rent on the rights in works whose reputation has made them conditions that others must license: the adaptation that needs the original, the venue that needs the catalogue, the product that needs the association. Reputation itself functions here as a monopolised condition, built partly by labour, partly by the collective activity of audiences and critics, and partly by the mere passage of time that thins the field of survivors. The rent on reputation is among the purest instances of monopoly rent in the framework's sense, price governed by desire and means against a condition without substitute, and it is also among the least politically charged, which usefully reminds the analyst that the category is an instrument of description before it is a weapon: not every rent identified is a scandal announced, and a tradition that can only denounce its objects will describe them badly.

The limits of the metaphor, then, are these. Properly used, the word picks out one thing only: a payment whose warrant is title to something scarce and fenced, nothing more. It does not name every large income, every resented income, every income detached from visible effort, or every extraction that feels feudal to its victims. Each extension beyond the definition must be argued as an extension, with the mechanism specified and the source of the transfer traced. Writers who observe that discipline will find the category sharp enough for the whole debate. Writers who do not will produce the atmosphere in which the debate usually drowns, where rent means bad income and the conclusion, that bad income has increased, was contained in the definition from the start.

## Reading the debate: a guide to the literature's shapes

A reader who wants to go further into this literature benefits from knowing its characteristic shapes in advance, since the debate's books and essays fall into families whose moves can be recognised and assessed quickly once the families are named. The first family is the announcement: a vivid tour of enclosures, tolls, and dependencies, culminating in the declaration of a new order. Announcements are best read for their phenomenology, which is often excellent, and discounted at their conclusions by exactly the amount of relational evidence they fail to supply. Varoufakis (2023) and Durand (2020) are the family's distinguished members in the present debate, and the discount this article applies to them is the standard one, not a special hostility: their descriptions enter the record, their classifications do not survive the presupposition test.

The second family is the anatomy: a patient catalogue of rent streams across domains, with mechanisms specified and magnitudes handled carefully, culminating in a distributional rather than a categorical conclusion. Christophers (2020) is the family's exemplar, and Mazzucato (2018) a close cousin with a broader canvas about value's public language. Anatomies are the literature's most durable contributions, because their findings survive the fate of any particular verdict about the mode: whatever later theory concludes, the streams will still need their mechanisms, and the anatomists will have mapped them. The third family is the defence of continuity, often shorter and more polemical, leaning on the theory's load-bearing walls and on the record of earlier announcements; Harvey's writings furnish its intellectual capital even when other hands write the pamphlets. Defences are best read for their criteria, and judged by whether the criteria were fixed before the evidence was consulted, as this article has tried to fix them.

### How should a newcomer sequence reading in this debate?

begin with the anatomy, so the phenomena are known in their mechanisms before any verdict is met; proceed to the announcement, whose descriptions will now be recognisable and whose classification can be tested; finish with the continuity defence and the framework's own rent chapters, judging every earlier claim against the architecture assembled last.

A fourth family deserves naming because examinations love it and students neglect it: the internal critique, which accepts a position's premises and derives an embarrassment from them. This article's decisive point is an internal critique of the strong position, and the family is worth cultivating because its products are the hardest arguments to answer in any debate, since they concede the opponent's evidence and dispute only the inference. When a student can construct one internal critique per debate, the student's answers acquire the quality examiners describe as judgement, which is less a gift than a technique: find the category a position cannot do without, state its definition from the position's own sources, and follow the definition where the position declined to follow it. The rent debate hands the technique its material gift-wrapped, in a category whose definition contains its limit, but the technique itself is portable across the whole cluster and far beyond it.

## What students should separate: distribution change and mode change

The pedagogical heart of this article can be compressed into a single separation that students should practise until it becomes automatic: a change in distribution is not a change in the mode of production, and every claim in this debate should be sorted, on first reading, into the register where it belongs. Distributional claims concern shares, weights, and balances: which fraction of the social surplus flows to owners of conditions, which to organising capitals, which to lenders, which remains with labour. Claims of the second kind interrogate machinery: who is hired, on what terms, producing which goods, and under what pressure the whole contraption renews itself each cycle. The two registers use different evidence, admit different kinds of change, and support different conclusions, and nearly every confusion in the debate, in print and in the examination room alike, is a crossing between them, a distributional fact asked to bear a relational conclusion or a relational continuity used to wave away a distributional fact.

Practise the separation on the debate's standard exhibits. Exhibit one: access charges levied by gateways have grown to claim a substantial portion of the margins of producers who sell through them. Sorted: distributional, a shift in the division of surplus between condition-owners and producing capitals, significant for the balance among capitals and for the phase's character, silent by itself about the mode. Exhibit two: the largest gateway enterprises employ their technical and organisational staffs on wages, dismiss them when revenues fall, and reinvest under competitive threat. Sorted: relational, evidence of the persistence of the mode's signature relations at the very commanding heights where supersession was announced. Exhibit three: households devote a rising portion of wages to access payments for necessities. Sorted: distributional with experiential weight, evidence for the phase thesis and for the politics of the toll structure, again silent about the mode. An answer that sorts its exhibits before arguing from them has already outperformed most of the published debate, and the sorting takes less time than the rhetoric it replaces.

### What is the commonest examination error in this debate?

the commonest error is crossing registers: offering a distributional fact, such as a rising rent share or a gateway's large charge, as proof of a relational conclusion about the mode of production. Examiners reward the opposite discipline, sorting each exhibit into its register before arguing, then stating what relational evidence the stronger conclusion would require.

The separation also protects the distributional register from a subtler neglect. Students who have learned that shares do not settle the mode sometimes conclude that shares do not matter, and write answers in which the entire drama of the phase, the toll structure, the asset politics, the recomposition of the owning classes, is dismissed as surface. That conclusion wastes the debate's best material. The framework itself treats distribution as a real and consequential level, the level at which classes experience the mode and organise to contest it, and the middle position's whole contribution is to insist that the period's defining struggles occur there. A complete answer therefore works both registers in sequence: relational continuity established by the architecture and the presupposition test, distributional transformation established by the anatomy literature, and a conclusion that assigns each claim to its level and derives the politics from the level where change actually occurred. That structure, relational floor, distributional drama, levelled conclusion, is the template this article has itself followed, and it transfers to the neighbouring debates of the cluster with minimal adjustment.

Teachers will recognise in the separation a version of a distinction their students meet everywhere in the social sciences and master nowhere reliably: the distinction between a system's constitution and the outcomes the system generates. Constitutions change rarely and are hard to change; outcomes change constantly and are the stuff of news. A commentator class that reports outcomes in constitutional language will announce a new era every season, and a citizenry educated by that commentary will oscillate between revolution and resignation as the headlines alternate. The rent debate is a nearly perfect teaching case for the distinction precisely because its outcome evidence is so strong and its constitutional evidence so absent: the temptation to promote is at maximum, and the discipline of refusing the promotion is therefore exercised at full resistance. That is why the series gives the debate an article of its own and why the transferable yield of the article is a habit, not a headline.

## Verdict and synthesis: the phase, the architecture, and the open threshold

Gather the argument's threads into the verdict they support. Begin the gathering where the article began: a toll is one slice of the social surplus, claimed by whoever holds a fenced condition, and it arrives wearing three costumes, the differential, the absolute, and the singular. Its share of income has risen over the span of decades, by the agreement of all serious positions, and the rise has shifted the balance among capitals, the texture of household dependence, and the politics of the period toward the owners of conditions. The strong position, that a new mode of production has superseded capitalism, fails on three independent grounds: the presupposition test, since rent cannot replace the production whose surplus it divides; the feudal comparison, beaten on bondage, on direct force, and on the missing drive to expand, the very ground its advocates selected; and the crisis test, since toll claims collapse like leveraged bets when the producing beneath them falters, instead of enduring like the dues of a sovereign order. The second position, continuity, survives every test and under-describes the period. The phase account, which keeps the system recognisably capitalist while its proceeds tilt toward proprietors, survives every test and describes the period, and it is adopted here, supplemented by the surrender terms its own literature left implicit.

The defeater bears one final repetition, because a verdict is only as good as its stated conditions of surrender. The continuity judgement falls if researchers ever demonstrate that levies for access, charged upon pursuits conducted wholly outside wage-staffed commodity making, have swollen into a major and expanding slice of all income received. That evidence, and no lesser evidence, would reopen the classification. Its absence, after a debate conducted at high volume over many years by motivated and able proponents, is itself a datum: the strong position has had every incentive to produce relational evidence and has produced distributional evidence instead, and at some point the pattern of production becomes the finding. The threshold stands open, the standard is public, and the verdict is held in the only way a verdict in a living tradition should be held, firmly on the evidence available and surrendered in advance to the evidence specified.

### What is this article's final judgement in one sentence?

growing tolls signal a phase dominated by proprietors inside a persisting capitalist order, not a successor system, since every toll draws breath from surplus thrown up by hired labour; the judgement carries its own surrender terms, falling the day toll income is shown flowing, at scale, from pursuits where that staffing pattern is absent.

Two closing observations concern the tradition rather than the verdict. The first is that the debate has exposed, usefully, the tradition's most urgent theoretical task: the systematic development of monopoly rent, the category on which the period's characteristic incomes depend and which the founding texts left a sketch. A tradition that knows its own unfinished business, and can rank it, is healthier than one that experiences every gap as a scandal to be concealed. The second observation is that the debate vindicates the framework's architecture at the moment of its greatest apparent embarrassment. An order visibly dominated by tolls, gates, and permissions looked, to many, like the refutation of a theory built on production and the wage relation. Handled with its own categories, the same order turned out to exhibit the theory's central claim in an unusually pure form: that distribution, however dramatic, is the division of a product whose origin decides the system's identity, and that origin remains where the theory placed it. The series hub devoted to Marxism and the modern capitalist order develops that vindication across the whole terrain. The present article's contribution to the hub's case is narrower: one argument, pursued through every application the debate could devise, that a levy on production cannot outlive the producing it levies upon.

## Rent and the reproduction of the system: who pays for the conditions of production

Every mode of production must reproduce its own conditions: the labour power, the materials, the infrastructures, and the institutions without which the next cycle cannot begin. Rent intersects that reproduction at a point worth examining, because the owners of conditions stand, in effect, as toll collectors on the road between one cycle and the next. The producer who must renew a licence, re-lease a location, or re-purchase access to a gateway before production can resume experiences rent not as a division of a finished surplus but as a precondition levied in advance, and the advance character of the payment changes its practical weight. A share taken after production is a quarrel over results. A charge levied before production is a power over whether results occur at all, and that power, held by owners of indispensable conditions, is the closest the phase comes to a genuinely structural leverage beyond distribution.

The framework absorbs the point without strain, and the absorption is instructive. Ground rent was always paid in advance in exactly this sense, contracted before the harvest whose surplus would fund it, and the classical analysis treated the advance as a deduction from the capital the producer could deploy, a friction on accumulation built into the mode's digestion of landed property. Advance payment therefore cannot be the marker of a new order, since it is among the oldest features of rent inside the old one. What the advance character does explain is the disproportionate political attention that gateway owners command relative even to their share of income: a claimant who can halt the cycle commands more fear than a claimant who merely shares its fruits, and the fear is rational. Analysis should register the leverage, name it as a power over reproduction, and still classify it by the flow it levies upon, which remains the surplus of a production the owner does not organise.

### Does advance payment of rent give owners power beyond a share of income?

yes. A charge levied before production resumes is a power over whether the next cycle begins, not merely a claim on the last cycle's results. That leverage explains gateway owners' political weight, while remaining, in the framework, a friction on accumulation of a kind ground rent always exerted.

Reproduction also exposes the collective dependence of condition-owners on the very public goods their ideology often discounts. A location is valuable because a society surrounds it: roads, safety, schools, a labour force educated and kept healthy at collective expense. A gateway is valuable because a public internet, a literate population, and a legal order underwrite it. A right is valuable because courts enforce it. The rent recipient's income is therefore, in substantial part, a private capture of collectively produced conditions, a point the middle position's literature presses and the framework's analysis of ground rent anticipated when it treated the urban increment as society's gift to the owner. The capture is not theft in any legal sense; it is the ordinary operation of a property order that assigns the yield of collective conditions to whoever holds title to the point where the conditions concentrate. But naming the capture correctly reframes the fiscal question once more: taxing rent is, in large part, the collectivity reclaiming the yield of its own contributions, which is why the politics of rent taxation, though ferocious, has a moral clarity that the taxation of production lacks, and why owners resist it with a ferocity proportional to the clarity.

There is a reproductive risk on the other side that completes the picture. If the tolls on reproduction rise too high, the cycle itself falters: producers decline to renew, investment migrates to jurisdictions or forms where the tolls are lighter, and the surplus from which all shares are drawn contracts. The system's self-correction operates through that faltering, crudely and with much collateral damage, as capitals vote with their feet against excessive extraction and condition-owners discover the host-geometry described earlier. The phase's stability therefore depends on a balance that no one is in charge of striking, between tolls high enough to reward condition-ownership and low enough to keep production renewing itself beneath them. Observers who wonder why gateway charges so often hover at levels producers describe as just bearable are observing that balance being struck in real time, by actors who understand their dependence even when their public rhetoric denies it.

## Fictitious capital and the capitalisation of access

The capitalisation of rent, introduced earlier, deserves a fuller treatment because it connects the rent debate to the theory of fictitious capital, one of the framework's most fertile and least popularised ideas. A capitalised rent is a price paid for an expected stream of transfers, and the price behaves in markets as though it were a sum of capital, although no corresponding value was produced in creating the condition. The third volume's analysis of interest-bearing capital supplies the template: any regular revenue, divided by the prevailing rate of interest, presents itself as the yield on an imaginary principal, and the imaginary principal then trades, is borrowed against, and anchors portfolios as though it were wealth of the same solidity as a factory. The tradition calls such capital fictitious not because it is imaginary in its effects, which are brutally real, but because its magnitude is a capitalisation of claims rather than an accumulation of produced value, and the claims can evaporate when the expected transfers fail, without a single machine rusting or a single skill being lost.

Access positions capitalise on an heroic scale, and the heroism of the scale is data for the debate. Valuations of gateway enterprises rest on expected future levies against the activity of producers and users, discounted into the present at rates that assume the activity's continued growth. The valuation is therefore a bet, placed by the whole market, on the future surplus of other people's production, and its fluctuations track changes in that expectation far more sensitively than they track anything the enterprise itself produces. A mode genuinely founded on rent would presumably value its rent assets with the steadiness of a ground rent in a settled agrarian order. The observed valuations behave instead like leveraged claims on a capitalist future, rising and collapsing with the prospects of the production they tax, which is the behaviour the framework predicts for fictitious capital and the opposite of the behaviour the strong position's imagery suggests.

### What does capitalisation do to a rent stream?

to capitalise a toll is to price tomorrow's expected takings as a lump sum in the present, treating the flow as though it were interest due on a principal nobody ever advanced. The price then trades and anchors credit as fictitious capital, real in its effects yet resting entirely on future surplus that production elsewhere must actually deliver.

The fictitious character of capitalised rent also explains a recurring puzzle in the debate's evidence: the coexistence of enormous owner wealth with the claim, made by the same owners' defenders, that their incomes are modest or their margins thin. Both can be true, because wealth in this register is capitalised expectation while income is the current transfer, and a thin current transfer, expected to persist and grow, capitalises into a fortune. Arguments that move between wealth and income without marking the conversion are therefore unreliable in both directions: the strong position points to fortunes as proof of a rent order, when the fortunes are expectations about a capitalist future; the continuity position points to modest margins as proof that nothing has changed, when the capitalised claims on the future division of surplus have in fact been rearranged on a grand scale. The middle position, which is about distribution, should properly be stated in both registers, current shares and capitalised claims, and its strongest form does so, treating the rearrangement of claims on future surplus as the phase's deepest fact.

For the student, the lesson of this section is a conversion rule: whenever a debate trades in valuations, translate them back into the streams they capitalise before arguing from them, and whenever it trades in streams, remember that markets have already capitalised them into the wealth figures that dominate the headlines. The translation is mechanical, a division by an expected rate of return, but performing it dissolves a surprising quantity of the debate's apparent paradoxes, including the paradox of owners who are simultaneously, in income terms, modest claimants and, in wealth terms, the richest actors in the system. Both descriptions are the same fact read at two points of the conversion, and the framework, alone among the debate's vocabularies, insists on reading it at both.

## Small producers, petty rent, and the debate's forgotten middle

The debate's dramatis personae are giants: gateway owners, great landlords of the digital and urban landscapes, and the capitals that pay their tolls. Between the giants moves a population the debate largely forgets, the small producers and petty owners whose relation to rent is double and contradictory, and whose experience complicates every camp's story. The artisan who sells through a gateway is a toll payer. The same artisan, owning a small premises whose location has appreciated, is a rent beneficiary. The tradesperson whose pension holds shares in gateway enterprises receives, at one remove, a fragment of the levies the tradesperson's own business pays. The framework has a classical name for the stratum occupying such crossed positions, the petty bourgeoisie, and a classical expectation that its politics will oscillate, since its interests genuinely point in opposed directions at once. The rentier phase intensifies the crossing, because access tolls reach down to the smallest producer while asset ownership, through housing and pensions, reaches down just as far in the other direction.

That crossing explains political patterns that baffle the debate's cleaner narratives. Campaigns against gateway charges draw support from small producers who simultaneously resist, as homeowners, any measure that would deflate the location rents capitalised in their houses. Governments court the asset-holding small owner while taxing, in effect, the access-paying small producer, and the same household applauds the first policy and resents the second without experiencing the pair as a contradiction, because the two policies touch two different relations that a single budget silently merges. Analysis that sorts households by a single relation, rentier or producer, will misread this politics every time. The decomposition habit, applied to households rather than to enterprises, is the corrective: most households in the phase are knots of opposed flows, and their politics is the resultant of the knot, not the expression of a side.

### Why do small producers often hold contradictory positions on rent?

the small producer stands on both banks of the river: charged at the gateway as a seller, yet quietly enriched as a homeowner whose plot has appreciated and as a pension saver holding fragments of the very gateways doing the charging. Ballots cast from such a knot naturally zigzag, following whichever flow pinches or swells that season.

The forgotten middle also supplies a corrective to the strong position's imagery of vassalage. The small producer inside a gateway is dependent, certainly, and the dependence can be abused, but the same producer typically multi-homes, selling through several channels, maintaining an independent presence where possible, and treating the gateway as one cost among others to be minimised, arbitraged, and occasionally abandoned when a rival offers better terms. That behaviour is the behaviour of a petty commodity producer in a market, calculating and mobile within constraints, and it resembles the conduct of tenants under feudalism, who neither chose their lord nor arbitraged among lords, very little. Where the dependence does approach the absolute, where a single gateway is the only road to a livelihood and its terms are take-or-leave, the case deserves the anger it attracts, and the remedy that the framework's analysis suggests is the remedy for monopoly in any of its forms: the construction of alternatives, the regulation of the barrier, the socialisation of the condition, in ascending order of ambition. None of the remedies requires, or is clarified by, the declaration of a new mode.

There is a lesson here about the scale at which classification should be performed. Modes of production are properties of whole social formations, not of individual experiences, and the most dependent small producer's experience, however feudal it feels, is an experience inside a formation whose classification is settled by its dominant relations taken together. Testimony is evidence about texture; classification is a judgement about structure; and a debate that promotes testimony into classification will be captured by whichever witnesses are most vivid. The strong position's literature is rich in vivid witnesses, and this article has treated their testimony as data about the phase throughout. What it has refused is the promotion, and the refusal, applied consistently, is the difference between an analysis and a mood with footnotes.

## The debate's stakes for how capitalism is criticised

Underneath the classification dispute sits a quieter dispute about the proper object of criticism, and surfacing it explains why the debate generates more heat than its apparently scholastic question seems to warrant. If the system's offence is located in rent, in the unearned income of owners who produce nothing, then the implied ideal is a capitalism purified of rent, where producers keep what they make and the industrious are rewarded, a criticism that the tradition has a name for and a history with: it is the criticism of the Ricardian socialists and of every producerism since, which Marx himself engaged and partly rejected, on the ground that profit and rent are branches of one tree and that sawing off the uglier branch leaves the tree, the extraction of surplus labour through the wage relation, standing untouched. If, by contrast, the offence is located in the wage relation itself, then rent's rise is a symptom and a redistribution among the beneficiaries of that relation, and criticising it while sparing the relation mistakes the family's internal quarrel for the family's secret.

The stakes are practical. A politics built on the rent critique alone tends toward an alliance of producers, productive capitals and workers together, against the unproductive owners, and that alliance has a long record of dissolving at the moment the productive partner's own extraction is questioned. A politics built on the framework's full architecture can still fight rent, on distributional grounds this article has elaborated, but it fights with its eyes open about the company it keeps and the point at which the company's interests diverge. The middle position, interestingly, is neutral equipment for this deeper dispute: its phase analysis can serve either politics, which may be why both the producerist critics and the framework's orthodox defenders can cite the anatomy literature with equal comfort. What the anatomy cannot do is settle the location of the offence, because that is a theoretical and political choice, not a measurement.

### Why does the location of the offence matter for criticism?

because a critique aimed only at rent implies an ideal of capitalism purified of unearned income, leaving the extraction of surplus through the wage relation untouched, while the framework treats profit and rent as branches of one tree. The choice between those critiques decides alliances, remedies, and what a successful reform would even mean.

Stating the deeper dispute also clarifies the strong position's appeal to its audience. The feudal thesis offers a criticism with a clean villain and a lost golden age: once, firms competed to produce; now, lords levy and others pay. The lost golden age is the tell. Capitalism in its competitive phases was not a commonwealth of rewarded producers; it was the system whose factory discipline, dispossession, and crisis the founding texts anatomised, and nostalgia for it, however unintended, is a strange position for its critics to occupy. The framework's alternative is less consoling and more exact: there was no golden age, there is a single mode passing through phases, its extraction wears different masks in different phases, and the mask of the toll, for all its archaic colouring, covers the same relation that the mask of the factory gate covered before it. Criticism that keeps that continuity in view cannot be recruited into nostalgia, and cannot be surprised when abolishing a toll leaves the relation that generated tolls busily generating their successors.

## Measurement without magnitudes: how the phase claim can be evidenced responsibly

This article has refused, under its decay discipline, to rest any claim on a snapshot magnitude, and a sceptical reader is entitled to ask what responsible evidence for a phase claim looks like when the usual exhibits, a fresh share estimate, a fresh valuation, a fresh ranking of fortunes, are all ruled out as dating and perishable. The answer is that phase claims rest on a different evidentiary diet: on the direction of movements sustained across decades, on the multiplication of rent-form relations across domains, on the legal archaeology of the scarcities involved, and on the testimony of the system's own actors about where reward concentrates. Each element of that diet is available without a single perishable figure, and the diet is arguably more nutritious than the snapshot habit it replaces, since a direction sustained across decades is harder to fake, and harder to reverse, than any single year's measurement.

Consider the legal archaeology in particular, since it is the element least used in the popular debate. Scarcities that yield rent leave a paper trail: statutes creating and extending exclusive rights, permissions rationed and then traded, planning decisions concentrating development, treaties harmonising the protection of claims across borders. A researcher can reconstruct, domain by domain, the sequence of decisions by which a condition became ownable, excludable, and chargeable, and can date the rent stream's birth to those decisions rather than to any technological necessity. Where the archaeology shows the scarcity arriving by statute before the income arrives by market, the rent interpretation is confirmed in the strongest available way, and the phase claim gains a mechanism, not merely a correlation: the period's distributional shift was built, piece by piece, by identifiable legal acts, each of which is in principle revisable. That is the kind of evidence on which the middle position, at its best, actually rests, and it is evidence a reader can verify in any decade without the article that reports it going stale.

### What evidence supports a phase claim without relying on perishable figures?

long arcs of movement rather than snapshots: toll-form relations breeding across unconnected fields, a paper trail in which statutes manufacture scarcities ahead of the incomes that follow them, and the spectacle of ambitious capital striving, above all, to possess passages and permissions instead of workshops. None of those exhibits carries an expiry date.

The revealed preference of capital is the diet's most underused element. Watch what the system's most successful actors strive to become. Where the commanding ambition of an enterprise is to own the condition on which others depend, the gateway, the standard, the indispensable right, the scarce location, rather than to excel at the production the condition serves, the ambition itself is evidence about where the gradient of reward points, and it is evidence supplied by the actors with the most at stake in reading the gradient correctly. Speeches by such actors, announcing their intention to be the toll rather than the traffic, deserve citation in this debate more than they usually receive, since a system's self-understanding, while never authoritative, is rarely worthless. When the traffic's owners aspire to become tolls and the tolls' owners never aspire to become traffic, the direction of the phase is legible without a single statistic, and the legibility survives the decay that destroys every dated exhibit.

A responsible phase claim also states its own measurement defeaters, in the same spirit as the verdict's defeater. The phase reading would be weakened if the multiplication of rent forms proved to be a relabelling, if the legal archaeology showed the scarcities to be technologically compelled rather than statutorily chosen, or if the direction of shares, sustained over decades, reversed while the legal architecture stood unchanged, which would suggest the architecture was not the driver the reading takes it to be. Stating those conditions does not weaken the position in the way debaters fear; it converts the position from a narrative into a hypothesis, and narratives, unlike hypotheses, can never be improved by evidence, only repeated at higher volume. The debate's literature contains too many narratives already, on every side, and the reader who learns to demand defeaters from each camp will have acquired, from this article, a tool worth more than its verdict.

## Objections from within the framework: three friendly challenges

A verdict that has answered its enemies should finally face its friends, since the sharpest remaining difficulties for this article's position come from inside the framework it defends. The first friendly challenge concerns absolute rent's foundations. The founding explanation of absolute rent depended on a particular claim about the composition of agricultural capital and the barrier landed property poses to the equalisation of profit, and later economists within the tradition have disputed whether the mechanism survives the equalisation it presupposes being blocked. If absolute rent's classical mechanism fails, does the analysis of withholding, on which the access debates lean, fail with it? The honest answer distinguishes the mechanism from the phenomenon. The phenomenon, owners of a necessary condition extracting a payment by the power to withhold, is observable wherever the power exists, and later reformulations ground it in monopoly pricing and in the general theory of barriers rather than in the contested composition claim. The challenge therefore disciplines the article's use of the category, requiring the withholding analysis to be stated in its reformulated, monopoly-based form, but the verdict stands clear of it, being carried by the architecture of surplus as a whole instead of by any one mechanism for slicing it.

The second friendly challenge concerns the value theory itself. The whole presupposition argument runs inside the labour theory of value's account of surplus, and a reader who rejects that account, on grounds argued in other articles of this series and in a vast external literature, will find the decisive point question-begging: rent presupposes production only if production by labour is where value originates. The challenge is correctly aimed, and the correct response is candour about scope. This article's verdict is a verdict within the framework the debate's participants themselves invoke, which is the standard the series pattern, internal incoherence, requires. The strong position claims the framework's authority for its categories; it is therefore legitimately judged by the framework's architecture. A reader who rejects the architecture wholesale is not refuted by this article but is also not addressed by it, since that reader's quarrel is with the tradition entire, and must be fought on the ground of the value theory itself, where the series maintains its own dedicated defences.

### Does the verdict depend on accepting the whole value theory?

the verdict is internal to the framework the debate's participants invoke, since the strong position claims that framework's categories for its own thesis. Judged by the architecture it appeals to, the thesis is incoherent; a reader rejecting the architecture entirely is engaged in a different and larger argument about the value theory itself.

The third friendly challenge is the most practical. Suppose the verdict is right and the phase reading holds: does anything follow for how the tradition should write about the present, beyond repeating that the mode persists? Three things follow, and they amount to a small programme. Write about distribution with the same seriousness the tradition has historically reserved for production, since the phase's battles are distributional and the tradition's tools for them, the rent categories above all, need the development this article has repeatedly demanded. Write about law as a productive force in its own right, since the phase's scarcities are statutory artefacts and the tradition's relative neglect of legal construction leaves its best political openings unmapped. And write about the experience of dependence without embarrassment and without promotion, registering the toll texture of daily life as the phase's lived truth while refusing, on the record and with reasons, to call that truth a new mode. A tradition that executes that programme will find the debate's next decade more fruitful than its last, whichever way the open threshold eventually breaks.

## The rent debate in the classroom and the newsroom: two professional uses

Professions that must compress the debate for a living, teachers and journalists, face opposite temptations, and naming both is a service to each. The teacher's temptation is symmetry: to present the three positions as a balanced menu, allocate them equal time and equal respect, and let the lesson end in a survey where a judgement was owed. Symmetry is a virtue in presenting positions and a vice in assessing them, since the positions are not equally supported, and a student who leaves believing the debate is a matter of taste has been taught the literature and cheated of the argument. The remedy is the worked grading this article modelled: present at full strength, then assess against a criterion stated in advance, then conclude with the defeater that keeps the conclusion honest. Students remember the structure long after the content fades, and the structure is the actual curriculum.

The journalist's temptation is the headline: a resurrected feudalism sells copies, while a distributional phase inside a familiar system sounds like a seminar title. Deadline pressure and the economics of attention both reward the announcement over the anatomy, and the result is a public sphere in which the strong position's vocabulary circulates far beyond its evidence, until the metaphor's familiarity is itself mistaken for confirmation. The remedy available to the conscientious journalist is not to suppress the vivid thesis, which is legitimately news, but to attach the sorting question to it as routinely as a health warning: what would have to be true, about production relations rather than income shares, for this claim to hold, and has that been shown? A single such sentence, standardised across coverage, would raise the debate's public quality more than any number of corrective essays, because it teaches the reader the criterion at the moment the claim is made.

### What single question should coverage of this debate always attach?

one enquiry, appended as routinely as a byline: what has been shown about the staffing and organisation of making itself, not about anybody's bank balance, that would justify talk of replacement? Posed at the instant a grand claim lands, the enquiry trains the audience's instincts and quietly empties claims that arrive carrying only income figures.

Both professions, notice, are served by the same underlying asset: the criterion, fixed in advance and stated in plain words. That is not an accident of this debate. In any controversy where levels of analysis are easily crossed, the professional's first duty is to the level distinction, and the amateur's characteristic failure is to argue across levels without noticing. The rent debate's gift to general education is that its levels are unusually clean, distribution here, relations there, and its crossing is unusually tempting, which makes it the ideal training ground. A student or a reader who has learned, on this material, to ask which level a claim lives on before asking whether it is true has learned something that will serve in controversies the authors of this series cannot foresee, which is the deepest justification for teaching a debate whose verdict might one day be reversed by its own stated defeater.

## Rent and monopoly in the theory of the firm: why enterprises seek conditions

Step inside the enterprise and the phase looks different again: not as a drama of lords and producers but as a gradient every management can feel. A firm that competes on production alone earns the average rate of profit, ground down by rivals toward the level that merely keeps capital in the industry. A firm that acquires a condition, a protected right, a scarce location, a gateway position, a standard others must meet, earns above that rate for as long as the condition holds, and the excess is rent or contains it. The gradient between the two earnings is the most powerful incentive in the system, more powerful than any exhortation to innovate or to serve, and enterprises follow it with the reliability of water flowing downhill. Research budgets bend toward patentable and protectable results rather than toward merely useful ones. Expansion budgets bend toward acquiring positions rather than toward enlarging capacity. Legal budgets grow, because the condition is a legal artefact and its defence is a production cost of the rent. None of this requires a single manager to intend anything discreditable; the gradient intends it for them.

That internal view corrects a sentimental error in some rent criticism, the picture of rentier income as the product of idleness. The defence and extension of conditions is intensely active: litigated, lobbied, engineered, and managed by large staffs of highly paid wage labour. The activity is real and its product is the barrier. The framework's category is unmoved by the bustle, the toll being classified by where its money originates, never by how energetically its collection was pursued, and indeed the bustle is itself one of rent's social costs, the resources consumed in creating and contesting transfers rather than in producing goods, which economists of several schools have catalogued under the heading of rent-seeking. A phase in which a growing share of the ablest labour is employed in seeking, defending, and arbitraging transfers is a phase misallocating its talent on a grand scale, and that misallocation, measurable in careers rather than in national accounts, may be the phase's most quietly damaging feature.

### What is rent-seeking, and why does its growth matter beyond the transfers themselves?

rent-seeking is the expenditure of real resources, talent, legal effort, lobbying, and engineering, on creating, capturing, or defending the barriers from which rent flows. Its growth matters because those resources produce transfers rather than goods, so a phase rich in rent-seeking misallocates its ablest labour even where every individual transfer looks small.

The firm's-eye view also explains the phase's characteristic corporate form, the conglomerate of conditions, in which a single enterprise assembles a portfolio of rights, locations, gateways, and standards whose common property is not any product but excludability. Such an enterprise is hard to describe in the vocabulary of industry, since its unity is not a technology or a market but a legal relation, and its strategy is the management of a portfolio of barriers: acquire where barriers are cheap, defend where they are challenged, extend where legislatures are receptive, and harvest where they are secure. Describing that form accurately, without either the feudal metaphor or the pretence that it is a factory with better branding, is one of the descriptive tasks the middle position's literature has begun and the tradition's theory of monopoly rent, once developed, would complete. Description of that quality is not a preliminary to the real work of analysis. In a debate drowned in metaphor, description of that quality is the real work.

## A note on method: the internal-incoherence pattern and its limits

This article instantiates a pattern the series uses wherever a debate permits it, and a short methodological note will help readers recognise the pattern elsewhere and judge its strength honestly. The internal-incoherence pattern assesses a claim against the framework its own proponents invoke. Its force comes from consent: the proponent has already accepted the premises, the definitions, and the architecture, so a conclusion derived from them cannot be evaded by disputing the framework without abandoning the claim's own foundations. The rent debate is the pattern's ideal case, because the strong position's central category, rent, carries its refutation inside its definition, and the derivation from definition to verdict is short enough to be checked by any reader in a few minutes. Arguments of this quality are rare in social theory, where premises are usually contested all the way down, and their rarity is why the series treats this article as an organising piece for its cluster.

The pattern has limits that honesty requires stating. It settles claims only for those who accept the invoked framework, and it settles them only at the level the framework's definitions reach. It does not settle empirical questions, such as the actual weight of rent in the period's distribution, which require the anatomy literature's methods. It does not settle questions the framework's categories fit badly, which is why the underdevelopment of monopoly rent matters: an internal critique is only as strong as the definition it unfolds, and a sketchy definition yields a sketchy critique at exactly the points where the sketch runs out. And it does not forbid a proponent from abandoning the framework and restating the thesis in other terms, as a claim about power, dependence, or governance without the rent category's commitments. Such a restated thesis might even be defensible. But it would be a different thesis, weaker in its claim to name a mode of production, and the pattern's service is precisely to force that choice into the open: keep the vocabulary and accept its limits, or keep the conclusion and find it a new foundation.

### When is an internal critique stronger than an external one?

an internal critique is stronger when the target claim borrows its authority from the very framework whose definitions refute it, because the proponent cannot dispute the premises without abandoning the claim's foundation. Its strength fades where the framework's relevant category is itself underdeveloped or where the claim is restated without the borrowed vocabulary.

Readers can apply the pattern's discipline in reverse, as a check on their own side. The verdict of this article also invokes the framework, and its definitions can be unfolded against it: if rent is a share of surplus, is the middle position's phase, defined by rent's dominance in distribution, coherent as stated, or does dominance in distribution imply, at some magnitude, a change in the relations that the position denies? The question was faced earlier and answered with the threshold: dominance becomes relational when the income arises from activity outside the wage and commodity form. The answer is available because the definitions are precise enough to generate the question and constrain its answer, which is the mark of a framework earning its keep. Traditions decay when their definitions stop generating hard questions for their own conclusions. By that test, whatever the fate of its verdict, the framework's performance in this debate is evidence of life.

## Synthesis for the working reader: the argument on one page

For the reader who must carry this debate into a seminar, a newsroom, a picket line briefing, or an examination hall, the whole argument compresses into a sequence that can be rehearsed in a minute and expanded for an hour. Start from the levy itself: money reaching a proprietor purely because a scarce, fenced condition stands in others' way, drawn always from value that workers somewhere else have already created. Profit is the form in which that surplus is first appropriated by the capitals that organise production. Three positions contest the meaning of rent's growth: a new mode has arrived; capitalism is behaving as it always has; a rentier phase has rearranged distribution within an unchanged mode. The decisive test is presupposition: rent cannot replace profit, because rent divides what profit's production creates, and a completed rent order would starve its own spring. Put the feudal comparison to its own chosen tests, bondage, personal coercion, and the absence of any drive to accumulate, and it fails each one in turn. Admit, next, the embarrassing gap: the pigeonhole reserved for singular holdings was left nearly empty by the founding author, so every confident filing under it deserves a visible discount. The verdict is the phase, held under a public defeater: the classification falls once access revenue is shown to spring, on a large and swelling scale, from pursuits that wage-staffed commodity making never touches.

Each sentence of that compression opens into a section of this article, and the compression is offered as a map rather than a substitute. Used well, it lets a reader locate any new contribution to the debate within seconds: identify which sentence the contribution addresses, check whether it brings relational evidence or distributional evidence, and file it accordingly. Used badly, as a catechism, it would betray the article's own teaching, since the sentences are conclusions of arguments, and conclusions detached from their arguments are exactly what the strong position's headlines are. The distinction between a map and a catechism is the distinction between knowing where the argument lives and merely knowing how the argument ends, and only the first kind of knowledge survives contact with a good opponent.

### What is the one-page version of the verdict?

tolls partition a product they never made, so swollen tolls mean a reshuffled division inside the old order rather than a new one; the feudal comparison loses on every axis it names; the phase account fits what is known; and the whole judgement is mortgaged to one specified discovery about how producing is staffed.

There remains the question of what to do with a verdict, once held, and the article closes its analytical work by answering at the level of intellectual conduct rather than of programme. Hold the classification firmly and the classifications loosely: firmly, because the mode question is settled by architecture and by the failure of every attempt to supply relational evidence; loosely, at the level of individual streams, because the category that classifies them is unfinished and each stream's decomposition is revisable work. Argue about distribution as though it mattered most, because for the period's inhabitants it does. And keep the defeater in view, not as a ritual of humility but as an instrument panel: the day the specified evidence appears, the honest analyst changes the verdict in public, and the tradition's credibility, which is its only real capital, appreciates by exactly the amount that such changes cost their makers.

## Rent in the theory of distribution: why the shares fight

Step back from the debate's particulars and the rent question reveals itself as one chapter in the general theory of distribution, the theory of how a society's product divides among the classes and categories that claim it. That theory has a peculiar status in the framework: distribution is determined by production, in the sense that the relations governing production fix the forms in which the product can be claimed, wages, profit, interest, rent, taxes, yet within those forms the actual division is settled by contest, by organisation, by law, and by the balance of forces in each period. The rentier phase is, in that light, a period in which one form of claim has been winning the contest persistently, across decades and domains, and the interesting theoretical question is not whether the winning is possible, the architecture always allowed it, but what configuration of law, organisation, and scarcity has made this claimant so successful for so long.

Part of the answer lies in the asymmetry of organisation among claimants. Owners of a condition are, by the nature of the asset, a defined and often small group with a permanent, concentrated interest in the barrier's defence, and the barrier is usually a legal artefact that a legislature can be petitioned to maintain. The parties who pay the rent, producers, users, households, are diffuse, differently affected in each domain, and rarely organised as payers of a particular toll. Contest theory predicts the outcome of such asymmetry without any conspiracy: the concentrated claimant wins the legislative margin repeatedly, each victory is small, and the accumulation of small victories across decades is a phase. The prediction has the virtue of explaining the phase's most puzzling feature, its advance under governments of every declared ideology, since the asymmetry operates on all of them impartially, rewarding whichever claimants are organised at the point where the rule is written.

### Why do rent claimants keep winning the distributional contest?

the toll's beneficiaries are few, identifiable, and permanently motivated to lobby for the statute or permission underpinning their inflow, whereas the toll's payers are millions of strangers who seldom organise around any single charge. Legislatures hear one side at full volume and the other as static, decade after decade, and the accumulated asymmetry is the phase.

A second strand of the explanation concerns scarcity's shifting material basis. Production has a long tendency to cheapen reproducible things: goods whose supply can expand fall in relative price as productivity rises, and the incomes attached to making them are competed toward the average. Conditions that cannot be reproduced, locations, rights, assembled audiences, unique positions, escape that tendency, and their relative weight in the economy's cost structure therefore rises mechanically as everything reproducible cheapens around them. Economists outside the tradition have noticed the same arithmetic in the rising share of housing and land in national wealth and in the cost disease of services that resist productivity growth. Inside the framework the arithmetic has a distributional translation: the surplus increasingly presents itself for division at points where a non-reproducible condition must be paid, so the rent form's share grows even if no claimant's behaviour changes and no legislature lifts a finger. A complete account of the phase needs both mechanisms, the organised defence of barriers and the arithmetic of reproducibility, and the debate's writing earns its keep precisely when it keeps the two distinct, since they imply different remedies: organisation answered by counter-organisation and legal reform, arithmetic answered only by socialising the yield of the non-reproducible or by expanding its effective supply.

That distinction yields the article's last analytical refinement of the middle position. The phase has two engines, one political and one arithmetical, and they require different handling. Statutory scarcities, the political engine's product, can be redesigned at their root. Natural and positional scarcities, the arithmetical engine's product, cannot be abolished, but their yield can be collected publicly rather than pocketed privately, which converts the rent from a private toll into a social fund without pretending the scarcity away. A programme that confuses the engines will either promise to abolish what cannot be abolished or neglect to redesign what plainly can be. The framework's categories, differential, absolute, monopoly, map onto the engines closely enough to guide the sorting, which is a final demonstration that the old machinery, honestly maintained and admittedly incomplete, still does work that no newer vocabulary on offer can do without it.

## The debate's future: what to watch

A verdict held under a defeater should specify its own watch list, the developments that would strain it soonest, so that readers can monitor the classification rather than merely inherit it. Four developments deserve watching, each chosen because it bears on relations rather than on shares. First, watch whether activity inside the great enclosures drifts from commodity production by wage labour and independent producers toward forms organised on other principles, administered allocation, status-based entitlement, direct provision against obligation, since that drift is the defeater's territory and its advance would be the strong position's first genuine evidence. Second, watch whether the owners of conditions begin to fuse economic levy with direct political jurisdiction in durable, hereditary, or status-bound forms, since that fusion, and not the levy alone, is the feudal signature. Third, watch whether wage labour's share of the working population, and its role in producing the social product, contracts toward marginality rather than merely sharing income less favourably, since marginality of the relation, not stinginess of its rewards, is what a mode change requires. Fourth, watch the framework itself: whether the constructive development of monopoly rent, when it comes, confirms the classifications this article has offered or dissolves them into a finer scheme in which the debate's very terms are redrawn.

None of the four, on the evidence available to this article, is advancing at a pace that threatens the verdict. The first shows experimentation at the margins of enclosures whose cores remain markets. The second shows lobbying and governance inside enterprises, formidable but exercised through the separated, legal forms of the mode. The third shows a wage relation under pressure in its rewards and its security, yet still the overwhelmingly dominant organiser of production, with the pressures themselves best explained as distributional defeats within it. The fourth is the slowest and the most certain to arrive eventually, since unfinished categories in living traditions do get finished, usually by a generation that finds the sketch exactly where the need is. When it arrives, this article's classifications should be re-examined in its light, and the article's verdict, resting on the surplus architecture rather than on the sketch, should emerge intact from the re-examination even if several of its stream-by-stream judgements do not.

### What developments would most strain this article's verdict?

drift inside enclosures away from commodity production and wage labour toward administered or status-based organisation; fusion of the access levy with durable political jurisdiction; contraction of wage labour toward marginality in producing the social product; and a future theory of monopoly rent that redraws the categories on which the stream classifications rest.

A closing word on the temper in which to hold all of this. The rent debate attracts eschatological excitement because it seems to promise that history has turned a corner and the old maps can be discarded. The framework's counsel is drier and, in the long run, kinder: maps are discarded when the territory changes its relations, not when its weather changes its mood, and the discipline of telling weather from territory is what analysis is for. The weather of the period is genuinely rentier, heavy with tolls and permissions, and nobody should be talked out of feeling its weight. The territory underneath remains the one the old map describes, and the map, corrected at its thin places and read with its levels kept separate, still guides the traveller better than the announcement that the world has ended and begun again.

## Rent and the price of necessities: the distributional transmission belt

One channel connects the rent debate to daily politics more directly than any other, and it merits extended treatment in its own right: the transmission of rent into the prices of necessities. Where a necessity, shelter, energy, transport, communication, the credentials required for employment, is supplied through a monopolised condition, the rent on that condition enters the price every household pays, and it enters regressively, since necessities claim a larger share of small incomes than of large ones. A rise in the rent component of necessities is therefore equivalent, in its incidence, to a tax levied at a rate that falls as income rises, collected not by a state answerable to electors but by owners answerable to no one, and spent not on public purposes but as private revenue. The framework's language for this is exact: it is a deduction from the real wage, effected through prices rather than through the wage bargain, and it explains why periods of rentier advance can feel like periods of wage decline even where money wages and even measured real wages, computed against an average basket, appear stable. The basket that matters to a household is weighted by its own necessities, and where the rent-laden necessities dominate that basket, the household's experience and the statistic part company.

The transmission belt also runs in the other direction, into the wage bargain itself. Employers facing a workforce whose cost of living is swollen by rent-laden necessities face pressure for higher money wages, and the contest over who finally bears the rent, the household through prices, the employer through wages, or the productive capital through squeezed margins, is a three-way distributional struggle that runs continuously beneath the surface of industrial relations. Classical theory knew this struggle in its agrarian form, where the price of subsistence governed the wage and the rent of land stood behind the price of subsistence, so that landlords appeared, to the classical economists' indignation, as taxing both labour and capital through the price of bread. The modern form substitutes a basket of accessed necessities for the loaf, but the structure is recognisably the same, and recognising it gives the lie, once more, to the novelty claimed for the phase: the transmission belt is old, its cargo is new, and the distinction between structure and cargo is the distinction this article has enforced from its first page.

### How does rent on necessities act like a regressive tax?

a levy hidden inside the price of essentials bites hardest where budgets are thinnest, since shelter, heat, travel, and credentials devour a far bigger fraction of a modest pay packet than of a fat one. No electorate voted for this levy and no public service answers for it, yet it shaves the real wage as surely as a pay cut.

A further turn of the belt concerns the state's own finances. Where necessities are rent-laden and households are squeezed, the state is drawn into compensating provision, subsidies for shelter, support for energy costs, tax credits that top up wages the market will not raise, and each compensation is, in distributional substance, a public transfer that flows through the household to the owner of the condition, underwriting the rent it purports to relieve. Analysts across traditions have noticed the pattern in housing support that capitalises into rents, and the framework generalises the noticing: a state that compensates payers without touching the scarcity or its yield becomes the rent's guarantor of last resort, socialising the payer's side of the toll while privatising the receiver's. The political economy of the phase is incomplete until that guarantor role is named, since it explains the otherwise puzzling fiscal weight of rentier advance, the way a distributional shift toward owners presents itself in budgets as a growing public obligation.

## Productive and unproductive once more: rent's place in the great division

The series has elsewhere drawn the distinction between productive and unproductive labour at length, and the rent debate gains clarity when its categories are set against that distinction explicitly. Rent receipt is not labour at all, productive or unproductive; it is a revenue, and the activities surrounding it divide by the usual test. The labour of creating a condition, building the infrastructure, writing the work, surveying the deposit, is productive where it is exchanged against capital and produces value. The labour of administering the rent, metering access, enforcing exclusions, litigating boundaries, collecting payments, creates nothing, in the theory's technical usage: indispensable to the proprietor's claim, frequently expert, and exhausted entirely by the securing of a transfer from one pocket to another. A rentier phase therefore has a characteristic employment signature, a swelling of the apparatus of metering, enforcement, and claim administration relative to the apparatus of production, and that signature is observable in occupational structure without any appeal to perishable magnitudes: the phase employs more gatekeepers, in proportion, and the gatekeepers' own wages are paid, in the final analysis, out of the surplus their gates divide.

The signature matters because it answers a sentimental objection sometimes raised against the framework's tone: that calling rent unproductive insults the people who work around it. The distinction, as the series has insisted in every application, tracks relations rather than dignity. The enforcement lawyer, the rights administrator, the metering engineer, each works, often brilliantly, and each is exploited or rewarded according to the usual laws of the labour market. What the analysis denies is only that their labour creates the value their employer's revenue represents. Confusing the denial with a sneer is the oldest misreading of the productive and unproductive distinction, and the rent debate, where the misreading is politically convenient to owners who shelter behind their staffs' evident industry, is a good place to refuse it once more. The tollbooth attendant's industry has never been an argument about the toll.

### What employment signature does a rentier phase leave?

look at the payrolls. Around every toll grows a workforce of measurers, checkers, enforcers, and collectors, handsomely occupied in guarding a transfer while adding nothing to the pile being transferred, their own wages drawn from that same pile. A swelling ratio of gate staff to makers is the phase's fingerprint, readable in any occupational census.

There is a corollary for how the phase's output should be assessed. Where national accounting counts the administration of claims as value added at its cost or its margin, a phase that multiplies claim administration will register growth that the framework reads as an expanding deduction from a surplus produced elsewhere. The divergence between the two readings is not a quarrel about arithmetic but about what the arithmetic means, and it surfaces in public argument as the suspicion, widely felt and rarely articulated, that measured prosperity and lived prosperity have parted company. The framework articulates the suspicion: an economy can grow its circulation and enforcement apparatus indefinitely while its productive core stagnates, and the growth will be real as income and illusory as wealth creation. Rent's debate, followed patiently to this point, thus ends by illuminating a question far larger than itself, which is the mark of a category doing genuine theoretical work rather than serving as a slogan for one season's discontents.

## Why the debate will not die: the permanent temptation of the rent thesis

If the argument of this article is sound, a puzzle remains: why does the supersession thesis keep returning, in this debate and in its predecessors, despite the presupposition test being available to any reader of the framework's definitions? The puzzle deserves an answer in the sociology of knowledge rather than in logic, because the thesis's persistence is a social fact with causes that analysis should understand rather than merely lament. Part of the cause is experiential and has been honoured throughout: dependence on condition-owners is the most visible and most resented relation of the phase, and a theory that names the visible relation as the system's essence will always out-poll a theory that locates the essence in a less visible relation underneath. Visibility is a powerful rhetorical asset, and production, dispersed across millions of workplaces and taken for granted precisely because it works, cannot compete for attention with the gate, which concentrates dependence into a single, nameable, blamable point.

Another part of the cause is professional. Announcements of new eras are rewarded, by publishers, by media, and by the lecture circuit, in a way that phase analyses are not, and the reward structure selects for supersession claims regardless of their evidence. A writer who concludes that the mode persists while distribution shifts has written a good book and a poor headline. A writer who concludes that capitalism is dead and something unnamed has replaced it has written a headline that markets itself. The tradition's defenders should therefore not expect the debate to close when its logic closes, and should plan for a permanent controversy in which the presupposition test must be reapplied, patiently and without irritation, to each new announcement as it arrives. That is not a failure of the framework. It is the normal condition of a true distinction in a culture that rewards its confusion.

### Why does the supersession thesis keep returning after each refutation?

because the dependence it names is the phase's most visible and resented relation, while production is dispersed and taken for granted, and because announcements of new eras are professionally rewarded in ways phase analyses are not. The thesis's persistence is a social fact about visibility and incentives, not evidence of its truth.

A third part of the cause is more sympathetic and should be stated with respect. The supersession thesis often functions, for its holders, as a refusal of consolation: an insistence that the present's distinctive miseries not be dissolved into the eternal return of the same, and that something be demanded of theory proportionate to the suffering of dependence. That insistence is honourable, and the framework betrays its own best traditions when it answers the insistence with complacency. The correct answer is the one this article has tried to embody: the miseries are real, they are specific to the phase, they are analysable in their mechanisms and contestable in their legal construction, and none of that specificity requires or survives the promotion to a new mode. Seriousness about suffering and accuracy about structure are not competitors. The debate goes wrong, on both sides, exactly when it treats them as competitors, the strong position sacrificing accuracy to seriousness and the complacent wing of the continuity position sacrificing seriousness to accuracy. The middle position, held with the framework's full architecture beneath it, is the refusal of that false choice, and it is on that refusal, as much as on any single argument, that this article rests its verdict.

There is a final, quieter cause worth naming, because it implicates the tradition itself. The framework's own rhetoric has always contained an eschatological undertow, a sense that the mode it analyses is a passing formation whose contradictions ripen toward supersession, and readers formed by that rhetoric are primed to detect supersession in every dramatic conjuncture. The priming is understandable and must be disciplined by the framework's own criterion, which is stern precisely because its authors knew the temptation from inside. A mode is superseded by the transformation of its production relations, usually through long, contested, and unmistakable upheavals in how labour is organised and the surplus extracted, not by a shift in which revenues glitter most brightly in a given decade. Holding that sternness, against the undertow in the tradition's own rhetoric and against the reward structure of the surrounding culture, is the intellectual virtue this debate most demands, and the one its next decade will most reward in the few who practise it.

## Gateways and the geography of dependence: centres, peripheries, and the toll map

Dependence has a geography, and drawing its map adds something the debate's abstractions miss. Conditions that command rent are not spread evenly across space or across sectors; they cluster where activity concentrates, at the junctions of transport, of finance, of information, and of urban life, and the clustering means that whole regions and whole trades can find themselves in the position of permanent toll payers to owners seated elsewhere. A producing district whose goods must pass through distant gateways, whose credit is priced in a distant centre, whose land values are set by demand generated in a distant metropolis, experiences the phase as a drainage: value created locally departs along channels owned elsewhere, and what returns is permission to continue. Political movements in such districts speak a language of extraction that predates the present debate by generations, and the framework gives that language its analytical dignity without endorsing every conclusion drawn in it: drainage is a distributional relation between places, superimposed on the relations between classes, and the two must be analysed together because each household stands at their intersection.

The map also has a vertical dimension inside single enterprises and single cities. Within a firm, the units that control a condition, the property portfolio, the rights library, the standard-setting committee, acquire an internal gravity out of proportion to their headcount, because every other unit's plans must be priced against their tolls. Within a city, the owners of well-placed ground acquire a veto, exercised through price, over which activities may exist at the centre and which are exiled to the fringe, so that the cultural and economic character of the centre is, in effect, curated by rent: only activities able to carry the toll survive in place, and the surviving mix then testifies, misleadingly, to the tastes of the age rather than to the arithmetic of the levy. Analysts who wonder why centres fill with the outlets of large chains while independent producers vanish to the periphery are watching rent perform its quiet curation, and the phenomenon requires no conspiracy and no new mode, only the old levy applied to a scarce map.

### How does rent curate the character of a city centre?

by price rather than by plan. Shops and workshops whose margins cannot stretch to the central levy quietly vanish toward the fringe, whatever affection residents held for them, and the mixture that remains is a portrait of the toll schedule wearing the disguise of public taste.

A geography of dependence also reframes the question of exit, which the feudal comparison mishandled when it lingered on single biographies. Exit for a district or a trade is not a matter of one producer's mobility but of collective alternatives: a second gateway, a public infrastructure, a rival centre, each of which must be built before it can be chosen. The cost of building alternatives explains why dependence persists even where everyone deplores it, since the alternative's benefits are diffuse and delayed while the toll is specific and immediate. Public provision enters the analysis here in its strongest form, not as charity and not as ideology, but as the deliberate construction of an outside option, whose mere existence disciplines the toll charged by the private condition even for those who never use the public route. A library system disciplines the price of access to books by existing; a public road disciplines the toll road by being driveable; a public option in any domain converts a monopoly of condition into a contestable one. The framework's politics of rent, spelled out, is largely a politics of outside options, and it is available in full without any verdict on the mode having been reached, which is one more reason the phase reading serves practice better than the announcement it competes with.

## Time, patience, and the rentier's clock

Different income forms keep different time, and the rentier phase has a distinctive temporal signature worth isolating. Profit is impatient: it must be realised in the sale, defended against rivals each season, and reinvested under threat. Wages are more impatient still, arriving and departing within the month. Rent, by contrast, is the income of patience. A condition, once owned, yields across decades; its value rests on expectations stretching far ahead; its owner can wait out storms that ruin producers, because the storm passes and the condition remains, ready to levy upon the recovery. That patience gives condition-owners a political temperament distinct from other fractions of capital: they favour stability of the property order above growth of the product, since growth is the producers' prize while stability is the toll's. Where their weight in the owning classes grows, the whole system's centre of political gravity shifts toward conservation, toward guarding paper wealth against every policy, however productive, that might disturb it, and the shift is observable in the priorities of states that court asset holders as their core constituency.

The temporal signature also explains a feature of rentier wealth that puzzles moralists: its apparent effortlessness across generations. Productive fortunes dissipate because each generation must re-win them against rivals; fortunes lodged in conditions persist because the condition does the work, and inheritance law, the quietest of all the statutes that manufacture the phase, transmits the barrier intact from hand to hand. A society in which the commanding fortunes are increasingly of the persistent kind will feel, to those born outside them, increasingly closed, and the feeling of closure, of a world whose gates were allocated before one's birth, is another tributary feeding the feudal metaphor's emotional river. The framework's answer is by now familiar in shape: closure through inherited conditions is a distributional and legal fact, contestable through the taxation of transfers and of yields, through the widening of access, and through the public construction of alternatives, and it neither needs nor survives translation into a claim about the mode, whose relations the closed gates presuppose at every step.

### Why does rentier wealth persist across generations more reliably than productive wealth?

because a productive fortune must be re-won against rivals in each period, while a fortune lodged in a condition yields without re-winning: the barrier itself performs, storms pass around it, and inheritance transmits the barrier intact. Persistence is therefore a property of the asset's relation, not of the family's virtue.

Patience has a price, and naming it completes the section. The rentier's clock is slow because the condition's yield depends on a future the owner does not control: the surrounding society must keep producing, keep needing the junction, keep honouring the right. Long horizons are therefore also long exposures, and the capitalised values of conditions embed assumptions about decades of political stability, of legal continuity, of productive vitality elsewhere, any of which can fail. The phase's commanding wealth is thus, paradoxically, both the most patient and the most leveraged bet in the system, a wager that the old mode will go on working. No feudal lord ever had to bet on the future productivity of a system he claimed to have replaced. The rentier bets on capitalism's continuation with every valuation, and the bet, recorded in prices across the whole asset landscape, is perhaps the single most eloquent piece of evidence in this entire debate, offered daily, at scale, by the very parties whose supposed supersession is under discussion.

## A short dictionary of the debate: terms fixed before use

Controversies conducted in borrowed vocabulary benefit from a pause in which the principal terms are fixed in one place, briefly, so that later slippage can be caught against a stated standard. Access charge: any payment whose substance is permission to use a condition, as distinct from payment for a good delivered or a service performed. Barrier: the excludability of a condition, whether arising from nature, from law, or from the accumulated structure of a network, in virtue of which permission can be charged at all. Capture: taking title to a barrier one did not build, whether the builder was an earlier owner, a public programme, or the slow accretion of surrounding society, as distinct from barriers raised by the claimant's own outlay. Condition: anything, land, right, position, standard, audience, whose use is necessary or advantageous for production or exchange and whose supply cannot be freely expanded by those who need it. Decomposition: the sorting of a blended revenue into its constituent kinds, wage, gain, lending cut, and toll, with every constituent followed home to the common fund from which all of them are carved. Levy: a charge considered from the payer's side, emphasising compulsion rather than exchange. Phase: a durable configuration within a mode, distinguished by its distributional centre and institutional forms. Toll: a levy at a point of passage, the experiential form of an access charge. None of these definitions is original, and that is their virtue: a shared, boring precision is the precondition of an interesting disagreement.

Two of the dictionary's entries deserve emphasis because the debate's slippage concentrates on them. Phase is the first. In loose usage a phase is a mood, a stretch of years with a flavour; in the usage fixed here it is a structural configuration, with a distributional centre that can be identified, mechanisms that reproduce it, and a politics characteristic of it. Claims that a phase exists should be required to supply those three elements, and the middle position, at its best, supplies all of them: the centre in asset ownership, the machinery in legislated scarcity and in the mathematics of what can and cannot be copied, the politics in the protection of paper wealth. Announcement is the dictionary's missing entry, supplied here for completeness: a claim that a mode has been superseded, which should be required to supply the relational evidence the defeater names, and which, in the absence of that evidence, should be catalogued as a phase claim in dramatic dress. The dictionary thus quietly reallocates the burden of proof across the whole debate, which is what dictionaries in controversies are for.

### Why fix a dictionary in the middle of a controversy?

because borrowed vocabulary slips, and slippage is how distributional findings get promoted into relational conclusions without anyone deciding to promote them. Stated definitions give every participant, including the article's own author, a standard against which later usage can be caught and corrected in public.

The second entry deserving emphasis is capture, because it carries the debate's most useful moral distinction in a neutral word. Creation and capture are both routes to owning a condition, and the payment qualifies as rent under the identical test on either route, but the social assessment of the two routes diverges, and policy that cannot distinguish them will either reward capture as though it were creation or penalise creation as though it were capture. The dictionary's discipline, fixing capture as acquisition of a condition built by others or by society, allows the distinction to be drawn case by case without moralising vocabulary: the question in any instance is empirical, who built the condition and at whose expense, and the answer distributes praise, tolerance, and reform pressure more accurately than any general verdict on owners. A debate equipped with that one distinction would lose half its heat and keep all of its light, which is the usual exchange rate when rhetoric is traded for analysis.

## Counterfactual histories: what a genuine supersession would have looked like

One way to sharpen a classification is to imagine, concretely, the world in which the rival classification would be correct, and then compare that world with the one actually observed. Imagine, then, a social order that genuinely deserved the name the strong position proposes. In that order, the typical producer would approach a proprietor not as an employee approaches a firm, nor as a seller approaches a marketplace, but as a supplicant approaches a gatekeeper of subsistence itself, owing the proprietor a portion of whatever might be made, by customary right, before any making began. The proprietor's claim would not need renewal by contract each season; it would descend with the proprietor's title and bind the producer's children as it bound the producer. Rival proprietors would not compete for the producer's presence, because presence would be assigned by birth and geography rather than chosen in a market. Investment, where it occurred, would flow toward pageantry, fortification, and the multiplication of dependents, since prestige and armed loyalty, not expanded output, would be the currency of standing among proprietors. Disputes would be settled in the proprietor's own court, under the proprietor's own law, with the proprietor's own retainers enforcing the judgement.

Hold that imagined order beside the observed one and the contrasts assemble themselves without any assistance from definitions. Producers in the observed order sign contracts they can, at a price, exit; they are courted, however cynically, by competing gateways; their obligations expire with the contract rather than descending to their children; the wealth of proprietors is held in tradable claims whose prices swing with markets the proprietors do not command; disputes run through courts neither party owns; and the proprietors themselves exhibit the anxious, expansionary behaviour of capitals that fear displacement, pouring revenue into growth because standing still is a prelude to absorption. Every load-bearing feature of the imagined feudal order is absent, and every load-bearing feature of the observed order is drawn from capital's own skeleton. Counterfactual comparison cannot replace the presupposition argument, which remains this article's decisive ground, but it performs a different service: it shows what the strong position's world would have to look like, and thereby shows how much of the present the position's vocabulary leaves undescribed.

### What would a genuinely feudal successor order actually display?

hereditary obligation binding producers to proprietors, dues owed by custom before any production began, no rivalry among proprietors for producers' presence, investment aimed at retainers and display rather than expanded output, and justice administered in the proprietor's own court. The observed order displays none of these, which is the comparison's verdict.

A second counterfactual disciplines the opposite camp. Imagine an order in which the continuity position's complacent version were wholly correct, where tolls were a trivial and shrinking feature, where rewards tracked the organising of production closely, and where no household's life was materially structured by permissions. That world, too, fails to match the observed one, in which permissions and levies texture nearly every transaction of an ordinary week, in which asset appreciation outruns the rewards of making across whole careers, and in which governments treat the confidence of condition-holders as a binding constraint on policy. The two counterfactuals together triangulate the actual: an order feudal in none of its relations and yet saturated, at the level of distribution and experience, with levies that earlier phases confined to narrower territories. Triangulation of that kind is what the phase concept is for. It names the region between the two failed imaginings, and it names it precisely, with mechanisms, a centre, and a politics, rather than gesturing at novelty or at sameness according to taste.

## A coda on intellectual honesty: what each camp owes the others

Debates that run for years accumulate debts among their participants, and a short accounting of those debts is a fitting place to end the analytical portion of this article. The supersession camp owes the others a concession it has been slow to make: that its evidence, however voluminous, speaks the language of shares and weights, while its conclusions speak the language of systems and successions, and that the exchange rate between those languages has never been supplied. Supplying it, or moderating the conclusions to match the evidence, would convert a provocative literature into a formidable one, and the phase camp, which shares most of the supersession camp's findings, would be the first beneficiary of the conversion. The continuity camp owes a concession in the opposite direction: that winning the argument about the system's name has too often served its holders as a licence to stop looking, and that the distributional transformation documented by its rivals is a legitimate object of theory in its own right, not a distraction from theory's real business. A continuity worth holding is one that can describe, in detail and without flinching, everything that has changed inside the thing that continues.

The phase camp owes the largest concession of all, because it holds the verdict and verdicts attract the heaviest debts. It owes the supersession camp an acknowledgement that its threshold problem is real: a phase defined by a distributional centre must say, somewhere, how much tilting a structure can absorb before tilt becomes transformation, and this article has had to supply that threshold on the camp's behalf, in the form of the surrender terms attached to its verdict. It owes the continuity camp an acknowledgement that its central finding, the persistence of the old relations beneath the new tolls, is the phase camp's own foundation rather than its rival's consolation prize. And it owes every reader a standing reminder that its triumph is a triumph of classification, not of consolation: to name the present a phase of an old order is to say where its levers are, not to say that its burdens are light, its tolls are just, or its proprietors are harmless. The gates are real, the levies are real, the dependence is real. Only the obituary was premature, and an obituary's prematurity, in the end, is what this entire controversy was about.

## Envoi: the gate and the road

An old image organises everything this article has argued, and it can be allowed the final word precisely because it is an image and not an argument. A gate across a road is a real power: whoever holds it can tax every traveller, delay every journey, and grow wealthy on movement the gatekeeper did not create. Travellers curse the gate, organise against it, dream of a world without it, and they are right to do all three, because the tax is real and its justice is questionable. But nobody who studies the scene carefully concludes that the road has ceased to exist, or that the journeys have become the gatekeeper's invention, or that a new civilisation of gates has replaced the civilisation of roads. The traffic is the substance; the gate is a claim upon the traffic; and the claim, however swollen, however fortified, however hereditary its keepers make it, lives entirely from what passes through. Abolish the traffic and the gate is a ornament in an empty landscape within a season. Abolish the gate and the traffic, after a season of confusion, flows on and finds new channels, new markets, new gates perhaps, which is why the work of analysis and of reform is never finished. Rent is the gate. Production is the road. The debate this article has examined is, at bottom, an argument about which of the two deserves to name the age, and the answer the framework gives, patiently, definition by definition, is that ages are named for their roads, while their gates are named, taxed, regulated, and occasionally torn down, in the long distributional struggle that the naming makes possible.

## Worked examples by type: applying the decomposition habit

Abstractions earn their keep in application, so this section works through four types of case in prose, naming no holder and no asset, and shows the decomposition habit producing a judgement in each. The first type is the marketplace gateway. An enterprise assembles buyers and sellers, charges sellers a percentage of each transaction, and supplies, alongside the access, services of payment, dispute handling, and logistics coordination. Decompose the percentage. The services are real production and circulation work, performed by wage labour, and the portion of the charge covering them at a normal return is profit and commercial margin. The remainder, the portion sellers pay because the assembled buyers cannot be reached as cheaply elsewhere, is monopoly rent on the assembled audience, shading into differential rent where rival gateways assemble smaller crowds. The judgement the decomposition yields is neither rent nor not-rent but a proportion, and the proportion, not the label, is what regulation, competition, and negotiation would each move by different means.

The second type is the licensed technique. An enterprise holds an exclusive right over a method that producers in a field increasingly need, and licenses it at a royalty bearing no visible relation to the cost of having developed the method. Decompose the royalty. A portion answers to the development cost and a normal return on the capital that funded it, the legitimate core of the incentive defence. A portion answers to the standard-setting process that made this method, among rivals, the one the field converged upon, a positional windfall the holder did little to earn beyond arriving early and organising well. The remainder answers to extensions and enforcements that widened the right after convergence, pure transfer defended by legal staffs. Policy that treats the royalty as one thing will either over-reward the windfall or under-reward the development; policy that decomposes it can set terms portion by portion, which is why the habit matters beyond the seminar.

### How does decomposition change the judgement in the gateway case?

instead of a verdict word, the analyst gets a recipe: so much of the percentage remunerates genuine work performed for the seller, so much more is tribute for a crowd the seller cannot gather alone. Each ingredient answers to a different instrument, bargaining, rivalry, or rule-making, so the recipe, unlike the label, tells practitioners where to push.

The third type is the urban dwelling near a centre of employment, already visited and revisited here at the level of its elements. The decomposition of its payment into structure, maintenance, capital return, and location rent yields the case's familiar judgement, and its political consequence deserves one more statement: of the four elements, only the location rent can be collected publicly without discouraging the supply of dwellings, since the location was not produced by its owner and cannot be withdrawn when taxed. Every serious housing programme in the framework's spirit is built on that asymmetry, taxing the element that cannot shrink while protecting the elements that can. The fourth type is the resource deposit. Its decomposition into extraction cost, normal profit, differential rent from the deposit's richness, and absolute rent from concentrated ownership yields the judgement that the deposit's host population has a claim, in the framework's logic, on the two rent elements, which are nature's and society's contributions, while the producing enterprise's claim covers the first two. Resource settlements across the world are, whether their negotiators know the vocabulary or not, arguments about exactly that decomposition, and the vocabulary's service is to show the parties the structure of what they are already arguing about.

Four types, one habit, and a generalisation: wherever an income looks anomalous, too large for the effort, too stable for the risk, too detached from any visible production, the decomposition habit asks the same three questions. What labour and capital does the income actually pay for? What monopolised condition does it levy upon? Whose surplus, traced back, supplies it? The answers sort the income among wages, profit, interest, and rent, and the sorting, once done, tells the analyst which literature, which remedies, and which politics apply. The habit is the article in miniature, and a reader who carries away only the habit has carried away the essential thing.

## The word feudalism: a short philology of a polemical term

Words that do polemical work deserve philological suspicion, and feudalism is among the hardest-working words in the present vocabulary. Historians of the actual feudal centuries have long complained that the term, as popularly used, names a system far tidier than any that existed: relations of lordship and tenure varied enormously across regions and periods, the classic pyramid of homage was a legal ideal more than a social description, and some specialists would retire the word from scholarly use altogether. That complaint matters for the present debate in an unexpected way. The strong position's analogy compares the present not with the messy historical reality but with the tidy popular model, a pyramid of lords extracting dues from tied producers, and the comparison therefore fails twice over: the present does not match the model, as this article's dimension-by-dimension examination showed, and the model does not match the past it claims as its reference. An analogy whose reference point is a fiction can still do rhetorical work, but it cannot do analytical work, and the debate is entitled to ask its proponents which kind they intend.

The word's polemical career explains its persistence better than its accuracy does. Feudal carries a charge of illegitimacy that capitalist, in much contemporary usage, has lost through familiarity: to call an arrangement feudal is to call it archaic, arbitrary, and ripe for abolition, in a single syllable-laden gesture. Writers reaching for that charge are often engaged in legitimate protest against dependence and toll, and the protest does not need the word's analytical pretensions to be honourable. What the protest loses by the borrowing is precision about its target. Abolishing feudalism meant dissolving a legal order of status and tenure. Abolishing the modern toll means redesigning rights, regulating gateways, socialising yields, and building public alternatives, a programme whose every item belongs to the distributional politics of the present mode. The borrowed word points its audience toward a revolution against an order that does not exist, and away from the reforms, unglamorous and available, against the order that does.

### Why does the feudal label persist despite failing the comparison?

because the label carries a charge of archaic illegitimacy that the accurate vocabulary lacks, and because the experience of dependence it names is real. Its persistence is explained by rhetoric and feeling rather than by fit, and its cost is misdirection toward an imaginary order and away from available distributional remedies.

None of this is an argument for policing metaphors in ordinary speech, where feudal will go on doing its loose work harmlessly enough. It is an argument for quarantining the metaphor at the border of theory, where claims about modes of production are made and judged. Inside theory, words are instruments with definitions, and an instrument borrowed for its charge rather than its calibration corrupts every measurement it touches. The series' discipline, applied across all its debates, is to let polemical words flourish in titles and headlines, where they belong, and to admit them into argument only after they have been exchanged for the defined categories they gesture at. Rent for feudal dues, phase for new era, distribution for supersession: the exchanges are this article's whole method, stated as a rule of the house.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Researchers approaching this debate will find its open problems unusually well defined, which is a credit to the controversy's structure rather than to any participant's generosity. The outstanding theoretical problem is the unfinished science of the singular holding: how tolls on copyable yet fenced conditions form, what rival fences do to them, how markets price them as wealth, and how they wither. The outstanding empirical problem is decomposition at scale: following each great river of toll income back to the pursuits it skims, then sorting those pursuits by the manner of their staffing, which is precisely the investigation the verdict's surrender terms demand. The outstanding historical problem is comparative: the phase's legal archaeology, domain by domain, set against the archaeologies of earlier phases, to establish how much of the present distribution was legislated and how quickly. A researcher who advances any one of the three will have contributed more to the debate than a dozen further verdicts.

Historians will recognise the debate as a recurrence and can contribute the recurrence's documentation: the earlier announcements of supersession, in the monopoly era and the managerial era and the first financial era, collected with their evidence and their fates, would give the present controversy the prior it borrows, for the moment, from intuition. Journalists, finally, hold the debate's public quality in their hands, and this article's request of them has been made already and is repeated here as the section's close: attach the sorting question to every supersession claim, name authors and works rather than vibes and trends, and treat the anatomy literature, slower and less quotable than the announcements, as the story's factual spine. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is rent replacing profit?

Owners of scarce conditions have unquestionably enlarged their slice of national income across recent decades. Yet enlarging a slice differs from abolishing the bakery. On the theory examined here, profit is the surplus at its point of creation, in the grasp of whichever capitals marshalled the producing, whereas rent is a later levy upon that same surplus by whoever holds a needed condition. A levy that swallowed everything would remove any reason to organise producing at all, and the stream being levied would dry up with it. Growth of the levy is real; succession is not. What has occurred is a redistribution favouring condition-holders inside a persisting capitalist order.

### Q: What is rent in Marxist theory?

Ask what a landowner, a mineral holder, or the proprietor of an exclusive right actually sells, and the answer is permission: entry to something scarce that others require and cannot simply make for themselves. The payment received for that permission is rent. Theory sorts it by mechanism. Where holdings differ in quality, the better ones yield a differential surplus. Where proprietors collectively refuse entry until paid, an absolute element appears even on the poorest holding. Where the holding is one of a kind, price answers to buyers' eagerness and wealth. Throughout, the money originates as value created by workers somewhere else and reaches the proprietor by transfer, never by any producing the proprietor personally performs.

### Q: Can rent replace profit entirely?

Entire replacement defeats itself on paper before any evidence is consulted. The fund out of which rents are paid comes into being only because capitals, chasing profit, set workers to producing commodities. Suppose the levy grew until nothing remained for those capitals: hiring would stop being worthwhile, plants and projects would be abandoned, and within a short span there would be no fresh surplus for proprietors to draw upon. Their triumph would be a famine of their own making. Nothing prevents the levy from growing large, squeezing producers hard, or dominating the pattern of incomes, and every camp in the controversy accepts that it has grown. Turning that growth into totality is where coherence ends.

### Q: What is wrong with the feudal analogy?

Score the comparison honestly, point by point, and it collapses. Dues under the old order were extracted by direct force of lordship: the peasant owed them by birth, under a superior who was simultaneously landlord, judge, and armed power. Nobody inside a modern enclosure owes anything by birth; obligations arise from contracts signed by legal equals, however unequal the bargaining behind them. Peasants could not lawfully depart; sellers inside enclosures can, at a price, and rival proprietors bid for their presence. Lords consumed their dues in display and war, under no pressure to transform production; modern condition-holders pour revenue back into expansion because rivals would otherwise swallow them. Dependence is genuinely felt. The feudal machinery meant to explain it is absent.

### Q: What is monopoly rent?

Some holdings have no rival at all: a plot whose soil gives a wine found nowhere else, a right covering the one technique an industry has standardised upon, a junction where an entire audience has gathered. Income from such a holding answers to neither fertility gaps nor the bare power to refuse entry. It answers to how badly buyers want the thing and how deep their pockets run, and theory files it under monopoly rent. The founding volume gestures at the category through its vineyard and moves on quickly, leaving later writers a label with little machinery attached, even though the label is precisely the one that modern arguments about rights and gateways reach for first.

### Q: Is intellectual property a form of rent?

In large part, yes, by the framework's relational test. Knowledge, once published, can be copied endlessly without wearing out, so any scarcity surrounding it exists because statutes forbid the copying. Fees charged for permission to use what law alone has fenced off are therefore payments to a barrier, funded from the earnings of the fee payers, and they satisfy every clause of the theory's definition of the phenomenon. The qualification is that creating the knowledge is production, and the wages of creators and the profits of enterprises organising creation are not rent. Real incomes in knowledge industries braid the streams together, salaries, enterprise profit, and rights rent in one margin, and analysis should decompose the braid rather than label the whole by any single strand.

### Q: What is rentier capitalism?

Brett Christophers gave the phrase its wide currency in a book of 2020 bearing it as a title. The phrase describes an economy whose rewards have swung toward holding scarce things, plots, deposits, protected rights, networks, junctions, and away from directing the making of goods, while the making itself proceeds, as before, through hired hands and rival capitals. Nothing in the phrase asserts a successor system. It asserts a tilt, in who gets what and in whose interests governments instinctively protect, and its usefulness lies in gathering scattered domains under that single tilt so their kinship can be argued about in one conversation. Its analytical strength is gathering unlike domains under one relation so their shared anatomy becomes visible. Its limit is that the gathered scarcities differ, natural, statutory, and positional, and remedies that ignore the differences will misfire.

### Q: Why is monopoly rent underdeveloped in Marx?

Land dominates the founding exposition because soil offered the clearest laboratory: plots of visibly unequal richness, side by side, selling into one market. Monopoly rent appears briefly, through the exceptional vineyard, without the systematic treatment of its formation, limits, and decay that the other forms receive. The cases that would have forced its development, legally exclusive rights over reproducible knowledge, gateways whose value grows with their crowd, standards owned by single parties, were embryonic in the founder's period. Later generations thus received a rough draft precisely where their own controversies would lean hardest, a gap that candour should acknowledge rather than paper over.

### Q: Has the mode of production changed?

Apply the test the theory itself prescribes and the answer is negative. Supersession would mean the basic staffing of production had altered, that people no longer predominantly worked for wages under capitals competing to accumulate, and that taking the surplus had migrated out of the workplace into open coercion or inherited obligation. Scanning the economy with those questions yields the old answers everywhere, including inside the headquarters of the greatest toll-takers, whose payrolls, rivals, and reinvestment pressures are capitalist to the core. The shift that has genuinely occurred sits one level down, in how the proceeds get shared out, and mistaking that level for the deeper one is the controversy's founding confusion.

### Q: What would settle the rent debate?

Only evidence concerning how producing is organised, as opposed to how large anyone's income has become, can close the argument. Specifically, the strong position would be vindicated, and the continuity verdict defeated, by showing that tolls on pursuits conducted entirely apart from the wage-staffed making of goods for sale have grown into a major slice of all income. Income figures are common ground, conceded on all sides before argument begins, so they cannot arbitrate between the camps. Relational evidence can: trace the great access streams to the activities they levy upon, classify those pursuits by the manner of their staffing and the route of their surplus, letting the resulting classification, rather than any magnitude, decide whether the description owed is of a phase or of a mode.

### Q: How does the rent presupposes production claim grade the three positions?

Turn the claim upon each camp and watch the grades separate. The supersession camp is hoist with its own definitions: a payment characterised as a slice of somebody else's product cannot headline an order supposedly built upon it. The continuity camp passes easily, its whole case being that slices and their sources keep their old arrangement, though the claim also shows up that camp's thin curiosity about how the slicing has altered. The phase camp gains most of all. It strengthens the middle position most, because a phase of rentier dominance within an unchanged mode is precisely what remains standing once the presupposition is granted: distribution transformed, production relations intact. The claim thus functions as a grading instrument, sorting the positions by whether their central assertions survive contact with the definition of their central category.

### Q: What should a student separate between a change in distribution and a change in the mode of production?

A student should separate the register of each claim before assessing it. Keep two ledgers apart. One ledger records proportions: what fraction of the whole product lands with wage earners, with organising capitals, with lenders, with proprietors of conditions, and how those fractions drift over time. The other records the machinery underneath: who hires, under what discipline, making what, for which buyers. Entries in the first ledger, however dramatic, never by themselves amend the second. Candidates and commentators err most often by carrying a figure across from the proportions ledger and spending it as though it were a finding about machinery. The remedy is bookkeeping discipline: label every exhibit with its ledger before letting it testify. Sorting every exhibit into its register first is the discipline this article most recommends.

### Q: Why can rent not replace the profit it draws on?

Because the drawing-on is constitutive, not incidental. Rent is defined within the framework as a transfer, a portion of surplus value that production organised by capital has already created and that profit, in the first instance, appropriates. If rent absorbed the entire surplus, the capitals organising production would retain nothing, the motive and the funds for organising production would disappear, and subsequent periods would generate no new surplus for rent to claim. The replacement thesis therefore describes a process that consumes its own precondition, like a tax set above the whole income it taxes. Rent can grow, squeeze profit, and dominate distribution, all while the relation of dependence that defines it remains exactly where the framework placed it.

### Q: How does the three positions grid order the rent arguments?

Picture a table with a row for each camp and four questions heading its columns, asking what has altered, where the surplus begins, what proof would count, and where the weakness hides. Traversing a row gives a camp's complete anatomy in a single glance. Traversing the proof column downward is the revealing journey, because the supersession row demands a species of proof, concerning production's organisation, that its advocates habitually fail to bring, arriving instead with figures about income size that belong to a different argument. The weakness column, finally, is the table's honesty device: no row leaves the page unmarked, the favoured row included. The grid's purpose is to prevent arguments being won by atmosphere instead of by fit between claims and evidence.

### Q: What separates monopoly rent from ground rent in the framework?

Begin with soil. Payments for farmland or urban ground split by mechanism into a part explained by uneven quality, the best sites outyielding the marginal one at a shared price, and a part explained by proprietors simply refusing access until terms suit them. That pair exhausts ground rent proper. Step away from soil altogether and a third mechanism appears wherever a holding is singular: a celebrated vineyard in the founding text, a protected technique or an unmatched gateway in later debate. Singular holdings charge what longing and purchasing power will bear, with no productivity yardstick anywhere in sight. Keeping the soil pair and the singularity mechanism apart is what the question asks, because remedies aimed at one mechanism glance off the others. Ground rent received the founding text's systematic exposition. Monopoly rent received a sketch, which is why the separation matters practically as well as theoretically.
