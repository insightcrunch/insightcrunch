## The Ambedkar Drafting Committee Question Behind the Familiar Story

Every school textbook in India carries the same short paragraph. On 29 August 1947 the Constituent Assembly appointed a Drafting Committee of seven members, B. R. Ambedkar took the chair, and the committee produced the Draft Constitution that the Assembly debated for the following two years. The paragraph is accurate in every particular and misleading in its total effect, because it invites the reader to picture a secretariat: a skilled lawyer at a desk, converting decisions taken elsewhere into numbered clauses. The Ambedkar drafting committee question, the question that serious scholarship has argued over for half a century, is whether that picture survives contact with the record. Did the chair merely hold the pen, or did the chair decide?

![Ambedkar chairing the Drafting Committee that framed the Indian Constitution - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-108.webp)

The minimising picture has a distinguished source. Granville Austin, whose The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation appeared in 1966 and remains the standard institutional history of the founding, treated constitution making as the work of an interlocking leadership: Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad set the political terms, the Congress Assembly Party ratified them, and Ambedkar, in Austin's account, supplied unrivalled drafting skill in executing a design whose main lines he had not chosen. On this reading the Drafting Committee was the Assembly's instrument, and its chairman the most talented technician in a collective enterprise. Austin did not write as a partisan, and his reading rests on a genuine feature of the evidence: the major committee reports of 1947, on union powers, provincial constitutions and fundamental rights, were prepared under other chairmen, and the Assembly's debates show decisions being fought out on the floor rather than announced from the drafting desk.

The revisionist picture also has distinguished sources, and it has grown stronger with each decade of research. Christophe Jaffrelot, in Dr Ambedkar and Untouchability, published in 2005, reconstructed Ambedkar's constitutional thought from his memoranda and speeches and showed a thinker who arrived at the committee table with a complete constitutional programme already written. Anand Teltumbde, in Republic of Caste, published in 2018, pressed the argument into the politics of memory: the technical drafter story, on his reading, functioned to transfer ownership of the Constitution to the Congress leadership that had opposed Ambedkar electorally for two decades. The namable claim of this article stands with the revisionists, on evidence set out in the sections that follow: Ambedkar did not draft what others decided; he decided what others later accepted.

That claim is tested here on four dimensions, because a general assertion about influence proves nothing. The first dimension is reservation, where Ambedkar's March 1947 memorandum States and Minorities supplied design elements that entered the constitutional text. The second is the secular settlement, where the text of the religion clauses carries the imprint of arguments Ambedkar had been making since Annihilation of Caste in 1936. The third is the rights architecture, where the division between court-enforceable rights and directives to government was a design he explained and defended in his own speeches of November and December 1948. The fourth is untouchability, where the single sentence of the abolition clause was chosen against broader alternatives debated on 29 November 1948. On each dimension the evidence is dated, named and checkable in the Constituent Assembly Debates, and on each dimension the pattern repeats: the committee's chairman proposed, argued and carried positions that were his before they were the Assembly's.

The wider frame matters as well. The founding debates worked through [the Constituent Assembly's three tensions that Ambedkar's Drafting Committee work addressed](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/): who counts as fully Indian, who governs whom, and which past the present is continuous with. Ambedkar's drafting years sit at the crossing point of all three. His reservation design answered the first tension with machinery rather than rhetoric; his rights design answered the second by binding future majorities; his attack on scriptural authority answered the third by refusing to treat the social order of the past as the constitution of the future. A chairmanship that engaged all three tensions at the level of text was not a secretarial function, and the record of the committee's 141 sitting days shows a chairman who understood exactly what he was doing.

One caution governs everything that follows, and the revisionist case is stronger for stating it plainly. Ambedkar was not the Constitution's sole author, and he never had the votes to impose a text of his own. The Assembly contained 299 members after Partition, the Congress held an overwhelming majority among them, and every clause the committee produced had to survive amendment, debate and division on the floor. The argument here is narrower and more defensible than sole authorship. It is that within the division of labour the Assembly created in August 1947, the substantive choices on reservation, religion, rights and untouchability bear Ambedkar's intellectual signature in a way that the phrase technical drafter cannot describe, and that the documentary trail, from the 1947 memorandum to the 1951 statement on the Hindu Code Bill, shows one continuous programme rather than a technician's sequence of commissions.

The distinction between authoring a constitution and controlling its career can be measured in the chairman's own electoral record, and the measurement should be made early because it frames the whole inquiry. Ambedkar, the Constitution's most consequential draftsman, lost the first general election he contested under it, defeated in Bombay North Central in January 1952 by his Congress opponent Narayan Kajrolkar, and lost again at the Bhandara by election of 1954. The republic's voters were free to reject the man whose text governed them, and the Congress organisation that had employed his drafting skill campaigned successfully against his party. No fact in the founding story separates intellectual authority from political power more cleanly. The minimising tradition trades on the confusion of the two: because Ambedkar could not command the Assembly's majority, win his elections or protect his bill in 1951, his chairmanship is filed as service. The revisionist case begins by refusing the trade. Influence over what a text says and power over what a polity does are different assets with different histories, and the drafting record, examined on its own terms, assigns the first asset to the chairman in a measure that the second asset's absence does nothing to reduce.

## The Programme Before the Office: Three Decades of Constitutional Argument

The technical drafter story requires the reader to believe that Ambedkar's constitutional ideas began with his appointment in August 1947. His biography refutes the premise at every stage. In January 1919, aged twenty seven and fresh from doctoral research at Columbia, he gave evidence before the Southborough Franchise Committee in Bombay and demanded separate representation and reserved seats for the Depressed Classes, arguing that political safeguards were the precondition of every other reform. The demand he carried into the committee room in 1947 was thus twenty eight years old, tested in committee rooms across three decades, and the men who appointed him knew its contents intimately because they had been resisting parts of it for most of that period.

At the Round Table Conferences in London, from November 1930 to December 1932, Ambedkar represented the Depressed Classes and pressed the safeguards case to its fullest extent: separate electorates, weightage in representation, reservation in the services and a declaration of fundamental rights with remedies against social disability. The Communal Award of 16 August 1932, issued by Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald, conceded separate electorates, and Mohandas Gandhi's fast against the Award in Yerwada Prison forced the settlement that replaced it. The Poona Pact of 24 September 1932 exchanged separate electorates for a larger number of reserved seats within joint electorates, 148 seats in the provincial legislatures against the Award's 71, and Ambedkar signed the exchange under a duress he described for the rest of his life as coercion. The episode is the seed of his drafting philosophy. A safeguard that depends on the majority's goodwill can be renegotiated under pressure at the moment it matters most; a safeguard written into the constitutional structure, supervised by courts and renewable only by constitutional process, cannot. The memorandum of 1947 and the clauses of 1948 are that philosophy generalised.

Between 1936 and 1947 the programme acquired its economic and institutional depth. Annihilation of Caste in 1936 supplied the theory of religion and reform. The Independent Labour Party, founded in 1936, and the Scheduled Castes Federation, founded in 1942, gave the programme party form. His service as Labour Member of the Viceroy's Executive Council from 1942 to 1946 gave him administrative command of employment exchanges, labour legislation and the machinery of the central state, experience that distinguishes his drafting from academic constitution making: he had operated the Government of India Act of 1935 from inside and knew its provisions as working law rather than as text. His books of the period, What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables in 1945 and Pakistan or the Partition of India, first published in 1940 and revised in 1945, show the analyst of federation, minorities and state structure whose services the Assembly would shortly require. When the Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946 created the Constituent Assembly framework, Ambedkar entered the Assembly from Bengal with the support of Jogendra Nath Mandal, and after Partition transferred his seat to Bombay. He arrived, in short, as the Assembly's most experienced constitutional controversialist, and the Drafting Committee's chairmanship formalised a standing the record had already created.

His Assembly career before the chairmanship already displayed the standing the appointment recognised. When Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution on 13 December 1946, setting the Assembly's goals of an independent sovereign republic guaranteeing justice, equality and freedoms, the debate of 17 December 1946 heard Ambedkar deliver one of its most praised speeches, welcoming the Resolution's social and economic objectives, regretting its silences, and pledging his cooperation to the constitution making enterprise despite his differences with the Congress. Members who heard the speech, delivered by the leader of a small opposition federation to an Assembly the Congress dominated, recorded its effect on the House; the speech announced that the safeguards question would be argued in the Assembly at the highest level of constitutional reasoning, by the man who had studied the subject longest. Three months later the memorandum arrived. Eight months later the Assembly, needing a chairman for the most technical of its committees, appointed the member whose command of the whole field, federation, rights, minorities, services and the machinery of the 1935 Act, no other candidate combined. Appointments of that kind are themselves evidence in an authorship dispute, because assemblies do not hand their text to a stenographer when a settlement of this complexity is required.

States and Minorities, submitted in March 1947, deserves a closer description than it usually receives, because its structure is the strongest single piece of evidence in this article. The memorandum is not a petition. It is a draft constitutional instrument for the protection of minorities, organised as articles, covering the United States of India it proposed, the terms of union, fundamental rights including remedies against social boycott, representation in legislatures, cabinets and services in proportion to population, separate settlement provisions, and an economic programme placing key industries and agricultural land under state ownership. Ambedkar submitted it to the Advisory Committee's sub committee on fundamental rights as the Scheduled Castes Federation's constitutional text. Parts of it the Advisory Committee absorbed; parts it declined; and the Drafting Committee, five months later, received a chairman whose positions on nearly every contested question the committee would face were a matter of printed record. The Austin reading asks readers to picture the committee's substantive direction descending from the Congress leadership into the chairman's pen. The memorandum's existence reverses the direction of flow on the questions it covers: the text was already written, and the committee's task, on those questions, was to decide how much of it India would adopt.

Jaffrelot's scholarship is valuable here precisely because it is not hagiography. His 2005 study distinguishes Ambedkar the political strategist, who adjusted demands to circumstance at the Round Table Conferences and at Poona, from Ambedkar the constitutional theorist, whose core demands, safeguards as structure, abolition as verdict, reform power over religious practice, remedies as the test of rights, remained constant across the adjustments. The distinction supplies the correct method for reading the drafting record. Where the final text departs from the memorandum, the departure marks a negotiation the chairman lost or a concession he judged necessary. Where the text follows the memorandum, and it follows it on reservation, on remedies, on the reform power and on abolition, the following marks authorship exercised and sustained. No theory of technical execution can accommodate a drafting output that reproduces, across four separate dimensions, the prior published programme of the man holding the pen, while departing from it exactly where the Assembly's majority is documented to have overruled him.

## Seven Seats, Nine Men, and 141 Days of Drafting Committee Work

The Assembly created the Drafting Committee by resolution on 29 August 1947, a fortnight after independence, and charged it with preparing a draft constitution from the committee reports already before the Assembly and from the constitutional text that the Constitutional Adviser, B. N. Rau, had prepared as a working basis. Rau's text, completed in October 1947 after his study tour of the United States, Canada, Ireland and Britain, contained 243 clauses and 13 schedules. It was raw material, not a constitution: Rau himself described his role as advisory, and the decisions that converted his comparative survey into Indian constitutional law belonged to the committee that elected Ambedkar as its chairman at its first meeting on 30 August 1947. That distinction, between the adviser's survey and the committee's decisions, is the first fact the technical drafter story blurs, because Rau held no vote, defended no clause on the floor, and answered to the committee rather than directing it.

The seven seats named on 29 August 1947 were occupied, across the committee's life, by nine men, and the arithmetic matters for the artifact presented in this section. The original seven were Ambedkar, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K. M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, B. L. Mitter and D. P. Khaitan. Mitter resigned on grounds of ill health and the Assembly appointed N. Madhava Rau, a former Diwan of Mysore and Travancore, in his place. Khaitan, the committee's commercial law authority, died in 1948, and T. T. Krishnamachari took the vacant seat. Illness, absence and state duties thinned attendance further. The committee sat for 141 days in total, a figure Ambedkar himself placed on the record in his closing speech of 25 November 1949, and the first Draft Constitution it produced, published on 21 February 1948, had grown Rau's 243 clauses into 315 articles and 8 schedules, a restructuring so extensive that the committee's text, rather than the adviser's, became the document the country debated.

The most quoted contemporary assessment of where authority sat inside that room came from Krishnamachari, speaking in the Assembly's November 1948 debate on the Draft Constitution. He told the House that of the seven members nominated, one had resigned and had not been replaced at the time, one had died, one was in America, another was occupied with state affairs, and one or two were kept away by distance and health, so that, in his words, the burden of drafting the Constitution fell on Ambedkar, whose performance of the task he called undoubtedly commendable. The speech is sometimes used as a devotee's flourish, which it was not: Krishnamachari was a Madras businessman and finance expert with no political debt to Ambedkar, and his point was administrative rather than heroic. Attendance failure had concentrated the work, and the chairman's office had absorbed drafting, revision and floor defence into a single pair of hands.

The committee's labelled reference artifact, the Drafting Committee Authority Chart, sets out the nine men, the seats they held and the substantive contribution each made, with an authority weighting that reflects the argument of this article: the Chair exercised deciding authority across the text, senior members exercised strong authority within assigned territories, and episodic members exercised limited authority because absence, however excusable, is a fact about influence as much as about attendance.

Three member portraits sharpen the chart's argument. K. M. Munshi came to the committee from the Fundamental Rights Sub Committee, where the rights list had been fought over in the spring of 1947, and his drafting hand is visible in the cultural and educational rights of minorities and in the free speech clause's architecture; he was a Congress loyalist and a cultural traditionalist whose presence on the committee gave its text a defender inside the majority party's counsels. N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, a former prime minister of Kashmir state and the Assembly's most experienced administrator, carried the language question with Munshi into the compromise of September 1949 that made Hindi in Devanagari script the Union's official language with English continuing for fifteen years, a settlement the committee's text had to house. Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, the Madras Advocate General and the committee's senior jurist, subjected clauses to a scrutiny his colleagues acknowledged as the body's legal conscience, particularly on the judiciary, property and the federation's financial scheme. Syed Mohammad Saadullah, the former Premier of Assam, connected the committee to the Sixth Schedule's tribal councils in the Assam hills, which the Bordoloi sub committee had designed and which his membership helped carry into the draft. These were men of standing, and the committee's text is better for their territories. The point of the weighting column is not their diminishment. It is that the territories were assigned, integrated and defended through the chair, and that the chairman alone had a published programme spanning all of them.

Read as a whole, the chart exposes the weakness in describing the committee as a college of equals whose chairman happened to write well. Two of the nine men left before the text was finished. Two more carried heavy responsibilities elsewhere. The members who did sustained work, Ayyar, Ayyangar and Munshi, worked in defined territories and frequently defended positions in the Assembly that the committee had settled under its chairman. Authority inside a drafting body is measured by whose formulations survive scrutiny, whose arguments answer objections on the floor, and whose earlier writings predict the final text. On all three measures the chairman's position was central in a sense that the word procedural cannot reach, and the next three sections test that centrality where it matters most: on the substance.

## Inside the Working Method: Scrutiny, Revision and the Second Draft

The committee's working method is recoverable from the record the Lok Sabha archives preserve and from Ambedkar's own accounting of 25 November 1949, and the method bears on the authorship question because a body's method decides whose mind the text passes through. The committee began from Rau's advisory draft, which had itself digested the committee reports of 1947. It proceeded clause by clause, hearing the adviser's comparative notes, testing formulations against the Government of India Act of 1935 where administrative continuity was intended and against foreign constitutions where the Assembly's committees had directed innovation. Decisions were taken in committee and recorded, and the chairman carried the settled clause into his drafting, returning with formulations for the committee's approval. The surviving attribution pattern, visible in the Assembly debates where members appealed to the chairman's explanations as the committee's reasons, shows Ambedkar functioning as the body's rapporteur, the member through whom its reasoning became public and answerable.

A second institution complicated the flow, and honesty about it strengthens rather than weakens this article's finding. In October 1947 the Congress Assembly Party constituted a committee of experts, and in the spring of 1948 a Union Constitution Committee review and a special committee of the Assembly, including Nehru, Patel and Prasad among its members, scrutinised the February draft before its general debate. Austin relied on these bodies to argue that political control over the text sat outside the Drafting Committee, and the minutes of the period do show the leadership amending provisions, on property and on the emergency powers among others. What the scrutiny process also shows is the direction of the remaining traffic: objections travelled to the Drafting Committee, and the Drafting Committee, under its chairman, returned the revised text in October 1948, incorporating what it accepted and defending what it retained. Control over a text in an assembly is rarely exclusive; the test is where the text's surviving form was produced, and the October draft, like the February draft before it, issued from the committee room over the chairman's name.

The revision cycle of 1948 is the least celebrated and most revealing phase of the committee's life. The February draft had been published for public comment, an unusual act of constitutional openness for its period, and the responses, from provincial governments jealous of their taxation powers, from the bar councils, from chambers of commerce alarmed at the property clauses, and from political organisations across the spectrum, reached the committee in volume. The committee spent the middle months of 1948 working through the responses, and the second draft of October 1948 emerged with alterations the chairman enumerated in his speech of 4 November 1948, on the President's powers, on the services, on the financial relations between the Union and the states. Revision on that scale is not copy editing. Each accepted criticism required a judgment about the design's priorities, and each rejected one required a defence the chairman would have to deliver on the floor. The man who performs both operations across a whole constitution is, by any usable definition, among its authors, and on the evidence of the two drafts he was the first among them.

The dated spine of the work is worth setting down in one place, because the sequence itself is an argument about stamina and control. The Assembly resolved the committee into existence on 29 August 1947; the committee met on 30 August and elected its chairman; Rau submitted his advisory draft in October 1947 after his foreign tour; the committee's first draft was published on 21 February 1948 and opened to public comment; the leadership's scrutiny bodies examined it through the spring of 1948; the revised draft appeared in October 1948; Ambedkar moved its consideration on 4 November 1948; the clause by clause stage ran, with adjournments, to October 1949; the third reading closed in November 1949; the Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949; members signed on 24 January 1950; and the text commenced on 26 January 1950. Across twenty nine months, one office appears at every station of the sequence, presenting, revising, answering and closing. Institutions remember sequences of that kind when they assign authorship, whatever vocabulary they later choose for the assignment, and the Assembly's own valedictory proceedings, in which speaker after speaker addressed the chairman as the text's expositor, assigned it in the plainest terms available to a parliamentary body.

One member of the production process is routinely forgotten, and restoring him clarifies the committee's method. S. N. Mukerjee served as the Assembly secretariat's chief draftsman, the professional hand that converted committee decisions into formal legislative language for scrutiny. Every mature drafting operation separates the deciding mind from the perfecting hand, and the Indian founding was no exception: the committee decided, the chairman directed, and the secretariat's draftsman polished. Textbook summaries collapse the three functions into the word drafting and then award the collapsed function to whichever reputation is at hand. The record supports the separation, and the separation supports this article's claim, because the existence of a professional draftsman in the building proves that drafting skill as such was available to the Assembly in more than one office. What the chairman supplied beyond that available skill, programme, doctrine and defence, is the surplus the substantive authority thesis names.

The consideration stage that followed, from November 1948 to October 1949, converted the committee's authority into the Assembly's verdict 114 days of debate, with the amendment figures already recorded in this article. A drafting chairman's authority faces its severest test in this stage, because every settled clause can be reopened by any member with an amendment and a speech. Ambedkar's performance across the 114 days is documented in twelve volumes of debates: he accepted amendments that improved the text without disturbing its design, resisted amendments that disturbed the design while conceding their drafting points, and on the contested social clauses, reservation, religion and abolition, he carried the committee's formulations substantially intact. The third reading and adoption on 26 November 1949 closed the process, and his closing speech of 25 November, with its warning about the life of contradictions, was accepted by the House as the text's authoritative exposition. Assemblies do not ask their clerks for the meaning of the work. They ask the author.

## Reservation: The Design Ambedkar Carried Into the Committee Room

Ambedkar arrived at the Drafting Committee with his reservation design already drafted, dated and published. In March 1947, five months before the committee existed, he submitted the memorandum States and Minorities to the Constituent Assembly's Advisory Committee on behalf of the All India Scheduled Castes Federation. The memorandum proposed constitutional safeguards with the precision of a bill: reservation of seats in legislatures for the Scheduled Castes, reservation in the public services, separate representation in the executive, reserved funds for education, and safeguards framed as fundamental rights enforceable against the state rather than as concessions revocable by majority vote. When scholars describe Ambedkar as executing other men's decisions, the memorandum is the document they have to explain away, because the architecture the Constitution eventually adopted, political reservation, service reservation and protective discrimination authority, is visible in it in embryo before Nehru or Patel had committed the Assembly to any settled design.

The constitutional text that emerged from the committee carries the memorandum's logic in three places. The equality code opens with a general guarantee and then authorises the state to make special provision for socially and educationally backward classes and for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes; the services chapter repeats the structure, permitting reservation in appointments for backward classes inadequately represented in the services. These enabling clauses, which became the first amendment's confirmation in 1951 after the Supreme Court's Champakam Dorairajan judgment of 9 April 1951 struck down the Madras communal order, were not decorative. They converted reservation from an administrative practice inherited from the colonial services into a constitutional permission that no ordinary statute or hostile judgment could remove. Ambedkar defended that structure in the Assembly during the November and December 1948 debates on the equality articles, arguing against amendments that would have confined state action to a narrower formula and insisting that equality of opportunity required authority to compensate for inherited disadvantage.

The territorial half of the design sits in the Fifth and Sixth Schedules, which govern the administration of Scheduled Areas and tribal areas and give the Governor and the President instruments of protective oversight, and in the special provisions for the north east that Saadullah's membership helped to shape. Here the memorandum's core demand, that vulnerable communities required constitutional machinery and not merely declarations, survived in institutional form. Ambedkar's interventions of October 1949, during the final consideration of the schedules and the reservation articles in their near final numbering, defended the ten year initial span for legislative reservation as a period subject to renewal by Parliament rather than as a concession extracted from him. The distinction mattered to him: a safeguard designed as renewable machinery belongs to the protected community's ongoing claim, while a safeguard designed as charity belongs to the giver.

The legislative half of the design deserves separate attention, because its details show drafting as decision at the smallest scale. The draft articles that became Articles 330 and 332 reserved seats for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies in proportion to population, within joint electorates inherited from the Poona settlement, and the companion provisions reserved seats for the Anglo Indian community by nomination where the President judged representation inadequate. Every element of that package was a choice. Proportionality to population, rather than the weightage Ambedkar had sought in 1932, fixed the arithmetic. Joint electorates with reserved seats, rather than separate electorates, fixed the constituency structure in which a reserved seat's winner needed votes beyond his own community, a feature Ambedkar had criticised since 1932 as diluting the representative's accountability to the represented, and accepted in 1949 because the Assembly would not reopen Poona. The ten year span fixed the review mechanism. A chairman transcribing decisions does not leave a trail of this kind, in which the final text can be decomposed, element by element, into positions its chairman had published, defended, lost or carried across twenty years of negotiation.

The framework's boundary decisions show the same negotiation continuing past adoption into the republic's first executive acts. The Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order of 1950, issued by the President under the article the committee had drafted for specifying the castes deemed Scheduled, confined the status in its third paragraph to persons professing the Hindu religion, extended to Sikhs in 1956 and to Buddhists only in 1990. The religion condition, a survival from the Government of India Act era's schedules, meant that a Dalit who converted to Christianity or Islam lost the reservation the Constitution's design had built for caste disability, a result Ambedkar's programme, aimed at caste rather than creed, sat awkwardly beside and which his own conversion of October 1956 protested by example. The episode belongs in this audit because it marks the exact frontier of the chairman's authority: inside the text, the safeguards architecture was his; at the text's administrative edge, where his office no longer governed, the architecture could be narrowed by the very state it was built to bind. Reservation politics in India has been fought along that frontier ever since, in litigation over the 1950 Order that reaches the Supreme Court in generation after generation.

One clause in the services chapter exposes the negotiation inside the chairman's own design with unusual clarity. The draft article that became Article 335 provided that the claims of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to services and posts would be taken into consideration consistently with the maintenance of efficiency of administration. Critics within the Assembly and outside it read the efficiency proviso as a built in excuse for administrators to defeat reservation, and it has been litigated and debated as such for decades. The proviso's presence in the text the chairman piloted records a compromise he accepted to carry the larger structure: representation guaranteed as a constitutional claim, with its quantum left to the state under a stated constraint. In the August 1949 debates on the services and representation articles in their final numbering, Ambedkar defended the combined settlement as a whole, refusing amendments that would have struck the proviso and amendments that would have widened it, because either change reopened the balance on which the package had passed. The episode is a useful corrective to two sentimentalities at once. Against the minimising reading, it shows the chairman managing a design's internal trade offs in public, the daily work of an author. Against the heroic reading, it shows the author's design carrying a flaw he could see and could not remove, the kind of flaw that separates constituent politics from pamphleteering.

The services provisions show the same signature under different pressure. The draft article that became Article 16 guaranteed equality of opportunity in public employment and then, in its fourth clause, authorised the state to reserve appointments for any backward class of citizens inadequately represented in the services. During the Assembly's debates in November and December 1948, members attacked the clause as a breach of the equality the same article proclaimed, and the chairman's defence rested on the distinction his memorandum had insisted upon: equality of opportunity between competitors of unequal starting position is a form of words, while representation in the services is a fact about who administers the state. He carried the clause. Its subsequent career, from the Champakam Dorairajan judgment of April 1951 through the First Amendment's extension of the special provision power to the equality article's companion clause, into the great reservation litigation of later decades, ran along the track his defence had laid. When a drafting decision structures litigation seventy years later, describing its author as a technician is a choice about memory, not about evidence.

### Why did Ambedkar accept a ten year limit on legislative reservation in 1949?

He accepted the limit as a renewable mechanism rather than a terminal date. In the October 1949 debates he treated the ten year span as a first period that Parliament could extend on evidence of continuing disadvantage, and Parliament has extended it by amendment every decade since. The burden of proof fell on continuation debates, not on the safeguard's existence.

Opposition inside the Assembly clarifies how much of this design was his to defend. Members such as H. J. Khandekar and others pressed the committee from one side for stronger and wider safeguards, while members wary of caste categories in a constitution pressed from the other side against communal electorates and separate representation, which Ambedkar had already surrendered at the Poona Pact of 24 September 1932 and did not attempt to revive. His position in 1948 and 1949 was therefore triangular: against separate electorates, which he had abandoned as politically impossible after 1932; against pure formal equality, which he argued would freeze the hierarchy it refused to see; and for a middle architecture of joint electorates, reserved seats, service reservation and protective schedules. That middle architecture is recognisably the States and Minorities design moderated by the Assembly's arithmetic, and moderation by arithmetic is what substantive authorship looks like inside a constituent body. A technician transcribes; an author negotiates his text through a hostile count and keeps its structure standing.

Jaffrelot's 2005 study gives this section of the record its sharpest formulation. Reading the memorandum beside the constitutional text, Jaffrelot showed that Ambedkar's safeguards thinking joined political representation to economic and educational instruments, a joining that distinguishes his programme from the purely electoral reservation the colonial state had operated since 1909. The Constitution adopted the joining in part: representation and services entered the text, the memorandum's more radical economic proposals, including state ownership of key industries and agricultural land as a constitutional mandate, did not. The pattern of adoption is itself evidence about the kind of authority Ambedkar held. His designs were the starting point of negotiation, amended at the margins and defeated at the radical edge, which is the biography of an author inside an assembly, not of a clerk outside it.

## Religion Clauses and the Secular Settlement Ambedkar Helped Design

The second dimension is the religion settlement of the draft articles that became Articles 25 to 28, guaranteeing freedom of conscience, the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, the autonomy of religious denominations, freedom from religious taxation and a settlement on religious instruction in state institutions. The conventional summary treats these clauses as a Nehruvian inheritance, secularism descending from the Congress leadership's ideology into the committee's text. The drafting record supports a more precise claim. Ambedkar's Annihilation of Caste, written in 1936 as an address the Jat Pat Todak Mandal of Lahore declined to let him deliver, had argued eleven years before the committee met that Hindu religious authority was the operating system of caste and that no merely political reform could succeed against it. A chairman holding that analysis did not approach the religion clauses as neutral drafting. He approached them as the point where the Constitution either licensed the state to reform religious practice or surrendered the citizen to it.

The decisive clause is the temple entry and reform provision within the freedom of religion article, which empowers the state to throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus and to regulate secular activity associated with religious practice. Ambedkar defended the reform power in the Assembly's December 1948 debates on the religion articles against members who argued that the state should leave religious practice entirely untouched. His argument, continuous with 1936, was that religion in India governed civil status, access to water, roads, schools and temples, so that a constitution guaranteeing equality could not declare the governing institution immune from law. The clause's drafting, which subjects religious practice to public order, morality and health and to the Constitution's other rights, subordinates religious freedom to the equality code at exactly the points where caste operated. That subordination is a doctrinal choice, and it is Ambedkar's signature: the same hierarchy appears in his 1947 memorandum, which made the abolition of social disabilities a condition of religious liberty rather than an exception to it.

The denominational clauses show the committee negotiating a settlement rather than imposing one, and the chairman's management of the negotiation is itself a form of authority. The draft protected the freedom of religious denominations to manage their own affairs in matters of religion, to own and administer property, and to maintain institutions, while the individual freedom article protected conscience, practice and propagation. The propagation guarantee drew sustained attack in the December 1948 debates from members who feared its use by organised missions, and amendments to delete or qualify the word propagate were moved and defeated, with the committee holding that a freedom of religion confined to private belief would be a lesser freedom than the Assembly had promised in its Objectives Resolution of 22 January 1947. On the other flank, members pressed for wider state power over religious endowments and processions. The settlement the chairman piloted balanced the two pressures with a lawyer's distribution of risks: belief absolute, practice qualified by public order, morality, health and the other rights; denominations autonomous in religion, regulated in property and secular activity; the state forbidden to tax for any religion, barred from religious instruction in wholly state funded institutions, and empowered to reform. Each element can be traced to the committee reports of 1947, and the balance among them, the point at which each pressure was answered and each line held, bears the chairman's arbitration throughout the religion debates.

The Sikh, Jain and Buddhist inclusion in the temple entry explanation, by which the reference to Hindus in the reform clause was construed to include those communities, illustrates the drafting mind at work on social fact. The clause's purpose was to open institutions whose exclusionary practice rested on the same scriptural economy as caste; the explanation ensured that the reform power reached the institutions of the Indic traditions that shared the economy, while leaving mosques, churches and other institutions under the general law. Critics have read the inclusion as an assimilation of distinct faiths, and the reading is a live scholarly dispute that this article records without adopting; what is not disputable is the deliberateness of the device. A sentence of explanation, inserted at the drafting stage, decided which communities the reform power could reach, and decisions of that reach are the substance of constitution making.

### How did Ambedkar's view of religious practice shape the temple entry provision?

His 1936 argument in Annihilation of Caste held that caste is maintained by religious sanction, so absolute protection of religious practice would protect untouchability itself. The religion article he piloted protected belief and worship while empowering the state to open public temples to all Hindus and regulate religion's secular side, making reform a constitutional permission.

The fiscal and instructional clauses complete the settlement's map. The draft article that became Article 27 forbade compelling any person to pay taxes whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination, a bar the committee framed against the background of colonial era religious endowment finance. The companion provision on religious instruction drew the line state institutions still observe: no religious instruction in institutions wholly maintained out of state funds, recognition based instruction permitted with consent in aided institutions, and state administered trust funded institutions left to their trust terms. Ambedkar's committee defended the distinctions as the operational meaning of a secular state in a deeply religious society: the exchequer neutral, the classroom partitioned by the source of its funds, and the reform power reserved for the points where religious practice collided with citizenship. The settlement's durability is its own commentary. The architecture of Articles 25 to 30 survived the republic's religious conflicts substantially unamended, and the Supreme Court's secularism jurisprudence, from the Shirur Mutt case of 1954 onward, worked outward from the committee's categories of belief, practice and secular activity associated with religion.

The minority institutions settlement of the same December days shows the chairman working the boundary between the religion clauses and the equality code. The draft article that became Article 30 guaranteed religious and linguistic minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice and barred discrimination in state aid to minority managed institutions. In the debates of 8 December 1948, members pressed to confine the guarantee to religious minorities or to subject minority institutions to fuller state control, and Ambedkar defended the wider text, treating educational autonomy as one of the securities by which a minority's culture survived majority rule. The guarantee sat beside Article 29's protection of any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture, a clause whose breadth, extending beyond minorities to any section, the committee deliberately chose. Read together, the religion and culture clauses reveal the settlement's sophistication: the majority's religion was opened to reform, the minorities' institutions were closed to interference, and the asymmetry was principled rather than accidental, because the danger each clause addressed ran in opposite directions. Ambedkar's defence of that asymmetry, against critics who called it unequal treatment, belongs to the same body of argument as his 1947 memorandum, in which safeguards were calibrated to the vulnerability of the group protected rather than distributed by a uniform formula.

The settlement also shows the limits of his authority, and the limits confirm the adjudication rather than weakening it. Ambedkar had argued in States and Minorities for a far more interventionist state, and his personal preference, recorded in his writings, ran toward a uniform civil framework; the Assembly instead preserved separate personal laws, deferred the uniform civil code to the directive principles, and left religious endowments under mixed state supervision. On the terrain where his prior programme met the text, the reform power, the temple entry clause and the subordination of practice to equality, the programme prevailed. On the terrain where the Congress leadership and the Assembly's religious conservatives combined against him, it did not. Austin's institutional reading explains the defeats well. It explains the victories badly, because the victories track a single member's published programme across a decade, and coincidence is not an explanation available to a careful historian.

The Constituent Assembly Debates of December 1948 supply the texture. When members moved amendments to narrow the state's reform power or to protect denominational practice from the equality articles, it was the chairman who answered, clause by clause, explaining the committee's reasoning, conceding drafting adjustments where the objection was technical and holding the line where the objection was doctrinal. Floor defence of this kind is not a clerical function in any assembly: the member who answers for a text, amendment after amendment, is exercising the authority of its author in public, and the Assembly treated him accordingly, directing its theological, legal and political objections to his desk.

## Two Orders of Rights: The Architecture Ambedkar Explained on 4 November 1948

On 4 November 1948 Ambedkar rose in the Assembly to move that the Draft Constitution be taken into consideration, and the speech he delivered, preserved in Volume VII of the Constituent Assembly Debates, is the fullest statement of the rights architecture he had built into the text. The design divides the Constitution's promises into two orders. Fundamental Rights, enforceable by courts against the state, protect the citizen's liberty, equality and remedies. Directive Principles of State Policy, expressly non-enforceable, instruct future governments toward social and economic ends: equitable distribution of resources, living wages, village panchayats, free legal aid in later form. Critics in the Assembly attacked the division from both directions, some demanding that the directives be made justiciable promises and others dismissing them as pious wishes. Ambedkar defended the division as the design's intelligence. A government that ignored the directives, he argued, would answer for the neglect to the electorate at election time, and a court asked to enforce a living wage against a treasury would be asked to govern rather than to judge.

The defence was not improvised on the floor. The distinction between rights a court can enforce and instructions a government must honour politically runs through Ambedkar's earlier constitutional writing, and States and Minorities itself separated safeguards requiring immediate legal protection from the economic programme the state was to pursue. When Professor K. T. Shah moved his amendments in November 1948 to declare India a union of socialist republics and to harden the directives, Ambedkar answered that the social and economic direction of the state should not be frozen into the text beyond the reach of future parliaments, a reply that reveals the design's purpose: the rights a citizen holds against the state are entrenched, while the policies a majority pursues remain democratic. That is a theory of constitutionalism, stated by the chairman in his own name, and it disposed of amendments that a mere draftsman would have had no standing to resist on principle.

The remedies clause completes the architecture, and Ambedkar's valuation of it is on the record. During the December 1948 debates he described the provision empowering citizens to move the Supreme Court directly for enforcement of fundamental rights as the Constitution's heart and soul, the provision without which, he said, the rights chapter would be a nullity. The five writs the clause carries, habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, converted rights from declarations into litigation, and the chairman's superlative was chosen with a litigator's knowledge of what unremedied rights are worth in a district court.

The free speech settlement inside the rights chapter adds a third decision to the pattern, this one shared between the chairman and Munshi. The draft's speech clause carried restrictions in the interest of public order and decency, and during the December 1948 debates the Assembly struck the word sedition from the restriction grounds, accepting K. M. Munshi's argument that the term had been the colonial state's instrument against the national movement itself. The committee absorbed the change and Ambedkar, answering for the clause, treated the deletion as consistent with the design: the republic would punish incitement and disorder as acts, and would not inherit the offence of exciting disaffection against the government as such. The distinction failed to survive the republic's first years intact, since the First Amendment of 1951 widened the restriction grounds after the Supreme Court's free speech judgments in the Romesh Thappar and Brij Bhushan cases of 26 May 1950, but the failure dates from the amendment and not from the drafting. In the text as adopted, the speech clause bore the founding generation's considered judgment about the state they had fought, and the chairman's defence of it is recorded in the same debates that carry his rights architecture.

Two further drafting decisions inside the rights chapter repay attention, because both were close run and both left permanent marks. The first is the rejection of due process of law in the draft article on life and personal liberty, which emerged as Article 21 with the Japanese formula, procedure established by law. B. N. Rau, during his 1947 study tour, had been warned by Justice Felix Frankfurter of the United States Supreme Court that an American style due process clause would hand judges a veto over social legislation; Rau reported the warning, and the committee, with Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar's legal authority behind the choice, settled on the narrower formula. The decision limited judicial review of liberty deprivations to the fact of legal procedure and shaped Indian rights jurisprudence until the Supreme Court's Maneka Gandhi judgment of 25 January 1978 rebuilt substantive review upon the article. The chairman defended the committee's settlement on the floor as a considered balance between judicial supremacy and legislative responsibility. Reasonable scholars dispute the wisdom of the choice; none dispute that it was a choice, made in the drafting process and argued for by the men who made it, rather than a line copied from a predecessor text.

The second decision is the property settlement, in the draft articles that became Articles 19 and 31, where the committee attempted to hold compensation for acquired property at a level courts could police without converting every land reform statute into litigation. The zamindari abolition programme of the Congress governments depended on the settlement, and the Assembly's property debates of September 1949 were among the founding's most technical. Ambedkar piloted the clauses that fixed compensation principles while protecting specified acquisition laws, and the settlement's instability, exposed when the Patna High Court and then the Supreme Court tested the Bihar land reforms legislation in 1951, produced the First Amendment's Ninth Schedule device within two years. The property episode is the adjudication's most honest exhibit: the committee's design failed to hold, the failure was structural and foreseeable in debate, and the failure nevertheless belongs to the record of authors exercising judgment under pressure, not to the record of clerks copying instructions, since clerks cannot fail at design.

### Why did Ambedkar call the remedies provision the heart and soul of the Constitution?

Speaking on 9 December 1948, in the debate on the draft article that became Article 32, he argued that rights without a direct route to enforcement are declarations rather than law. The provision lets any citizen move the Supreme Court for writs protecting fundamental rights. His superlative was precise: the clause converts every other right into a claim.

The preventive detention clauses test the rights architecture at its weakest point, and the chairman's handling of them belongs in an honest audit. The draft article that became Article 22 authorised preventive detention with safeguards of advisory board review and a three month threshold beyond which a board's report was required, a provision liberals in the Assembly attacked in the September 1949 debates as a betrayal of the freedom the rights chapter existed to secure. Ambedkar defended the clause as the price of the state's survival in the conditions of 1949, partition violence, insurgency in Telangana and the integration of the princely states still incomplete, and he did not pretend the defence was comfortable. The episode rounds the portrait this article has drawn. The chairman's authority was exercised in defence of his opponents' priorities where the committee had settled them, as faithfully as in defence of his own programme, and the fidelity is part of the evidence that the office he held was the Assembly's drafting authority in substance: the man to whom the whole text, congenial clauses and uncongenial alike, belonged for purposes of explanation and defence.

How did the Draft Constitution settle the question of votes for all adults? A later section of the founding settlement, universal adult franchise, shows the same chairman operating where no personal programme of his was at stake, which is useful evidence about the office itself. The committee's text adopted adult suffrage for elections to the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies, a commitment the Assembly debated in 1949 against doubts about literacy and administrative capacity in a country where the 1941 census had recorded literacy below a fifth of adults. Ambedkar defended the franchise articles as the democratic premise of the whole document, the point at which the Constitution's promises acquired an enforcer more reliable than any court: an electorate of roughly 173 million voters at the first general election of 1951 to 1952. The franchise defence matters for the adjudication because it shows the chairman's authority being used to carry the Assembly's collective commitments with the same energy he spent on his own programme. Authority that only appears on pet subjects is hobby; authority exercised across the text is office.

The rights section of the record therefore cuts against the minimising reading in a specific way. Austin's account treats the rights design as a consensus product of the Fundamental Rights Sub Committee under J. B. Kripalani, the Advisory Committee under Patel and the Assembly's general liberalism, and the committee reports of 1947 do support a collective genealogy for the rights list itself. What the genealogy does not explain is the architecture's articulation in the final text, the two orders and their stated rationale, defended by one man in a set piece speech and a dozen amendment exchanges. Ideas in constitutions have many parents; their arrangement into a defensible system, and their public defence under attack, is authorship of the kind that matters.

## Article 17: One Sentence Against Untouchability

The fourth dimension is the shortest text in the founding and the most personal. The draft article debated on 29 November 1948, which became Article 17, reads in full substance: untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden, and the enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. The drafting choice is the sentence's refusal of qualifications. Alternatives before the Assembly would have framed the provision as a general anti-discrimination guarantee or tied abolition to the practices of particular communities; the committee's text named the institution, abolished it without adjective or exception, and attached criminal enforcement to its practice. Ambedkar's hand in that phrasing is the brief's fourth claim, and the debate record supports it: he piloted the clause through the Assembly, answered the objections, and treated its brevity as its strength.

Consider what the sentence does doctrinally. A general equality guarantee, of the kind the preceding articles supply, forbids the state from discriminating; Article 17 forbids a social practice, binds private actors through the penal law Parliament was instructed to make, and declares the abolition as accomplished fact rather than as aspiration. For a chairman whose 1936 text had located untouchability in religious sanction and whose 1947 memorandum had demanded its abolition as a fundamental right, the clause was the point where biography and doctrine meet. The Assembly's discussion on 29 November 1948 included members who sought wider wording against all social disabilities and members who feared the clause's breadth; the text that survived was the committee's sentence, and its survival through the Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955 and the stronger Protection of Civil Rights Act of 1976 shows a design that later parliaments built upon rather than replaced.

The contrast with the technical drafter story is sharpest here, because no theory of secretarial drafting explains why this clause, alone among the rights, is written as a verdict. Ambedkar had announced his verdict in advance: the 1927 Mahad satyagraha, the 1930 to 1935 Kalaram Temple struggle at Nashik and the 1935 Yeola declaration that he would not die a Hindu had each stated, in the language of movement politics, the proposition Article 17 states in the language of law. When the chairman's two decades of public argument and the committee's single sentence coincide word for word in intent, the economical explanation is authorship. The burden falls on the minimising reading to explain the coincidence away, and Austin's book, for all its archival depth, never attempts the explanation at the level of this clause.

Even the punctuation of the final text carries the committee's decision. Article 17 as adopted prints the word untouchability within quotation marks, a drafting device signalling that the term names a known social practice in its lived forms rather than a legal category the article pauses to define. Members who wanted definitions, lists of disabilities or a statutory schedule attached to the clause were answered by the design itself: definition would have licensed argument about whether a given humiliation fell inside the list, and the committee's sentence was built to leave no such doorway. The breadth of practice in any form performs the same work from the other side, reaching disabilities not yet named. Drafting at this level of compression is a form of argument, and the argument is continuous with the chairman's speeches across two decades, in which untouchability appears never as one discrimination among others but as a system requiring abolition by verdict. The 29 November 1948 debate also records the Assembly's instinctive grasp of the moment: members rose to support the clause across the House's divisions, and the congratulations offered that day were directed to the chairman personally, by name, in a manner the debates reserve for a text's acknowledged author.

The clause's drafting history contains one further decision worth isolating. The committee placed the abolition inside the fundamental rights chapter, between the equality code and the freedom articles, rather than in the directive principles where social aspirations were gathered. Placement is doctrine: a right in the enforceable chapter can be pleaded in court, and the abolition's concluding words, making the enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability an offence punishable in accordance with law, directed Parliament to build the penal machinery the right required. The Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955, renamed and strengthened as the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976, and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act of 1989 stand in a direct line from that drafting placement. Later parliaments chose the penalties; the committee, under its chairman, chose the location that made the penalties a constitutional obligation rather than a policy option. Granville Austin's narrative, organised around the Assembly's consensus leadership, passes over the placement quickly. The revisionist reading lingers on it, correctly, because placement decisions are where drafting committees quietly decide the future's menu of possible laws.

## Austin Against Jaffrelot and Teltumbde: Adjudicating the Drafting Committee's Authority

The disagreement can be stated fairly, at its strongest on both sides, because the evidence is in view. Austin's reading, set out in Cornerstone of a Nation in 1966, holds that the Constitution was the product of a national consensus managed by the Congress leadership, that its main decisions on federation, rights and minority protection were taken in the committees of 1947 and on the Assembly floor, and that Ambedkar's contribution, immense as drafting and as floor management, was the contribution of the Assembly's chosen instrument. Jaffrelot's reading, in Dr Ambedkar and Untouchability in 2005, holds that the chairman brought a systematic constitutional programme from his own movement writings, that the programme's core entered the text, and that the consensus story understates the conflict Ambedkar's presence embodied. Teltumbde's reading, in Republic of Caste in 2018, adds the politics of the aftermath: the consensus story became the governing class's title deed to the Constitution, and recovering Ambedkar's authorship is a condition of recovering the document's anti-caste purpose.

The verdict of this article follows the four dimensional audit. On reservation, the 1947 memorandum predicts the text's architecture too closely for transmission to be coincidence. On religion, the reform power and temple entry clauses enact the argument of 1936. On rights, the two order design was explained and defended by the chairman as theory, not transcribed as instruction. On untouchability, the single sentence carries his movement's verdict into law. Against this, Austin's evidence establishes real and continuing collective authorship at the level of the Assembly, the committees and the Congress leadership's political vetoes; Ambedkar lost on separate electorates, on the uniform civil code's placement, on the economic radicalism of his memorandum and on much else. The two findings together yield the precise conclusion the minimising formula obscures: Ambedkar held substantive intellectual authority over the Constitution's most consequential social choices, exercised inside a collective process that defeated him where the majority combined against him. Authorship and defeat are compatible. They are, in constituent assemblies, the normal condition of authorship.

### What does Ambedkar's 1951 statement on the Hindu Code Bill reveal about his drafting years?

His 1951 statement, issued as the Hindu Code Bill stalled in the provisional Parliament, defended the programme he had carried into the committee room in 1947: reform of Hindu personal law on marriage, inheritance and women's property as the equality code's necessary continuation. Read beside his resignation as Law Minister on 27 September 1951, it refutes the technician picture.

The strongest counter to the substantive authority reading comes from Ambedkar's own mouth, and it must be answered rather than avoided. In the Rajya Sabha on 2 September 1953, during the debate on the Andhra State Bill, he told the House that his friends said he had made the Constitution, but that he was quite prepared to be the first person to burn it, that he had been a hack who did much against his will. Austin's defenders cite the outburst as the author's own verdict on his authority: a man who calls himself a hack, they argue, is telling the truth about the drafting room. The context dissolves the argument. The 1953 speech was an attack on the Constitution's working, on linguistic states, on the treatment of minorities and on governments that had, in his judgment, emptied his safeguards of force; its target was implementation by the Congress governments of 1950 to 1953, not the authorship of 1947 to 1949. A writer who denounces the performance of his play is not denying that he wrote it. Intellectual authorship and political control are different assets, and Ambedkar held the first in full while never holding the second, which is the tragedy his 1953 anger records.

Teltumbde's contribution requires one qualification in the opposite direction, and stating it keeps the adjudication honest. Republic of Caste is not a celebration of its subject at every point: its author criticises Ambedkar's compromises, his acceptance of the Poona settlement's logic, his faith in constitutional methods against the social power the book anatomises, and the limits of the programme the memorandum embodied. The criticism matters because it separates the substantive authority thesis from ancestor worship. Teltumbde restores Ambedkar's authorship in order to argue with the author, which is the treatment scholars give to thinkers whose texts decided things. A draftsman is not argued with across seven decades; an author is. When the fiercest contemporary critic of the constitutional settlement and the institutional historian most associated with its consensus story both organise their books around the chairman's decisions, the decisions' centrality is the premise they share, and premises shared by adversaries are the closest thing historiography offers to settled ground.

Fairness to the minimising reading also requires recording the chairman's own distribution of credit, because it is the exhibit his defenders quote most. In the closing speech of 25 November 1949 Ambedkar told the Assembly that the credit given to him did not really belong to him, that it belonged partly to B. N. Rau, who had prepared the rough draft, and partly to S. N. Mukerjee, the chief draftsman, whose difficult formulations he praised, and he thanked his committee colleagues by implication throughout. Read as the Austin tradition reads it, the speech is the author's confession of instrumentality. Read in full, it is a chairman's formal courtesy of a conventional kind, delivered in the same speech that delivered the Assembly its most quoted political warning and its authoritative summary of the text's meaning, and followed by his unembarrassed defence of the draft's every contested choice. Generosity in distributing credit is evidence about a man's manners in victory. It is weak evidence about the distribution of intellectual labour, and it cannot outweigh the documentary trail of programme, draft and defence that the preceding sections have assembled. Authors who shared nothing do not need to be generous; chairmen who carried a text through 114 days of amendment can afford to be.

A word is owed to the quality of Austin's book itself, because adjudication that caricatures the losing side proves nothing. Cornerstone of a Nation remains, six decades after publication, the account against which every later study of the founding defines itself, and its archival foundations, the Assembly's records, the leaders' correspondence and Austin's interviews with participants, are foundations this article also stands upon. The correction the revisionists administer is one of emphasis and archive rather than of fact. Austin foregrounded the consensus the leadership needed and treated the chairman's programme as one input among many; Jaffrelot widened the archive to the movement writings and showed the input arriving with its architecture complete; Teltumbde asked why the narrower archive had governed memory for so long and who benefited from the governing. Synthesis, rather than victory, is the honest description of the outcome: the founding was a collective process with a centre of intellectual gravity, and the centre sat, on the social constitution, in the chairman's office. A scholarship that can hold both halves of that sentence has outgrown the textbook paragraph with which this article began.

Teachers and readers who work through this debate in seminars frequently turn the Authority Chart and the four dimensional audit into slides, and presentation files of that kind can be checked in a browser with the [presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before they are shared with a class. The habit is worth recommending because the chart's weightings are arguments, not facts of record, and a reader who rebuilds the chart with different weightings is performing exactly the adjudication this article invites.

What, then, does the conventional minimisation obscure, and why did it persist for so long? Teltumbde's answer names the political function: a Constitution authored by a Congress consensus could be owned by the Congress, and its chairman reduced to staff. Jaffrelot's answer names the scholarly mechanism: Austin wrote from the Assembly's official record and its leaders' papers, sources in which the chairman's programme appears only where it surfaced in debate, while the movement archive, the memorandum, the Mahad and Yeola speeches, the Marathi and English journalism, sat outside the frame. Both answers can be true together, and their combination predicts the subsequent history: as the movement archive entered scholarship and as Dalit parties entered government after 1990, including the Bahujan Samaj Party's victories in Uttar Pradesh in 2007, the substantive authority reading moved from polemic to the position the evidence supports.

## The Drafting Settlement and the Politics That Inherited It

The founding settlement this article has described distributed authority in a pattern that later politics inherited. The Assembly's collective process gave the Constitution democratic legitimacy no single author could have supplied; the chairman's programme gave its social clauses a direction the consensus would not have chosen unaided; and the defeats Ambedkar absorbed, on the civil code, on economic safeguards, on separate electorates long before, defined the agenda his successors inherited as unfinished business. A later study in this series takes up the Hindu Code Bill struggle of 1948 to 1951 in full, tracing how the equality code's promise was fought over in the provisional Parliament until the bill's defeat drove its author from the cabinet. That sequel belongs to the founding's second act; the drafting years are the first, and their settlement explains why the second act took the form it did.

### How did Ambedkar justify giving the Union power to redraw state boundaries?

In the November 1948 debates he defended the provision that became Article 3 by arguing that Indian states were not sovereign parties to a compact and held no veto over reorganisation. Parliament, after consulting the affected legislature, could form new states and alter boundaries by law. Units derive authority from the Constitution itself, not from any bargain among sovereigns.

The federal design is the one dimension on which the brief for this article ranks Ambedkar's role as secondary, and the ranking is honest. The strong centre of the draft, the Union List's breadth, the emergency powers and the single citizenship were Congress leadership commitments after Partition's trauma, shared by Patel and Nehru and defended by the chairman as the Assembly's mandate rather than as his private programme. Yet even here the drafting hand shaped doctrine, since the text's asymmetry, special provisions for particular states and regions layered over a strong centre, bears the committee's workmanship, and the chairman's defences of the federation articles in 1948 and 1949 gave the design its public rationale. Secondary authorship is still authorship of a kind the technician label cannot describe.

The reservation lineage supplies the longest demonstration. The services clause the chairman defended in 1948, authorising reservation for backward classes inadequately represented, became the textual ground on which the V. P. Singh government built its 1990 decision to reserve twenty seven percent of central government posts for the Other Backward Classes, and on which the Supreme Court ruled in Indra Sawhney v. Union of India on 16 November 1992, upholding the clause's use for caste based reservation subject to a fifty percent ceiling, a creamy layer exclusion and a bar on reservation in promotions as the text then stood. Judges arguing across nine opinions in 1992 were construing a sentence the Drafting Committee had settled in 1948 and its chairman had explained in the Assembly. Constitutional lineage rarely offers so clean a specimen: a drafting decision, defended by its author against the charge of betraying equality, supplying the operative law for the republic's largest affirmative action conflict two generations later. The Nehruvian consensus story has no comparable thread to pull, because the thread runs from a memorandum of March 1947 through a committee room to a courtroom of 1992, and it runs in one man's hand for the decisive first stretch.

The legacy question can be put concretely. When the Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan, decided on 9 April 1951, read the equality code against the Madras reservation order, Parliament answered with the First Amendment of 18 June 1951, writing into the equality article the special provision authority the drafting debates had contemplated. The episode is usually told as Nehru's amendment; its doctrinal content was the repair of Ambedkar's architecture by Ambedkar's method, constitutional text adjusted to protect reservation against a reading that would have dismantled it. Lineage of that kind, in which later actors must work with a dead chairman's design because no alternative design exists, is the strongest form the substantive authority thesis can take, and it is the form the evidence supports.

The abolition clause's judicial career offers a final check on the design claim, because courts reveal what drafting choices made possible. In State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale, decided in 1993, the Supreme Court treated Article 17 as a right available against private persons and read the abolition in the wide sense its drafting invited, as the constitutional destruction of a practice rather than a regulation of it. Judges reaching that reading leaned on the clause's uncompromising structure, the very feature this article attributes to its chairman's hand. Drafting choices are hypotheses about the future's needs; when the future arrives and the text performs as designed, the hypothesis and its author are confirmed together. Article 17 has performed, through the Protection of Civil Rights Act's prosecutions and the atrocities legislation of 1989, as the verdict its sentence pronounced, and no alternative authorship theory has explained the performance better than the one the record supports.

The recovery of the drafting record by later scholarship followed a path the politicians opened. Eleanor Zelliot's research on the Mahar movement and her 1992 study From Untouchable to Dalit carried Ambedkar's political thought into the American academy; Gail Omvedt's Dalits and the Democratic Revolution of 1994 placed the movement's constitutionalism at the centre of Maharashtra's modern history; and the Government of Maharashtra's publication of Babasaheb Ambedkar: Writings and Speeches under Vasant Moon's editorship from 1979 onward put the primary archive, including States and Minorities, into libraries at a price students could pay. The sequence matters for the adjudication. The substantive authority reading did not triumph because sentiment changed. It triumphed because the evidence base widened until the narrower reading could no longer be sustained from it, a sequence Jaffrelot's 2005 book completed by joining the movement archive to the Assembly record in a single argument. Scholarship that wins on evidence is the only kind whose victory stays won.

Ambedkar's own afterlife in the republic's calendar records the political half of the same recovery. His conversion to Buddhism at Nagpur on 14 October 1956, with roughly half a million followers, six weeks before his death on 6 December 1956, enacted outside the Constitution the religious exit his drafting had made legally costless, and Deekshabhoomi became a site of annual pilgrimage. The state that had minimised the draftsman slowly absorbed his image: his portrait entered the Central Hall of Parliament in 1990 during the birth centenary period, and the Government of India declared 26 November as Constitution Day in 2015, converting the adoption date he had closed with a warning into an annual civic observance. Attribution followed the archive. A political culture that teaches the adoption speech, celebrates the adoption date and garlands the chairman's statue has, whatever its textbooks say, already adjudicated the drafting question in the revisionists' favour.

The Assembly's President left the attribution question in no doubt at the moment of adoption. Rajendra Prasad, in his concluding address on 26 November 1949, surveyed the work and singled out the Drafting Committee's chairman, observing that never had he realised, in choosing Ambedkar, how brilliant a choice the Assembly had made, and that the choice was more than justified by the work the chairman had done. Prasad was no Ambedkar partisan; the two men had disagreed across the founding, most sharply over the Hindu Code Bill's direction, and would collide again in 1951 over that bill's fate and the President's powers under the new Constitution. Praise from that quarter, delivered from the chair at the founding's closing ceremony, is testimony of the Krishnamachari kind: informed, checkable and against the grain of the witness's own alignments. The minimising tradition, which rests so much of its case on the Assembly's collective character, has never explained why the Assembly's own presiding officer assessed the chairman's contribution in the vocabulary of individual brilliance at the very moment the collective work concluded.

The courts completed the attribution by practice. When the Supreme Court's thirteen judges decided Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala on 24 April 1973, the largest constitutional case in the republic's history, the opinions reached repeatedly for the drafting record, and for the chairman's speeches in particular, to construe the amending power and the Constitution's identity. Counsel and judges treated the Constituent Assembly Debates as the founding's authoritative exposition and Ambedkar's interventions as its weightiest strand, a forensic habit that has continued in the reservation, secularism and rights litigation of every decade since. Institutions reveal their attributions in their working methods more honestly than in their ceremonial statements. A legal system that construes its constitution by asking what the Drafting Committee's chairman said the text meant has answered the question this article opened with, and has answered it in the revisionists' sense, whatever the civics paragraph in the school textbook continues to imply.

The closing speech of 25 November 1949 supplies the author's own summary, and it is not the summary of a technician. Ambedkar warned the Assembly that India was entering a life of contradictions, political equality beside social and economic inequality, and that the contradiction, unaddressed, would put the political democracy in peril. He had spent two years building the instruments, reservation, remedies, reform power and abolition, with which the republic was to address it. Whether those instruments sufficed is the argument the rest of this series inherits. That they were his instruments, designed in his writings before the committee met and defended by his voice until the Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, is the finding the drafting record sustains.

## Reading the Drafting Record: Sources, Silences and Verification

A reader who wants to test this article's verdict against the evidence has an unusually rich archive to work in, and the archive's shape explains the historiography. The primary layer is the Constituent Assembly Debates in twelve volumes, where every intervention relied upon here, the 4 November 1948 presentation speech, the December 1948 exchanges on religion and remedies, the 29 November 1948 abolition debate, the closing accounting of 25 November 1949, can be read in the speaker's own words. Beside the debates stand the committee records, reports and minutes preserved through the Lok Sabha secretariat, B. Shiva Rao's documentary collection The Framing of India's Constitution, published between 1966 and 1968, which prints the committee papers, the Rau drafts and the correspondence that the debates only summarise, and Ambedkar's own writings in the Maharashtra government's edition of his works, where States and Minorities and the 1951 Hindu Code Bill statement can be checked against the uses made of them here.

The archive also has silences, and the silences have done historiographical damage that a careful reader should discount. Committee rooms keep minutes of decisions more faithfully than records of argument, so the chairman's influence inside the room must often be inferred from the text's movement between drafts, from colleagues' testimony such as Krishnamachari's of November 1948, and from the floor defences in which he supplied the committee's reasons without contradiction from its members. Austin, writing in the 1960s with the official record and the leaders' papers before him, weighted the visible layer heavily. The revisionists weighted the movement archive, the memorandum, the speeches outside the Assembly, the journalism, that the official record touches only at its edges. Neither archive lies. They illuminate different parts of the process, and the adjudication offered in this article follows the rule that a claim about authorship should be tested against both: against the floor record, where the chairman must answer as the text's defender, and against the prior writings, where his programme either predicts the text or fails to. On the four dimensions audited here it predicts the text to a degree that no rival explanation has matched.

One near contemporary witness deserves mention before the exercises, because he wrote before the minimising tradition formed. Dhananjay Keer's biography Dr. Ambedkar: Life and Mission, published in 1954 with its subject alive and prickly, already treated the chairmanship as the summit of Ambedkar's public career and the text as carrying his impress, and Keer had interviewed the participants of the 1940s for the purpose. The biography's closeness to its subject invites discount, and historians apply it; but its existence proves that the substantive reading is not a retrospective invention of the post 1990 decades. It was the reading of the drafting generation's own record keepers, displaced in the interval by an institutional history that the universities canonised, and restored when the archive widened. Traditions of memory have careers of their own, and the career of this one, assertion, displacement, restoration on evidence, is the pattern of a claim that was true before it was convenient.

Weighing testimony is the skill the dispute most demands, and two rules serve the reader well. Testimony about workload, Krishnamachari's of November 1948 being the prime specimen, should be weighted by the witness's incentives: a colleague with no debt to the chairman, speaking on the record in the chairman's hearing, about facts of attendance every member could check, carries high credibility, and his statement has never been contradicted by any member of the committee in the decades since. Self testimony should be weighted the other way and in both directions: Ambedkar's distribution of credit in November 1949 and his denunciations of 1953 are each discounted as the speech of an interested party in a charged hour, and each is then checked against the documents, which is why this article rests its verdict on the memorandum, the drafts and the debates rather than on either speech. A historiography that applies both rules consistently reaches the position taken here; the minimising tradition characteristically applies the second rule to the 1953 outburst's context while exempting the outburst itself from it.

A verdict should also state what would overturn it, and this one can. The substantive authority reading would fail if the committee's minutes, fully published, showed the four audited designs entering the text from the leadership's instructions against the chairman's recorded positions; if a draft of the safeguards architecture in Nehru's or Patel's hand, predating March 1947, were produced from the archives; or if the floor defences on the audited clauses were shown to have been delivered from briefs the chairman disowned in correspondence of the period. No such document has surfaced in seventy five years of archival work that has every incentive to find one, and the searches have been thorough: the Nehru and Patel papers are edited and published, the Assembly's records are digitised, and the biographical literature on every principal is deep. Absence of evidence is not generally proof, but absence after motivated search across open archives is evidence of the kind courts and historians both accept, and it is entered here as the audit's closing exhibit.

A note on the committee's minutes completes the source picture. The minutes record decisions, attendance and the allocation of drafting work, and their survival in the Lok Sabha archives lets a researcher test the attendance claims on which part of this article's argument rests: the pattern of presence and absence that Krishnamachari described to the Assembly in November 1948 is a matter of record rather than of memoir. Where the minutes fall silent, on the arguments inside the room, the debates and the drafts must carry the weight, and this article has weighted them accordingly, treating silence as a limit on knowledge rather than as an invitation to fill the room with imagined dialogue.

Three verification exercises are worth any reader's hour. Compare the March 1947 memorandum's safeguards articles with the adopted equality and services clauses and count the structural correspondences. Read the 4 November 1948 speech entire, rather than in the extracts textbooks favour, and mark each design decision the speaker claims as reasoned. Then read the 2 September 1953 Rajya Sabha exchange in its Andhra State Bill context and judge whether its anger is aimed at the text or at the text's custodians. The exercises are the method of this article performed by other hands, and they are commended to readers precisely because the verdict survives them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was the Chair of the Drafting Committee of the Indian Constitution?

B. R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee from its first meeting on 30 August 1947 until the Constitution's adoption on 26 November 1949. The Constituent Assembly had created the committee by resolution the previous day, and its members elected Ambedkar as chairman when they convened. He was fifty six years old, a Columbia and London trained economist and barrister, the leader of the Scheduled Castes Federation, and, since 15 August 1947, Law Minister in Jawaharlal Nehru's interim cabinet. The chairmanship made him responsible for the committee's text, for its revisions after public criticism, and for defending every clause against amendment on the Assembly floor.

### Q: What was Ambedkar's role in writing the Indian Constitution?

His role joined three functions that are usually separated. As chairman he directed the committee that converted committee reports and B. N. Rau's advisory text into the Draft Constitution of February 1948 and its revised successor. As programme author he supplied designs he had published in advance, above all the March 1947 memorandum States and Minorities, whose reservation architecture, safeguards thinking and rights arguments entered the text. As floor manager he moved the draft on 4 November 1948, answered amendments clause by clause for a year, and delivered the closing defence on 25 November 1949. The combination is why this article describes his authority as substantive rather than technical.

### Q: How many members were in the Drafting Committee of the Indian Constitution?

The committee had seven seats, filled across its life by nine men. The seven named on 29 August 1947 were B. R. Ambedkar, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K. M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, B. L. Mitter and D. P. Khaitan. Mitter resigned because of ill health and N. Madhava Rau, formerly Diwan of Mysore and Travancore, took his seat. Khaitan died in 1948 and T. T. Krishnamachari replaced him. Attendance was uneven even among continuing members, which is why Krishnamachari told the Assembly in November 1948 that the burden of drafting had fallen disproportionately on the chairman.

### Q: Did Ambedkar draft the Indian Constitution alone?

No, and the claim appears in none of the serious scholarship, including the revisionist work of Christophe Jaffrelot and Anand Teltumbde. The text rested on the committee reports of 1947, on B. N. Rau's advisory draft of 243 clauses, on six committee colleagues and two replacements, and on the Assembly's own debates, in which roughly 7,635 amendments were tabled and 2,473 moved. Ambedkar also lost arguments his programme had pressed, including the economic mandates of States and Minorities. The defensible claim is different: within a collective process, his programme supplied the design on reservation, religion, rights and untouchability, and his voice defended the whole text.

### Q: What is the Granville Austin reading of Ambedkar's role?

Granville Austin's The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation, published in 1966, reads constitution making as a managed national consensus. On Austin's account, the substantive decisions were taken by the Congress leadership quartet of Nehru, Patel, Rajendra Prasad and Azad, by the Assembly's committees and by debate on the floor, and Ambedkar's contribution was supreme drafting skill plus unmatched floor management in executing that collective design. The reading treats the chairmanship as an instrument of the Assembly's will. Later scholarship, including Jaffrelot's study of 2005, accepts Austin's institutional frame but contests the minimisation, pointing to Ambedkar's pre published programme and its visible entry into the constitutional text.

### Q: What did Ambedkar contribute to the reservation framework in the Constitution?

He contributed its architecture. His March 1947 memorandum States and Minorities had already designed safeguards as constitutional machinery: reserved legislative seats, reservation in public services, protective oversight for Scheduled Areas and rights enforceable against the state. The Constitution adopted that structure in the equality code's special provision authority, in the services chapter's reservation permission, in the ten year initial span of legislative reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and in the Fifth and Sixth Schedules. In the debates of 1948 and October 1949 he defended the design against narrower amendments, arguing that formal equality alone would freeze inherited disadvantage into the republic's institutions.

### Q: How did Ambedkar shape Article 17 on untouchability?

He piloted the draft article through the Assembly on 29 November 1948 and held its phrasing against attempts to soften or generalise it. The sentence that became Article 17 names untouchability, abolishes it without qualification, forbids its practice in any form and makes the enforcement of disabilities arising from it a punishable offence. That structure, a verdict rather than a declaration, matches the argument of his 1936 text Annihilation of Caste and his movement record from Mahad in 1927 onward. The clause binds social practice through penal law, reaching private conduct that the general equality articles, addressed to the state, could not reach directly.

### Q: When was the Drafting Committee appointed, and what task did the Constituent Assembly give it on 29 August 1947?

The Assembly appointed the committee by resolution on 29 August 1947, a fortnight after independence, and elected its chairman at the committee's first meeting on 30 August. Its charge was to prepare a draft constitution for India, working from the reports the Assembly's committees had already produced on union powers, provincial constitutions, fundamental rights and minorities, and from the constitutional text the Constitutional Adviser, B. N. Rau, had prepared as a basis for discussion. The committee's own minutes and records, preserved in the Lok Sabha archives, show it treating both inputs as material to be decided upon rather than instructions to be copied.

### Q: What did Ambedkar argue in his speech of 4 November 1948 when he presented the Draft Constitution to the Assembly?

Moving that the draft be taken into consideration, he defended its main design choices as a connected theory. He justified the parliamentary executive over the presidential form, a strong centre within a federation whose units were not sovereign, and the division of rights into court enforceable fundamental rights and directive principles left to the electorate's enforcement. He answered the charge of excessive borrowing by conceding the debt to the Government of India Act of 1935 without apology. He also attacked the Indian village as a sink of localism and a den of ignorance, refusing the Gandhian case for village republics as the Constitution's foundation.

### Q: Which original members of the Drafting Committee were replaced before its work ended, and who took their seats?

Two of the original seven left the committee. B. L. Mitter, a former Advocate General of Bengal, resigned on grounds of ill health, and the Assembly appointed N. Madhava Rau in his place; Madhava Rau brought the administrative experience of a Diwan who had governed Mysore and Travancore. D. P. Khaitan, the committee's authority on commercial law, died in 1948, and T. T. Krishnamachari, a Madras businessman with financial expertise, took the vacant seat and served through the final revision. The replacements mattered beyond arithmetic: departures and absences concentrated the drafting, revision and floor defence in the chairman's office, as Krishnamachari himself told the Assembly.

### Q: How did the Drafting Committee use the months between its appointment in August 1947 and the publication of the Draft Constitution in February 1948?

The committee met first on 30 August 1947 and sat, in Ambedkar's later count, for 141 days across its whole life, with the heaviest concentration in this opening phase. It worked through B. N. Rau's advisory text clause by clause after Rau completed his study tour and submitted his draft of 243 clauses and 13 schedules in October 1947, tested the committee reports against it, and recast the material into a coherent draft. The result, published on 21 February 1948, contained 315 articles and 8 schedules, a growth and restructuring that marked the text as the committee's own work rather than the adviser's survey transcribed.

### Q: What did T. T. Krishnamachari tell the Assembly in November 1948 about the burden of drafting falling on Ambedkar?

Speaking in the debate on the Draft Constitution, Krishnamachari asked the House to notice how thin the committee had become. Of the seven members nominated, he said, one had resigned and had not been replaced at the time, one had died, one was away in America, another was engaged in state affairs, and one or two were kept away by distance or health. It had therefore happened, he concluded, that the burden of drafting the Constitution fell on Ambedkar, and he had no doubt the House was grateful to him for discharging it in a manner that was undoubtedly commendable. The tribute came from a colleague with no political debt to the chairman.

### Q: Which proposals from States and Minorities did the Constitution leave out?

The Assembly declined the memorandum's economic radicalism. Ambedkar's March 1947 text had proposed that key industries and agricultural land be owned by the state as a constitutional mandate, with agriculture organised as a state industry and insurance nationalised by constitutional command; those proposals entered no article. His demand for safeguards administered partly outside ordinary legislative majorities was also moderated, and separate electorates for the Scheduled Castes, which he had defended before the Poona Pact of 1932 forced their abandonment, were not revived. The pattern is instructive: representation, services and protective schedules entered the text, while the economic programme was defeated, which is the record of an author negotiating, not of a victor dictating.

### Q: How did Ambedkar's office as Law Minister interact with his chairmanship of the Drafting Committee?

The two offices ran together from 15 August 1947. As Law Minister in Nehru's cabinet he carried the government's legislative business and spoke for the draft with ministerial authority; as chairman he answered to the Assembly for the committee's text. The combination gave the draft a defender who could commit the government in debate, and it gave Ambedkar a platform from which his constitutional programme continued into legislation, above all the Hindu Code Bill he piloted from 1948. The same combination created the collision of 1951: when the cabinet let the bill stall, the Law Minister who had authored the equality code resigned on 27 September 1951 rather than administer its dilution.

### Q: Why did Ambedkar say in the Rajya Sabha in 1953 that he would be the first person to burn the Constitution?

He spoke on 2 September 1953, in the Rajya Sabha debate on the Andhra State Bill, answering members who credited him as the Constitution's maker. The outburst, including his description of himself as a hack who had worked against his will, attacked the document's working: the treatment of minorities, the rush toward linguistic states without safeguards, and Congress governments that he believed had emptied his constitutional protections of force. It was a verdict on implementation between 1950 and 1953, delivered by a former Law Minister outside the government, not a retraction of his drafting authorship. Scholars who cite it against the substantive authority reading rarely supply the debate's subject, which was governance rather than drafting.

### Q: How many amendments were tabled and moved during the Assembly's consideration of the Draft Constitution?

Ambedkar placed the figures on record in his closing speech of 25 November 1949. Approximately 7,635 amendments to the Draft Constitution were tabled by members, of which 2,473 were actually moved, discussed and disposed of in the House. The Assembly spent 114 days on the consideration stage alone. The numbers describe the process through which the committee's text was tested: every clause the chairman defended had survived, or been altered by, an amendment procedure of industrial scale, and the final document grew from the committee's 315 articles at first publication to 395 articles and 8 schedules in its adopted form.

### Q: What happened between the Constitution's adoption on 26 November 1949 and its commencement on 26 January 1950?

Adoption and commencement were deliberately separated. The Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, and a defined set of provisions, covering citizenship, elections, the provisional Parliament and transitional matters, took effect that day so that electoral and governmental machinery could be built. The remainder, the Constitution as a working whole, commenced on 26 January 1950, a date chosen to honour the Purna Swaraj declaration of 26 January 1930. Members signed the document on 24 January 1950 at the Assembly's final session. The two month interval let the republic prepare its first electoral rolls and its provisional institutions under constitutional authority rather than improvisation.

### Q: How did the Drafting Committee respond to the public criticism and suggestions that followed the February 1948 draft?

The February 1948 draft was published for comment, and criticism arrived from provincial governments, bar associations, political organisations and the press, much of it directed at the strong centre, the emergency powers and the property clauses. The committee reconsidered its text in the light of the comments, criticisms and suggestions, and prepared a revised draft that was published in October 1948 with the changes the committee had accepted marked and explained. Ambedkar, in his 4 November 1948 speech, walked the Assembly through the principal alterations. The procedure matters for the authorship question: a committee that revises its own text in response to a nation's criticism is exercising continuing authority over it, not transcribing a settled instruction.

### Q: How did Ambedkar answer the charge that the Draft Constitution had copied the Government of India Act of 1935?

He made no apology, and said so in terms during his speech of 4 November 1948. Nobody, he argued, would venture to claim that any constitution framed so late in the history of constitution making could be novel in its main provisions; the only new things possible were variations made to remove the faults of earlier texts and adaptations to a country's needs. The borrowing charge, pressed by critics who counted the draft's administrative provisions against the 1935 Act, he treated as a complaint about chronology rather than about judgment. His answer is a useful measure of the man: where the inheritance served, he kept it and defended the keeping; where his programme required originality, on safeguards and rights, he built.

### Q: What part did the Assembly's other committees play alongside the Drafting Committee in producing the constitutional text?

The Drafting Committee worked downstream of a committee system that had divided the founding into provinces of inquiry. The Union Constitution Committee under Nehru and the Provincial Constitution Committee under Patel reported on the structure of the two levels of government; the Advisory Committee under Patel, with its sub committees on fundamental rights under J. B. Kripalani and on minorities, produced the rights and safeguards reports of 1947. Those reports supplied decisions and language that the Drafting Committee integrated, tested for consistency and converted into a single text. The division of labour is the factual core of the collective authorship case, and it coexists with the chairman's substantive authority, because integration, selection and defence are themselves decisions about what the Constitution would say.
