## The Reformer and the Tyrant: Why Muhammad Ali Divides Historians

Say the name Muhammad Ali and most people picture the boxer. Historians of the Middle East picture someone else entirely: an Albanian Ottoman officer who landed in Egypt in 1801 with the expeditionary force sent to expel the French, and who by 1805 had clawed his way to the governorship of the province, a position he would hold until 1848, dying in 1849 after transforming the country beyond recognition. The question of what to make of him has never been settled. Was he the founder of modern Egypt, the visionary who dragged a decaying Ottoman province into the nineteenth century, or was he a ruthless adventurer who rebuilt the state on the conscripted bodies of Egyptian peasants? The dispute is one of the sharpest in the historiography of the modern Middle East, and it remains instructive precisely because both sides can point to genuine evidence.

![Muhammad Ali, the Ottoman governor who remade Egypt, at the center of the reformer-or-tyrant debate - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-15.webp)

On one side stands the celebratory school, which has cast Muhammad Ali as a heroic modernizer. Henry Dodwell's 1931 biography, "The Founder of Modern Egypt: A Study of Muhammad Ali," gave this interpretation its classic English-language form, presenting the Pasha as a statesman of rare ambition whose achievements ranked with those of the great nation builders of Europe. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot carried the argument forward in "Egypt in the Reign of Muhammad Ali" (1984), contending that his reign laid the institutional foundations of the modern Egyptian state: the centralized administration, the standing army, the schools, the factories, the land survey, the beginnings of an educational mission to Europe that would produce Egypt's first modern intellectuals. In this telling, Muhammad Ali rescued Egypt from the chaos left by the French occupation and the Mamluk beys, imposed order, and set the country on the path that would eventually lead to sovereignty. His failures, the celebratory historians concede, were failures of timing and circumstance: a second-class start in a poor country, the hostility of the Ottoman sultan, and finally the intervention of the European powers, which crushed his army in Syria and forced him back within Egypt's borders by the treaties of 1840 and 1841.

On the other side stands the critical school, which argues that the modernization narrative mistakes the instruments of domination for the engines of progress. The earliest voice in this camp is Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti, the Azhari chronicler who lived through the first years of Muhammad Ali's rule and recorded, with a scholar's precision and a witness's bitterness, the requisitions, the new taxes, the destruction of the old landholding regime, and the suffering of ordinary Cairenes. Khaled Fahmy revived and deepened this critique in "All the Pasha's Men: Mehmed Ali, His Army and the Making of Modern Egypt" (1997), arguing through meticulous archival work that the Pasha's famous reforms were mechanisms of state control constructed on conscript bodies. The army, the hospitals, the quarantine system, even the medical school: in Fahmy's account these were not philanthropic projects but technologies of power designed to produce docile, healthy, governable soldiers and subjects. The critical historians do not deny that institutions were built. They ask, relentlessly, for whom they were built, and at whose expense.

The verdict this article defends is the builder-at-a-price one: Muhammad Ali genuinely built modern Egypt, and he did so on the backs of its peasants, so he was simultaneously a great modernizer and a harsh exploiter, and the either-or framing itself is the real error. This is not a compromise for the sake of politeness. It is the only reading that fits the evidence, because the celebratory school has the stronger record on what was constructed and the critical school has the stronger record on how it was constructed, and neither record can be discarded without falsifying the story. The factories existed; the corvée laborers who died building the Mahmudiyya Canal died as well. The army was a formidable fighting force that humbled the sultan's troops at Konya in 1832 and again at Nezib in 1839; it was also a machine that fed on the fellahin, seized through press gangs, chained, and marched to barracks where desertion was punished with torture or death. To call Muhammad Ali only a reformer is to erase the price. To call him only a tyrant is to pretend the price bought nothing. The debate stays alive because each camp has half the truth and mistakes its half for the whole.

Understanding why the argument took this shape requires a brief look at the man and the moment. Muhammad Ali was born around 1769 in Kavala, a port town in what is now northern Greece, into an Albanian family of modest standing. He served as a commander of Albanian irregulars in the Ottoman force dispatched to Egypt in 1801 after Napoleon's expedition had collapsed, and he remained in the country during the confused years that followed, when Mamluk beys, Ottoman governors, and British agents scrambled for advantage. Through a combination of military skill, political cunning, and an ability to read the mood of Cairo's religious and popular leaders, he maneuvered himself into power: the ulema and the guilds acclaimed him as governor in 1805, and the Ottoman sultan, lacking better options, confirmed the appointment. He would rule Egypt for more than four decades, from 1805 to 1848, when senility forced him to hand effective power to his son Ibrahim before his death in August 1849. The arc matters because it explains both his audacity and his method. He was an outsider, an Albanian in an Arab province, an Ottoman officer who owed his throne to no hereditary right and therefore could treat Egypt as a blank slate for experiment. Nothing in his background constrained him to respect the existing order, and nothing in it inclined him to ask Egyptians what kind of order they wanted.

What is at stake in the debate is larger than one man's reputation. Muhammad Ali is the hinge on which modern Egyptian history turns, and the verdict on him shapes the verdict on everything that followed. If he was primarily a nation builder, then Egypt's modern history is a story of indigenous agency triumphing over backwardness, interrupted only by European imperialism; this is the version enshrined in much Egyptian nationalist writing, where the Pasha appears as a precursor of independence. If he was primarily an exploiter, then Egypt's modern history begins with the state preying on society, and the celebrated institutions of modernity arrive as instruments of coercion; this version resonates with the postcolonial suspicion of top-down development and with the lived memory of peasant communities for whom the nineteenth century meant conscription, forced labor, and tax collectors. The disagreement also mirrors a wider argument about modernization itself: whether it can be judged by its outputs, the schools and factories and armies, or must be judged by its human cost, the bodies conscripted, taxed, and worked to death. Muhammad Ali is a test case for the proposition that progress and cruelty can be the same project.

The dispute also turns on evidence that is genuinely difficult to weigh. The celebratory school leans on the testimony of European observers, French engineers and British consuls who admired the Pasha's energy and recorded his achievements in detail, and on the Pasha's own administrative records, which naturally present his policies as rational and benevolent. The critical school leans on Arabic sources that the European admirers could not read or chose to ignore: al-Jabarti's chronicle, the petitions of peasants, the records of military tribunals, the registers that document desertion, punishment, and death. Fahmy's great contribution was to show that even the Pasha's own archives, read against the grain, reveal a state obsessed with surveillance, discipline, and the extraction of labor. The archives of a modernizing state are the archives of its control apparatus, and the same document that records the founding of a hospital can record the flogging of the conscripts sent to fill it. Neither side lacks sources. They read the same archive and see different states.

It helps to keep the chronology of the great reforms in view, because the two schools narrate the same sequence so differently. In 1809 and 1810, Muhammad Ali broke the power of the tax farmers, the multazims, confiscating their lands and converting them into state domains; the celebratory reading calls this the destruction of a parasitic intermediary class and the first step toward a modern land system, while the critical reading notes that the peasant cultivator simply exchanged one master for a more efficient and inescapable one. In 1811 came the famous massacre of the Mamluk beys in the Cairo Citadel, an act of political murder that even Dodwell could not quite defend but which cleared the field of the Pasha's most dangerous rivals; the critical historians treat it as the founding crime of the regime, the moment when negotiation gave way to annihilation. The Hijaz campaign of 1811 to 1818, waged nominally in the sultan's name against the Wahhabis, gave Muhammad Ali the pretext and the experience to build a new army; the failure of the slave-soldier experiment, in which Sudanese captives died in the training camps faster than they could be replaced, forced the turn to Egyptian peasant conscription from about 1820, producing the Nizam al-Jadid, the new-order army trained by French officers, which by the 1830s numbered somewhere in the range of 100,000 to 130,000 men at its peak, with the sources disputed and the higher contemporary guesses unreliable. Factories followed the army's needs: textiles for uniforms, foundries for cannon, gunpowder works, shipyards at Alexandria. Schools followed the factories' and the army's needs: a medical school to keep soldiers alive, an engineering school to build fortifications, a school of languages to translate European manuals, and the famous educational missions that sent Egyptian students to Paris. Land was surveyed, canals were dug, cotton was introduced as a cash crop under state monopoly, and the revenues of the monopoly funded the entire edifice. Every celebrated institution has its shadow in the archive: the canal dug by corvée labor under the lash, the cotton monopoly that forced peasants to grow a crop they could not eat at prices the state dictated, the schools that trained a tiny elite while the countryside was bled for taxes.

The geography of the argument matters as well. The Pasha's empire, at its height in the 1830s, stretched from the Sudan, conquered in 1820 to 1822 in a brutal campaign remembered in Sudanese memory as the Turkiyya, through Egypt and into Syria, which his son Ibrahim occupied after the victories over the Ottoman armies. The celebratory school reads this expansion as the natural expression of a great power's ambition, the recovery of Egypt's ancient sphere under a modernizing dynasty. The critical school reads the Sudanese conquest in particular as straightforward imperial predation: a hunt for gold and slaves that delivered neither in the expected quantities and left a legacy of bitterness that poisoned Egyptian-Sudanese relations for generations. The Syrian adventure, meanwhile, is the favorite exhibit of those who argue that Muhammad Ali's project contained the seeds of its own destruction, because it was the Syrian occupation that finally provoked Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia into the 1840 intervention that reduced his army, closed his factories to protective tariffs, and confined him to Egypt under the London settlement of 1841. The empire collapsed because it had overreached; or it was crushed because Europe would not tolerate an independent power in the eastern Mediterranean. Both statements are defensible, and the choice between them tracks the larger divide.

There is also a question of comparison that the framework of this article invites directly. Muhammad Ali is often set beside his great contemporary reformers, and the comparisons illuminate the debate. Like Peter the Great, he built a new army and a new bureaucracy by main force and left the social order beneath them largely untouched; like Meiji Japan's oligarchs, he used state power to industrialize from above, though unlike them he did so without a broad social coalition and without surviving the European veto on his ambitions. The comparison with the Ottoman Tanzimat reformers is perhaps the most pointed: where the Tanzimat statesmen of the 1840s and after tried to reform the empire through law and citizenship, Muhammad Ali had reformed Egypt through the barracks and the tax office a generation earlier, and his example haunted Ottoman reformers as both inspiration and warning. Set beside these peers, his distinctiveness sharpens: he was the most thoroughgoing of the early nineteenth-century modernizers in the Middle East, and also the most ruthless in his methods, because he combined the centralizing drive of a Peter with the extractive logic of a colonial regime, and he did it all in a country that was not his own.

The dates and figures in this article follow the scholarly consensus where it exists and flag uncertainty where it does not. Muhammad Ali's birth year is conventionally given as 1769, though the precise date is uncertain; his assumption of power in 1805 and his death in 1849 are settled. Army figures are disputed across sources, which is why ranges are given rather than false precision. Where historians disagree, the disagreement is named rather than smoothed over, because the disagreement is the subject. And the scholarship cited here is drawn from work available through the end of 2014, the better to judge the debate as it stood in the classic phase of its development. For the fuller biographical narrative, see [the birth of modern Egypt under Muhammad Ali](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/). The argument here concerns what that birth cost, and whether the cost can be separated from the achievement.

That Egypt he found in 1801 was, by every account, a province in advanced decay. The Mamluk beys, a military caste of slave-soldiers turned hereditary oligarchs, had reduced Ottoman authority to a fiction and turned tax farming into a system of plunder. The French occupation of 1798 to 1801 had shattered what remained of the old order without replacing it: Napoleon's savants had mapped the country and founded the Institut d'Égypte, but the occupation had also bankrupted the treasury, alienated the population through requisitions and the repression of the Cairo revolts, and demonstrated to every ambitious observer that Egypt was governable by whoever could master European military technique. Into this vacuum stepped Muhammad Ali, and the celebratory historians are right about one thing above all: the vacuum was real, and he filled it with a state. Whether that state served Egypt or consumed it is the question that sits at the center of the debate.

## The Reformer Case: What Muhammad Ali Built

When Muhammad Ali took power in Egypt in 1805, he inherited a province, not a state. The Ottoman writ ran weakly, the treasury was empty, and real authority belonged to the Mamluk beys, a slave soldier caste that had outlasted every attempt to dislodge it. The strongest version of the reformer case begins here, with the argument that nothing Muhammad Ali built afterward would have been possible without first breaking this old order. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot, whose 1984 study remains the most sympathetic modern treatment of the man, argues that his project was coherent from the start: to convert a fragmented Ottoman dependency into a centralized, revenue producing, militarily self sufficient polity under his own dynasty. Everything else, the monopolies, the factories, the schools, followed from that single political logic.

The destruction of the Mamluks was the opening act, and it was deliberate. On March 1, 1811, Muhammad Ali invited the Mamluk leadership to the Cairo Citadel for a ceremony marking his son Tusun's departure for Arabia. As the procession of beys and their retinues descended through the narrow Bab al-Azab gate, the gates were shut and Albanian troops opened fire from the walls above. Estimates of the dead vary across the sources, with most accounts placing the number of beys at about two dozen and the total killed in the Citadel passage, including their retainers, in the range of 400 to 500, with further killings across Cairo and the provinces as troops hunted down Mamluk households that day and in the days after. What matters for the reformer case is not the arithmetic but the result: the Mamluk order as a political force was finished. Over the next several years Muhammad Ali confiscated Mamluk estates, revoked the iltizam tax farms through which the beys and their clients had farmed the state's revenues, and broke the power of the Bedouin shaykhs who had acted as the beys' allies and enforcers in the countryside. In place of the old mosaic of tax farmers, grandees, and tribal intermediaries, he imposed a hierarchy of salaried officials answerable to Cairo: provincial governors known as mudirs, district sub governors, and village headmen who held office at the ruler's pleasure. The central council, or diwan, coordinated finance, war, and public works under his direct supervision. By the early 1820s, for the first time in centuries, one authority collected the land tax, policed the countryside, and conscripted men across the whole of Egypt. The institutional scaffolding of this centralization deserves a closer look, because the reformer case rests on it. In 1829 Muhammad Ali created a consultative council, the Majlis al-Mashura, composed of senior officials and ulama, which debated budgets, public works, and administrative regulations; it advised rather than decided, but its existence introduced the practice of formal deliberation over policy into Egyptian government. The same period saw the first systematic land survey and population counts of the modern era, without which neither the monopolies nor conscription could have been administered, and a penal code promulgated in 1829 that replaced the arbitrary punishments of the old order with graduated penalties applied, at least in principle, uniformly. Public health was treated as a state function: a quarantine board established in 1831, run with European doctors, policed pilgrims and ports against plague and cholera, and a corps of trained midwives and vaccinators carried smallpox inoculation into the villages. These were not ornaments on the regime. In the reformer reading, the survey, the code, and the quarantine board show a ruler building the unglamorous infrastructure of a modern state, the capacity to count, to regulate, and to keep his population alive for the purposes of the state. Marsot's verdict on this transformation is unambiguous: centralization was the precondition of every reform that followed, and no ruler before him had achieved it.

The army was the instrument and the proof of the new order. Muhammad Ali's first attempts at a reformed force relied on Albanian and Turkish mercenaries, the same unruly soldiery that had plagued his predecessors, and they mutinied when pay fell short. He drew the lesson quickly. Beginning around 1815 he began raising a new army, the nizam al-jadid, on the European model, and in a fateful decision he manned it not with mercenaries but with conscripted Egyptian peasants, officered at first by Turks and Circassians and trained by French instructors. The most famous of the Frenchmen was Joseph Anthelme Seve, a former officer of Napoleon's army who converted to Islam, took the name Sulayman Pasha, and built the training system at Aswan and later at al-Khanqah. French drill, French artillery doctrine, French military schools: the whole apparatus was imported and then reproduced locally, with manuals translated at the Bulaq press and cadets drilled in camps that contemporaries described as astonishingly orderly. Historians dispute the size of the force at its height. French observers in the 1830s spoke of more than 100,000 men under arms; Egyptian administrative returns suggest lower figures; a cautious working range is 60,000 to 130,000 effectives depending on the year and on whether irregulars are counted. Even at the lower bound, it was the largest disciplined standing army any ruler of the eastern Mediterranean had fielded in generations, and unlike the Ottoman janissaries it answered to a single master and was paid, fed, and clothed by the state.

The army then did what armies are built to do, and its record is the second pillar of the reformer case. The first great test came in Arabia. The Wahhabi movement, allied with the Saudi amirs of Najd, had seized Mecca and Medina and humiliated the Ottoman sultan, who ordered Muhammad Ali to restore them. The Egyptian campaign in the Hijaz and Najd, fought from 1811 to 1818 under his sons Tusun and then Ibrahim, was a logistical feat across deserts that had swallowed other armies: supply depots, camel transport organized on a state scale, artillery hauled where artillery had never gone. It ended with the capture of the Saudi capital at Diriyah in 1818 and the execution of its amir in Istanbul. The second venture, the Sudan expedition of 1820, sent an army up the Nile under Ismail, the ruler's third son, and then under the Defterdar Muhammad Khusraw, partly in search of gold and slaves for the new regiments and partly to secure the southern frontier; whatever its motives, it planted the Egyptian flag at Khartoum and made the Sudan an Egyptian dependency. The third was the Morea campaign of the mid 1820s, when the sultan again called and Ibrahim's Egyptian troops, better disciplined than any Ottoman force in the field, reconquered the Peloponnese for the empire before the European powers intervened and destroyed the Egyptian Ottoman fleet at Navarino in 1827. Navarino was a catastrophe, and in the reformer narrative it is also a turning point: Muhammad Ali rebuilt, and within a decade he commanded a fleet and army that no longer merely served the sultan but threatened him.

That threat materialized in Syria. Denied the governorships he believed his services had earned, Muhammad Ali sent Ibrahim into Palestine in late 1831. Acre fell in 1832 after a siege that European engineers had declared hopeless; Ibrahim then marched north, shattered three Ottoman armies in succession, and at Konya in December 1832 annihilated the grand vizier's host. The road to Istanbul lay open, and only Russian intervention and the Convention of Kutahya in 1833, which granted him Syria and Adana on top of the Crete he had been given in 1830, stopped him. When war resumed in 1839, Ibrahim won an even more crushing victory at Nezib in June, destroying the main Ottoman army a second time, and the Ottoman fleet itself defected to Alexandria. Only the combined intervention of Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia, embodied in the London Convention of 1840 and the bombardment of Beirut and Acre, forced the Egyptians out of Syria. The reformer reading of these campaigns, advanced most forcefully by Henry Dodwell in his 1931 study of the founder of modern Egypt, holds that they demonstrated something no other Muslim ruler of the age had achieved: an army raised from the peasantry of a poor province, drilled by a handful of Frenchmen, had twice defeated the armies of the Ottoman Empire and had done so with artillery, engineering, and staff work that European observers rated as fully modern. Defeat in 1840 came from diplomacy and sea power, Dodwell insists, not from any failure of the military machine itself.

### What were Muhammad Ali's state monopolies and how did they work?

Muhammad Ali required every peasant to sell wheat, rice, and later cotton to state agents at fixed, low prices; the state then resold those crops abroad at market rates, pocketing the spread. The monopolies financed the army and factories, and in Marsot's reading they were Egypt's first modern instrument of fiscal control.

The detail behind that summary is worth setting out, because the monopoly system was the fiscal engine of the entire project. Beginning in the 1810s and extended crop by crop through the 1820s, the state claimed a monopoly over the purchase of the principal cash crops: wheat, rice, sugar, indigo, flax, sesame, and above all cotton. Village shaykhs received state orders specifying what each village would plant and in what quantity; at harvest, government agents collected the crop at prices fixed by Cairo, typically well below what merchants would have paid. The state then sold abroad, above all to British and French buyers, at world prices. The difference between the two prices was, in effect, a hidden tax on agriculture, and it flowed directly into the treasury rather than passing through the hands of tax farmers or merchants. Marsot estimates that by the 1830s the monopolies accounted for the bulk of state revenue, and she treats them as the single most important institutional innovation of the reign: they replaced a medieval fiscal system of farming out collection to grandees with a centralized state trading apparatus. The logic was straightforward and, in the reformer telling, rational. Egypt's wealth lay in its soil; the army and the factories had to be paid for; and only the state, buying cheap and selling dear, could mobilize agricultural surplus on the scale the project required. Foreign consuls and merchants protested endlessly, and British pressure eventually forced the dismantling of the system under the Anglo Ottoman commercial convention of 1838, extended to Egypt in 1841. But for two decades the monopolies worked exactly as designed, converting the Delta's harvests into guns, ships, and schools.

### How did Muhammad Ali fund his wars and factories?

He paid for war and industry out of three streams: monopoly profits from exported cotton and grain, taxes squeezed from land made newly productive by irrigation, and, in lean years, confiscated Mamluk and religious endowment lands. Dodwell credits this fiscal machinery with making Egypt the first Muslim state to field a Western armed standing army.

The figures behind this claim are contested, and the reformer case is careful to present them as orders of magnitude rather than certainties. Egyptian state revenue at the start of the reign is usually put in the low single digit millions of piastres; by the 1830s, at the height of the monopoly system and the cotton boom, French and British consular estimates place annual revenue in the range of 60 to 100 million piastres, a multiple that reflects both the monopolies and the expansion of cultivated land. Where the money went is less disputed. The army absorbed the largest share, with estimates of military spending running to half or more of the budget in the war years of the 1830s. The factories and dockyards took a second large share. What is striking, and central to the celebratory reading, is what did not happen: Muhammad Ali did not borrow from European bankers. Unlike his grandson Ismail, whose loans would bankrupt the country a generation later, the founder financed his wars and his industrial program out of current revenue, confiscations, and the forced mobilization of domestic resources. Dodwell makes this the moral center of his portrait: a ruler who built an empire without mortgaging it. The confiscations deserve emphasis in the reformer account, because they were also an administrative revolution. The Mamluk estates seized after 1811, the iltizam holdings resumed to the state, and the religious endowments brought under government administration in the 1810s transferred an enormous share of Egypt's cultivable land from private and corporate hands to the ruler's direct control. Marsot argues that this land settlement, harsh as its methods were, created for the first time a single national domain whose revenues could be directed by policy rather than dissipated among grandees.

Industry was the most visibly modern face of the project, and the reformer case treats it as an audacious attempt at import substitution a century before the term existed. The motive was military: an army that depended on Europe for muskets, uniforms, and gunpowder was an army that Europe could disarm. Beginning in the late 1810s, the state built a network of factories, some thirty textile mills by the most common count, spinning and weaving cotton and wool for uniforms; foundries casting cannon at the Cairo Citadel; gunpowder works; paper mills; sugar refineries; glass and chemicals works; and above all the great dockyard and arsenal at Alexandria, laid out under the French engineer Lefebvre de Cerisy, which built warships on European lines. European travelers in the 1830s described Alexandria as a second Toulon, with slipways, ropewalks, and machine shops turning out frigates. The factories were staffed in part by imported European artisans and in part by conscripted Egyptian labor, including women and children in the textile mills, and their products were often costlier and poorer than European imports. The reformer reading does not deny the waste; it insists on the ambition. No ruler between the Atlantic and India had attempted to build a state owned industrial base at this scale, and the experiment proved, in Marsot's phrase, that Egypt could be made to produce the material of modern power rather than merely buying it. The same logic governed the arsenals: powder magazines, small arms workshops, and a cannon foundry meant that the victories in Arabia, the Morea, and Syria were won with weapons substantially of Egyptian manufacture.

Education was the project that looked furthest ahead, and it is the part of the record the reformer case invokes with the least qualification. Muhammad Ali needed engineers, artillery officers, doctors, translators, and administrators, and he understood that importing Europeans forever was no foundation for independence. The first schools were strictly military and technical: a school of engineering, the Muhandiskhana, founded in 1816; an artillery school; and in 1827 a school of medicine at Abu Zaabal under the French physician Antoine Clot, known as Clot Bey, which trained Egyptian doctors and, remarkably for the period, included instruction in anatomy and surgery that required confronting religious scruples about dissection. The missions to Europe extended the same logic outward. Beginning in 1826, the state sent successive groups of students to France and later to Britain and Austria, more than a hundred young men in the first waves, to study engineering, medicine, agriculture, printing, and military science at government expense, on the explicit understanding that they would return to staff the new institutions. The most consequential of these students was Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, the Azhar trained imam who accompanied the first mission as chaplain, stayed to master French, and returned to found the School of Languages in 1835, the Madrasat al-Alsun, which became the translation bureau of the new Egypt. Under Tahtawi's direction, teams of translators rendered European works on law, geography, military science, and history into Arabic, creating a modern Arabic vocabulary for concepts the language had never needed to name. The Bulaq press, established in 1821 as the first major Arabic printing press in Egypt, gave this work its material form: it printed the translated textbooks, the military manuals, the administrative regulations, and from 1828 the official gazette al-Waqa'i al-Misriyya, the first Arabic newspaper in the country. Dodwell's judgment on this cultural program is the most quoted line in the celebratory literature: that Muhammad Ali, a man who did not learn to read until middle age, nonetheless founded the institutions through which modern Arabic thought would later speak. What gave the program its cumulative force was that the returning students actually staffed the state. The engineers came home to lay out canals, survey land, and supervise the factories; the doctors took up posts in the military hospitals and the quarantine service; the translators filled the bureaus of the School of Languages, which by the early 1840s had rendered well over a hundred European works into Arabic. Tahtawi himself embodied the synthesis the ruler was after: an Azhar scholar who wrote admiringly of French civic life while remaining within the Islamic tradition, and whose students carried his curriculum into the schools of the 1840s. The reformer case asks the reader to weigh that paradox and find in it the mark of a builder rather than a mere conqueror.

Agriculture, the base of everything, received the same systematic attention. The reformer argument here is that Muhammad Ali was the first ruler of Egypt since antiquity to treat the land and water of the country as a single technical system to be improved by the state. The Mahmudiyya Canal, cut from the Nile to Alexandria in the late 1810s to restore the port's fresh water supply and open a transport artery, was the first of the great works; contemporary accounts put the labor force in the tens of thousands and the death toll from disease and exhaustion in the thousands, figures the celebratory histories record without dwelling on them. Summer canals in the Delta, deepened and extended year by year, made possible the perennial irrigation on which long staple cotton depended. That cotton was itself an agricultural revolution. Around 1820 a French engineer named Jumel noticed a superior long fibered variety growing in a Cairo garden; Muhammad Ali seized on it, ordered its cultivation across the Delta under the monopoly system, and within a decade Egyptian long staple cotton was commanding premium prices on the Liverpool market and financing the state. Land reclamation proceeded alongside: marshes in the northern Delta were drained, new villages laid out, and the cultivated area, which historians place at roughly 3 to 4 million feddans at the start of the reign, expanded by estimates ranging from several hundred thousand to over a million feddans by the 1840s. New crops were introduced and rotations improved; the state distributed seed, advanced credit through its agents, and punished villages that failed to meet planting orders. The Delta barrage project, the great regulator dams across the Nile branches designed by the French engineer Mougel Bey and begun in the 1840s, was the capstone of this hydraulic ambition, intended to raise the summer level of the river and water the whole Delta by gravity. Marsot treats the agricultural program as the least appreciated of the achievements: a pre industrial state, using coercion and planning, raised the productive capacity of its soil by a margin no previous Egyptian regime had approached, and cotton revenues made Egypt a significant participant in the world economy for the first time.

The navy completes the picture of a ruler who thought in terms of complete systems of power. Before Muhammad Ali, Egypt had no navy worth the name; the ships destroyed at Navarino had been built largely with Ottoman resources. The Alexandria dockyard changed that within a few years. By the late 1830s the Egyptian fleet counted, by the most widely cited estimates, between 25 and 35 warships, including ships of the line carrying 100 guns built on French designs by Egyptian labor under French direction, supported by a naval school, hospitals, and victualling yards. British naval observers, not given to flattery, rated the Egyptian fleet the strongest native built force in the eastern Mediterranean and a genuine second rank naval power. In the reformer narrative the fleet matters for what it symbolized: a state that could build, crew, and maintain ocean going warships had crossed the threshold from dependency to sovereignty in the most literal sense, and it was this fleet, defecting Ottoman squadrons and all, that made Muhammad Ali a figure the chancelleries of Europe had to reckon with rather than merely instruct.

Taken together, this is the edifice the reformer case asks the reader to contemplate: a centralized administration where none had existed, a conscript army that defeated the Ottomans twice, a fiscal system that paid for war without foreign debt, factories and a dockyard that manufactured the instruments of power, schools and a press that translated Europe into Arabic, canals and cotton fields that multiplied the revenue of the soil, and a fleet that carried the Egyptian flag into the councils of the great powers. The full panorama of [how Muhammad Ali modernized Egypt](/2014/06/01/how-muhammad-ali-modernized-egypt/) is set out in the companion survey, and the celebratory historians are explicit about the verdict they draw from it. Marsot presents him as the founder of the modern Egyptian state, the first ruler to govern the whole country through a single bureaucracy and to direct its resources by policy. Dodwell goes further, ranking him among the great state builders of the nineteenth century and arguing that his failure to secure full independence was the work of European diplomacy, not of any defect in his design. On this reading, the ruthlessness was the price of the achievement, and the achievement was real: Egypt entered the modern world as a centralized, armed, and increasingly literate state because one man willed it so and built the machinery to make the will effective. The human price of these achievements, borne overwhelmingly by the peasantry whose labor and harvests paid for them, is taken up in the following section.

## The Tyrant Case: What It Cost

The case against Muhammad Ali as a ruler begins with an accounting question: who paid for the new Egypt, and who decided how the bill would be settled. The harshest reading, advanced most forcefully by Khaled Fahmy in All the Pasha's Men (1997), holds that the schools, factories, dockyards, and drilled regiments were not gifts to a nation but instruments of a dynasty, and that the peasantry financed every one of them with labor, grain, and blood. The contemporary Cairene chronicler Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti, who lived through the French occupation and Muhammad Ali's seizure of power and died in 1825, supplies the prosecution's earliest witness. In his 'Aja'ib al-athar he recorded year after year of levies, seizures, and punishments, and his tone darkens as the Pasha's government tightens its grip on the countryside. Fahmy's argument gives that chronicle a modern frame: Muhammad Ali built a conscript army and a centralized fiscal apparatus to wage war and secure his family's rule, and modernization was the means rather than the end. On this view, the fellahin were not citizens being uplifted but resources being mobilized, and the measure of the reign is found in villages emptied of young men, canals dug by forced hands, and harvests taken at prices the cultivator never chose.

That reading does not deny that things were built. It insists on asking at whose expense. The sections that follow lay out the evidence in the form its strongest defenders use: the machinery of conscription, the corvee labor that cut canals and fed factories, the fiscal system of taxes and monopolies that stripped the countryside of its surplus, the concentration of land in the hands of the ruler's household and favorites, and the calculated violence by which rival power was destroyed and dissent was kept down.

### How did conscription into Muhammad Ali's army actually work?

Conscription drew fellahin by village quotas enforced by press gangs. Shaykhs delivered names, soldiers seized men, and recruits served for years under harsh discipline. Contemporary European observers reported peasants maiming themselves, blinding an eye or severing fingers, to fail the medical inspection and escape service.

The decision to draft Egyptian peasants grew out of failure and expense. Muhammad Ali's early power rested on Albanian and Turkish irregulars whose mutinies over arrears of pay repeatedly shook Cairo, most dangerously in the years before 1815. Such troops were costly, unreliable, and politically dangerous to the man who paid them. From 1820 to 1822 the Pasha began replacing them with a standing infantry raised from the fellahin and drilled on the European model, the force known as the nizam al-jadid, the new order. Recruits were concentrated at Aswan, far from their homes and from the political temptations of the capital, where French officers, most famously Joseph Seve, who took the name Sulayman Pasha, imposed barrack discipline, European drill, uniforms, and a medical service under the French surgeon Clot Bey. The experiment worked well enough as a military instrument that it was extended year after year, and by the 1830s estimates of the army's strength, which historians regard as approximate and partly overlapping with irregular and naval forces, run from about 100,000 to 130,000 men.

The machinery of [the conscript army Muhammad Ali built](/2014/07/01/egypt-army-under-muhammad-ali/) depended on village quotas that made the entire countryside into a recruiting ground. Provincial governors received demands for fixed numbers of men, district officials apportioned them among villages, and the shaykh al-balad, the village headman, produced the names. Fahmy shows how this chain converted local authority into an arm of the state: a headman who failed to deliver his quota faced punishment himself, which gave him every reason to hand over the poorest and least protected households first. Roundups were carried out by soldiers and police, often at night or at markets and festivals where men gathered, and recruits were chained together for the march to the depots. Exemptions existed on paper for only sons, for certain occupations, and for the medically unfit, but contemporary accounts agree that bribery decided many cases, so that families with means bought their sons out while families without means lost them.

Service was long, discipline was severe, and desertion was treated as a crime against the state. Terms of service stretched across years, and although regulations set limits, men were routinely retained beyond them while the Pasha's wars in the Sudan, Greece, and Syria demanded replacements. Punishments for desertion, insubordination, and theft included flogging, imprisonment, and in grave cases execution, and Fahmy documents how the army's medical and penal bureaucracy recorded, counted, and processed bodies with a thoroughness that was itself new in Egypt. Disease killed steadily in crowded barracks and on campaign; cholera, plague, ophthalmia, and dysentery recur in the records. Mortality figures for particular campaigns are disputed, and historians warn against accepting the highest contemporary guesses, but the pattern is consistent: a substantial share of each intake never returned to his village, and many who did came back broken in health.

It is against this background that the reports of self-mutilation must be read. European consuls, travelers, and physicians in Egypt in the 1820s and 1830s recorded peasants blinding themselves in one eye, severing fingers, breaking teeth, or otherwise injuring their bodies to fail the recruiting surgeons. John Bowring's 1840 report on Egypt and Syria, compiled for the British government, repeated such accounts, and French medical men described the measures taken to detect fraud, including holding suspects for observation before examination. Historians treat individual anecdotes with caution, since horror stories about Oriental despotism sold well in Europe, and some injuries attributed to draft evasion may have had other causes. Fahmy's judgment, after working through the army's own registers, is that the phenomenon was real even if its scale cannot be fixed: the registers show sustained official anxiety about evasion, repeated orders against it, and punishments designed to deter it, which is not the footprint of an invented problem. Whether a young man put out his own eye or fled to the desert or to Syria, the meaning was the same. The army was feared as a place men did not expect to leave, and the villages experienced it as a levy on their sons comparable to a tax in blood.

The social cost extended beyond the men taken. Conscription removed prime-age male labor from agriculture in a country where the harvest depended on human and animal muscle at precise seasonal moments. Villages were left to women, children, and the old during the months when the Nile's flood had to be managed and the basins drained and sown. Flight compounded the loss: men hid in the desert fringe, joined Bedouin groups, or crossed into Palestine and Syria to escape the recruiters, and officials complained of villages unable to meet either their labor quotas or their tax assessments because so many able-bodied men had gone. Population estimates for the period are fragile, and claims of outright demographic collapse in particular districts should be read as ranges rather than certainties, but the direction of movement is not in doubt. The conscription system transferred the countryside's most productive labor to the Pasha's wars, and the countryside paid the difference in thinner harvests and heavier burdens on those who remained.

Forced labor did not end at the barracks gate. The corvee, the state's claim on peasant labor for public works, expanded under Muhammad Ali into one of the largest mobilizations of workers in Egypt's recorded history. The Mahmudiyya canal, cut from 1817 to 1820 to link Alexandria with the Nile, is the most cited case. European accounts spoke of hundreds of thousands of laborers gathered for the digging and of deaths running into the tens of thousands, with some versions reaching as high as 100,000 dead. Historians regard the uppermost figures as unverifiable and almost certainly inflated, but even heavily discounted estimates describe a severe toll from exhaustion, disease, and inadequate food and shelter, and al-Jabarti's chronicle records the suffering the project imposed on the districts that supplied the gangs. The canal served Alexandria's commerce and the Pasha's revenues; the men who dug it were fed, when they were fed, at subsistence levels and were driven by overseers with sticks.

Canals were only the largest category. Corvee gangs maintained and deepened the irrigation network on which the new summer crops, above all cotton, depended, and the expansion of perennial irrigation under Muhammad Ali multiplied the demand for such labor. Factories drew their own forced contingents: the textile mills and workshops of the Pasha's industrial program employed workers who were assigned rather than hired, paid little or nothing beyond rations, and punished for absence or slow work. Transport corvee seized boatmen, porters, and camel drivers to move grain, timber, and military supplies along the Nile and to the ports. In each case the mechanism was the same village quota system that fed the army, and the same headmen who delivered recruits delivered laborers. The state's appetite for work was limited only by the seasons, since men could not be taken from the fields at sowing or harvest without destroying the tax base itself, and officials calibrated their demands against that constraint with a precision that shows how fully the fellahin had been converted into a managed labor force.

The fiscal system that accompanied this mobilization took the surplus the labor produced. Muhammad Ali replaced the old tax farmers with salaried officials answerable to Cairo, surveyed and reassessed the land, and collected in a manner al-Jabarti described as relentless. The land tax remained the foundation of revenue, and its collection was backed by soldiers: villages that fell into arrears faced the seizure of livestock and grain, the imprisonment of headmen, and collective liability that punished the community for the shortfalls of its members. On top of taxation came the monopolies. The state claimed exclusive rights over the principal commercial crops and commodities, buying the harvest from the cultivator at fixed official prices and reselling at a profit at home and abroad. Grain, the staff of subsistence, was taken at prices set by the government rather than the market, which meant that in years of high prices the fellah captured none of the gain while in years of low prices he still bore the tax. Cotton, promoted as the great cash crop of the new economy, was subject to state purchase at administered prices that left the cultivator the costs of the perennial irrigation it required and the state the margin on its sale to European mills. Contemporaries from Bowring to French consular agents described the result in consistent terms: the peasant had lost the freedom to sell to the highest bidder, and with it the incentive and the means to accumulate.

The defenders of the tyrant case press the point that this was not incidental hardship but the design of the system. Fahmy argues that the monopolies and the conscription apparatus were two faces of a single project: the construction of a fiscal-military state that extracted from the countryside the men and the money needed for war and for the dynasty's standing. Al-Jabarti, writing without any theory of fiscal-military states, recorded the same reality in the language of moral witness, describing officials who stripped villages bare and a ruler whose building projects rose while his subjects sank into debt. Peasants borrowed to pay taxes, sold livestock, and in bad years went hungry with grain stacked in government warehouses. The famines and shortages that struck in the 1830s are attributed by historians to a combination of low floods, epidemic disease, and the state's exactions, with the proportions disputed, but there is no dispute that the monopolies left cultivators with minimal reserves when crisis came.

Land policy completed the transfer. The destruction of the Mamluk elite in 1811 was followed by the confiscation of their iltizam holdings, the tax farms that had been the basis of their wealth, and those revenues flowed to the Pasha. Over the following decades Muhammad Ali granted vast estates to members of his family, above all to his son Ibrahim, and to favored officials, officers, and Bedouin shaykhs whose loyalty he wished to purchase. The uhda and ib'adiyya grants studied by Kenneth Cuno in The Pasha's Peasants (1992) placed large tracts under holders who owed their position to the ruler and who extracted labor and dues from the peasants settled on them. Religious endowments, the waqfs that had supported mosques, schools, and the ulama, were brought under state administration, which both increased revenue and broke an independent base of property and influence. The fellahin who had cultivated as holders of recognized use-rights found themselves increasingly reduced to tenants and laborers on estates whose owners answered to Cairo. Cuno shows that this was not a single decree but a cumulative process of grants, confiscations, and administrative pressure, and its outcome was a countryside in which a thin layer of great proprietors, headed by the Pasha's own household, stood above a mass of cultivators with shrinking claims on the land they worked.

### Why did Muhammad Ali order the massacre of the Mamluks?

Muhammad Ali ordered the 1811 massacre to eliminate the Mamluk beys as a rival power center. After years of unstable condominium rule, he lured their leadership to the Cairo Citadel under pretense of ceremony, then had his troops kill them, removing the only military elite that could challenge his authority.

The Mamluks had ruled Egypt as a military caste for centuries, and although the Ottoman reconquest of 1517 and the French expedition of 1798 to 1801 had shaken them, their beys remained the only force in the country organized for war and accustomed to command. Muhammad Ali, an Ottoman officer of Albanian origin who maneuvered his way to the governorship in 1805 with the backing of Cairo's ulama and populace, spent his first years balancing between the beys, the Ottoman sultan's authority, and his own Albanian troops. By 1811 he had decided the balance could not hold. The beys retained armed households, territorial claims, and a legitimacy rooted in long dominance; as long as they lived, any setback, a mutiny, a British landing, an order from Istanbul, could rally opposition around them. The expedition he was preparing against the Wahhabis in Arabia on the sultan's orders supplied the occasion: his son Tusun was to lead it, and a ceremonial departure offered a pretext to gather the beys inside the Citadel.

On 1 March 1811 the Mamluk leadership, some twenty-four beys with several hundred retainers and household troops, processed up to the Citadel for the investiture ceremony. After the ceremonies and a meal, they were invited to descend in procession through the narrow passage of Bab al-Azab. The gates closed behind them and before them, and Albanian soldiers posted on the walls above opened fire. Trapped in the defile, the Mamluks were shot down, and those who survived the volleys were killed with swords and lances. Estimates of the dead vary with the source: most accounts place the number of beys at about two dozen and the total killed, including retainers, in the range from 400 to 500, though some versions run higher and a few lower. One bey, Amin Bey, is reported to have spurred his horse over the walls or through a gap and escaped; a small number of others survived by absence or flight. Simultaneously, orders went out across Cairo and the provinces for the killing or arrest of remaining Mamluks and the seizure of their property, and in the following months Muhammad Ali's forces hunted down those who had fled to Upper Egypt, some of whom eventually escaped south into Nubia and the Sudan.

Al-Jabarti's account of the massacre is the indispensable contemporary record, and it is unsparing. He describes the deception, the slaughter in the pass, and the plundering of the victims' houses, and he places the event within a narrative of a ruler who used treachery where force alone might have failed. Fahmy, drawing on the Ottoman and Egyptian archives as well as the chronicles, reads the massacre as exemplary state violence: not a battle but a demonstration, designed to show every potential rival, the ulama, the remaining Ottoman officials, the turbulent soldiery, that opposition would be met with annihilation rather than negotiation. The confiscations that followed support that reading. The wealth, households, and iltizam revenues of the dead passed to the Pasha, financing the very army that had killed them and removing at a stroke the economic base of an entire elite. Political terror and fiscal gain were not alternatives; they were the same act.

The massacre stands as the most dramatic case in a wider pattern of suppression. In 1809 Muhammad Ali exiled Umar Makram, the naqib al-ashraf whose popular following had helped raise him to power, to Damietta when the sayyid's influence and his protests against taxation became inconvenient; the ulama as a corporate body never recovered an independent political role. Cairo itself saw the suppression of urban disturbances with executions and imprisonments. In the countryside, peasant revolts against conscription and taxation, notably in Upper Egypt and the Delta in the 1820s, were put down by military columns, and villages that resisted faced collective punishment: fines, the seizure of livestock, the burning of houses in the hardest cases reported by contemporaries. Censorship and surveillance were rudimentary by later standards, but the Pasha's government kept watch on correspondence, restricted the movement of Europeans whose reports might embarrass it, and punished officials who failed in their quotas. None of this required an ideology of terror. It required only the logic Fahmy identifies: a state built for war could not tolerate rival centers of armed power or organized refusal, and it possessed, in the conscript army and the centralized bureaucracy, the instruments to crush both.

The steelman of the tyrant case therefore closes where it began, with the ledger. The factories at al-Khurunfish and the mills of the Delta, the dockyard at Alexandria, the frigates that carried Ibrahim's armies to Greece and Syria, the irrigation works that made cotton a great export: each drew on revenue taken from the fellahin at fixed prices and on labor taken from their villages by quota. Fahmy's formulation is deliberately stark, that Egypt's modernization was the modernization of the means of coercion, and that the peasantry experienced the state above all as the taker of sons, grain, and work. Al-Jabarti, who watched the process year by year, supplied the contemporary verdict in the only terms available to him, recording a ruler whose power grew as his subjects' substance diminished. The achievements catalogued elsewhere in this article do not disappear under this reading; they are repriced. On the tyrant case, Muhammad Ali was a builder, and the bill was presented to the people least able to refuse it, who paid it in full.

## The Muhammad Ali Debate Table

The argument over Muhammad Ali has been running since his own lifetime, and it has never been a polite disagreement. Al-Jabarti, writing in Cairo while the Pasha still ruled, denounced his methods even as the new order took shape around him. A century later Henry Dodwell called him the founder of modern Egypt and meant it as praise. Another half century on, Khaled Fahmy's archival work recovered a ruler who squeezed the peasantry to fund his ambitions. The dispute is not about whether he built things; nobody denies the army, the factories, the schools. The dispute is about what the building cost and whether the cost was justified. The table below sets out the strongest version of each case, claim by claim, so the reader can weigh the evidence rather than inherit a verdict.

| Claim | Reformer case and evidence | Tyrant case and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |

| The economy and monopolies | State monopolies on cotton, grain, and other exports captured the surplus that had leaked to tax farmers and foreign merchants and redirected it into the army, the factories, and the schools; the cotton boom of the 1820s made the whole modernizing project financially possible. | The state bought the cultivator's crop at fixed low prices and sold it dear, keeping the margin by force of law; critics of the system argue it enriched the treasury by impoverishing the countryside, and the 1838 Balta Liman convention exposed how quickly the edifice crumbled once free trade was imposed. |

Read across the rows, a pattern emerges that no single label captures. Every reform in the left-hand column was financed by a compulsion listed in the right-hand one, and every compulsion was justified, at the time, as the price of a reform. The table does not settle the debate; it explains why the debate refuses to die. The reformer case and the tyrant case are not rival interpretations of different facts. They are rival readings of the same facts.

## Where the Either-Or Framing Fails

The debate table invites a tidy conclusion, and that is its trap. Reformer or tyrant, builder or destroyer, father of the nation or butcher of the fellahin: the question assumes the two descriptions point at different things. In Muhammad Ali's Egypt they point at the same thing. Conscription was the instrument that built the modern army and the instrument that emptied villages of their young men; the two statements are not in tension, they are the same event described from Cairo and from the Delta. The cotton monopoly was the revenue engine of the schools and the mechanism that paid the fellah a fraction of his crop's value; again, one policy, two ledgers. Centralization ended the Mamluk anarchy that had made every traveler and every tax collector fear for his life, and it concentrated in one man's hands a power no Egyptian ruler had held since the pharaohs, unchecked by any council, clergy, or custom he could not overrule.

This is why the either-or framing fails: it asks the reader to choose between the institution and its cost, when the historical record insists they were a package. The historian who admires the Nizam al-Jadid is admiring an army that could only exist because the state claimed the bodies of its subjects; the historian who condemns the conscription is condemning the price of an army that, by every military measure, worked. Fahmy's great contribution in "All the Pasha's Men" was to refuse the choice. His army is simultaneously the most impressive military creation of the nineteenth-century Middle East and a vast apparatus of surveillance, punishment, and bodily control. To call it one or the other is to misdescribe it; it was both, by design.

The same holds for the economy. Marsot's indictment of the monopoly system does not require denying that the revenues built real schools and real factories, and Dodwell's celebration of those schools and factories does not survive contact with the fiscal machinery that paid for them. The error of the pure reformer image is that it treats the price as incidental, a regrettable friction on the road to progress. The error of the pure tyrant image is that it treats the achievement as a sham, as though the army that beat the sultan at Konya were an illusion and the graduates of the Paris missions mere decoration. Neither image survives the evidence, because the evidence was produced by a project that was genuinely both: a modernizing project that could only be carried out by coercion, and a coercive project that genuinely modernized.

Consider what "reformer" and "tyrant" actually describe here. They are not rival theories about Muhammad Ali's character, though character sketches abound. They are labels for two different ledgers of the same enterprise. The reformer's ledger records institutions: the army, the bureaucracy, the schools, the factories, the hereditary state. The tyrant's ledger records bodies: the conscripts who died of disease in Syria, the villagers ruined by requisition, the Mamluk beys shot in the Citadel courtyard, the fellahin taxed into debt. The ledgers balance against each other because they record the same transactions. Every entry on one side has its counterpart on the other. To close either ledger and declare the account settled is to falsify the books.

There is a further complication that honest history has to face. The modern Egyptian state and the suffering of its builders are not two stories that happen to share a protagonist; they are one story. The centralized administration that later nationalists would inherit, staff, and turn against foreign occupation was built by the same decrees that crushed the peasantry. The army that became the vehicle of Egyptian nationalism was raised by the conscription that Egyptians cursed. This does not excuse the coercion, and it does not diminish the achievement; it states the condition under which both happened. The builder-at-a-price verdict is the only one the evidence supports: Muhammad Ali built a state, and the price was exacted from people who never consented to pay it.

That verdict will satisfy nobody who came looking for a hero or a villain, which is precisely its virtue. The father-of-the-nation image, lovingly maintained by Dodwell and by much Egyptian official memory, requires averting the eyes from the Citadel in 1811 and from the conscription sweeps that Fahmy documented. The pure tyrant image, tempting to anyone who reads Fahmy on the army or Cuno on the countryside, requires dismissing as worthless the institutions that demonstrably worked and demonstrably lasted. Both are evasions. The evidence leaves room for admiration of what was built and for horror at how it was built, and it leaves no room for choosing between the two.

To hold the debate honestly, then, is to resist the question as posed. Separating [Muhammad Ali Pasha, the man himself](/2014/04/01/muhammad-ali-pasha-of-egypt/) from the legends later attached to his name is the necessary first step, but even the man, known plainly, will not resolve into one figure or the other. He was a ruler who understood that power in Egypt had to be built before it could be used, and who built it out of the only materials available to him: the land, the crop, and the bodies of his subjects. The instruments were the same in every department. Conscription, monopoly, and centralization were simultaneously the tools of modernization and the tools of exploitation. That simultaneity is the whole of the matter, and any framing that splits it in two has stopped doing history and started doing advocacy.

## The Verdict

A verdict, if it is to be worth anything, has to name its deciding evidence. Here it is. On the reformer side: the institutions endured. The conscript army fought and won campaigns from Arabia to Anatolia. The bureaucracy he created administered Egypt long after his death. The schools produced the engineers, doctors, and translators who staffed the modern state. The hereditary settlement of 1841 gave Egypt a dynasty that ruled until 1952. These are not claims about intentions; they are facts about outcomes, and no serious historian disputes them. Dodwell's admiration, however effusive, rested on something real.

On the tyrant side: the coercion was systematic, not incidental. The Citadel massacre of 1811 was not a battlefield excess but a planned treachery against guests, and it announced the method of the reign: no rival, no law, no custom would be allowed to stand in the ruler's way. The conscription system, documented in the army's own records by Fahmy, seized men by force, punished desertion with flogging and worse, and lost thousands to disease and hardship. The fiscal system, documented by the critical historians, taxed the cultivator to the edge of ruin and beyond it. These too are facts about outcomes, not rival interpretations.

The verdict that both sets of facts compel is this: Muhammad Ali was a genuine state-builder whose project depended on systematic coercion. The reforms were real, and so was the price. His greatness as a ruler and his ruthlessness toward his subjects are both documented, and neither cancels the other.

This is not a compromise verdict, and it should not be read as one. A compromise would apportion blame and credit, as though some percentage of the man were reformer and the remainder tyrant. The evidence does not permit that arithmetic. The coercion was not a regrettable accompaniment to the reforms; it was their precondition. Without the monopolies there was no revenue for the schools; without the conscription there was no army; without the destruction of the Mamluks there was no central authority to command either. And the reforms were not a mask for the coercion; the army really fought, the schools really taught, the state really governed. Each side of the ledger is the condition of the other.

The deciding consideration is the question of consent, and it cuts against the Pasha. Nothing in his project asked the fellahin whether they wished to be modernized. The peasant who lost his son to the conscription, his crop to the monopoly, and his land to the tax collector was not a participant in a national project; he was its raw material. A ruler can be great in the historical sense, consequential, transformative, the founder of an order that lasts a century, while remaining ruthless in the moral sense, and Muhammad Ali is the clearest case in modern Middle Eastern history of the two judgments coexisting without contradiction.

The 1840 settlement supplies the final piece of deciding evidence, because it tests the project against reality. When European power intervened, the Syrian conquests vanished, the army was capped, and the monopolies were dismantled under the Balta Liman regime. What survived was the core: the centralized Egyptian state, the dynasty, the administrative machinery. That survival is the measure of what was genuinely built, as opposed to what was merely seized. It is also the measure of the project's limits: a modernization deep enough to last, but not deep enough, and not free enough, to stand against the European order on its own terms.

So the verdict stands as stated. He built a state out of coercion, and the state outlasted the coercion's justification. The reformer and the tyrant were not two men, and not two phases; they were one ruler, doing one thing, and the thing he did was to drag Egypt into the modern age at a price its people did not choose to pay.

## What Remains Honestly Uncertain

A defended verdict is not a closed case. Several questions about Muhammad Ali's reign remain genuinely open, and honesty requires stating them as questions rather than smuggling in answers.

The first is the counterfactual: could the industrialization have been achieved without the coercion? The factories needed capital, labor, and markets. The capital came from the monopolies, the labor from conscription and the corvee, the market from the army's needs. Remove the coercion and the whole structure loses its inputs at once. But whether a slower, voluntary industrialization was possible in 1820s Egypt is unknowable, because no one tried it and no contemporary parallel exists in the region. The Ottoman Empire's own reformers faced the same dilemma and reached for the same tools. This does not excuse the Pasha's choices; it marks the boundary of what the evidence can say. To assert that gentler means would have worked is speculation. To assert that they would have failed is speculation too.

The second open question concerns the collapse of the industrial drive after 1840. European pressure is undeniable: the Balta Liman convention of 1838 abolished the monopolies on which state industry depended, the 1840 settlement capped the army that was the factories' great customer, and British manufactures flooded the Egyptian market under free-trade terms Egypt had not chosen. But structural weakness is equally undeniable: the factories lacked coal, lacked skilled labor, and were managed as military departments rather than enterprises. Historians divide on the balance. Some stress the strangling effect of the imposed free-trade regime, arguing that Egyptian industry was sacrificed to European commercial interests. Others, following Marsot's emphasis on the internal fragility of the whole edifice, argue that the factories were already failing on their own terms before 1840 finished them. The records do not settle the apportionment, and any honest account leaves the ratio open.

The third uncertainty is the human cost in hard numbers. How many conscripts died? The army's records, as Fahmy showed, are fragmentary: they document the machinery of recruitment, punishment, and desertion in detail, but they do not yield a reliable death toll. Estimates in the literature span a wide range. Some accounts speak of thousands dead of disease and hardship in a single campaigning season; cumulative totals across the Syrian, Arabian, and Sudanese campaigns run far higher in other estimates, into the tens of thousands. Desertion, which in some years stripped whole regiments, further clouds the figures, since a deserter and a dead man look identical in a muster roll's silence. What can be said with confidence is that mortality was heavy, that disease killed far more than battle, and that the precise scale will likely never be known. The thinness of the records is itself part of the story: a state meticulous about revenue was incurious about the lives it spent.

A fourth question, quieter but persistent, concerns the land. The surveys of 1813 to 1814 are often presented as a rationalizing reform, and in administrative terms they were. But the distribution of the great estates to the Pasha's family and officers created a landed elite whose exactions would burden the peasantry long after the monopolies were gone. How much of the countryside's later misery traces to this original land grab, and how much to subsequent developments under his successors, is difficult to disentangle. Vatikiotis, writing the structural history of the dynasty, treats the land settlement as one of its most consequential and least examined acts.

These uncertainties do not weaken the verdict; they define its edges. The core findings, the reality of the reforms and the reality of the coercion, rest on evidence thick enough to convince. Around that core lies a penumbra of questions the sources cannot answer, and the honest historian names them rather than filling them in.

## Study and Revision: How to Hold the Debate

The way to master this debate is to refuse to let either side go slack. For every claim in the table, force yourself to state the strongest version of the opposing case before you allow yourself a judgment. If the army's victories impress you, make yourself recite Fahmy's account of the conscription machine. If the Citadel massacre revolts you, make yourself acknowledge what Mamluk anarchy had cost Egypt and what centralization made possible. The student who can argue both sides from evidence, and say exactly where each side's evidence runs out, understands Muhammad Ali better than the student who has memorized a verdict. The verdict matters, but the ability to hold both ledgers open at once is the actual skill.

Track the scholars by name, because each one sees a different part of the elephant. Dodwell, writing in 1931, gives you the admiring founder narrative in its purest form; read him to understand why the reformer case once seemed self-evident, and then read him against the evidence he minimizes. Vatikiotis gives you the long structural view, the dynasty and the state as institutions with a logic of their own. Cuno, in The Pasha's Peasants, gives you the countryside's view: the taxes, the monopolies, and the price paid by the fellahin, documented from the archives. Fahmy, in "All the Pasha's Men," gives you the army from the inside, through its own records, and shows how modernization and discipline were the same process. And al-Jabarti, the contemporary chronicler who watched the French come and go and watched the Pasha rise, gives you the one witness no later historian can replace: a brilliant, furious Cairene writing while it happened, hostile to the Pasha's methods and invaluable precisely because of it.

Fix the dates, because the debate moves on a skeleton of chronology. In 1805 Muhammad Ali secured the governorship of Egypt; if the mechanics of that seizure are hazy, review [how he seized power in the first place](/2014/05/01/how-muhammad-ali-seized-power/) before going further. In 1811 came the Citadel massacre, the act that announced the character of the reign. From 1831 to 1833 the Syrian campaigns carried Egyptian power to its greatest extent and exposed the cost at which it was bought. In 1840 the European settlement cut the empire down to Egypt and fixed the terms, hereditary rule in exchange for submission, under which the dynasty would live. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Was Muhammad Ali a reformer or a tyrant?

Both labels capture something true, and the tension between them is the central debate of the historiography. Khaled Fahmy (1997) documents a ruler who built schools, hospitals, and a printing press while running a surveillance state that punished deserters by amputation. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) portrays him as an Ottoman pragmatist whose reforms served his own power rather than any liberal ideal. Henry Dodwell (1931), writing from an older British imperial perspective, admired Ali's modernizing energy and downplayed its cost. The evidence supports a reasoned lean: he was a reformer by design and a tyrant by method. Land surveys, the 1821 military conscription system, and factory monopolies genuinely transformed Egypt's administration, yet every one of these projects relied on coercion against the fellahin. Historians treat "reformer" and "ruthless" not as alternatives but as two faces of the same state-building logic.

### Q: Was Muhammad Ali good for Egypt?

The answer depends on whose Egypt one means. For the officers, administrators, and foreign experts who staffed his state, his rule opened careers and institutions that had not existed under the Mamluks. For the fellahin who made up most of the population, the ledger is harsher: conscription pulled hundreds of thousands from their fields, tax burdens rose under the state land monopoly, and forced labor gangs dug canals and built barracks. P.J. Vatikiotis judges the era as one of real material growth purchased at steep human cost. The contemporary chronicler al-Jabarti, writing from Cairo, recorded both admiration for Ali's energy and grief at the suffering of ordinary people. Modern Egyptian nationalist historiography once celebrated him as founder of the modern state, but Khaled Fahmy (1997) insists the benefits flowed upward. The settled verdict: he built a stronger Egypt, not a gentler one.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali exploit Egyptian peasants?

Yes, by every available measure, and this is among the least contested points in the literature. The state's grain monopoly forced peasants to sell crops at fixed prices, below market value, while taxes were collected in cash at punishing rates. Corvée labor drafts removed villagers from their own harvests to dig canals and transport military supplies; contemporary accounts describe entire districts depopulated by flight. Khaled Fahmy (1997) shows that the army's conscription machinery fell hardest on rural families, with punishments for evasion extending to relatives. Al-Jabarti recorded forced levies and confiscations with evident distress. Defenders argue that peasant exploitation predated Ali under the Mamluks, which is true but not exculpatory. The dispute is only over degree and framing: Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Fahmy (1997) presents the exploitation as the fiscal engine of his projects, while Henry Dodwell (1931) treats it as a regrettable but normal cost of nineteenth-century modernization.

### Q: Was Muhammad Ali a visionary?

He was visionary in the narrow sense of seeing farther than his contemporaries toward a centralized, industrializing Egyptian state. Few rulers of the 1810s imagined building textile mills, a naval arsenal at Alexandria, medical schools, and a modern army in a province of the Ottoman Empire; Ali imagined and attempted all of them. Khaled Fahmy (1997) credits him with an unusually clear program of military-fiscal state building. Yet Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Fahmy (1997) warns that calling him a visionary smuggles in nationalist romance: his goals were dynastic and personal, not national. His "vision" also had blind spots, including an overconfidence in state-run factories that economists like the British consul John Bowring judged uneconomical. The settled reading is that his imagination was real and his ambition unprecedented, but vision alone did not make his plans sound, and much of what looked like foresight was imitation of European models applied with force.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali modernize Egypt or just enrich himself?

He did both, and the two were structurally linked. The modernization is undeniable: a standing army, a bureaucracy paid in cash, the Bulaq press, schools training engineers and doctors, and irrigation works that expanded cultivable land. But the enrichment was equally real. Ali and his household amassed vast estates through the ib'adiya land grants, and his family's wealth grew alongside, not apart from, the state's revenues. P.J. Vatikiotis notes that public and private accounts were barely distinguished in Ali's administration. The contemporary chronicler al-Jabarti observed the ruler's appetite for revenue and land with suspicion. Henry Dodwell (1931) framed the personal gain as the incidental reward of a great builder; Khaled Fahmy (1997) treats it as evidence that the state existed to serve its founder. The reasoned lean: modernization was the instrument and dynastic enrichment the purpose, and separating the two misdescribes how his regime worked.

### Q: Was Muhammad Ali ruthless?

The record is unambiguous here. The 1811 massacre of the Mamluk leadership at the Citadel, lured to a banquet and killed in the streets below, was deliberate and premeditated, and al-Jabarti described the aftermath in Cairo with horror. Military conscripts who deserted faced flogging or mutilation; tax defaulters were beaten; villages that resisted levies were burned. Khaled Fahmy (1997) documents the routine violence of the conscription system, including the punishment of entire communities for individual flight. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) agrees that coercion was his method while cautioning that Ottoman governors generally ruled by fear. Even Henry Dodwell (1931), his most sympathetic historian, does not deny the violence. The only debate is comparative: whether he was crueler than the Mamluk beys he displaced or the Ottoman norm he operated within. On ruthlessness as a fact, the evidence is settled, and the massacre of 1811 remains its starkest symbol.

### Q: How do historians judge Muhammad Ali?

Judgment has shifted with each generation's politics. Henry Dodwell (1931) wrote in the age of empire and saw a great modernizer dragging Egypt toward civilization. Mid-century Egyptian nationalists claimed him as the founder of modern Egypt, a framing P.J. Vatikiotis both records and complicates. The revisionist turn came with Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Fahmy (1997), who stripped away the nationalist halo and presented him as an ambitious Ottoman officer building a personal empire. Khaled Fahmy (1997) deepened this with archival research on the army and bureaucracy, showing a state built on the bodies of conscripts and fellahin. The contemporary chronicler al-Jabarti remains the indispensable witness, admiring and appalled by turns. No consensus has replaced the old hero narrative; instead historians now hold the paradox steady, treating Ali as the founder of a modern Egyptian state apparatus whose foundations were laid through systematic coercion.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali's reforms last?

Some endured for decades; the industrial project did not. The army, the bureaucracy, and the land tenure changes survived him and shaped Egypt under his successors, with hereditary rule confirmed in 1841 passing power to his son Ibrahim and then his grandson Abbas. The schools, hospitals, and the Bulaq press left institutional traces. But the state factories collapsed quickly after the 1840 Convention of London forced Ali to dismantle his monopolies and shrink his army; by the early 1840s most of the mills were shuttered or sold. Khaled Fahmy (1997) argues that the coercive extraction system persisted longer than the industries it funded. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) notes that his successors kept the administrative machinery while abandoning the economic program. The settled view: the state survived, the factories did not, and the land regime he created shaped Egyptian rural life into the twentieth century.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali build a modern nation or a family dynasty?

He built the machinery of a modern state and handed it to his family. The army, the ministries, the schools, and the land survey were impersonal institutions, but every one of them answered to Ali and his household. The 1841 settlement with the Ottoman Porte granted hereditary rule to his descendants, converting a provincial governorship into a dynastic throne. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) argues the dynasty was always the point: his wars in Syria from 1831 to 1833 and his defiance of the sultan served family ambition, not Egyptian nationhood. Khaled Fahmy (1997) adds that the officer corps and bureaucracy became a new elite loyal to the household. Nationalist historians once recast him as a patriot, but the evidence favors the dynastic reading. Egypt got modern institutions because Ali needed them to make his family unassailable, and the dynasty outlasted the empire that created it.

### Q: Were Muhammad Ali's state factories a success or a failure?

As an economic project they failed; as a political statement they partly succeeded. Ali founded textile mills, sugar refineries, armories, and a shipyard at Alexandria, employing tens of thousands, many of them conscripted. British observers, including the consul John Bowring in his 1840 report, judged the goods too expensive and too poor to compete with European imports. The factories survived only because the state bought their output at fixed prices and shielded them behind monopolies. When the 1840 settlement stripped those protections, the mills collapsed within a few years. Khaled Fahmy (1997) presents the factories as an impressive but unsustainable experiment in forced industrialization. Henry Dodwell (1931) was more forgiving, crediting the ambition. The settled judgment is economic failure: the factories never turned a durable profit, though they trained a generation of Egyptian workers and technicians in industrial skills.

### Q: What did the 1840 settlement cost Muhammad Ali?

The Convention of London in 1840 ended his imperial ambitions and much of his state project. Facing a European coalition backing the Ottoman sultan, Ali was forced to withdraw from Syria, which his son Ibrahim had conquered in the campaign of 1831 to 1833, and to accept severe limits on his army and navy. The settlement dismantled the state monopolies that had funded his factories and his treasury. In exchange, the 1841 firman granted hereditary rule over Egypt to his family, a prize Ali prized above all. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) frames it as a shrewd trade: empire lost, dynasty secured. Khaled Fahmy (1997) stresses that the economic cost was fatal to industrialization, since free trade exposed his protected factories to ruin. The cost, then, was his dream of a great eastern empire; the compensation was that Egypt would belong to his house.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali's land grants change who owned Egypt?

Before Ali, land was held under a tangle of Ottoman, Mamluk, and religious claims, with the multazim tax farmers dominant. Ali broke this system by confiscating estates, abolishing the iltizam, and making the state the ultimate owner of agricultural land. He then redistributed it as ib'adiya grants to family members, officers, officials, and favored foreigners, creating a new landed elite loyal to him. The fellahin became, in effect, state tenants paying fixed taxes and labor obligations rather than smallholders. P.J. Vatikiotis describes this as the birth of Egypt's modern landlord class. Khaled Fahmy (1997) shows that the reform concentrated ownership upward while deepening rural dependence on the state. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) notes that the old Mamluk landholders were destroyed and replaced by men whose titles derived from Ali himself. Ownership did not become more equal; it changed hands from one elite to another.

### Q: Who profited most from Muhammad Ali's reforms?

Ali's household and the new Turco-Circassian elite he created stood at the top of the ledger. Family members received vast ib'adiya estates; officers and officials accumulated land, salaries, and commercial privileges; European merchants and technical advisers won lucrative contracts and concessions. Below them, a thin stratum of Egyptian administrators and educated functionaries gained careers. The fellahin, who bore the taxes, conscription, and corvée, profited least, though some benefited from expanded irrigation and steadier order than under the Mamluks. Khaled Fahmy (1997) is explicit that the reforms transferred wealth upward into the state and its servants. The contemporary chronicler al-Jabarti watched Cairo's new rich with disapproval. Henry Dodwell (1931) suggested the country as a whole gained, but the distributional evidence supports the harsher view: the reforms enriched the men who ran the machine and the Europeans who supplied it.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali use titles and ceremonies to legitimize his rule?

Ali was a master of Ottoman symbolism. He accepted the sultan's titles, wali and later pasha, and staged elaborate ceremonies, military reviews, and mosque restorations to display himself as the empire's loyal servant even while building an autonomous power. The 1811 Mamluk massacre was followed by public celebrations framing it as the restoration of order. He cultivated the ulama with patronage and endowed religious institutions, while his court adopted European-style military pageantry to impress foreign consuls. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) emphasizes that legitimacy mattered because Ali had no traditional claim to rule: born in Kavala, he was an outsider who seized power in 1805 with the backing of Cairo's notables. P.J. Vatikiotis notes the careful balancing act of defying the sultan in practice while honoring him in ritual. The ceremonies worked: they clothed raw power in the language of law and faith.

### Q: Was Muhammad Ali's Egypt more advanced than the Ottoman Empire's other provinces?

In administration, military organization, and education, yes, and the gap was wide. By the 1830s Egypt had a standing conscript army trained on European lines, a centralized bureaucracy, technical schools, and the Bulaq press, while provinces like Syria and Iraq remained under looser, older forms of Ottoman governance. His Syrian campaign of 1831 to 1833 demonstrated the military disparity directly, as Ibrahim's forces overran Ottoman armies. Henry Dodwell (1931) presented this as proof of Ali's exceptional modernizing genius. Khaled Fahmy (1997) cautions that the advance was narrow: it concentrated in the army and the extractive state, not in broad economic or civic life. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) adds that the sultan's Tanzimat reforms later tried to catch up. The settled view: Egypt under Ali was the empire's most formidable province, but its advancement was a state-building project, not a society-wide transformation.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali's sons and grandsons continue his project?

Only in part, and with diminishing energy. His son Ibrahim, the brilliant general of the Syrian campaigns, briefly held power as wali in 1848 before dying later that year. His grandson Abbas I (reigned from 1848 to 1854) reversed course, closing schools, dismissing European advisers, and shrinking the army, though he kept the dynasty's land wealth. Sa'id (1854 to 1863) reopened some reforms and granted the Suez Canal concession, while Isma'il (1863 to 1879) revived the grand modernizing style on borrowed money, ending in bankruptcy and British occupation. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) argues the successors preserved the dynastic state but abandoned the industrial program. P.J. Vatikiotis traces a pattern of ambitious starts and fiscal collapse. The verdict: the family kept the throne and the estates, but the coherent project of the 1820s and 1830s died with its founder.

### Q: Could Muhammad Ali have industrialized Egypt without forced labor?

The evidence suggests not under his model, though the question itself is contested. His factories depended on conscripted workers, fixed low wages, and state monopolies that eliminated competition; without coercion the mills could not have been staffed or kept cheap. British observers argued that free labor and open markets would have produced better, cheaper goods, and Khaled Fahmy (1997) shows the human machinery of compulsion in detail. But Egypt in the 1820s had no free labor market, no private capital pool, and no domestic demand for factory goods, which makes the counterfactual hard to test. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Roger Owen's work on the region's economy implies the project was premature regardless of labor policy. The reasoned lean: forced labor was not an accidental cruelty but the load-bearing wall of his industrialization, and removing it would have required a different economy, a different state, and probably a different century.

### Q: Was Muhammad Ali's treatment of the fellahin worse than the Mamluks'?

This is genuinely disputed, and the comparison is complicated by different kinds of harm. The Mamluks were predatory tax farmers whose irregular exactions could be arbitrary and violent; al-Jabarti recorded their depredations with bitterness. Ali replaced arbitrary plunder with systematic extraction: heavier, more regular taxes, mass conscription, and organized corvée. Khaled Fahmy (1997) argues that the regularity of Ali's state made its demands inescapable in a way Mamluk rule never was; flight, the peasant's traditional escape, was policed by a bureaucracy that tracked deserters. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) notes that Ali at least provided more order and expanded irrigation. The contemporary chronicler al-Jabarti, who lived under both, lamented the suffering under each. The reasoned lean: Ali's rule was more total and therefore harder to evade, though whether "worse" is a moral or a material judgment remains open.

### Q: Did Muhammad Ali's Egypt develop a middle class?

Only in embryo, and historians debate whether the term fits at all. His reforms created a salaried stratum of Egyptian clerks, officers, teachers, doctors, and technicians, men educated in his schools and paid by the state. European merchants, Levantine traders, and Coptic financial administrators formed a commercial layer in Alexandria and Cairo. But this was not a middle class in the European sense: its wealth derived from state service and privilege, not from independent property or enterprise, and the fellahin majority remained outside it. P.J. Vatikiotis describes a new effendi class taking shape without yet constituting a bourgeoisie. Khaled Fahmy (1997) shows that advancement ran through the army and bureaucracy, channels controlled from above. Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot (1984) agrees the foundations were laid but the building was incomplete. The settled view: a state-dependent professional stratum emerged, while a true commercial middle class would wait for later decades.

### Q: Why did Muhammad Ali's industrial project collapse after 1840?

The immediate cause was the 1840 Convention of London, which dismantled the monopolies and tariff walls that had kept his factories alive. Without state purchases at fixed prices and protection from European imports, the mills could not compete: British textiles were cheaper and better. Underlying causes compounded the blow. The factories had always depended on forced labor and conscript management, producing poor-quality goods at high cost, as the consul John Bowring documented in his 1840 report. The army, the factories' main customer, was cut down by treaty. Ali himself was aging and, after his death in 1849, his successor Abbas showed no interest in industry. The collapse was therefore both imposed from outside and rotten from within.
