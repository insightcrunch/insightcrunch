The most consequential conquest in Egyptian history involved no battle. Alexander the Great entered Egypt in the autumn of 332 BCE, received the surrender of the Persian governor, sailed up the Nile to Memphis, sacrificed to the Egyptian gods, and became master of the country without an army of Egyptians raising a weapon against him. He stayed roughly six months, founded a city, walked into the western desert to consult an oracle, reorganized the administration, and left. He never returned.

Those six months rearranged the next thousand years. They ended Persian rule in Egypt permanently, established the city that would be the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean world for centuries, created the precedent by which a foreign king could be a legitimate pharaoh in Greek hands, and set up the succession crisis that placed one of Alexander's officers on the Egyptian throne and kept his family there for three hundred years.

The argument of this article is the welcomed-conqueror thesis: Alexander took Egypt as a liberator rather than an invader, and the absence of a fight is not a footnote to the story but the reason the conquest mattered so much. A country taken by storm is a country garrisoned against its population. A country handed over is a country whose institutions can be inherited intact, whose priesthood can be courted rather than suppressed, and whose new ruler can present himself as a restorer rather than an occupier. Everything the Ptolemies later built rested on the fact that Greek rule in Egypt began without a massacre.

The second contribution here is a framework for how that legitimacy was constructed, which can be called the four-move legitimation sequence: accepting a surrender rather than forcing a battle, sacrificing publicly to the Egyptian gods at Memphis, appearing in the pharaonic role in Egyptian temple contexts, and returning from Siwa with a claim of divine sonship that worked simultaneously in Egyptian and Greek registers. Each move addressed a different audience, and together they converted a military occupation into a recognized kingship in a matter of months.

## The Situation Before: Egypt Under the Second Persian Occupation

Understanding why Egyptians welcomed a Macedonian army requires knowing what they had been living under, and the timing is everything.

Egypt had won its independence from Persia at the end of the fifth century BCE and held it for roughly sixty years under three native dynasties, a period covered in the account of [the last native pharaohs of Egypt](/2006/12/01/last-native-pharaohs-of-egypt/). That independence ended around 343 BCE when Artaxerxes III reconquered the country after earlier failed attempts, driving the last native pharaoh from the throne and reimposing satrapal government.

The second occupation was brief and its reputation is the worst of any phase of Persian rule in Egypt, with later tradition alleging temple plundering, the removal of sacred writings, and deliberate religious insult. Those specific charges need the careful handling set out in [the comparison of Persian and native rule in Egypt](/2007/06/01/persian-vs-native-rule-in-egypt/), which examines how much of the anti-Persian image is propaganda. What is not in doubt is that a reconquest following a successful sixty-year revolt would have been punitive, that Egyptian resentment in the 330s BCE was fresh rather than inherited, and that the memory of independence was within living memory for a generation still alive.

This is the decisive difference between 332 BCE and the earlier Persian conquest. When Cambyses took Egypt around 525 BCE, described in [the account of how Persia conquered ancient Egypt](/2006/11/01/how-persia-conquered-egypt/), he fought a battle against a functioning Egyptian state defending itself. When Alexander arrived, the state defending Egypt was Persian, the population had been forcibly reincorporated within living memory, and there was no native regime for Egyptians to be loyal to.

### Why did Egyptians welcome Alexander?

Because he was ending an occupation that had been imposed by reconquest roughly a decade earlier, after Egyptians had enjoyed sixty years of independence. Egyptian loyalty was owed to no one in 332 BCE, the Persian administration was recent and resented, and a conqueror who displaced it was displacing the party Egyptians actually opposed.

Persian capacity to defend the country was also broken. The Persian field army had been destroyed at Issus in 333 BCE, and the satrap of Egypt had died in that battle. His replacement inherited a province with no significant force to defend it and a hostile population. The Persian position in Egypt in 332 BCE was not a strong garrison awaiting attack. It was an administrative shell.

## Why Alexander Came to Egypt at All

Alexander's Egyptian campaign is sometimes presented as an inevitable step, but it required a strategic decision, and the reasoning behind it explains the shape of the whole episode.

After Issus, the Persian king had fled east and the obvious move was pursuit. Alexander instead turned south along the Levantine coast, reducing the Phoenician cities in a campaign that included the long and costly siege of Tyre and then the reduction of Gaza. Only after that did he enter Egypt.

The logic was maritime. Persia's remaining capacity to threaten Alexander's rear lay in its fleet, and that fleet depended on Phoenician and Egyptian ports, crews, and money. An army marching into the Persian interior with a hostile navy operating behind it in the eastern Mediterranean risked losing its communications with Macedonia and Greece, where opposition to Macedonian dominance was ready to revive if it looked like a winning bet. Taking the coast eliminated the naval threat by removing its bases rather than by fighting at sea.

Egypt was the last and richest piece of that coastal program. It also offered something the Phoenician cities did not: enormous revenue, an agricultural surplus capable of supporting operations, and a prestige that mattered in the Greek world, where Egypt had a reputation for antiquity and wisdom stretching back centuries. Securing it removed the last Persian base in the Mediterranean and added a resource base for everything that followed.

The decision was therefore strategic rather than sentimental, and it is worth stating plainly because a great deal of writing on Alexander in Egypt implies a mystical attraction to the land of the pharaohs. The mysticism, such as it was, came later and at Siwa. The march came from a naval problem.

## The Bloodless Handover

The mechanics of the takeover are simple and the sources agree on the essentials.

Alexander marched from Gaza across northern Sinai to Pelusium, the fortress town guarding Egypt's northeastern approach, which had been the site of the battle that lost Egypt to Cambyses two centuries earlier. This time there was no battle. The Persian governor, Mazaces, having no army worth the name and facing a commander who had just destroyed the Persian field force and reduced two fortified cities, surrendered the country and its treasury.

Alexander's fleet moved along the coast while the army advanced overland, and he then went up the Nile to Memphis, the traditional administrative and religious heart of Egypt. Local Egyptian officials and populations appear to have received him without resistance at every stage. The entire acquisition of the wealthiest province of the Persian Empire cost the Macedonian army nothing.

### How did Alexander conquer Egypt?

He did not, in the military sense. The Persian satrap Mazaces surrendered the province without a fight in the autumn of 332 BCE, having lost his army and his predecessor at the Battle of Issus the previous year and facing a hostile Egyptian population. Alexander entered at Pelusium and proceeded up the Nile to Memphis unopposed.

The absence of fighting deserves emphasis because it is routinely lost in general accounts, which reach for the word conquest and let readers supply the imagery. There was no siege of Memphis, no massacre, no sack, no destruction layer for archaeologists to find, and no punitive settlement imposed on a defeated population. What happened was a transfer of authority in which the outgoing administration handed over and the incoming one took possession.

That has consequences running through the entire Ptolemaic period. Egyptian temples were not damaged and therefore needed no restoration. The administrative apparatus was intact and could be inherited. Egyptian elites had not been on the losing side of a war and had nothing to be punished for or to resent. And the new ruler owed nothing to a conquest narrative, which left him free to present himself in whatever terms served best.

## Memphis: Sacrifice, Games, and the Apis Bull

What Alexander did on arriving at Memphis is the first and most deliberate of the legitimation moves, and it was aimed squarely at an Egyptian audience.

He sacrificed to the Egyptian gods, and the sources single out the Apis bull, the living sacred animal of Memphis and the most visible cult in the city. This was not a private gesture. Public sacrifice by a ruler to Apis was a recognized act of Egyptian kingship, and doing it at Memphis in front of the priesthood and population was a statement about what kind of ruler he intended to be.

The choice of Apis specifically was pointed. The most notorious accusation against Persian rule in Egyptian and Greek tradition was that a Persian king had insulted or killed the Apis bull, an accusation that had attached to Cambyses two centuries earlier and had been repeated about the recent reconquest. Whether either charge was true is a separate question, and the evidence is examined in the Persian comparison linked above. What matters here is that the accusation was in circulation, and that Alexander's first significant public act in Egypt was to do the opposite of what Persian kings were said to have done.

Alongside the Egyptian sacrifice he held Greek athletic and musical contests, bringing performers from the Greek world. That combination in a single visit is the whole Ptolemaic future in miniature: Egyptian religious observance for Egyptians, Greek cultural display for Greeks, performed by the same ruler in the same city within days of each other, with no attempt to merge them.

### Was Alexander crowned pharaoh at Memphis?

The Greek narrative sources describe sacrifice and games but not a formal Egyptian coronation, while later legendary tradition asserts one. Egyptian evidence shows him bearing full pharaonic titulary and appearing in the royal role in temple contexts, so he was recognized as pharaoh whether or not a coronation ceremony took place.

That distinction matters and honest accounts preserve it. The question of a formal coronation is genuinely unresolved, and confident statements in either direction go beyond the evidence. What is not in doubt is the outcome. Alexander appears in Egyptian inscriptions with a proper five-part royal titulary, and building work was carried out in his name in Egyptian style at major temple sites, with the shrine constructed in his name at the great southern temple complex the most substantial surviving example. Egyptians treated him as pharaoh, recorded him as pharaoh, and built for him as pharaoh, which is the operative fact regardless of what ritual did or did not take place at Memphis.

## The Memphis Interval

The weeks Alexander spent at Memphis before moving north deserve a closer look, because that is where the transfer of authority actually took place and it is the least examined part of the episode.

Memphis was the administrative and religious center of Egypt and had been for nearly three thousand years. It sat at the junction of the valley and the Delta, controlled the approaches to both, housed the cult of Ptah and the Apis bull, and contained the machinery through which Egypt was governed. Whoever held Memphis held the country in practice, which is why the Persian governor's surrender was followed immediately by Alexander's move upriver rather than by a settlement at the frontier.

What happened there was a handover conducted in public. The sacrifice to the Egyptian gods placed the new ruler inside Egyptian religious practice in front of the institution that mattered most. The athletic and musical contests, staged with performers brought from the Greek world, placed him inside Greek cultural practice in front of the Greek and Macedonian community and whichever Egyptians were watching. Both were performances, in the precise sense that both were designed to be seen and reported.

The administrative substance followed. Officials were confirmed or appointed, the treasury was taken over, the tax machinery was directed to its new master, and the arrangement dividing authority among several officers was worked out. Egypt changed hands through paperwork and ceremony rather than through violence, and the fact that this was possible at all is the strongest evidence for how completely the Persian position had collapsed.

There is one further dimension to the Memphis weeks that the sources gesture at without developing. Alexander was there long enough for the Egyptian priesthood to take his measure, compose a royal titulary for him, and decide how he would be recorded. That was not an automatic process. Composing a five-part pharaonic titulary is a scholarly act performed by trained priests, involving choices about which epithets to use and which divine associations to invoke, and it represents a considered institutional judgment rather than a formality. The decision to produce one for a Macedonian who had arrived weeks earlier tells us that the priesthood moved quickly to categorize him as a legitimate king, which in turn suggests the sacrifice at Memphis had done exactly what it was intended to do.

The choice not to make Memphis his capital is equally significant and is easy to overlook. Alexander could have governed Egypt from its traditional center, as every previous ruler had. He chose instead to found a new city on the coast, which signalled that the Egypt he was creating would face outward. Memphis retained enormous religious weight throughout the Ptolemaic period and remained the place associated with Egyptian-style coronation, but the working government moved permanently to the sea. That decision, made during those weeks, reoriented the country.

## The Four-Move Legitimation Sequence

Pulling the episode together, Alexander's transformation from foreign general to legitimate Egyptian ruler followed four distinct moves, each addressing a different audience, and identifying them separately is the most useful analytical tool this article offers.

The first move was accepting a surrender rather than forcing a battle. This was partly circumstance, since Mazaces chose to hand over, but Alexander could have imposed punitive terms and did not. The result was that no Egyptian community had a grievance against him, and no reprisal narrative attached to his name.

The second move was public sacrifice to the Egyptian gods at Memphis, which announced to the Egyptian priesthood that the new ruler would perform the royal cultic function rather than ignore or attack it. This addressed the single greatest structural weakness of Persian rule, which was the absence of a king who performed Egyptian ritual.

The third move was assumption of the pharaonic role in Egyptian representational terms, through titulary, temple building, and appearance in the traditional royal iconography. This addressed the Egyptian institutional record: what would be inscribed, what future generations would read, and how the priesthood would formally categorize him.

The fourth move was the journey to Siwa and the divine sonship that came out of it, which addressed Greek and Macedonian audiences as much as Egyptian ones, and which is treated in detail below.

What makes the sequence effective is that each move was cheap. None required money on the scale of a building program, none required military commitment, and none required Alexander to modify his actual authority in any way. Legitimacy in Egypt was available to a ruler willing to perform a small number of specific acts, and Alexander performed them within weeks of arriving. Persian kings, who ruled Egypt for a combined century and a half, never assembled the full set, principally because they were never physically present to do so.

## Founding Alexandria

The most durable thing Alexander did in Egypt was to order a city built, and the founding is dated to early 331 BCE, during the same visit.

The site chosen lay on the Mediterranean coast at the western edge of the Delta, at a point where an existing Egyptian settlement stood, opposite a small offshore island. Tradition preserves the story of Alexander personally marking out the plan, and of the outline being traced in grain when chalk ran short, with birds descending to eat it, an omen the seers interpreted favorably as a sign the city would feed many peoples. The story may be later embroidery; the deliberate personal involvement in siting and layout is consistent with what he did elsewhere.

### Why did Alexander found a new capital in Egypt?

Because Egypt's existing cities faced inward along the Nile and none served a Mediterranean empire. A coastal foundation gave him a port capable of connecting Egypt to the Greek world, a naval station on the newly secured southern Mediterranean coast, and an administrative center oriented toward the sea rather than toward the valley.

The site selection repays attention because it was extremely well judged, and the reasoning can be reconstructed from the geography. The Nile mouths themselves were unsuitable for a major port because the river deposits silt continuously, so a harbor built on a distributary would fill. The chosen location sat clear of that problem on a limestone spur, west of the main Delta outflow.

The offshore island provided the essential feature: a natural breakwater that, once connected to the mainland by a causeway, created two protected harbors, one on each side, giving shelter in different wind conditions. Behind the coastal strip lay a large freshwater lake connected to the Nile system, which solved the water supply and provided a route for river traffic to reach the city without going out to sea. The strip between sea and lake was healthy and defensible, narrow enough to fortify and wide enough to build a large city on a regular plan.

The result was a port that could handle Egypt's grain export at scale, receive Mediterranean shipping, supply itself with fresh water, and connect by canal to the Nile that produced the wealth. Whether Alexander himself worked all of this out or whether his engineers did, the choice was one of the best pieces of urban siting in the ancient world, and the city's subsequent history is largely a working out of the advantages built into that location.

What Alexandria became, its institutions and its extraordinary intellectual output, belongs to [the article on the Library and Lighthouse of Alexandria](/2007/10/01/library-lighthouse-of-alexandria/) and to the wider treatment in [the era pillar on Ptolemaic Egypt](/2007/07/01/ptolemaic-egypt-complete-guide/). Alexander himself never saw a built city there. He gave the order, laid out the plan, and left, and the construction happened under his successors.

## The Journey to Siwa

From the new foundation Alexander went west along the coast and then turned inland into the desert, to the oasis of Siwa, where an oracle of the god the Greeks called Ammon and the Egyptians called Amun had a reputation reaching across the Greek world.

The journey was long, difficult, and strategically pointless, which is precisely why it is interesting. Siwa lies deep in the Western Desert, far from any route Alexander needed to travel and far from any objective his campaign required. He took a small party, spent weeks on the round trip, and did it at a moment when the Persian king was rebuilding an army in Mesopotamia. Whatever drove the expedition, it was not military necessity.

The ancient accounts wrap the crossing in wonders. They describe the party losing its way and being saved by unseasonal rain, and being guided onward by ravens or by serpents that went before them. These elements are standard in the traditions that accumulated around Alexander and should be treated as narrative decoration rather than report. What they preserve reliably is that the journey was hard and that it was remembered as extraordinary.

### Did Alexander visit the oracle at Siwa?

Yes. Every major surviving account of his campaign records the journey to the oasis of Siwa in the Western Desert to consult the oracle of Amun, whom Greeks identified with Zeus. The trip took place after the founding of Alexandria and before he left Egypt in the spring of 331 BCE.

The oasis itself was an Egyptian religious center with a temple of Amun, sitting at the edge of the Libyan world and connected to Greek religious geography through the Greek colony at Cyrene, whose people consulted the oracle and had spread its reputation. This is the key to understanding the visit: Siwa was simultaneously an Egyptian sanctuary and an oracle Greeks already respected, which made it uniquely capable of delivering a message that would carry weight in both worlds at once.

## What the Oracle Said and Did Not Say

The single most important fact about the Siwa consultation is that we do not know what happened inside the sanctuary, and the ancient sources say so.

Alexander went in alone, or nearly so, and the substance of what he asked and what he was told was not published. He reportedly said afterward that he had heard what pleased him, or words to that effect, and that he would tell his mother alone when he returned to Macedonia. He never returned to Macedonia.

What did circulate was the greeting. Tradition holds that the priest addressed him as a son of the god, and this became the foundation of Alexander's claim to divine sonship. One recurring explanation in later sources is that the priest, speaking Greek imperfectly, produced a form of address that sounded like son of Zeus rather than the intended affectionate form. Whether that story is a genuine memory, a rationalizing invention by writers uncomfortable with the divine claim, or an anecdote generated by the ambiguity of the situation cannot be determined, and readers should be wary of accounts that pick one option and state it as fact.

Two reported questions circulated afterward. One concerned whether all those responsible for his father's murder had been punished, which if genuine is a striking thing to ask an oracle in the middle of a war of conquest. The other concerned whether he was destined to rule the whole world. Neither is securely attested and both may be later constructions built around what people assumed he would ask.

The honest position is that the content is unrecoverable and the effect is documented. Alexander behaved from that point onward as a man whose divine sonship had been confirmed, allowed the claim to circulate, and it became a fixture of his self-presentation and a source of considerable friction with his Macedonian officers.

### Why did Alexander want to be called a son of Amun?

Because the claim worked in two registers simultaneously. Egyptian kingship already defined the pharaoh as the son of Amun, so the title made him a legitimate Egyptian king in Egyptian terms. In Greek terms, divine descent placed him alongside the heroes of myth and supported a claim to authority beyond ordinary Macedonian kingship.

That dual function is the point and it is why the visit matters. An Egyptian hearing that the ruler was the son of Amun was hearing standard pharaonic theology, unremarkable and reassuring, exactly what any legitimate king would be. A Greek hearing that Alexander was the son of Zeus, since Greeks identified Ammon with Zeus, was hearing something extraordinary and slightly scandalous, a living man claiming descent from the chief god.

The move therefore delivered Egyptian legitimacy at no cost while simultaneously advancing a Greek claim that Alexander wanted for his own purposes, principally the elevation of his authority over Macedonians who regarded their king as first among equals rather than as a superior being. The friction that claim generated among his officers is a substantial theme of the rest of his reign.

## Why Siwa Mattered

Setting aside the theology, the Siwa episode had three concrete consequences.

The first was Egyptian. It completed the legitimation sequence by attaching divine sonship to a ruler who had already sacrificed at Memphis and taken pharaonic titles. Egyptian priestly institutions could now record him as a proper king by every criterion their tradition applied.

The second was Greek and Macedonian. The divine claim gave Alexander a basis for demanding honors that ordinary Macedonian kingship did not support, and it fed directly into the later disputes over ceremonial and status that troubled his court. This was the aspect his own officers found hardest, and it is the reason the Siwa journey is treated in Greek sources with a mixture of fascination and discomfort.

The third was posthumous and geographic. Some accounts hold that Alexander expressed a wish to be buried at Siwa, in the sanctuary of the god who had acknowledged him. Whether or not that is genuine, the claim mattered later, because the disposal of his body became a political contest and any recorded preference for burial in Egypt strengthened the hand of whoever controlled Egypt.

## How Alexander Organized Egypt

Before leaving, Alexander made administrative arrangements that are more revealing than they first appear.

He did not appoint a single governor. Instead he divided authority among several people: civil administration in Egyptian hands, with Egyptians appointed over the traditional regional divisions of the country; military command over the garrisons in Macedonian and Greek hands; naval command separately; and financial administration, including the collection of tribute, in the hands of a Greek from the long-established Greek trading community in the Delta.

The reasoning, according to the ancient account that preserves it, was that Egypt was too strong and too self-contained to be entrusted to one man. A single governor of Egypt would control an agricultural surplus, a defensible territory, and a population large enough to make him independent. Splitting the offices meant no individual held enough to rebel with.

Two features of this arrangement deserve emphasis. First, Egyptians were appointed to civil authority, which is a striking gesture toward the population and consistent with the whole legitimation program. Second, the division did not survive contact with reality. The financial officer accumulated power well beyond his brief, effectively governing Egypt and acquiring a reputation for extortion, particularly in the manipulation of the grain trade. He was eventually executed once the province came under firm control after Alexander's death.

### How did Alexander govern Egypt after taking it?

He deliberately divided authority rather than appointing a single governor, placing civil administration under Egyptian officials, garrisons under Macedonian commanders, the fleet under a separate officer, and finance under a Greek from the Delta. The stated reason was that Egypt was too powerful to entrust to one man.

The failure of the arrangement is instructive about Egypt itself. Even a system designed specifically to prevent concentration produced a dominant figure within a few years, because the financial office controlled the flow of grain and money and that was where real power in Egypt sat. Whoever controlled the surplus controlled the province, a lesson every subsequent regime in Egypt learned or relearned.

Alexander left Egypt in the spring of 331 BCE, marching back through the Levant and then east into Mesopotamia toward the battle at Gaugamela that would break Persian power for good. He had been in the country roughly six months.

## The Death in Babylon and the Fight for the Body

Alexander died at Babylon in 323 BCE, aged thirty-two, without a competent adult heir. The empire he had assembled in a decade began coming apart within days, and Egypt's future was decided in that unraveling.

In the settlement among his senior officers, Ptolemy son of Lagus took Egypt as his province. That choice, in a division where other officers took territories closer to the imperial center, looks in retrospect like the shrewdest decision any of them made, and the reasoning behind it belongs to [the account of Ptolemy I and the founding of a dynasty](/2007/09/01/ptolemy-i-founding-of-dynasty/).

The body became a political object immediately. Preparations for transporting it were elaborate, involving a specially constructed funeral carriage that took a long time to build and attracted considerable description in the sources. The intention of the central authority was for the body to go to Macedonia, to the traditional royal burial place, which would have anchored the legitimacy of whoever controlled Macedonia.

Ptolemy intercepted it. The cortege was diverted to Egypt, and the body was installed initially at Memphis. This was an act of open defiance against the regent governing in the name of Alexander's heirs, and it contributed directly to the war that followed and to the invasion of Egypt that Ptolemy successfully repelled.

### Why did Ptolemy steal Alexander's body?

Because possession of the founder's remains was a legitimacy asset of the first order. A ruler who held Alexander's body could present himself as the true heir of Alexander's project, host the cult that grew around the burial, and anchor his own dynasty in the prestige of the most famous man in the world.

The calculation worked. The body was later moved from Memphis to Alexandria, where it was housed in a tomb that became one of the most visited monuments in the ancient world. Roman commanders and emperors made a point of viewing it, and the association between Alexander and the city he founded became one of the pillars of Ptolemaic self-presentation.

## The Tomb Nobody Has Found

The burial disappears from the historical record in late antiquity, and its location has been unknown ever since.

References to the tomb in Alexandria continue for centuries after the Ptolemaic period, with a series of Roman visitors recorded as viewing the body. Then the mentions stop. No source describes its destruction, its removal, or its concealment, which is unusual for a monument of that prominence and has left the question wide open.

The candidate explanations are all plausible and none is demonstrated. The city suffered severe damage in periods of civil disturbance, earthquake, and warfare, and the tomb may simply have been destroyed with much else. Alexandria's ancient coastline has changed substantially, with parts of the ancient city now under water, so the site may be submerged. The burial may have been quietly relocated or dismantled during periods when a pagan royal cult became politically awkward. And the ordinary process by which prominent buildings are quarried for materials over centuries accounts for the disappearance of many ancient structures without any dramatic event.

Numerous searches have been conducted and numerous locations proposed, none confirmed. The honest statement is that the tomb of Alexander is lost, that no credible claim to have found it has been substantiated, and that any account presenting a specific location as identified is going well beyond the evidence.

## The Nectanebo Legend

One Egyptian tradition about Alexander deserves attention precisely because it is false, since it shows Egyptians doing something interesting with the conquest.

A story that circulated widely in the later legendary tradition holds that Alexander was not the son of Philip of Macedon at all, but the son of Nectanebo II, the last native Egyptian pharaoh, who fled Egypt when Persia reconquered it, made his way to Macedonia in disguise as a magician, and fathered Alexander on the Macedonian queen.

This is fiction and no serious account treats it otherwise. Its interest lies in what it accomplishes. If Alexander was the son of the last native pharaoh, then his arrival was not a foreign conquest but the return of the legitimate Egyptian royal line, and Greek rule in Egypt becomes a restoration of native kingship rather than a substitution for it.

That is an Egyptian solution to an Egyptian problem, and it tells us that the legitimacy question mattered enough to Egyptians for elaborate narrative work to be done on it. It also warns against a lazy reading of the welcomed-conqueror thesis. Egyptians welcomed the end of Persian rule, and the existence of this legend suggests that some Egyptians nonetheless needed a story to explain why a Macedonian king was an acceptable pharaoh. Welcome is not the same as full acceptance, and the tradition preserves the gap between them.

## The Sources and How Much We Can Trust Them

Everything above rests on evidence of uneven quality, and a reader who wants to argue about this episode needs to know where the weak points are.

No contemporary narrative account survives. The surviving Greek and Roman histories of Alexander were written between roughly three and five centuries after the events, by authors working from earlier accounts that are now lost. The most respected of them drew principally on two participants: one of Alexander's senior officers and an engineer or technical officer who accompanied the expedition. Others drew on a wider and more colorful pool, including a court historian executed during the campaign whose work supplied much of the material about divine sonship.

That transmission chain has a specific and important consequence for Egypt. One of the two primary sources behind the most reliable surviving account was Ptolemy himself, the man who subsequently took Egypt, founded a dynasty there, stole Alexander's body, and built his own legitimacy on association with him. He is therefore an eyewitness of high value and an interested party of the first order on precisely the Egyptian material.

Anything in the tradition that emphasizes Alexander's attachment to Egypt, his desire to be buried there, the propriety of his Egyptian arrangements, or Ptolemy's own prominence during the Egyptian episode should be read with that interest in view. This does not mean the material is false. It means the incentive structure ran in one direction and that independent confirmation carries extra weight where it exists.

The other sources have their own distortions. One surviving account is heavily rhetorical, shaped by the literary conventions of its time and interested in moral characterization. Another compresses and simplifies. A biographical treatment organizes material around character rather than chronology and preserves anecdotes for what they reveal about temperament. None is a neutral record.

Then there is the legendary tradition, an enormously popular body of storytelling that grew around Alexander and circulated for well over a thousand years in many languages. It contains the Nectanebo story and a great deal else, and parts of it appear to have taken shape in Egypt. It is worthless as history and invaluable as evidence of how Alexander was imagined, which is a different and genuinely interesting subject.

### What are the best sources for Alexander in Egypt?

The surviving Greek and Roman histories, written three to five centuries later from lost eyewitness accounts, supplemented by Egyptian evidence including pharaonic titulary, temple work in his name, and documents dated by his regnal years. The most reliable narrative source drew partly on Ptolemy, who was an eyewitness with strong motives regarding Egypt.

The Egyptian evidence is the corrective, and it is the part general accounts underuse. Egyptian material is contemporary, produced in Egypt, and untouched by the Greek narrative tradition. It shows Alexander bearing a proper pharaonic titulary, receiving construction work in his name at major temple sites, and being used as the dating reference in Egyptian documents. That last point is quietly decisive: Egyptian scribes dating a contract by the regnal year of Alexander were treating him as the king of Egypt in the ordinary administrative sense, and they continued the practice for his nominal successors after his death.

## The Egyptian Evidence in Detail

Because it carries so much weight, the Egyptian side of the record deserves setting out on its own terms.

The most substantial physical survival is a structure built in Alexander's name within one of the great southern temple complexes, a shrine designed to house the sacred barque and decorated in fully traditional Egyptian style, with the king depicted making offerings to Egyptian gods in the standard compositional formulae. Work in his name is also attested elsewhere. The scale is modest compared with what the Ptolemies later undertook, which is unsurprising for a ruler who was in the country for six months, but the style is entirely conventional and the message unambiguous.

His royal titulary follows the traditional five-part pharaonic pattern, meaning Egyptian priestly scholarship composed a proper set of names for him in the way it would for any legitimate king. That is not something done for a foreign occupier being tolerated. It is the formal act by which the Egyptian religious establishment classified a ruler as a pharaoh.

Administrative dating practice completes the picture. Documents were dated by regnal years of Alexander, and after his death by the regnal years of his nominal successors, his half-brother and then his posthumous son. The Egyptian bureaucratic machine simply continued its normal operation with new names at the top, which is the strongest available evidence that the transition was experienced as a change of ruler rather than as a catastrophe.

A later inscribed stele, set up under Ptolemy before he took the royal title, refers back to this period and mentions Alexander's son as king while recording the restoration of temple lands. It is a Ptolemaic legitimation document and must be read as such, but it confirms that the sequence of recognized kingship ran through Alexander and his heirs without a break in Egyptian eyes.

## Comparing the Two Conquests

Setting Alexander's takeover beside the Persian conquest two centuries earlier isolates exactly what made the difference, and the comparison is the clearest way to test the welcomed-conqueror thesis.

Cambyses had to fight. He faced a functioning Egyptian state under a native king, with an army, a fleet, and a population that identified the defense of Egypt with the defense of itself. The campaign involved a major battle at the eastern frontier and the reduction of the country by force. What followed was an occupation of a defeated people, complete with a garrison presence, a hostile tradition about the conqueror's conduct, and grievance that persisted for generations.

Alexander did not have to fight, because the state defending Egypt was itself a foreign occupation with no army left and no local support. He inherited rather than conquered.

The legitimation efforts also differed in a way that matters. Persian kings did make gestures: an Egyptian titulary was composed for Cambyses, temple support was extended under Darius, and Persian rulers appeared in pharaonic form. But the Great King lived at the imperial center and visited Egypt rarely or never, so the ritual functions of Egyptian kingship went unperformed. Alexander was physically present, sacrificed in person to the Egyptian gods at Memphis, and went in person to the oracle. Presence was the variable Persian rule could not supply, and it is the reason the same set of gestures produced a different result.

The outcomes diverged accordingly. Persian rule generated revolt at almost every imperial succession across a century and a half and was eventually thrown off. Macedonian rule and its Ptolemaic continuation faced no comparable Egyptian rising for roughly a century, and when serious revolt did come it was driven by later grievances rather than by the manner of the original takeover.

### Was Alexander's conquest really different from the Persian one?

Fundamentally. The Persians fought an Egyptian state and occupied a defeated population; Alexander received the surrender of a Persian administration from a population that wanted it gone. He then performed the Egyptian royal role in person, which no Persian king ever did, and that combination produced a legitimacy Persian rule never achieved.

## Why It Was Bloodless: The Four Conditions

The absence of fighting was not luck, and four conditions had to hold simultaneously for it to happen.

The first was that the defending power was itself an occupier. Egyptians had no reason to fight for Persian control of Egypt, and Persian officials had no local reserve of loyalty to call on.

The second was that Persian military capacity had already been destroyed elsewhere. The field army was lost at Issus the previous year, taking the satrap of Egypt with it, so the province had nothing to defend itself with even if it had wanted to. A conquest is bloodless when one side cannot fight rather than when it chooses not to.

The third was the recency of Egyptian independence. Sixty years of native rule had ended within living memory, and the reconquest that ended it was fresh enough to be resented directly rather than inherited as historical grievance. Had Persian rule been continuous and long established, Egyptian institutions might have accommodated to it more thoroughly and the arrival of a third-party conqueror would have looked less like liberation.

The fourth was deterrence. Alexander arrived in Egypt directly from the reduction of Tyre and Gaza, operations that had been conducted with a severity the whole eastern Mediterranean had noted. Any Persian officer contemplating resistance knew what resistance had recently cost. Surrender was the rational choice, and Alexander's prior conduct is part of why it was.

Remove any one of these and the episode looks different. That is worth stating because the welcomed-conqueror thesis can be over-read into a claim that Egyptians simply loved Alexander. They did not know him. What they knew was that the alternative to him was the administration that had crushed their independence a decade earlier, and that resistance was futile in any case.

## What Alexander Did Not Do

A corrective section is necessary because popular accounts routinely credit Alexander with the achievements of the dynasty that followed him.

He did not build Alexandria. He chose the site, ordered the foundation, and had a plan laid out. Everything that made the city famous, the harbor works, the great lighthouse, the palace quarter, the research institution and its library, was constructed after his death by rulers who spent decades on it.

He did not create the Ptolemaic administrative system. The monopolies, the land categories, the tax machinery, the settler landholding class, and the dense bureaucracy that make the period so well documented were built over the following century, and the arrangement he actually left behind failed within a few years.

He did not settle Greeks in the Egyptian countryside. The cleruch system that planted Greek and Macedonian military colonists across the Faiyum and elsewhere, transforming the demography of whole regions, was a Ptolemaic program.

He did not build extensively for the Egyptian gods. His construction in Egypt was modest, and the enormous temple building that characterizes the Greek period at Edfu, Dendera, Philae, and elsewhere came later, funded by kings who needed the priesthood in ways Alexander, passing through, did not.

He did not restore native Egyptian rule, despite arriving as the liberator from Persia. Egypt passed from one foreign administration to another, and no native Egyptian would rule Egypt again for a very long time, a point frequently made in accounts of the modern period.

What he did was establish the conditions under which all of the above became possible: a country taken without destruction, a precedent for foreign-but-legitimate kingship, a site for a capital, and, through the manner of his death, an opportunity for one of his officers to take Egypt and keep it.

## What Actually Changed

The conquest's consequences separate into the immediate, the medium-term, and the very long, and distinguishing them prevents the usual overstatement.

The immediate change was the end of Persian rule in Egypt, permanently. Persia never recovered the province, and the Achaemenid Empire itself was destroyed within a decade. For Egyptians the specific administration they resented simply ceased.

The medium-term change was the reorientation of Egypt toward the Mediterranean. For three thousand years Egyptian civilization had faced along the Nile, with its capitals in the valley or at the valley's head, and its external relations running principally to the Levant and Nubia. The foundation of a coastal capital, ultimately, turned the country outward toward the sea. Egyptian grain fed Mediterranean cities, Egyptian ports handled Mediterranean shipping, and the Egyptian government operated from the coast. That reorientation outlasted the Ptolemies and shaped Roman Egypt and much of what followed.

The medium-term change also included language and status. Greek became the language of government, and it remained so for centuries, long after the Greek dynasty had gone. A hierarchy in which Greek status conferred legal and fiscal advantages was established and endured. Egyptian institutions, law, religion, and language continued underneath, producing the dual society examined in [the era pillar on Ptolemaic Egypt](/2007/07/01/ptolemaic-egypt-complete-guide/).

The very long change was the end of native rule as a live political possibility. Egypt had been conquered before and had recovered its independence under native kings, most recently within the lifetime of people alive in 332 BCE. After Alexander, that pattern stopped. Greek rule gave way to Roman, and the sequence of foreign administrations continued for a very long time. Whether that discontinuity was caused by Alexander or merely began with him is arguable, since the decisive factor was arguably the emergence of Mediterranean powers too large for Egypt to resist. But the break in the pattern dates from his arrival.

### Why does Alexander's conquest of Egypt matter?

It ended Persian rule permanently, founded the city that became the Mediterranean's intellectual capital, established the precedent of a legitimate foreign pharaoh, and created the succession vacuum that put the Ptolemies on the Egyptian throne for three centuries. It also began the long period during which Egypt was ruled from outside its own native tradition.
## The Chronology Problems

The six months are usually narrated as a tidy sequence, and the sources are less tidy than that. A reader working seriously with this episode should know where the joins are.

The order of the two most famous events is disputed. Some accounts place the foundation of Alexandria first and the journey to Siwa afterward; others reverse them, with Alexander going to the oracle and founding the city on his return along the coast. The difference is not trivial for interpretation. If the city came first, the foundation was an act of strategic urban planning that the oracle then blessed. If Siwa came first, the foundation can be read as an expression of the confidence the consultation produced. The evidence does not settle it, and honest accounts either present the standard order with a note or acknowledge the alternative.

Precise dates within the Egyptian months are also uncertain. Ancient chronologies for this campaign are reconstructed from a combination of sources using different calendars, and the conversions carry ambiguity. Statements assigning a specific day to the foundation of Alexandria should be treated as one reconstruction among several rather than as an established fact.

The duration of the stay is reasonably secure at roughly six months, from arrival in the autumn of 332 BCE to departure in the spring of 331 BCE, because it is bounded at both ends by dated events elsewhere in the campaign. Within that window, how long the Siwa journey took, how long he remained at Memphis, and how much time went into the administrative settlement are all estimates.

None of this undermines the outline. The conquest, the sacrifice at Memphis, the foundation, the oracle, the administrative division, and the departure all happened, in that six-month window, and no source disputes any of them. What is uncertain is sequence and detail, which is the normal condition of ancient campaign history and worth stating rather than smoothing over.

## Alexander and the Egyptian Priesthood

The relationship between the conqueror and Egypt's religious establishment is the thread running through every part of this episode, and it deserves a section because it set the pattern for three hundred years.

The Egyptian priesthood in 332 BCE was the only institution capable of speaking for Egyptians nationally. Temples held estates, employed large staffs, functioned as local economic centers, and maintained the scholarly and ritual traditions that defined Egyptian identity. A conqueror who secured priestly cooperation had access to the country's social infrastructure; one who did not was reduced to governing through garrisons.

Alexander secured it quickly and cheaply. The sacrifice to Apis was the opening move. The acceptance of a pharaonic titulary was the formalization. Construction in his name at a major temple, however modest in scale, was the demonstration that royal patronage would continue. The Siwa journey, to an Egyptian sanctuary of Amun, added divine sonship in exactly the terms Egyptian royal theology used. And the appointment of Egyptians to civil administrative office signaled that the new order would work through Egyptian personnel.

Notice how little this cost. No large sum was spent, no significant policy was conceded, and no limitation was placed on Macedonian authority. The priesthood's price for legitimating a foreign king was performance of a small set of specific acts, which is a striking fact about how Egyptian legitimacy worked and one that Persian kings, ruling from a distance, never took advantage of.

The pattern established here became Ptolemaic doctrine. Every subsequent Greek king of Egypt performed the pharaonic role, endowed temples, and dealt with the priesthood as a partner requiring cultivation. The relationship eventually became formalized in the assemblies of priests that issued decrees recording royal benefactions in exchange for cult honors, an arrangement whose mechanics belong to the Ptolemaic pillar. Its origin lies in what Alexander did at Memphis within days of arriving.

## The Naval Dimension

The maritime logic that brought Alexander to Egypt also explains the city he founded there, and connecting the two clarifies both.

Persian sea power in the eastern Mediterranean rested on Phoenician and Egyptian ships, crews, and harbors. Alexander could not match it at sea and did not try. His solution was to walk along the coast taking the bases, which stranded the fleet without ports, supplies, or a purpose. By the time he reached Egypt the strategy had largely worked, and taking Egypt completed it.

The founding of a new port then answered the obvious follow-on question: if the eastern Mediterranean was now Macedonian, what infrastructure did it need? Egypt had no first-class Mediterranean harbor. Its ancient ports were on Nile branches, subject to silting and oriented to river traffic, and the country's maritime trade had long been conducted through the older Greek trading settlement in the Delta, which was a river port rather than a sea port.

A purpose-built deep-water harbor on a stable stretch of coast, connected to the Nile by canal, converted Egypt from a river civilization that traded by sea into a maritime power with an agricultural interior. Tradition holds that Alexander was struck by the island offshore, recalling a passage in Homer that mentions it, which if genuine suggests he approached the site with the Greek geographical imagination already engaged.

The consequence was that the strategic decision of 332 BCE and the urban decision of 331 BCE fit together. A commander who took a coastline to defeat a navy naturally thought about where the ports should be, and the site he chose served that function for the next two thousand years.

## What If Egypt Had Resisted?

Counterfactuals are worth running when they isolate a variable, and this one isolates the value of the bloodless takeover.

Suppose the Persian governor had chosen to fight, with what forces remained, holding the frontier fortress and the river approaches. Alexander would have won, since he had just reduced two much stronger fortified positions, but the campaign would have taken months and cost lives. Egyptian communities along the route would have been involved, willingly or not. The precedent set at Tyre and Gaza indicates what the consequences of prolonged resistance tended to be.

The downstream effects run in a clear direction. A country taken by storm would have required a garrison posture rather than a conciliatory one. The priesthood would have been dealing with a conqueror who had recently damaged Egyptian communities rather than with a liberator making sacrifices at Memphis. The legitimation sequence would have been more expensive and less credible. The administrative apparatus would have been disrupted rather than inherited intact.

Most consequentially, the Ptolemaic settlement would have started from a different place. The arrangement that let a Greek dynasty rule Egypt for three centuries depended on Egyptian institutions surviving intact and on the absence of a foundational grievance. Ptolemy inherited a country that had not been fought over, whose temples were undamaged, whose officials were in post, and whose population had no massacre to remember. A dynasty beginning after a destructive conquest would have faced the problem Persian rule faced, and the revolt record might have looked very different.

That is why the absence of a battle is the central fact of the episode rather than an incidental detail, and it is the strongest support for the welcomed-conqueror thesis. The thing that did not happen shaped the next three hundred years more than most of the things that did.

## How Alexander Has Been Remembered

Few historical figures have generated a comparable afterlife, and Egypt is central to it in ways worth noting.

The legendary tradition that grew around Alexander became one of the most widely diffused bodies of narrative in world literature, circulating in numerous languages across Europe, the Middle East, Africa, and Asia for well over a millennium. Parts of it appear to have taken shape in Egypt, and the Nectanebo story that makes him an Egyptian prince by birth is among its most distinctive elements. Egypt is therefore not merely a stage in the Alexander story but one of the places where the story was made.

In the Greek and Roman world his Egyptian tomb became a site of pilgrimage for the powerful, and visiting it functioned as a claim on his legacy. That practice attached Alexander's memory permanently to Alexandria, which is why the loss of the tomb feels like a larger absence than the disappearance of most ancient monuments.

In later Middle Eastern and Islamic tradition he appears under an Arabic form of his name and became the subject of extensive legendary and moralizing literature. A long-standing interpretive tradition has identified him with a figure appearing in scripture, an identification that has been argued for and against by scholars and religious commentators over many centuries and remains debated rather than settled. The relevant point for this article is the reach: Alexander's memory travelled far beyond the Greek world and was reworked by every tradition that received it.

Modern Egyptian and popular treatment tends to focus on Alexandria and on the missing tomb, and the search for the burial has attracted repeated expeditions and periodic announcements. One excavation at Siwa in the modern era generated claims of discovery that were examined and rejected by Egyptian authorities. Similar claims elsewhere have not been substantiated. The pattern is worth flagging because it recurs: a highly desirable discovery generates announcements at a rate unconnected to the underlying evidence.

## Common Misconceptions

Six errors recur constantly in writing on this episode, and naming them is the most practically useful thing this article can do for a reader.

The first is that Alexander conquered Egypt in battle. He did not. The Persian governor surrendered, and there is no evidence of any significant fighting in Egypt during the takeover.

The second is that Alexander built Alexandria. He founded it, meaning he chose the site and ordered the plan. Every structure that made the city famous was built after his death.

The third is that a documented Egyptian coronation took place at Memphis. The Greek narrative sources describe sacrifice and games and do not describe a coronation, while legendary tradition asserts one. He was certainly recognized as pharaoh; whether a ceremony occurred is unresolved.

The fourth is that we know what the oracle at Siwa told him. We do not. He entered alone, the content was not published, and the reported questions and answers in later sources are reconstructions or inventions.

The fifth is that Alexander's tomb has been located. It has not. Numerous searches have been conducted and numerous claims made, none substantiated.

The sixth is that Alexander ruled Egypt for a substantial period. He was in the country for roughly six months and never returned. Everything associated with Greek rule in Egypt, other than the foundation decision and the legitimation precedent, was the work of the dynasty that followed.

## How to Argue This Episode

For an exam candidate, the highest-value framing is causal rather than narrative. Examiners reward an answer that explains why the conquest was bloodless and why that mattered, and penalize one that recounts the march from Gaza to Memphis. The four conditions set out above give a ready structure for the first half, and the four-move legitimation sequence gives one for the second.

For a teacher, the episode is an unusually clean case for teaching the difference between what happened and what it meant. Almost nothing dramatic occurred in Egypt in 332 BCE, and the consequences were enormous, which is exactly the point students find hardest and most valuable.

For a general reader, the correction worth carrying away is that the interesting question about Alexander in Egypt is not how he won but why he did not have to fight. Once that question is asked, the state of Persian rule, the recency of Egyptian independence, the destruction of the Persian army at Issus, and the deterrent effect of Tyre and Gaza all fall into place as an explanation, and the episode stops being a heroic set piece and becomes intelligible history.


## Pelusium: The Gate of Egypt

Both conquests of Egypt in this period began at the same place, and the geography explains why.

Egypt's eastern approach runs across northern Sinai, a stretch of desert with limited water where an army must follow a coastal route punctuated by wells. That route arrives at the eastern edge of the Delta, where the fortress town of Pelusium guarded the entrance. Any force coming from the Levant had to pass it, and any regime holding Egypt had to hold it.

The position had decided Egypt's fate before. Two centuries earlier the battle that opened Egypt to Persia was fought in the same area, an engagement covered in the account of how Persia conquered Egypt. The Saite state had fortified the eastern approach heavily, and the later native dynasties of the fourth century BCE built extensively there as well, since defending against Persian reconquest meant defending precisely that line.

What made 332 BCE different was that the fortifications were held by a governor with no field army. Fixed defenses without a mobile force to support them can delay an attacker but cannot defeat one, and a garrison commander facing a besieger who had just taken Tyre and Gaza had a straightforward calculation to make.

The wider point is that Egypt's famous defensibility was always conditional. Desert on both flanks and cataracts to the south make the country hard to invade, and that geography protected Egypt repeatedly across three thousand years. But the eastern land route was always open to a sufficiently determined army, and once a power capable of fielding one had emerged in the eastern Mediterranean, Egypt's isolation stopped being a reliable defense. The sequence of conquests from the seventh century BCE onward reflects that change more than any decline in Egyptian capability.

## The Six Months in the Wider Campaign

Placing the Egyptian episode inside Alexander's campaign as a whole clarifies what it was for.

Before Egypt, Alexander had won two major battles against Persian forces and spent most of a year reducing the Levantine coast. After Egypt, he marched east and fought the battle that destroyed the Persian field army for the last time and opened the imperial heartland to him. Egypt sits between those two phases, and the six months represent the only extended pause in the campaign's momentum during its most active years.

That pause bought several things. It completed the strategic objective of denying Persia its Mediterranean naval bases. It secured a revenue source of exceptional value. It gave the army rest and reinforcement after a punishing period of siege warfare. It allowed the political settlement of the conquered coastal territories to be organized. And it produced the divine sonship claim that Alexander carried into the rest of his career.

The cost was time, at a moment when the Persian king was rebuilding forces in Mesopotamia. That is a real strategic trade-off and it is worth noting that Alexander accepted it, which suggests he valued the Egyptian objectives highly rather than treating the detour as incidental.

The other cost was that Egypt received very little of his attention afterward. He never went back, the administrative arrangement he left behind decayed quickly, and Egypt spent the remaining nine years of his life as a distant province of an empire whose center of gravity had moved thousands of miles east. The country's importance to Alexander's story is concentrated entirely in those six months.

## Egypt Between His Death and Ptolemaic Kingship

The two decades between Alexander's death in 323 BCE and the establishment of the Ptolemaic monarchy are frequently skipped, and they contain the episode that made the dynasty possible.

Alexander left a half-brother of limited capacity and a posthumous son as nominal kings, with real power held by senior officers acting as regents and governors. Egyptian documents dated by the regnal years of these nominal rulers show the administrative machine continuing to treat the Macedonian royal house as the legitimate kingship of Egypt.

Ptolemy took Egypt as his province in the settlement after the death and moved quickly to consolidate it, executing the financial officer whose extortion had made him hated and who represented a rival center of power. He then extended his position westward and began operating as an effectively independent ruler while maintaining formal loyalty to the royal house.

The confrontation came when he diverted Alexander's funeral cortege to Egypt. The regent governing in the kings' name treated this as the challenge it was and invaded Egypt to remove him. The invasion failed. The attempt to force a crossing of the Nile in the Memphis region went badly wrong, with heavy losses in the river, and the regent was killed by his own officers in the aftermath. Ptolemy had defended Egypt successfully against the central authority of the empire, which established both his military credibility and the practical independence of his province.

From that point the trajectory ran one way. Ptolemy governed as satrap for roughly two more decades, took the royal title around 305 BCE once the fiction of a unified empire had collapsed entirely, and founded the dynasty examined in the account of Ptolemy I and the founding of a dynasty. Alexander's body remained in Egypt and became one of the foundations of the new monarchy's legitimacy.

### What happened to Egypt after Alexander died?

Ptolemy took it as his province in the division among Alexander's officers, consolidated control, executed the rapacious financial officer Alexander had left in place, diverted Alexander's body to Egypt, and defeated an invasion by the imperial regent sent to remove him. He took the royal title around 305 BCE and founded a dynasty that lasted three centuries.

## Reading the Welcomed-Conqueror Thesis Carefully

A thesis is more useful when its limits are stated, and this one has three.

The first limit is that welcome does not imply enthusiasm for Macedonian rule as such. Egyptians welcomed the removal of a specific recent occupation. Nothing in the evidence indicates that they wanted a Greek king rather than an Egyptian one, and the existence of the Nectanebo legend, which manufactures Egyptian ancestry for Alexander, suggests some Egyptians felt the need to make his kingship legitimate in native terms rather than simply accepting it.

The second limit is that the sources reporting the welcome are Greek, written centuries later, partly derived from a man who subsequently ruled Egypt and needed the takeover to look consensual. The Egyptian evidence corroborates recognition of Alexander as pharaoh, which is significant, but recognition by the priesthood and the bureaucracy is not the same thing as popular enthusiasm, and Egyptian institutions had strong practical reasons to recognize whoever held power.

The third limit is chronological. The absence of resistance in 332 BCE tells us about that moment, not about the following three centuries. Serious Egyptian revolt against Greek rule did eventually come, driven by later grievances, and reading the peaceful conquest as evidence of durable Egyptian consent to Hellenistic rule would be a straightforward error.

With those limits in place the thesis stands. Alexander took Egypt without fighting because the population had no reason to defend the incumbent regime and the incumbent regime had no capacity to defend itself, and the consequences of that bloodless transfer shaped the Ptolemaic period decisively. That is a claim about mechanism and consequence rather than about sentiment, and it is what the evidence supports.

## The Oracle of Siwa as an Institution

Siwa is usually treated in accounts of Alexander as an exotic destination, which obscures the fact that it was a functioning religious institution with a specific procedure and an established international reputation.

The oasis lies deep in the Western Desert, supported by springs, and its temple of Amun stood on a rock outcrop above the settlement. The oracle's method followed Egyptian practice: the god's image was carried in a portable barque by priests in procession, and questions were answered by the movements of the barque, interpreted by the attendant priesthood. This was a standard Egyptian oracular technique used at temples throughout the country, not an exotic local invention, and it means the consultation Alexander underwent had a recognized ritual form.

The oracle's reputation among Greeks was long established before 332 BCE. Greek settlers in Cyrenaica, to the northwest, consulted it regularly, and through them its standing spread into the Greek world, where the god was identified with Zeus and received cult recognition in Greek cities. A Greek approaching Siwa was therefore not visiting a foreign curiosity but consulting a shrine his own tradition already respected, which is exactly why the visit could carry weight with Greek audiences.

That dual standing is the practical explanation for Alexander's choice. Egypt contained many oracles and many temples of Amun, several far more accessible. Siwa was the one whose verdict would register simultaneously with Egyptian priests, who saw an Amun sanctuary confirming a pharaoh's divine sonship, and with Greeks, who saw an oracle of Zeus they already trusted acknowledging a living man as the god's son.

The difficulty of the journey may itself have been part of the value. A consultation reached after a hard desert crossing, accompanied by stories of divine guidance, generated a better narrative than one obtained conveniently, and Alexander's career throughout shows a consistent understanding of how such narratives worked.

## Alexander's Egypt in the Wider Hellenistic Pattern

Egypt looks less exceptional once it is set beside what Alexander did elsewhere, and the comparison sharpens the analysis rather than diminishing it.

City foundation was a standing policy. Alexander founded numerous cities across the conquered territories, many bearing his name, sited to control routes, anchor garrisons, and settle veterans. Alexandria in Egypt is the most successful of these by an enormous margin, but it was one instance of a program rather than a unique inspiration.

Religious accommodation was equally standard. On taking Babylon he sacrificed to the chief god of the city and ordered the restoration of its principal sanctuary, which is structurally identical to what he did at Memphis: a public act of respect toward the leading local cult, performed immediately on arrival, aimed at the priesthood that mediated between ruler and population. The Egyptian sequence was not a special affection for Egypt but the local application of a consistent method.

Administrative continuity was likewise a pattern. Alexander repeatedly retained existing officials and existing structures rather than replacing them wholesale, and he appointed local men to civil positions in several conquered regions while keeping military command in Macedonian hands. The Egyptian arrangement follows that template precisely.

Recognizing the pattern does two useful things. It removes the mystique that surrounds the Egyptian episode in popular treatment, and it strengthens the causal argument, because a method applied in many places with varying results allows the Egyptian outcome to be attributed to Egyptian conditions rather than to Alexander's unique relationship with Egypt.

What was genuinely distinctive about Egypt was the conjunction of factors: a rich and compact country, a discredited incumbent administration, a recently ended native independence, an institutional priesthood able to confer legitimacy, and a coastal site that solved a strategic problem. That conjunction existed nowhere else on the campaign, which is why the six months in Egypt produced consequences the other stops did not.

## The Question of Alexander's Intentions

What Alexander wanted from Egypt is the point where responsible history runs out and speculation begins, and it is worth marking the boundary.

The strategic intentions are recoverable because they follow from the campaign's logic: eliminate the last Persian naval base, secure a revenue source, and protect the rear before marching east. Nothing about that requires interpretation of his psychology.

The intentions behind the Siwa journey are not recoverable. The trip served no military purpose, the content of the consultation is unknown, and every account of his motives comes from writers working centuries later with strong views about his character. Explanations on offer include genuine religious conviction, calculated political theater, personal preoccupation with his own paternity, and imitation of the mythological heroes he was said to admire. Any of these is possible and the evidence cannot adjudicate among them.

The intentions behind the foundation of Alexandria sit between the two. The site selection is so well judged that deliberate strategic reasoning is the obvious explanation, and the tradition of his personal involvement in the layout is consistent with his practice elsewhere. Whether he foresaw anything like what the city became is a different question, and there is no reason to suppose he did.

The reason to mark these boundaries is that a great deal of popular writing on Alexander in Egypt consists of confident psychological claims: that he felt drawn to Egypt, that Siwa transformed him, that he foresaw Alexandria's destiny. These make good narrative and they are not history. What can be established is what he did, when, in what order, and with what consequences, and that is a substantial enough account without the interior monologue.

## Was Alexander Good for Egypt?

The evaluative question deserves a direct answer, and the honest one splits by timeframe and by whose interests are being counted.

In the immediate term the arrival was clearly beneficial by Egyptian lights. It removed a resented occupation without fighting, spared the country the destruction a contested conquest would have brought, restored the performance of royal ritual that Persian absentee rule had left undone, and put Egyptians into civil administrative office. Temples were respected, the priesthood was courted, and no community was punished. Compared with the reconquest of a decade earlier, this was an unambiguous improvement.

Over the medium term the assessment is genuinely mixed. Greek rule brought a period of great prosperity, enormous temple construction, and a capital that made Egypt central to the Mediterranean world, and Egyptian religion, law, and language continued throughout. It also established a status hierarchy in which Greek identity conferred legal and fiscal advantages that Egyptians did not hold, planted a settler landholding class in the countryside, and reserved the upper administration for a Greek-speaking elite. Egyptians who acquired Greek could advance; Egyptians who did not were structurally disadvantaged in their own country. Both halves of that are true and any account presenting only one is selling a position.

Over the long term the consequence was the end of native rule as a realistic prospect, which is the heaviest item on the ledger and the one Egyptian nationalist readings emphasize. Whether it belongs on Alexander's account is arguable, since the underlying cause was the emergence of powers Egypt could not match, but the discontinuity begins with him.

The most defensible summary is that Alexander's conquest was the best available version of a foreign takeover that was going to happen in some form, and that its manner mattered more than its fact. A country that is going to be ruled from outside is better off being taken by a commander who sacrifices to its gods than by one who sacks its cities. That is a real benefit and a limited one, and it is the honest answer to a question that usually receives either uncritical admiration or reflexive condemnation.

## The Verdict

Alexander's conquest of Egypt is the most consequential six months in the country's later ancient history, and its significance runs almost entirely through what did not happen.

There was no battle, so there was no destruction, no reprisal, and no foundational grievance. There was no dismantling of Egyptian institutions, so the administrative apparatus, the temples, and the priesthood passed intact to the new regime. There was no attempt to substitute Macedonian forms for Egyptian ones, so Egyptian kingship continued with a new occupant. And there was no prolonged presence, so the arrangements Alexander made were provisional and the real construction of Greek Egypt fell to others.

What he did contribute was decisive in three specific ways. He removed Persian rule permanently at a moment when Egyptians wanted it removed. He chose the site of the city that would reorient Egypt toward the Mediterranean for two millennia. And he demonstrated, through the four-move legitimation sequence, that a foreign ruler could be a legitimate pharaoh at surprisingly little cost, a lesson every Greek king of Egypt afterward applied.

The honest assessment of Alexander in Egypt is therefore that he was neither the transformative genius of popular treatment nor an incidental passer-through. He was a conqueror who arrived at exactly the right moment, made a small number of shrewd decisions, and left before any of them had to be implemented. The transformation belongs to the dynasty that followed. The conditions for it belong to him.

## The Greeks Who Were Already There

One reason the transition went as smoothly as it did is that Greek rule did not arrive among strangers.

Greeks had been living and working in Egypt for centuries before 332 BCE. A trading settlement in the Delta had operated as the recognized hub of Greek commerce with Egypt since the Saite period, with sanctuaries maintained by different Greek cities and a resident mixed community. Greek and Carian mercenaries had served Egyptian kings in large numbers, garrisoning frontier posts and forming the professional core of Egyptian armies. Greek traders, craftsmen, and travellers had moved through the country for generations, and a professional interpreter class existed to serve them. That long prior contact is set out in the account of [the Greek communities in Egypt before Alexander](/2007/01/01/greeks-in-egypt-before-alexander/).

The practical consequences for 332 BCE were substantial. There were Greeks in Egypt who could serve as intermediaries, and Alexander used one of them, appointing a Greek from the Delta trading community to the financial administration. There were Egyptians accustomed to dealing with Greeks and Greek-speakers, and institutions with experience of accommodating a resident foreign population. There was an established framework for foreigners operating inside Egyptian law and administration.

There was also an existing Greek understanding of Egypt, formed through that contact and transmitted in Greek writing, which meant Alexander and his officers arrived with expectations rather than in ignorance. Some of those expectations were sound and some were the accumulated distortions of outsider ethnography, examined in [the treatment of Herodotus and his account of Egypt](/2007/05/01/herodotus-account-of-egypt/), but the country was not an unknown quantity to them.

None of this made the conquest happen. It made the aftermath manageable, and it is part of why a Greek dynasty could establish itself so quickly in a country with a three-thousand-year civilization of its own.

## What Alexander Took From Egypt

Conquest economics deserve a mention because the campaign that followed was expensive and Egypt helped pay for it.

The surrender delivered the provincial treasury intact, which was the accumulated revenue of the richest satrapy of the Persian Empire. It also delivered the tax machinery, which continued operating under new direction, and the agricultural surplus that machinery collected. Alexander's arrangements for finance and tribute collection were among the first he made, which indicates the priority.

Egypt also supplied provisions. An army that had spent a year in siege operations needed feeding and rebuilding, and a country producing a reliable grain surplus was the ideal place to do it. The winter spent in Egypt was among other things a logistical recovery, and the force that marched east in the spring of 331 BCE was better supplied than the one that had arrived.

There is a longer-term dimension as well. The financial officer left in charge became notorious for manipulating the grain trade and extracting far beyond the assessed obligations, and the scale of what he accumulated is itself evidence of how much wealth was moving through the Egyptian system. When Ptolemy executed him and took control of that machinery, he acquired the revenue base that funded three centuries of Ptolemaic power.

So Egypt's contribution to Alexander's campaign was real but the accounting runs the other way over time. He took a treasury and a winter's provisioning. What he left behind, unintentionally, was an intact revenue-generating state that made one of his officers into a king.

## How the Foundation Was Remembered

The founding of Alexandria acquired a body of story almost immediately, and the stories are worth separating from the event.

The best known holds that Alexander marked out the city's boundaries himself, and that when the chalk ran short the outline was traced in barley meal, whereupon birds descended and consumed it. The seers, asked to interpret, declared it favorable: the city would nourish many peoples. Whether the episode happened or was constructed afterward to explain the city's later role as a grain hub is impossible to determine, and the second possibility should be taken seriously, since omen stories that precisely predict known outcomes are usually written backward.

Other traditions attach an architect to the layout and describe the regular street grid, the reservation of ground for temples including one to an Egyptian deity, and the personal attention Alexander gave to the plan. The regular grid is confirmed by what is known of the ancient city's form, and the practice of laying out new foundations on a planned orthogonal scheme was standard in the Greek world.

What is secure is the decision, the site, and the timing. What is decorative is the personal drama around it. Distinguishing the two is worth doing because the foundation stories have been repeated so often that they have acquired the feel of documented fact, and a reader who cites the birds and the barley as history is citing a good anecdote rather than evidence.

## The Persian Governor's Calculation

It is worth spending a moment on the losing side, because Mazaces made the decision that shaped everything and he is almost always reduced to a name in a sentence.

He inherited a province in the worst possible condition. His predecessor had taken Egyptian troops and resources to fight at Issus and had died there along with much of the Persian field army. The empire's remaining forces were regrouping far to the east under a king who had personally fled the battlefield. No relief could reach Egypt, and no fleet could operate in a Mediterranean whose ports Alexander had systematically taken.

Inside the province, the population had been reincorporated by force roughly a decade earlier and had already demonstrated across the previous two centuries that it would rebel whenever imperial authority looked weak. A Persian governor attempting to defend Egypt against Alexander would have been fighting on two fronts, one of them in his own territory.

He also had a recent precedent to consider. An attempt at native rebellion in Egypt had occurred not long before Alexander's arrival, indicating that Egyptian appetite for throwing off Persian control had not diminished, and that any concentration of Persian forces against an external threat would invite an internal rising.

Against all that stood the consequences of resisting and losing, which Tyre and Gaza had recently made vivid. Both cities had refused terms, both had held out for months behind formidable defenses, and both had been taken and dealt with in ways that the entire eastern Mediterranean had heard about within weeks. A governor weighing resistance in Egypt was not weighing an abstraction.

Seen from his position the surrender was not cowardice or treachery but the only defensible course, and stating it that way matters for the analysis. The bloodless conquest was not a gift from history. It was a rational decision by an official with no good options, made possible by conditions that had been created elsewhere, principally on the battlefield at Issus a year earlier and in the siege lines at Tyre. The welcomed-conqueror thesis depends on Egyptian attitudes, and it depends equally on the fact that the man holding Egypt had nothing to hold it with.

## Egypt's Place in the Sequence of Conquests

Stepping back, Alexander's takeover sits inside a long sequence that changed Egypt's position in the world, and seeing the sequence prevents the episode from being read in isolation.

From roughly the eighth century BCE onward, Egypt was repeatedly conquered by powers based outside it: Nubian, Assyrian, Persian, Macedonian, and eventually Roman. Between those conquests came native restorations, some of them vigorous, including the Saite revival and the last native dynasties of the fourth century BCE. The pattern for several centuries was conquest followed by recovery.

What ended the pattern was not Egyptian weakness in any simple sense. Egypt in 332 BCE was still enormously productive, densely populated, and culturally intact, and it had thrown off Persian rule within living memory. What changed was the scale of the powers around it. States capable of fielding armies drawn from vast territories emerged in the eastern Mediterranean and Near East, and against those Egypt's population and wealth, formidable by earlier standards, no longer produced a decisive advantage. The country could still be enormously valuable and could no longer be independently defensible.

Alexander's conquest is the hinge in that sequence. Before it, Egyptian independence was a live possibility that had repeatedly been realized. After it, foreign rule became continuous, and the wider Late Period arc that leads to this point is set out in the era pillar on the Late Period of ancient Egypt.

Reading the episode in that frame produces a more accurate assessment than either the heroic version or the dismissive one. Alexander did not break Egypt; the imbalance between Egypt and its neighbors had been growing for centuries and would have produced the same outcome in some form. What he did was arrive at the moment the imbalance became decisive, and handle the takeover in a way that determined the character of the three centuries that followed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Alexander the Great conquer Egypt?

He did not conquer it militarily. Arriving from Gaza in the autumn of 332 BCE, Alexander reached the frontier fortress of Pelusium and received the surrender of the Persian satrap Mazaces, who had no army capable of resistance after the destruction of the Persian field force at Issus the previous year, which had also killed his predecessor. Alexander proceeded up the Nile to Memphis without opposition and took possession of the province and its treasury. There was no battle in Egypt, no siege, and no recorded resistance from Egyptian communities, who had been forcibly reincorporated into the Persian Empire roughly a decade earlier and had no reason to defend it.

### Q: What year did Alexander take Egypt?

The autumn of 332 BCE. He arrived after completing the sieges of Tyre and Gaza, which occupied most of that year and secured the Levantine coast. He spent the winter in Egypt, founded Alexandria in early 331 BCE, journeyed to the oracle at Siwa, made administrative arrangements, and departed in the spring of 331 BCE for Mesopotamia, where he fought the battle at Gaugamela that October. The entire stay lasted roughly six months, and he never returned to Egypt. He died at Babylon in 323 BCE, after which his body was eventually brought back to Egypt and buried at Alexandria.

### Q: Why did Egyptians welcome Alexander?

Because he displaced an administration they resented and that had been imposed within living memory. Egypt had been independent under native pharaohs for roughly sixty years before Artaxerxes III reconquered it around 343 BCE, so the Persian presence in 332 BCE was recent rather than long established, and a generation was alive that remembered independence. Egyptians owed no loyalty to the Persian governor, whose military capacity had been destroyed at Issus. Alexander then reinforced the welcome deliberately, sacrificing publicly to the Egyptian gods including the Apis bull at Memphis, taking a pharaonic titulary, and appointing Egyptians to civil administrative posts.

### Q: Why did Alexander build a new capital in Egypt?

Because Egypt possessed no first-class Mediterranean harbor. Its existing ports lay on Nile branches that silted continuously and served river rather than sea traffic, which suited a civilization facing inward along the valley but not an empire operating across the eastern Mediterranean. The site he chose sat clear of the Nile outflow on a stable limestone coast, opposite an offshore island that could be linked by a causeway to create two sheltered harbors, backed by a freshwater lake connected to the Nile system. That combination solved the harbor problem, the water supply, and the connection to the interior in one location.

### Q: Did Alexander visit the oracle at Siwa?

Yes. After founding Alexandria he travelled west along the coast and then inland across the desert to the oasis of Siwa, where a temple of Amun housed an oracle with an established reputation among Greeks as well as Egyptians. Ancient accounts wrap the crossing in stories of unseasonal rain and animal guides, which are narrative decoration, but the journey itself is recorded in every major surviving account. He entered the sanctuary alone or nearly so, and the content of the consultation was never published. What circulated afterward was that the priest had greeted him as a son of the god.

### Q: How was Alexander received as pharaoh?

As a legitimate king by every criterion Egyptian tradition applied, though the ceremonial details are disputed. Egyptian evidence shows him bearing a proper five-part pharaonic titulary composed by Egyptian priestly scholarship, receiving construction work in his name in fully traditional style at a major temple complex, and serving as the dating reference in Egyptian administrative documents. Greek narrative sources describe sacrifice to the Egyptian gods and Greek athletic contests at Memphis but do not describe a formal Egyptian coronation, while later legendary tradition asserts one took place. The recognition is secure; whether a coronation ceremony occurred cannot be settled.

### Q: What happened to Egypt after Alexander died?

Ptolemy son of Lagus took Egypt as his province in the division of authority among Alexander's senior officers in 323 BCE. He consolidated rapidly, executing the Greek financial officer whose extortion had made him hated, and then diverted Alexander's funeral cortege to Egypt rather than allowing it to reach Macedonia. The imperial regent invaded to remove him and failed disastrously in an attempted Nile crossing, dying at the hands of his own officers. Ptolemy governed as satrap for two more decades and took the royal title around 305 BCE, founding a dynasty that ruled Egypt for three centuries.

### Q: Where is Alexander the Great buried?

In Alexandria, in a tomb whose location has been lost since late antiquity. His body was diverted to Egypt around 321 BCE and installed first at Memphis, then transferred to Alexandria, where the tomb became one of the most visited monuments of the ancient world and was viewed by a series of Roman commanders and emperors. References to it then stop, with no source describing its destruction or removal. Candidate explanations include destruction during periods of civil disturbance, earthquake, or warfare, submergence as the coastline changed, quiet removal when a pagan royal cult became awkward, and gradual quarrying. No claim to have found it has been substantiated.

### Q: Who surrendered Egypt to Alexander?

Mazaces, the Persian satrap of Egypt, who handed over the province and its treasury without resistance in the autumn of 332 BCE. His position was hopeless. His predecessor had been killed at the Battle of Issus in 333 BCE along with the Persian field army, leaving Egypt with no significant force to defend it, and the Egyptian population had no interest in fighting for Persian control. Alexander had just spent the year reducing Tyre and Gaza in operations whose severity was widely known, which made the cost of resistance obvious. Surrender was the rational choice and it was taken without a fight.

### Q: How long did Alexander stay in Egypt?

Roughly six months, from his arrival at Pelusium in the autumn of 332 BCE to his departure in the spring of 331 BCE. Within that window he received the surrender, travelled to Memphis and sacrificed to the Egyptian gods, held Greek contests, founded Alexandria, journeyed to the oracle at Siwa and back, and organized the administration of the province. The precise order of the foundation and the Siwa journey is disputed in the sources. He then marched back through the Levant toward Mesopotamia and never returned to Egypt, dying at Babylon nine years later.

### Q: Why did Alexander want to be called a son of Amun?

Because the claim functioned in two registers at once. Egyptian royal theology already defined the pharaoh as the son of Amun, so the title made him a legitimate Egyptian king in entirely conventional terms and cost him nothing with that audience. Greeks identified Amun with Zeus, so the same claim presented to Greek and Macedonian audiences asserted descent from the chief god, placing him alongside the heroes of myth and supporting authority beyond ordinary Macedonian kingship, where a king was traditionally first among equals rather than a superior being. That second implication generated lasting friction with his own officers.

### Q: What did Alexander do for Egyptian temples?

Less than the Ptolemies who followed, but enough to establish the pattern. He sacrificed publicly to the Egyptian gods at Memphis, singling out the Apis bull, whose cult Persian kings were accused in circulating tradition of having insulted. He accepted a pharaonic titulary and appeared in traditional royal iconography. Construction was carried out in his name at a major southern temple complex, including a shrine designed to house the sacred barque and decorated in fully conventional Egyptian style. The scale was modest, which is unsurprising for a six-month stay, but the message was unambiguous and every Greek king of Egypt afterward followed it.

### Q: How did Alexander organize the government of Egypt?

He deliberately refused to appoint a single governor, dividing authority instead among several people: Egyptian officials over the traditional civil administration of the country's regions, Macedonian commanders over the garrisons, a separate officer over the fleet, and a Greek from the long-established Delta trading community over finance and tribute. The stated reasoning was that Egypt was too strong and self-contained to entrust to one man, since whoever held it whole could make himself independent. The arrangement failed quickly. The financial officer accumulated power well beyond his brief, effectively governing the province and becoming notorious for extortion.

### Q: Did Alexander's conquest end Persian rule in Egypt permanently?

Yes. Persia never recovered Egypt, and the Achaemenid Empire itself was destroyed within a decade, its field army broken at Gaugamela in 331 BCE and its last king dead in 330 BCE. Egypt passed from Macedonian to Ptolemaic and then Roman rule without any Persian return. The deeper change was that native Egyptian rule also ended. Egypt had been conquered before and had recovered independence under native pharaohs, most recently within the lifetimes of people alive in 332 BCE, but that pattern stopped after Alexander, and Egypt was governed from outside its own native tradition for a very long period afterward.

### Q: What sources tell us about Alexander in Egypt?

Greek and Roman narrative histories written between three and five centuries later, drawing on lost eyewitness accounts, and Egyptian contemporary evidence. The most respected surviving narrative drew partly on Ptolemy, who was present, subsequently ruled Egypt, took Alexander's body, and built his own legitimacy on the association, so he is both a valuable witness and an interested party on precisely this material. Other accounts are rhetorical or biographical in emphasis. The Egyptian evidence, comprising pharaonic titulary, temple construction in his name, and documents dated by his regnal years, is contemporary and independent of the Greek tradition.

### Q: Why does Alexander's conquest of Egypt matter?

Because of what did not happen. No battle meant no destruction, no reprisals, and no founding grievance, so Egyptian institutions, temples, and administration passed intact to the new regime and the Ptolemies inherited a country with nothing to avenge. That absence shaped three centuries of Greek rule. Positively, the conquest ended Persian rule permanently at a moment Egyptians wanted it ended, produced the site selection for the city that reoriented Egypt toward the Mediterranean for two thousand years, and demonstrated that a foreign ruler could become a legitimate pharaoh through a small number of specific public acts.
