The plan to stop Khvicha Kvaratskhelia begins by refusing the single instruction every touchline in world football shouts when a dribbler receives the ball on the left: send help. Against this profile, help is the problem. The counter-plan here is a containment-by-angle scheme in which the near defender holds a fixed distance and a fixed body angle outside the range where the attacker's close control does its damage, the second defender drops rather than closes, and the team accepts a specific and deliberate concession in exchange. What you give up is the harmless first touch, the sideways carry, and a share of possession in the left channel that will look uncomfortable on the touchline and will read as passivity to anyone watching without the plan in their head.

That trade is the whole article. Most counter-plans against wide attackers are built to deny space, because most wide attackers convert space into danger. This one is built to deny proximity, because this attacker converts proximity into danger. Getting that inversion right is the difference between a defensive left side that holds and one that leaks two-versus-one situations in the final third every four minutes.

## The plan against Kvaratskhelia in one sentence

Hold the elimination radius, refuse the second body, and shrink the exit routes with depth rather than with numbers.

Every element of the plan that follows is an expansion of that sentence. The elimination radius is the distance inside which his close control can remove a defender with a single touch. Refusing the second body means the covering player never steps toward the ball while the first defender is still on his feet and still square. Shrinking exit routes with depth means the covering player takes an angle behind and inside the first defender rather than beside him, so that the inside cut runs into a body that is already positioned rather than a body that is arriving.

The plan is designed for a mid-block or a settled defensive shape. It is not a pressing scheme and it is not a plan for the first phase of a build-up. Those are different problems with different owners in this series, and the general vocabulary for wide attacking threats is set out in the cluster hub on [stopping elite wingers as a category](/2021/04/01/stopping-elite-wingers-complete-guide/), which this article assumes rather than repeats.

What the plan is not designed to do is win the ball. That distinction matters more here than in almost any wide duel, because the single most common way teams lose the left channel against this profile is by treating each individual engagement as a contest to be won rather than a situation to be survived. A defender who tries to take the ball is a defender who has committed weight, and a defender who has committed weight is a defender who has already lost the duel he thinks he is about to win. The plan asks for something psychologically harder than tackling: it asks for a full-back to stand at a specific distance, in a specific shape, for the length of a carry that will feel like an eternity, and to be content with the attacker still having the ball at the end of it.

### What is the fastest way to describe the counter-plan to a squad?

Tell them the near defender never dives in and never gets closer than roughly two metres, the covering player drops behind rather than steps across, and the midfield screens the pass inside rather than the man. Then tell them the cost: he will keep the ball, he will carry it sideways, and that is acceptable.

## Why the standard answer fails: the pressure paradox

The universal amateur and semi-professional instruction, and a fair proportion of the professional one, is to double up on a dangerous dribbler. Get bodies around him. Crowd him. Make the pitch small. Against a large majority of wide attackers, that instruction is sound, and this article is not written to reverse it into a new dogma. It is written to give a coach a discriminating rule, because against one particular profile the arithmetic runs backwards.

Here is the arithmetic. A wide attacker who wins by reaching top pace before a defender can turn is beaten by numbers, because a second defender positioned in his running lane removes the lane and the pace becomes irrelevant. Every body you add to that problem subtracts from his threat. Now take an attacker whose defining quality is control of the ball inside a very small radius, with the ability to change the ball's line by half a metre at a time and to shift his own weight faster than a defender can shift his. Every body you add to that problem is a body standing inside the radius. It is not a barrier. It is a target.

This is what I call the pressure paradox, and it is the namable claim of this article: against a close control dribbler, every extra defender you commit increases the number of players he can remove with one touch, so the correct response to a beaten defender is a deeper second line rather than a closer second body.

The paradox has a specific mechanism, and the mechanism is worth stating precisely because it is the reason the plan works. When a defender is eliminated in open play, two things happen at once. He is removed from the duel, which is obvious, and he is removed from the recovery structure, which is not. A defender who has been beaten while stationary at three metres can turn and recover into the covering line within a couple of strides. A defender who has been beaten while lunging at one metre is travelling in the wrong direction with his weight over the wrong foot, and he is out of the picture for the next four seconds. Those four seconds are the entire attacking phase.

So the number that matters is not how many defenders are near the ball. It is how many defenders can be removed from the recovery structure by a single action. Send one extra body inside the radius and you have not doubled your defensive resource in that zone. You have doubled the number of players who can be taken out of the picture by one touch, and you have done it in the part of the pitch where the consequence of being taken out of the picture is a shot.

### Does sending a second defender ever work against this profile?

Yes, but only from behind and only after the ball has moved. A second defender who arrives while the attacker still has settled control is a target. A second defender who arrives after a heavy touch, a change of direction, or a pass, is a genuine numerical advantage. The trigger is the ball's state, not the danger level.

The counter-reading deserves a fair hearing, because plenty of intelligent coaches will read the paragraphs above and say that they have watched sides crowd this attacker successfully. They have, and the successful versions almost always share a feature that gets lost in the description: the crowding happened after the ball had already been slowed, deflected, or turned backwards, and the defenders arrived from behind the ball rather than toward it. That is not doubling up in the sense the touchline means it. That is a recovery press on a settled situation, and it is a completely different action with a completely different risk profile.

The second counter-reading is broader and more interesting. There is a persistent belief, particularly among analysts who came to the game through data, that close control is a small-space skill with limited value in open play, on the grounds that elite defensive structures rarely allow the confined situations where it pays. The belief has a kernel of truth and a large error. The kernel is that a well-organized block does reduce the frequency of one-versus-one situations in dangerous areas, which is true of every attacking threat and is not specific to this one. The error is the assumption that a close control specialist needs a confined situation to be dangerous. He does not. He manufactures the confined situation, by attracting defenders into a radius they would not otherwise enter, and the manufacturing is the skill. A player who converts space into danger needs you to give him space. A player who converts pressure into danger needs only for you to be a normally coached defender doing the normally coached thing.

## The elimination radius, defined

The plan needs a number, or at least a range, because "do not get too close" is not a coaching instruction and no defender has ever executed it. So define the radius concretely.

The elimination radius is the distance from the attacker inside which he can reach the ball, move it, and re-establish control before a defender can reposition his feet. It is not a fixed figure across all attackers. It is a function of two things: how far the attacker can move the ball while keeping it inside his own reach, and how quickly he can change his own body's line of travel. For a close control specialist of this type, both numbers are unusually good, and the practical consequence is a radius of roughly one and a half to two metres in which a defender is effectively at his mercy.

Inside that radius, the defender's feet are the target. Not his position, not his balance, his feet. A touch that moves the ball forty centimetres across a defender's standing foot forces that defender either to reach, which unweights him, or to step, which turns his hips. Either response is an elimination. The attacker does not need to beat him with pace or with a trick that would look good in a compilation. He needs the defender to be close enough that a small movement of the ball produces a large movement of the defender.

Outside that radius, and this is the point the whole plan rests on, the same touch produces nothing. Move the ball forty centimetres when the defender is three metres away and the defender simply adjusts his angle without moving his weight. The touch has cost the attacker a fraction of a second and gained him nothing. He is still facing a defender who is balanced, who is goal-side, and who has not committed. The skill has not disappeared. Its target has.

### How far should a defender actually stand off?

Roughly two metres in the middle third, extending to two and a half when the attacker faces goal with momentum, and shortening only when the touchline and a covering player have already closed the exit routes. The working rule is that his front foot must never sit within reaching distance of the ball.

This is where the article has to be honest about difficulty, because standing off is easy to say and genuinely hard to do. The instinct to engage is not stupidity. It is a defender's correct and hard-earned reflex that a dangerous attacker facing your goal with the ball at his feet is a problem you should solve now rather than later. Every coaching environment a defender has ever passed through has reinforced it. Undoing it requires more than an instruction; it requires a reason the defender believes, which is why the elimination arithmetic above is worth explaining to players rather than just converting into a rule. A full-back who has been told "stand off" will step in under pressure at the first opportunity. A full-back who understands that his stepping in is what creates the two-versus-one behind him will hold.

The related craft, which is the body shape, the footwork, the recovery line, and the timing of the engagement window, is the technique layer beneath this plan and it belongs to the article on [defending an inverted winger one on one](/2021/08/15/defending-inverted-wingers-1v1/). This article decides what the team does. That one teaches the defender how to do his part of it. Read them together and the plan becomes coachable rather than merely correct.

This is the artifact to take into a meeting. It crosses the engagement distance with the number of defenders genuinely at risk from one action, the exit routes the attacker has available at that distance, the containment angle that closes them, and what you concede by holding it.

The table is not a set of instructions to memorize. It is a single argument rendered as rows: the number in the second column is what you are actually managing, and it is the only number in the plan that moves when a coach changes a decision. Every good outcome in the right-hand columns follows from keeping that number at one.

## The shape and line height that frame the plan

A containment plan needs a shape that makes containment survivable, because a defender holding two metres and refusing to engage is buying time and time has to be spent on something. In this scheme it is spent on two structural jobs: shrinking the pitch behind the duel and occupying the lane the attacker most wants.

The default is a mid-block in a 4-4-2 or a 4-1-4-1, with the defensive line sitting high enough that the space between the back four and the goalkeeper is not an inviting target, and compact enough laterally that the inside cut runs into occupied ground rather than open ground. A low block is not required and is often counterproductive, because a deep line places the containment duel very close to the penalty area, which is exactly where the attacker's manufactured proximity is most valuable and where the foul calculation turns against you.

The line height question deserves precision rather than a preference. The relevant constraint is not the attacker's pace, which is good but not the defining quality of the profile, and it is not the run behind, which is not his primary threat. The constraint is the distance between the containment duel and the edge of your penalty area. You want that distance to be large enough that a successful elimination does not immediately produce a shooting position. Push the line up and you increase the number of duels that occur in the middle third, where being beaten costs territory rather than a chance. Drop the line and you reduce the running space behind, which is a real benefit against most wide attackers, but you concentrate every duel in the area where an eliminated defender is a conceded goal.

The verdict, for a side with average recovery pace across the back four, is a line that sits around the halfway line when the ball is in the opponent's half and drops no deeper than the edge of the D when the ball arrives in the wide channel. That is a conventional mid-block and there is nothing exotic about it. What is not conventional is what the block does when the ball reaches the left channel, which is: nothing. It does not shift toward the ball with the usual urgency. It compresses inward and holds.

### Which shape handles a close control dribbler best?

A compact 4-4-2 or 4-1-4-1 mid-block, because both give the near-side midfielder a natural screening position inside the duel without asking him to leave a central zone unoccupied. A back three works if the wing-back holds containment and the near centre-back covers inside, but it invites the touchline overload that this profile enjoys.

The inward compression is the structural half of the plan and it is worth describing in detail, because it is what makes the containment distance affordable. When the ball goes to the attacker in the left channel, the near-side central midfielder does not move toward the touchline. He moves to a position roughly ten metres inside the full-back and slightly behind him, occupying the diagonal lane between the attacker and the top of the penalty area. The near centre-back does not slide fully wide; he takes a position that lets him cover the inside cut without leaving the near post unguarded. The far-side midfield and the far full-back tuck in and accept that a switch to the opposite flank is available.

That last acceptance is the biggest structural cost of the plan and it should be stated plainly rather than buried. You are choosing to be beatable by a fast, accurate switch of play. Against a side with a genuinely elite passing centre-back or a deep playmaker who can find the far flank in one action, that cost is high and the plan needs modification, which is covered later in the fallbacks. Against most sides, the switch is available but slow, and slow switches are survivable because the block has time to shift.

## The individual assignment and the defender profile that fits

The full-back assignment against this profile is unusual, and the unusual part is that pace is not the first requirement.

Against a wide attacker whose threat is running past you, the defender profile that fits is the one who can turn and match top speed, because the duel is a race and the race is winnable. Against a wide attacker whose threat is manipulating your balance inside two metres, speed is close to irrelevant, because the elimination happens before any running occurs. The qualities that matter are, in order: the discipline to hold distance, lateral balance under feint, the ability to retreat while staying square, and the willingness to be publicly patient.

That last one is a real selection criterion and not a soft one. A full-back who is temperamentally competitive, who hates being dribbled at, who wants to prove something in the duel, is a poor fit for this assignment regardless of his physical qualities. He will engage. He will engage at the worst moment, which is the moment the attacker has settled control and is waiting for exactly that. The assignment wants a defender who is comfortable looking passive for ninety minutes and who measures his own performance by the absence of two-versus-one situations behind him rather than by tackles won.

### Which defender profile handles this attacker best?

A composed, laterally balanced full-back with strong retreating mechanics and a low competitive itch, ideally on the taller side so his stride covers the containment distance without lunging. Raw pace helps at the margins. Discipline is the load-bearing quality, because the plan fails through engagement, not through being outrun.

Height and stride length matter more than they usually do in a wide duel, for a specific reason. A defender with a long stride can adjust his position by half a metre without transferring weight, because the adjustment is one small step rather than two. A defender with a short stride has to take two steps to make the same adjustment, and the second step is where his weight goes. Against an attacker who reads weight transfer for a living, that difference is the duel. It is one of the few cases in football where a slightly slower, longer-limbed defender is the better selection than a quicker, shorter one, and it runs against most coaches' instincts.

The second-choice profile, when the composed full-back is unavailable, is a converted centre-back playing wide. This is less exotic than it sounds and it has a genuine logic: a centre-back's default defensive posture is to stay goal-side, delay, and wait for support, which is precisely the behaviour the plan wants. What he lacks is the recovery pace to survive the occasional beat, and the plan compensates by dropping the covering line another two metres when he is the assigned defender.

The third profile, and the one to avoid unless the alternatives are worse, is the aggressive, front-footed full-back who defends by anticipation and interception. He will read the pass into feet, he will step to intercept it, and roughly a third of the time he will be wrong, and each of those times will be a full-back eliminated in the attacking half with the covering structure not yet set.

## The collective triggers and the cover behaviour

A containment plan without triggers is a passive plan, and a purely passive plan against a good side simply hands over the ball and waits to be broken down. The triggers here are the moments when the team stops containing and starts competing, and they all share a single feature: they occur when the ball is not under settled control.

The first trigger is the heavy touch. Any touch that puts the ball more than roughly a metre from the attacker's feet converts the situation instantly. The near defender engages, the covering midfielder steps, and the objective changes from delay to recovery. This is the moment doubling up is correct, and the difference between this and the touchline instinct is entirely a matter of timing.

The second trigger is the turn away from goal. When the attacker receives with his back to play and shields, the danger drops sharply and the team may press the shield, because a player facing his own goal cannot eliminate two defenders with a touch; he can only lay the ball off. Pressing a shielded reception is one of the few free actions available in this plan.

The third trigger is the pass. The moment the ball leaves his foot, he stops being a containment problem and becomes a runner, and the containment defender's job changes from holding distance to tracking movement. This transition is where a large number of goals against containment schemes originate, because the defender who has spent eight seconds concentrating on holding a distance is mentally set in that job and loses the run off the ball.

The fourth trigger is the touchline itself. When the carry has taken him within roughly three metres of the line, the number of exit routes has halved and the containment defender may narrow his distance, because the touchline is doing the work a second defender would otherwise do badly. This is the one moment in the plan where closing down is both safe and productive.

The covering behaviour, which is the other half of the collective scheme, is defined by a rule that is easy to state and hard to install: the covering player's position is behind and inside the first defender's shoulder, never level and never beyond. Behind means roughly two metres deeper. Inside means toward the centre of the pitch, on the line between the attacker and the top of the penalty area. The purpose of that position is not to help win the ball. It is to make the inside cut, which is the attacker's highest-value exit, run directly into an occupied lane.

The most common installation failure is that the covering player, who is usually a central midfielder and who has spent his career being told to press the ball, drifts forward as the duel develops. He wants to help. Every instinct he has says the ball is the problem. By the time the elimination happens he is two metres from the first defender rather than four, and the single touch that beats the first defender also puts him behind the play. Fixing this is a rehearsal problem rather than an understanding problem, and it is the reason this plan needs actual session time rather than a whiteboard briefing.

## The plan phase by phase

The counter-plan runs in five phases, and each phase has a defined objective, a defined behaviour, and a defined signal that it has transitioned.

### Phase one: before the ball arrives

The work starts before he receives, and it is the cheapest work in the plan. The objective is to influence the reception rather than to prevent it, because preventing the pass into feet from a settled build-up is not realistically achievable and attempting it costs more than it gains.

The behaviour is that the near-side midfielder positions on the inside shoulder of the receiving position and the full-back sets his starting distance at three metres rather than two. Three metres before the reception, closing to two as the ball travels, gives the defender the time to arrive stationary, which the technique article calls the set and which is the single largest determinant of a wide duel's outcome. A defender who arrives at two metres already stationary has a duel. A defender who arrives at two metres still moving has already lost half of one.

The influence on the reception comes from the inside shoulder. If the midfielder occupies the inside line, the natural pass is to the outside foot, which means the reception is angled toward the touchline rather than toward goal. That is a small gain and it compounds across a match. It does not stop the pass and it is not supposed to.

The transition signal for phase one is the ball leaving the passer's foot.

### Phase two: the reception and the first two seconds

The objective is survival with structure intact, and the behaviour is almost entirely negative: do not engage, do not reach, do not turn the hips, do not let the covering player step.

The first two seconds after a close control specialist receives are the highest-risk window in the entire sequence, because it is the window in which his control is most settled and the defensive structure is least set. It is also the window in which the touchline shouting is loudest, which is why the players need the reasoning rather than the rule.

The specific behaviour for the containment defender is to hold a body angle with his front foot positioned outside the attacker's shooting-side shoulder and his hips turned perhaps thirty degrees toward the touchline, weight on the back foot, knees bent enough to move laterally without a preparatory step. He is not showing him one way in the sense the coaching cliche means; the show-inside-or-outside question is a genuinely contested decision with its own owner in this series, and this plan deliberately does not resolve it here. What the angle does is remove the direct line to goal while leaving both lateral options open, so that the attacker's first move has to be a lateral one and lateral moves cost time.

The transition signal for phase two is the attacker's first touch after the reception.

### Phase three: the carry

This is the phase that tests the plan, because it is long, it looks bad, and nothing happens. The objective is to let the carry happen while shrinking the destination.

The behaviour is that the containment defender retreats at the attacker's pace, maintaining distance and angle, taking small steps and never crossing his feet. The covering midfielder holds his position behind and inside, adjusting laterally with the carry but never reducing his depth. The back line compresses toward the near post without sliding fully across. The far-side players tuck in.

The important detail in phase three is what the containment defender does with his eyes, which sounds like a coaching platitude and is not. He watches the ball, not the feet and not the hips. A close control specialist's entire craft is the manipulation of the defender's read, and the hips and shoulders are the instrument of that manipulation. The ball cannot lie. A defender who watches the ball will be beaten occasionally by genuine quality of touch; a defender who watches the body will be beaten repeatedly by a feint that costs the attacker nothing.

The transition signals for phase three are the touchline arriving, the heavy touch occurring, the pass leaving, or the attacker cutting inside.

### Phase four: the inside cut

The inside cut is the attacker's highest-value action, because it opens the shot and because it crosses the covering player's lane. The objective in this phase is to make the cut expensive rather than to prevent it.

The behaviour is that the containment defender does not follow the cut across; he steps backward and inward, staying goal-side, and hands the duel to the covering player who is already in the lane. The covering player does not engage either. He shows the attacker further inside, into the congested central area, where two more bodies are already positioned. The near centre-back holds the near post line and does not step out.

That handover is the moment the plan either works or falls apart, and it fails in exactly one way: the containment defender chases. He has spent eight seconds not engaging, he sees the attacker cut inside into a crowd, and he decides this is finally his moment to make the tackle from behind. It is not. A defender chasing from behind into a crowded area produces one of two outcomes, both bad: a foul in a shooting position or a defender running past the ball and out of the picture. The instruction is absolute. Once the cut has beaten him, his job is to recover goal-side and do nothing else.

The transition signal for phase four is the shot, the pass, or the ball being turned backwards.

### Phase five: the reset

The objective is to re-establish the containment structure before the next reception, and the behaviour is a coordinated retreat rather than a sprint back to a position.

Reset discipline is the least glamorous part of the plan and the most predictive of whether it holds for ninety minutes. A team that resets in four seconds will contain. A team that resets in eight will be facing the same attacker with a broken structure, and against a player who thrives on the manufactured proximity of a disorganized defensive line, a broken structure is a permanent state.

The specific reset instruction for the containment defender is that he re-establishes his distance before he looks for the ball. Position first, information second. Defenders who reverse that order arrive at their position late and moving, and the set is lost before the next duel begins.

## Stopping the pass that reaches his feet

The plan above assumes he receives. A complete counter-plan has to address the layer above, which is the supply, and this is where an honest article separates from an optimistic one.

You cannot reliably prevent a pass into the feet of a wide attacker who is comfortable receiving under pressure. The reason is structural rather than technical: any pressing scheme aggressive enough to deny that pass leaves the areas behind the pressure open, and this attacker's teams are typically built to use those areas. Attempting to starve him is a plan that concedes more than it saves.

What you can do is influence three things about the supply: the height of the reception on the pitch, the body position he receives in, and the number of supporting runners available when he does.

The height of the reception is managed by the block's vertical position, and the objective is to push his receptions back toward the halfway line, where an elimination costs territory rather than a chance. That is achieved not by pressing him but by pressing the player who supplies him, which is usually a left-sided centre-back or a deep midfielder, and pressing them at a moment that forces the pass earlier than they want to play it.

The body position is managed by the inside shoulder positioning described in phase one, and the gain is real but small.

The number of supporting runners is the most tractable of the three and the least discussed. A close control specialist's threat multiplies enormously when he has an overlapping full-back and an inside runner available, because the containment plan's core instruction, which is to refuse the second body, becomes impossible to hold when the attacker himself has two bodies attached. The answer is a specific tracking assignment: the near-side midfielder's screening job takes priority over pressing the ball, and the near-side forward drops to take the overlapping full-back when the ball enters the left channel. That is a genuine cost. It removes a forward from the counter-attacking structure for the duration of the phase, and against a side that recycles patiently, it removes him for long stretches.

### How do you defend the pass into a close control dribbler's feet?

You do not prevent it; you degrade it. Press the supplier rather than the receiver so the pass arrives earlier and flatter, occupy the inside shoulder so the reception is angled toward the touchline, and assign a tracker to the overlapping full-back so he receives without an immediate second option.

## The touchline as a defensive asset

The touchline is the only defender in this plan that never lunges, never gets tired, and never gets eliminated, and a containment scheme should be designed around using it.

The mechanism is straightforward. A dribbler's exit routes are defined by the arc of ground available to him. In the middle of the pitch that arc is a full semicircle. Three metres from the touchline it is roughly a quarter of one. Every metre of lateral compression toward the line removes options without adding bodies, which is precisely what the pressure paradox says you should be doing.

The practical instruction is that the containment defender's angle should always leave the inside route slightly less attractive than the outside route, without ever fully committing to showing him outside. That is a subtle distinction. Showing outside as an absolute rule is a decision this article deliberately does not make, because the inside-versus-outside question is genuinely contested, depends heavily on the covering structure, and has its own dedicated treatment in this series. What this plan does is weight the invitation, so that the natural path of the carry drifts toward the line rather than toward the middle, and the compression happens by itself.

Once the carry reaches roughly three metres from the line, the situation changes materially. The attacker's radius now has a boundary on one side, which means his lateral options are halved, and a defender may reduce his distance to a metre and a half without becoming a target, because the touch that would eliminate him has nowhere to go. This is the crowded touchline area, and it is the only zone on the pitch where crowding this profile is correct.

The crowded touchline situation has one specific failure mode worth naming: the foul. A defender who has been patient for eight seconds, who finally has the attacker pinned against the line, and who now has support arriving, is a defender who feels the duel is finally winnable and who reaches. The attacker in this profile is exceptionally good at inviting exactly that reach, and the resulting free kick, if it occurs in the attacking third, hands over a delivery into the penalty area. The instruction is that in the crowded touchline area you may close the distance and you may not tackle. Support arrives, the ball is trapped, and the attacker's options reduce to a backward pass or a lost ball. Both are wins.

## The foul calculation

Every plan against a dribbler of this type produces free kicks, and pretending otherwise is dishonest. The question is not whether you concede them but where.

The foul calculation is a location problem with a card dimension, and it should be briefed as explicitly as any other part of the plan. A foul committed in the middle third, at the moment a carry begins, costs almost nothing and gains time for the block to reset. A foul committed within roughly twenty-five metres of goal and in the wide half of the pitch produces a delivery into the penalty area from an angle that most teams do not defend well. A foul committed centrally within thirty metres produces a direct shooting opportunity. And a foul committed by an already-booked defender in any location produces a substitution or a reorganization that costs the plan its designated containment player.

The briefing, therefore, has three lines. First, if you are going to foul, foul early and high, before the carry gains ground. Second, in the wide attacking third, do not foul at all, contain instead and accept the cross. Third, a booked containment defender changes assignment; he does not change behaviour, and the coaching staff makes that change rather than the player.

That third line matters because the alternative, which is asking a booked full-back to be even more passive, is not workable. Passivity has a floor, and below it the attacker simply carries the ball into the penalty area unchallenged. When the containment defender picks up a card in the first half, the correct adjustments are either to swap the flank assignments at half time, to drop the covering midfielder a further two metres so the containment defender can hold a greater distance, or to substitute. Leaving a booked player in the assignment with no structural change is a decision to defend the left channel with a passenger.

The discipline framing of this section deserves one clarification, because the topic invites a kind of commentary this series does not do. Drawing fouls is a legitimate and coached attacking skill, in the same category as protecting the ball or attacking the front foot of a defender. A forward who positions his body so that a defender's reach becomes contact is doing his job well. The defensive answer is not complaint; it is a discipline plan that removes the reach. That is entirely within the defending team's control, which is why this is briefed as a defensive problem rather than described as anything else.

### How do you stop a dribbler winning free kicks in dangerous areas?

Move the contact zone up the pitch and remove the reach. Foul early in the middle third if you must, never in the wide attacking third, and instruct the containment defender that once beaten he recovers goal-side without a trailing arm or leg. Most dangerous free kicks come from recovery challenges, not from initial duels.

## The shooting threat and the goalkeeper's job

Containment plans have a characteristic weakness and it is worth confronting rather than hiding: a defender holding two metres is a defender not blocking a shot. Against a wide attacker with a genuine finishing threat from the left half-space, that is a real cost, and the plan has to price it.

The threat pattern is well established and observable in any viewing. He carries from the left channel, cuts inside onto his stronger foot, and strikes across the goalkeeper toward the far post from a position somewhere between the corner of the penalty area and the top of the D. It is the standard inverted-attacker shooting pattern and it is standard because it works: the ball travels away from the goalkeeper's starting position, the angle opens as the carry continues inward, and the defender who follows the cut across arrives late and unbalanced and cannot get a foot to it.

The plan's answer is structural rather than individual. The containment defender does not attempt to block the shot, because attempting to block it means following the cut across, which is the exact action that eliminates him and opens the lane further. The block comes from the covering midfielder who is already positioned in the diagonal lane, and from the near centre-back holding a compact position rather than stepping out.

The goalkeeper's job in this scheme has three components, and the first one is a starting position that acknowledges the shot is coming from a predictable zone. Against this profile the goalkeeper should set slightly further from his near post than his default against a wide carry, because the near post is protected by the touchline angle and the covering bodies, while the far post is the target. The second component is patience on the set. A goalkeeper who commits his weight as the attacker cuts inside is subject to the same manipulation the defenders are; the disguise between the shot and the cutback is a genuine part of this attacker's game, and a goalkeeper who has already dropped his weight toward the far post cannot recover for the cutback to the penalty spot. The third component is communication about the second defender. The goalkeeper has the best view of the covering structure in the entire team, and the single most useful thing he can shout during a containment sequence is not "close him down" but the position of the cover.

### What should a goalkeeper do differently against this shooting pattern?

Set a half-step further from the near post than normal, delay the weight transfer until the striking foot is planted, and treat the cutback to the penalty spot as an equally likely outcome. The shot is disguised as a pass and the pass is disguised as a shot, so committing early loses to both.

The related preparation point is that this shooting threat is materially reduced when the cut inside happens further from goal, which loops back to the line height decision made earlier. A block that forces the containment duel to begin near the halfway line pushes the cut inside into a zone where the shot is a low-probability effort rather than a chance. That is one of the strongest arguments for the mid-block over the low block against this specific profile, and it is worth stating because most coaches' instinct against a dangerous dribbler is to sit deeper.

## Set-piece and transition addenda

Two situations sit outside the phase structure above and both need their own instruction.

### The defensive transition

The most dangerous version of this attacker is not the one who receives against a set block. It is the one who receives four seconds after your team has lost the ball in the opposition half, when your containment structure does not exist and there is no covering midfielder within twenty metres.

The transition instruction, therefore, is a rest-defence instruction, and it belongs to the phase of your own attack rather than to the phase of his. Whenever your side is in settled possession in the attacking third, one central midfielder and the near-side full-back on his side of the pitch hold positions that allow the containment structure to form within three seconds of a turnover. In practice, that means your right-back does not join the attack at the same time as your right-sided midfielder, and one of the two central midfielders stays in a position between the ball and your own left channel.

This costs attacking numbers. Against a side built around a left-sided close control specialist, it is the correct cost, because the alternative is a duel with no cover, which the elimination-radius table describes accurately: one action, two defenders removed, direct route to goal.

The second transition instruction concerns the first defender in the moment of turnover. In a normal counter-press, the nearest player engages the ball immediately. Against this profile the nearest player delays and angles instead, because an immediate engagement in an unstructured situation is the purest form of the pressure paradox. The rest of the counter-press proceeds normally around him. That is a genuinely awkward instruction to install, since it asks one player to do the opposite of what everyone around him is doing, and it needs rehearsal.

### Set pieces

Two dead-ball dimensions matter and neither is about his delivery.

The first is the defensive free kick your containment plan concedes. Because the plan accepts fouls in the middle third and refuses them in the attacking third, the free kicks you concede should mostly be at a distance where a direct effort is not the threat but the second phase is. The instruction is to defend those with a full complement rather than leaving three forward, because a cleared delivery that falls to a close control specialist thirty metres from goal with a scattered defensive structure is a worse situation than the free kick was.

The second is the attacking corner. When your own side wins a corner against a team with this attacker, the rest-defence assignment applies with unusual force, because a cleared corner is the single most common route to an unstructured transition. The standard two-players-back arrangement is insufficient here. Three back, with one of them being the designated containment defender rather than whoever happens to be nearest, is the correct arrangement, and it costs an attacking body in the penalty area. Price it and accept it.

## Fallbacks when the first plan fails

Any counter-plan that does not specify what happens when it does not work is a whiteboard exercise. This one fails in four identifiable ways, and each has a defined response.

### Failure one: the containment defender cannot hold the distance

The most common failure, and it is a personnel problem rather than a scheme problem. A full-back who keeps stepping in is not going to stop stepping in because of a half-time instruction. He has thirty years of coaching telling him to engage.

The response is a swap rather than a lecture. Move the assignment to the more composed of your two centre-backs by shifting to a back three with the composed defender on the left of it, and let the wing-back play higher where his instincts are useful. This is a mid-match structural change and it should be pre-planned rather than improvised, which means the wing-back version of the shape needs to be a rehearsed alternative rather than an idea.

### Failure two: the covering midfielder keeps stepping toward the ball

The second most common failure, and it is a rehearsal problem. The response during a match is to change who holds the covering position, moving it from a naturally aggressive central midfielder to a holding midfielder whose defensive default is already to sit. The response between matches is session time with a constraint that punishes stepping, which is training territory and is best designed as a specific drill rather than as a coaching point shouted during a game.

### Failure three: the overload

When the opposing side commits an overlapping full-back and an inside runner into the left channel consistently, the containment structure becomes a three-versus-two and the plan's core instruction, which is to refuse the second body, becomes impossible, because the second body is now required simply to match numbers.

The response is a numerical one and it costs you elsewhere. Drop the near-side forward permanently into the wide midfield line, converting a 4-4-2 into a 4-5-1 or a 4-1-4-1 for that side of the pitch, and accept that your own attacking transitions from that flank are finished for the remainder of the phase. The alternative response, which is to pull the far-side midfielder across, is worse: it opens the switch, and against a side patient enough to build an overload it will also be a side patient enough to punish the switch.

### Failure four: he moves inside

The most sophisticated failure, and the one that most often decides matches. A left-sided close control specialist who drifts into the left half-space or into central areas removes the touchline, which was the plan's most reliable asset, and doubles his exit arc.

The response is a handover rather than a follow. The containment defender does not track him inside; the near-side central midfielder inherits the containment assignment with the same distance and angle instructions, and the containment defender holds width to prevent the overlap becoming a free crossing position. Getting that handover clean is the hardest single piece of choreography in the plan, and the practical simplification for a side that cannot rehearse it is a zonal rule: whoever is in the zone he enters takes the containment job, and the surrounding players adjust to provide the covering depth behind that new first defender.

If all four responses have been used and the left channel is still leaking, the honest last resort is to change the terms of the match. Push your own line up, hold more of the ball, and reduce the number of times he receives in a dangerous position, accepting the increased risk behind. That is not a defensive adjustment; it is a decision to make the match about something else, and it is sometimes the correct one.

## What you accept conceding

A counter-plan that pretends to give up nothing is a counter-plan nobody has tested. Here is the bill, stated in full so a coach can decide whether he wants to pay it.

You concede the ball in the left channel, repeatedly and comfortably. He will receive, he will turn, he will carry, and your defender will be two metres away doing nothing about it. This will look bad from the stands and it will feel bad on the pitch, and both of those are costs, because a defensive scheme that erodes a team's confidence in itself has a shelf life.

You concede territory. Containment retreats, so the block's average position will sit lower than your default, and lower blocks produce longer counter-attacks and fewer of them.

You concede the switch. A block that compresses toward the ball side without sliding fully across leaves the far flank available, and against a side with a passer who can hit it accurately, you will spend the match watching the ball travel from your left to your right.

You concede the early cross. A defender at two metres cannot block a cross, and the plan's touchline compression makes the early cross from a wide position the attacker's easiest available action. That is a deliberate trade: you are choosing to defend crosses into your penalty area rather than duels at the edge of it, and it is only a good trade if your centre-backs are competent in the air. If they are not, this plan needs modification, and the modification is to accept more inside cuts in exchange for closing the crossing angle, which changes the whole balance.

You concede fouls in the middle third and the free kicks that follow.

What you do not concede, if the plan holds, is the two-versus-one at the edge of your penalty area, which is the situation that actually produces goals against this profile. Everything on the list above is bought in exchange for that single denial, and whether the trade is worth it depends on one thing: how good your side is at defending crosses and long-range shots. Teams with tall, organized central defenders and a good goalkeeper should take this trade every time. Teams without them should think harder.

## How the plan degrades at lower levels

The full scheme assumes eleven players who can hold positional discipline for ninety minutes, a covering midfielder who will follow a counterintuitive instruction, and a rehearsal environment. Most sides reading this have none of those things, so here is the version that survives contact with an amateur Saturday.

Cut the plan to two instructions. First: the defender nearest him never dives in, ever, under any circumstances, no matter what the touchline says. Second: the nearest teammate stands ten metres behind and inside him and does not move forward. That is it. Everything else in this article is refinement on those two lines, and at amateur level the refinement is worth far less than the compliance.

The reason those two instructions carry so much of the value is that at amateur level the elimination arithmetic is even more brutal than at the top. Amateur covering structures are slower to reset, amateur defenders lunge more, and an amateur side that loses two players to a single touch is frequently conceding a clear chance rather than merely a dangerous situation. The pressure paradox does not weaken as you go down the levels. It strengthens.

### How should an amateur side handle a close control dribbler?

Give the nearest defender one job, which is to stay on his feet and stay two metres away, and give the next nearest one job, which is to stand behind him and not move forward. Ignore everything else. At amateur level, compliance with two simple rules outperforms sophisticated shape that nobody executes under pressure.

Two additional adjustments help at lower levels. The first is that on a poor surface the containment distance should increase rather than decrease, because a bad pitch makes the attacker's touch less predictable and makes the defender's footwork less reliable, and the defender is the one who suffers more from that uncertainty. The second is that a youth or amateur coach should explain the reasoning rather than issuing the rule, because a player who does not understand why he is standing off will abandon the instruction the first time he is beaten and blamed.

The physical demand of this assignment is different from the demand of a conventional wide duel, and it is worth a word. Containment defending involves repeated deceleration, lateral shuffling, and retreating steps rather than sprinting, and it loads the hip and groin musculature in ways that a straight-line running warm-up does not prepare for. Any side installing this plan should prepare for it accordingly, and decisions about an individual player's readiness, load, or return from injury belong to a qualified medical professional rather than to a coach with an article.

## What analysts track when scouting this profile

The scouting job here is a classification job before it is a detail job, and getting the classification right in the first viewing saves a week of wasted preparation.

The question the analyst is answering is whether this attacker converts space into danger or converts pressure into danger, because the entire plan flips on the answer. There are four observable markers that separate the two, and they can all be checked in a single match viewing.

The first marker is where his successful actions start. A space-beater's dangerous sequences begin with a first touch that pushes the ball away from his body into a running lane. A pressure-beater's begin with a first touch that keeps the ball inside his own reach with a defender already engaged. Watch ten receptions and count.

The second marker is what happens when a second defender arrives. A space-beater's success rate drops when the second defender arrives, because the running lane closes. A pressure-beater's success rate holds or improves, because the second defender is another body inside the radius. This is the single most diagnostic marker and it is easy to count.

The third marker is the direction of his first movement after receiving. A space-beater's first movement is forward or diagonally forward, into the ground he intends to attack. A pressure-beater's first movement is frequently lateral or even backward, because he is not trying to reach space; he is trying to attract a defender into his radius.

The fourth marker is the tempo of his carries. A space-beater accelerates. A pressure-beater carries at a deliberately moderate pace, because acceleration removes the very proximity his game depends on.

The analyst's output is not a highlight reel. It is a distance recommendation, a covering depth, and a shape for the block, and it should fit on one page. The temptation with a spectacular dribbler is to build a video package showing everything he can do, which is the least useful possible product because it teaches defenders to fear him and to prepare for actions rather than for positions.

The dossier that actually helps a coach contains three things: the receiving zones he uses most, the covering depth the plan requires against him, and the two triggers that convert containment into engagement. That page, plus a rehearsed session, is the whole preparation. Coaches and analysts who want to keep those dossiers organized across a season, with the clips and the notes attached to the plan rather than scattered across folders, can [build your opposition plan free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html), which is built for exactly this kind of match-week preparation.

### How do you classify a dribbler in a single viewing?

Count what happens when a second defender arrives. If his success rate drops, he beats space and numbers work against him. If it holds or rises, he beats pressure and numbers help him. That single count is worth more than a full match of qualitative note-taking, and it takes twenty minutes.

## Tight areas or open space: where is he actually harder to defend?

This is the question that most often gets answered backwards, and the reasoning behind the correct answer is the reasoning behind the whole plan.

The instinctive answer is open space, because open space is where dribblers do damage in most people's mental model. For a space-beater that answer is right. For this profile it is wrong, and the reason is that open space contains no defenders to eliminate.

A close control specialist alone in open space with one defender ten metres away has a straightforward problem to solve and it is the same problem any competent attacker faces: beat one man in a foot race or find a pass. That is a duel he wins often, but it is a duel with a low ceiling, because beating one man in space produces a one-versus-one against the covering defender rather than a clear chance. Now put the same attacker in a congested area near the touchline with three defenders inside three metres of him. To most eyes that is a trapped attacker. To this profile it is a target-rich environment, because a single touch and a body swerve can remove two of the three, and removing two defenders from a congested area near your penalty box produces a shooting position rather than another duel.

So the honest answer is that he is harder to defend in tight areas, and the corollary is uncomfortable: your defensive structure's instinct to make the pitch small is, against this specific profile, an instinct to hand him the situation he wants. The plan's response is not to open the pitch up, which would be absurd, but to make the congestion structural rather than proximate. Have the bodies present but positioned outside the radius and behind the ball. The area is still congested. The radius is still empty.

This distinction between congestion and proximity is the most transferable idea in the article, and it generalizes well beyond this one player. The broader category treatment, which classifies dribblers by winning mechanism and gives each class its own containment doctrine, is developed in the guide to [the greatest dribblers and how to contain them](/2021/09/01/greatest-dribblers-how-to-contain/), and a coach who has read this article will find that his player sits in a class with a shared answer rather than being a unique problem.

## The approaches that have actually worked

Rather than reconstruct specific fixtures from memory, which is where football writing usually starts inventing, it is more useful to describe the three approaches that recur when a side comes off the pitch having contained this profile, because the patterns are observable across many viewings and none of them depends on a scoreline.

The first approach is the deep, disciplined, narrow block with a designated non-engaging full-back. This is the plan described in this article, and the sides that execute it well share a visible signature: their left channel looks conceded for long stretches, their central defenders barely move laterally, and their statistics for tackles in the wide area are low while their statistics for crosses faced are high. It is unmistakable on video once you know to look for it, and the tell is a full-back retreating at walking pace with his hands low and his hips square.

The second approach is the structural one, which is to remove the supply rather than to solve the duel. Sides that do this press the left-sided build-up aggressively, forcing the ball to the opposite flank, and accept a much higher defensive risk in exchange for reducing the number of times the duel occurs at all. It is a legitimate plan and it belongs to sides confident in their pressing coordination, because a press that fails against a team with this outlet is a press that produces exactly the unstructured transition the plan was meant to avoid.

The third approach is the aggressive one, which is to attack the flank he defends rather than the flank he attacks. This is the least discussed and often the most effective, particularly against a side that leaves the space behind their left-sided attacker exposed. It is not a defensive plan at all. It is a decision that the cheapest way to reduce his influence is to occupy him with defensive work, and it is a legitimate answer for any side with a genuine attacking threat on their own right.

What does not appear in the successful versions, and this is the negative finding worth as much as the positive ones, is the aggressive man-oriented double-team in open play. Sides that try it do not generally look like sides in control. They look like sides producing a series of two-versus-one situations at the edge of their own penalty area, which is the exact outcome the pressure paradox predicts.

## The positions worth arguing with

Five arguments recur whenever this profile is discussed seriously, and each deserves a real answer rather than dismissal.

The first is that you must always double a dangerous dribbler. The answer is that the instruction is correct against most of them and wrong against this class, and the discriminating test is the marker described in the scouting section: does his success rate rise or fall when the second defender arrives. Coaches who apply the doubling instruction universally are not being stupid; they are applying a rule that is right most of the time to the minority of cases where it inverts. The fix is a test, not a reversal.

The second is that close control does not scale to the highest level, because elite defences are too organized to allow the confined situations it needs. The evidence does not support it, and the reasoning behind the claim contains the error identified earlier: it assumes the attacker requires a pre-existing confined situation, when the defining feature of the profile is that he creates one. A more defensible version of the argument is that close control alone does not scale, and that is true. Close control combined with an end product, which in this case means a shot from the left half-space and a delivery from the touchline, scales fine.

The third is that physical defending unsettles him. This one has a genuine kernel, and the kernel is that any attacker who operates in contact ranges is affected by contact, which is not a controversial claim. But the practical version of the argument, which is that a physically aggressive defender will get on top of him, does not survive contact with the plan. Physical defending in the sense most people mean requires proximity, and proximity is what the elimination radius says you must not give. A defender who defends physically against this profile is a defender committing his weight to make contact, and committing weight is the failure mode. The version of physicality that does help is the structural kind: strong, tall central defenders who win the crosses the plan concedes, and a midfield that wins second balls. That physicality does not require anyone to get close to him.

The fourth is the free-kick argument, and it belongs in the tactical column rather than any other. Attackers who operate at close quarters draw more contact than attackers who operate at distance, which is arithmetic rather than character. The defensive answer is entirely within your control and it is the foul calculation set out earlier: move the contact zone up the pitch, remove the trailing arm and leg from recovery challenges, and refuse to tackle in the attacking third. A side that briefs this properly concedes fewer dangerous free kicks against this profile than against a conventional winger, because it has stopped tackling in the zone where tackles produce them.

The fifth is that he is dependent on a specific system. There is something in this, and it applies to almost every wide attacker in the game, which is why the claim carries less weight than it seems to. Any inverted wide attacker who cuts inside needs someone to occupy the outside lane, someone to attack the far post, and a structure that lets him receive facing forward rather than facing his own goal. Remove all three and his output drops. That is a statement about the requirements of the role, not about the player's quality, and it applies with equal force to every attacker in his positional class. Where it does have a specific application here is defensive: a plan that occupies the overlapping full-back and denies the far-post runner reduces the value of every carry he makes, which is why the tracking assignment described earlier is worth its cost.

## What to rehearse in the week

The plan is not installable through a meeting, and the reason is that its core instructions contradict trained reflexes. Two rehearsal priorities carry almost all of the value.

The first is a constraint that punishes engagement. Set up a wide channel with an attacker, a containment defender, and a covering player, and score the exercise so that the defensive pair loses a point every time the defender's front foot comes inside a marked distance, regardless of whether he wins the ball. Defenders will hate this for two sessions and then it will start to hold, because the scoring makes the reasoning physical rather than verbal. The critical detail is that winning the ball must not score points. The moment the exercise rewards tackling, the defenders will revert to hunting.

The second is a constraint that punishes the covering player for stepping forward. Mark a line behind the containment defender and score the attacking side a point every time the covering player crosses it while the attacker still has settled control. This is the harder of the two to install because the covering player's forward step feels like effort and looks like commitment, and players resist being penalized for trying.

Both exercises need to be run at genuine intensity with a genuinely good dribbler, because the whole plan is about resisting an urge that only appears under pressure. A containment drill against an attacker who cannot beat anyone teaches nothing. The full session design for containment work, including the progressions and the small-squad variants, sits in the training article for this part of the series rather than here, and this plan links across to it rather than expanding into a full week's programme.

The third rehearsal item, which is smaller but worth ten minutes, is the handover when he drifts inside. Walk it at half pace with the two players who will actually do it, three or four times, and then leave it. It does not need a full drill; it needs the two players to have done it once so it is not novel on Saturday.

## The delivery dimension: what happens when he goes outside

A containment plan that weights the invitation toward the touchline has an obligation to say what it does about the cross, because a plan that trades the inside cut for a delivery has only improved the situation if the delivery is genuinely less dangerous than the cut.

For this profile the trade is favourable, but it is favourable by a margin rather than overwhelmingly, and the margin depends on two things a coach controls. The first is who attacks the far post for the opposition. An inverted attacker whose outside route ends in a delivery is only as dangerous as the runners arriving on it, and a defensive structure that has already committed to compressing inward is a structure with bodies in the penalty area. The second is the height of the delivery, which is a function of how quickly the containment defender narrows once the touchline is reached. A defender who has held two metres for the whole carry and then closes to a metre and a half in the crowded touchline zone forces the delivery to be lifted rather than driven, and lifted deliveries into a compressed box are the easiest kind to defend.

The failure version, which is worth naming, is the defender who holds his distance all the way to the byline without ever narrowing. He has followed the letter of the plan and missed the point of it. The distance instruction exists to protect him from elimination, and once the touchline has removed the elimination routes the instruction has served its purpose and should be replaced by the narrowing behaviour. Coaches installing this plan should teach the transition explicitly, because a player told to hold two metres will hold two metres into the corner flag if nobody tells him when to stop.

The related point concerns his weaker-side delivery. Inverted attackers who cut inside habitually are frequently underestimated as outside threats, and the underestimation is a scouting error rather than an observation. A wide attacker who has spent a career being shown outside by defenders who assume he cannot use that route develops the route, because the alternative is to become one-dimensional and be eliminated from matches. The practical instruction is that the containment defender must not treat the outside route as a safe outcome; he must treat it as the chosen outcome, which is different. A chosen outcome still has to be defended.

## Defending him by game state

The plan described so far is a plan for a level match. Game state changes it, and the changes are large enough that a coach who has not thought about them will improvise badly.

When you are ahead and defending a lead, the plan gets easier and the temptation gets worse. Easier, because a side ahead can accept possession in the left channel without anxiety, which is exactly what containment asks for. Worse, because a side ahead usually drops its line, and a dropped line moves every containment duel closer to the penalty area, where the elimination arithmetic becomes lethal. The correct adjustment when protecting a lead is to keep the line where it was and add a body to the midfield screen rather than to the back line, which keeps the duels far from goal while reducing the space between the lines. That is a counterintuitive instruction for a side seeing out a result and it needs to be pre-agreed rather than discovered in the eighty-second minute.

When you are behind and chasing, the plan becomes expensive, because containment surrenders possession and a chasing side needs the ball. The honest adjustment is not to abandon containment but to move the whole scheme up the pitch: press the supply higher, contain higher, and accept that the duels now occur in the middle third where being beaten costs territory rather than a chance. That is a genuine risk increase and it is priced correctly. What is not priced correctly, and what sides do reflexively, is to keep the block where it was and start doubling up in the wide area to force turnovers. That produces the two-versus-one situations the plan exists to prevent, at the exact moment your side can least afford to concede again.

When the match is level in the closing stages and neither side is committed, the highest-value adjustment is the substitution one. A containment defender who has held distance and discipline for seventy minutes is a defender whose concentration is the resource being depleted, not his legs. The tell that he is going is not slower recovery; it is a first step toward the ball where there previously was none. Coaching staff watching for the tell can make the change before the goal rather than after it, and this is one of the few substitution triggers in football that is genuinely observable in advance.

### When should a coach change the containment defender?

When his first step starts going toward the ball rather than backward. That reflex returns as concentration fades, and it appears before any physical decline is visible. It is a more reliable substitution signal than distance covered or duel outcomes, and it usually shows up ten to fifteen minutes before it costs a goal.

## A worked sequence in the left channel

Descriptions of a plan are easier to follow when they are attached to a sequence, so here is one, narrated from the containment defender's point of view.

The opposition centre-back has the ball at the edge of his own third and your side is in a mid-block. Your first job happens now, before anything else: you set your starting position at three metres from where he is standing, on his inside shoulder, hips already angled. Your near-side midfielder has taken a position ten metres inside you and slightly behind. Neither of you is looking at the ball. Both of you are looking at him.

The pass comes, flat and slightly behind him, and he takes it with the outside foot. You have closed from three metres to two as the ball travelled and you are stationary at the moment his foot touches it. This is the whole duel and it has already happened. If you had arrived still moving, you would be beaten in the next second and would not know why.

He faces you. He touches the ball twenty centimetres to his left and steps across it, and your instinct is that this is the moment. It is not. You have not moved your weight, so the touch has cost him time and gained him nothing. He touches it again, this time toward the inside, and drops his shoulder. You take one small step backward and inward, keeping your front foot outside his shooting-side shoulder, and you have still not moved your weight.

He begins to carry, at a moderate pace, angled toward the touchline. You retreat with him, matching his speed, taking short steps and never crossing your feet. Your midfielder has moved laterally with the carry and has not reduced his depth. Behind you, your left centre-back has compressed toward the near post without sliding fully across, and your right-sided players have tucked in and are watching a switch of play they know is available.

Eight seconds have passed and nothing has happened, which is the plan working. The touchline arrives. He is now three metres from the line and his options are halved, so you narrow to a metre and a half. Your midfielder steps for the first time in the sequence, from behind. Your left centre-back holds.

One of four things now happens. He lifts a cross, which your centre-backs deal with. He passes backward, which is a reset and a win. He takes a heavy touch under the compression, which is your trigger and everyone engages. Or he threads a ball inside to a runner, at which point your job changes instantly from holding distance to tracking movement, and this is the transition where most containment schemes concede, because you have spent eight seconds concentrating on a distance and your head is still in that job.

The sequence is unremarkable and that is the point. A well-executed containment phase produces no highlights and no statistics, which is why it is difficult to sell to players and to supporters, and why the reasoning has to be taught rather than the rule.

## The five installation errors

Five errors account for most of the failures when a side tries to install this plan, and four of them are behavioural rather than tactical.

The first is arriving at the containment distance while still moving. It looks like compliance and it is not, because a defender in motion cannot respond laterally without a preparatory step, and the preparatory step is the elimination. The fix is the three-metre starting position and closing as the ball travels rather than after it arrives.

The second is the covering player's forward drift. It happens gradually across a sequence rather than in one movement, so nobody notices it, and it converts a plan with cover into a plan without. The fix is a marked line in rehearsal and a specific player selected for the job rather than whoever is nearest.

The third is the chase after being beaten. A containment defender who has been patient for eight seconds and then sprints after the ball into a crowded area produces a foul in a shooting position or takes himself out of the picture. The instruction has to be absolute and it has to be repeated, because the urge is strongest in exactly the players who have executed the rest of the plan best.

The fourth is failing to reverse on the trigger. Containment is a mode, and a side that stays in it after the trigger has fired is a side that never wins the ball back. The heavy touch, the shielded reception, and the released pass all mean the mode has ended, and a team that treats containment as an attitude rather than a phase will contain its way to a nil-nil defeat.

The fifth, and the only tactical one, is dropping the line too deep. It is the most common coaching error against any dangerous dribbler and it is specifically wrong here, because depth moves every duel into the zone where an elimination is a chance rather than a nuisance. The instinct to sit deeper against a good dribbler is understandable and it should be resisted, which is easier to do when a coach has the elimination-radius table in front of him and can see what happens to the second column as the duels move closer to goal.

## When the assignment moves off the left flank

Wide attackers do not stay wide, and a plan built entirely around a left-channel duel is a plan that stops existing the moment he starts appearing elsewhere. Three variations are common enough to prepare for.

The first is the drift into the left half-space, which was covered in the fallbacks but deserves a positional note. In the half-space he has an exit arc on both sides and no touchline to help you, but he also has more bodies within reach, which by the logic of this plan is a mixed outcome rather than a clearly worse one. The containment principle does not change: the nearest defender holds distance, the next nearest holds depth behind and inside, and nobody steps. What changes is that the depth behind must now be provided by a centre-back rather than a midfielder, because the danger runs directly at goal rather than diagonally across it. Brief the near centre-back that when the ball enters the half-space he drops rather than steps, and that his partner covers the vacated ground centrally.

The second is the central reception between the lines, facing his own goal. This is the easiest version to defend and the one most sides handle badly, because they treat it as more dangerous than it is. A close control specialist receiving with his back to play has no elimination available to him; he can shield, he can lay the ball off, and he can attempt to turn. All three are containable and the shielded reception is one of the few moments when pressing him is free. The error is to back off out of respect, which gifts him the turn.

The third is the switch to the opposite flank, which good sides use precisely because it breaks the preparation. A right-sided version of the same player is a mirror problem with one meaningful difference: the cut inside now moves onto his weaker side, which reduces the shooting threat and increases the passing threat. The containment instruction is identical and the covering angle changes, since the lane to protect is now the outside one rather than the inside one. Coaches who have designated one full-back as the containment specialist should decide in advance whether the assignment follows the player across the pitch or stays with the flank, and the answer for most sides is that it stays with the flank, because a full-back chasing a positional swap creates the disorganization the whole plan is designed to avoid.

## What a young defender should take from this

The transferable lesson from this plan is narrower than it looks and more useful than a general instruction about patience.

It is this: a defender's distance from the ball is a decision, not a reflex, and it should be made based on what the attacker in front of him is good at rather than on a habit. Most young defenders have exactly one distance, and they arrived at it by accident. They stand close to everyone, or they stand off everyone, and they have never been asked which attacker requires which. A defender who leaves this article with a single question in his head, which is whether the man in front of him is beaten by numbers or fed by them, is a defender who will make better decisions against every opponent for the rest of his career.

The second transferable lesson concerns what a defender should measure himself by. A young player judges his own defending by tackles and by the number of times he was beaten, because those are the visible events. This plan asks him to judge it by whether a two-versus-one occurred behind him, which is not visible to him during the match and requires review afterwards. A coach who teaches that standard early is teaching a habit of mind that separates good defenders from busy ones.

The third is about the touchline, and it is small but practical. Learning to use the line as a teammate, meaning learning the angles that push a carry toward it without ever announcing that you are showing him outside, is a genuinely coachable skill that most defenders acquire late. It costs nothing to teach at fourteen and it pays for twenty years.

The developmental progression for a young defender working on this should be intensity-graded rather than rushed, and any physical programme supporting repeated deceleration and lateral work should be built with proper preparation and appropriate rest, with decisions about an individual player's load and readiness taken by a qualified professional rather than from a written plan.

## Closing verdict

The plan against this attacker is a containment plan and its defining decision is a refusal. You refuse to send the second body toward the ball, and you spend the entire match refusing it, against your own instincts and against the noise from the touchline. Everything else in the scheme, the two-metre distance, the covering depth, the inward compression, the foul calculation, the goalkeeper's set, is machinery built to make that refusal survivable.

The reason is the pressure paradox, and it is the one idea to carry out of this article: every extra defender you commit inside the radius is another player who can be removed by a single touch, so the correct answer to a beaten defender is a deeper second line rather than a closer second body. Against a wide attacker who converts space into danger, numbers are the answer. Against one who converts pressure into danger, numbers are the ammunition.

The plan costs you the ball in the left channel, territory, the switch, the early cross, and a run of middle-third free kicks. It buys you the absence of two-versus-one situations at the edge of your penalty area. That trade is a good one for any side with organized central defenders and a competent goalkeeper, and a doubtful one for a side that defends crosses badly, in which case the honest recommendation is to fix the crossing defence first and then install this.

The three things to take into Saturday, if nothing else survives the week, are these. The containment defender holds two metres and never dives in. The covering player sits behind and inside and never steps forward while the attacker has settled control. And the moment the ball leaves his feet or the touch gets heavy, everything reverses and the whole side engages. A team that does those three things will not stop him. It will stop him from producing the situation he is actually looking for, which is a different and more achievable objective, and it is the one the scoreline notices.

And the vocabulary for wide attacking threats generally, which this plan assumes throughout, sits in the elite wingers hub. Read across the three and the left channel stops being a mystery and starts being a decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Khvicha Kvaratskhelia?

You contain him rather than pressing him. Hold a designated full-back at roughly two metres with hips angled toward the touchline, position a covering midfielder two metres behind and inside rather than beside him, and compress the block inward instead of sliding it across. Engage only on three triggers: a heavy touch, a reception with his back to goal, or the moment the ball leaves his foot. Accept that he keeps possession, carries sideways, and gets crosses away. The plan is not built to win the ball in the wide area; it is built to prevent the two-versus-one at the edge of your box that produces chances, and every concession in it is bought in exchange for that single denial.

### Q: What is Kvaratskhelia's biggest weakness?

His threat is heavily dependent on defenders entering his working range, which means a disciplined block that refuses proximity reduces his output more than a physical or aggressive approach does. The secondary limitation is structural rather than technical: like every inverted wide attacker, his carries lose value when the outside lane is unoccupied by a teammate and the far post is unattended, because the cut inside then has no end product to feed. Occupy the overlapping full-back, deny the far-post runner, and a large share of his carries end as recycled possession rather than as chances. Neither of these is a flaw in his technique; both are conditions an opponent can create.

### Q: How do you defend a winger who beats pressure rather than space?

Invert the standard instruction. Against a space-beater you add bodies to close running lanes; against a pressure-beater every added body inside his working range is another player he can remove with one touch. The correct response is depth rather than proximity: the first defender holds distance and stays on his feet, the second defender drops behind rather than stepping across, and the team creates congestion structurally, with bodies present but positioned outside his reach and behind the ball. Identify which type you are facing by counting what happens to his success rate when a second defender arrives.

### Q: Does doubling up on Kvaratskhelia backfire?

In open play with the ball under his settled control, yes, and the arithmetic is the reason. A second defender arriving toward the ball is a second body inside the range where a single touch removes a player, and a defender eliminated while moving forward is out of the recovery structure for several seconds rather than merely beaten. That converts a one-versus-one into a two-versus-one behind the play. Doubling works only after the ball has already moved, meaning after a heavy touch, a turn away from goal, or a pass. The trigger is the state of the ball, not the level of danger.

### Q: Which defender profile copes with Kvaratskhelia?

A composed, laterally balanced full-back with good retreating mechanics, a long stride, and a low competitive itch. Pace matters far less than usual, because the elimination happens before any running occurs. Height and stride length matter more than usual, because a longer-limbed defender can adjust half a metre with one step rather than two, and the second step is where weight transfers. The profile to avoid is the front-footed, anticipatory full-back who defends by interception, because he will step to the pass into feet and be wrong often enough to cost you the channel.

### Q: How do you stop Kvaratskhelia winning free kicks in dangerous areas?

Move the contact zone up the pitch and remove the reach. Brief three rules: if a foul is necessary, commit it early in the middle third before the carry gains ground; in the wide attacking third do not tackle at all, contain and concede the cross instead; and once beaten, recover goal-side with no trailing arm or leg. Most dangerous free kicks against this profile come from recovery challenges rather than initial duels, so the discipline point is about what a beaten defender does next. A booked containment defender needs a structural change from the bench rather than an instruction to be even more passive.

### Q: How do you defend Kvaratskhelia in a crowded touchline area?

This is the one zone where closing the distance is correct. Three metres from the line his working arc is halved, so a defender may reduce to roughly a metre and a half without becoming a target, because the touch that would eliminate him has nowhere to travel. The absolute rule is that you may close and you may not tackle. Support arrives from behind, the exit routes shrink to a backward pass or a lost ball, and both are wins. The failure mode is the patient defender who finally sees a winnable duel and reaches, which is exactly the invitation being offered.

### Q: Should you let Kvaratskhelia go outside?

This plan deliberately weights the invitation outside without turning it into an absolute rule, because the inside-versus-outside decision depends on your covering structure and has its own dedicated treatment in this series. What the containment angle does is make the outside route marginally more attractive than the inside one, so the carry drifts toward the touchline and compression happens without extra bodies. Committing fully to showing outside is a different decision with different costs, particularly against a delivery threat, and a coach should make it deliberately rather than inheriting it from a coaching cliche.

### Q: How do you stop the pass into Kvaratskhelia's feet?

You do not prevent it, you degrade it. Any press aggressive enough to deny that pass consistently opens the areas his side is built to attack, so starving him concedes more than it saves. Instead, press the supplier rather than the receiver so the pass arrives earlier and flatter, occupy his inside shoulder so the reception angles toward the touchline rather than toward goal, and assign a tracker to the overlapping full-back so he receives without an immediate second option. Those three influences do not stop the supply; they lower the quality of it, and quality of reception is what the whole duel turns on.

### Q: How do goalkeepers prepare for Kvaratskhelia shooting?

Set a half-step further from the near post than the default against a wide carry, because the touchline angle and covering bodies already protect the near side while the far corner is the target. Delay the weight transfer until the striking foot is planted, since committing early loses to both the shot and the disguised cutback to the penalty spot. Treat those two outcomes as equally likely. The most useful thing a goalkeeper can communicate during the sequence is not an instruction to close down but the position of the covering defender, because he has the clearest view of the structure behind the duel.

### Q: What do you accept conceding in order to contain Kvaratskhelia?

Possession in the left channel, territory, the switch of play to the far flank, the early cross, and a run of middle-third free kicks. Those are real costs and the plan should be sold to players with the costs stated, because a scheme that looks passive erodes confidence unless everyone knows the passivity is purchased. What you buy is the absence of two-versus-one situations at the edge of your penalty area. The trade is good for sides with organized, aerially competent central defenders and a reliable goalkeeper, and questionable for sides that defend crosses badly.

### Q: What is the fallback if the plan against Kvaratskhelia fails?

Four failures with four responses. If the containment defender cannot hold his distance, swap the assignment to a composed centre-back by shifting to a back three rather than lecturing him at half time. If the covering midfielder keeps stepping forward, move that job to a natural holding midfielder. If the opposition builds a wide overload, drop the near-side forward permanently into the midfield line and write off your attacking transitions from that flank. If he drifts into central areas, hand the containment job to whoever owns that zone rather than following him with the full-back.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle a close control dribbler like Kvaratskhelia?

Cut the plan to two instructions and ignore the rest. The nearest defender never dives in, ever, regardless of the touchline. The next nearest teammate stands roughly ten metres behind and inside him and does not move forward. At amateur level, compliance with two simple rules beats sophisticated shape nobody executes under pressure, and the elimination arithmetic is harsher at lower levels because recovery structures reset more slowly. On a poor surface, increase the containment distance rather than reducing it, because unpredictable bounce hurts the defender's footwork more than the attacker's touch.

### Q: What do analysts track when scouting Kvaratskhelia?

Four observable markers, all checkable in one viewing. Where his dangerous sequences begin, meaning whether the first touch pushes the ball into a running lane or keeps it inside his own reach. What happens to his success rate when a second defender arrives, which is the single most diagnostic count. The direction of his first movement after receiving, since attracting a defender often means moving laterally rather than forward. And the tempo of his carries, because acceleration removes the proximity his game depends on. The output should be a one-page distance recommendation and covering depth, not a highlight package.

### Q: Which sides have coped with Kvaratskhelia and what did they do?

Three approaches recur. The disciplined narrow block with a designated non-engaging full-back, whose signature is low tackle numbers in the wide area and high crosses faced. The structural approach, which presses the left-sided build-up to force the ball to the opposite flank and accepts higher risk in exchange for fewer duels. And the aggressive approach, which attacks the flank he defends rather than the one he attacks, occupying him with defensive work. What does not appear among the successful versions is the man-oriented double-team in open play, which produces exactly the two-versus-one situations the arithmetic predicts.

### Q: Is Kvaratskhelia harder to defend in tight areas or in open space?

Tight areas, and the answer runs against instinct. Open space contains no defenders to eliminate, so a close control specialist alone in open ground faces the same problem any attacker does and beating one man there produces another duel rather than a chance. Congested areas near the touchline are target-rich, because one touch and a body swerve can remove two of three nearby defenders and turn a trapped situation into a shooting position. The plan's answer is to make congestion structural rather than proximate: bodies present, positioned outside his working range and behind the ball.
