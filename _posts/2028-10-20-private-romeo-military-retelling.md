## Private Romeo: The Military Retelling and Its Verdict

Private Romeo is the most instructive of the modern screen retellings of Romeo and Juliet because it changes nearly everything about the play except the words. Alan Brown's film, written and directed by Brown and first shown at the Frameline festival in San Francisco on 20 June 2011, moves the tragedy into the fictional McKinley Military Academy, where eight cadets left behind on an emptied campus are reading the play for a literature class and find its speeches escaping the classroom and reorganising their lives. The lovers are two of the cadets: Sam Singleton, played by Seth Numrich, who takes Romeo's part, and Glenn Mangan, played by Matt Doyle, who takes Juliet's. Nothing in the verse is re-gendered. The pronouns stand exactly as the Oxford text prints them. The deaths, however, are withheld: Brown refused, on stated principle, to end a film about young gay love with the lovers punished or killed. The result is a work that keeps the play's language with unusual fidelity, discards its catastrophe with unusual deliberateness, and rebuilds its feud as a single institution policing its own members. The verdict of this article is that the film earns its place in the play's screen history on those three decisions: it is a genuine queering of the tragedy rather than a novelty casting stunt, its academy is a true substitute for the two households rather than a backdrop, and its survival ending is an argument about the play, not an evasion of it. The film's limits are real, and they are named where they fall: the original dialogue Brown allows his cadets is thin, the picture depends on the hostility of its setting for its stakes, and its scale is small. Within those limits, no other retelling tests the play's machinery as cleanly.

![Two cadets in uniform inside a military academy dormitory in Private Romeo - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The facts of the production frame everything that follows. Brown produced the film with Agathe David-Weill and Kevin Ginty; Derek McKane photographed it; Nicholas Wright is credited with the music; the running time is 98 minutes; Wolfe Releasing distributed it. The cast of eight was drawn entirely from New York theatre, at Brown's insistence on actors with Shakespeare training, and the ensemble shared the Grand Jury Prize for Outstanding Actor in a Feature Film at Outfest, the Los Angeles gay and lesbian film festival, in 2011. The New York Times made the film a Critic's Pick on its theatrical opening in February 2012, while other reviewers divided sharply, and the scholarly literature has since produced two flatly opposed accounts of what the film does: Anthony Guy Patricia, in Queering the Shakespeare Film (2017), holds that Brown succeeds at fully queering the play, and Roel van den Oever, writing in 2021, holds that the film achieves the opposite of its affirmative intention. Both scholars are answered in this article, and the disagreement between them is the sharpest tool available for measuring the film. The play's text is cited throughout from Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition, by act, scene and line, because the film's wager is precisely about that text: what happens to it when the bodies change and the words do not.

## Eight Cadets Left Behind: The Premise and the Frame

The film's situation can be stated in one sentence, and its economy is the first thing to admire. McKinley Military Academy empties for a training exercise; eight cadets remain behind on the deserted campus; in their literature class they are reading Romeo and Juliet; and the play seeps out of the classroom and permeates their lives. The cadets do not travel to Verona, and Verona is not rebuilt around them. They keep their own names, their uniforms, their ranks and their dormitories. What changes is their speech. Scene by scene, the play's text becomes the language in which their alliances, rivalries and desires are conducted, until the boundary between recitation and life is the film's central uncertainty. Sam Singleton drifts into Romeo's part because the part is where his feeling for Glenn Mangan can be spoken; Glenn takes Juliet's lines because the class and the corps assign them, and then because he chooses them. The other six cadets distribute the remaining roles among themselves, doubling as the play's families, its authority and its casualties. The device belongs to a family of classroom-haunting adaptations, but Brown's version is distinguished by the completeness of the takeover: by the film's middle, the cadets have almost no language left that is not Shakespeare's.

### Why does Private Romeo open in a classroom instead of with the Prologue?

The film opens in the classroom because the classroom is its contract with the audience: the play enters as set work, spoken by cadets in uniform at desks, and every later scene measures how far the text has travelled from that tame beginning. Brown cut the Chorus's Prologue, so no voice announces the ending in advance.

The omitted Prologue deserves a paragraph of its own, because the omission is the film's first and most consequential cut. Shakespeare's Chorus promises the whole shape of the story before it starts: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, 1-2), and, six lines later, "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 6). Tom Ue, interviewing Brown for Film International in May 2012, put the absence among his first questions, and Brown's answer was editorial rather than conceptual: he had written and shot more contemporary material than the finished film keeps, and in the cutting room he felt the urge to reach Shakespeare's play faster, since Shakespeare is, as he put it, a far better dramatist than he is. The effect, whatever the motive, is structural. A film that withholds the Prologue withholds the tragedy's death sentence. The audience that knows the play waits for the machinery of fate to engage; the audience that does not know it watches a love story whose ceiling has not been announced. Brown's ending, in which the lovers live, is prepared by this silence at the start. The star-crossed contract is never signed, so its terms can be renegotiated.
The film's own banner confesses the design. Its publicity took as tagline a line from the balcony scene, "Love goes toward love, as schoolboys from their books" (2.2), Juliet's simile for the eagerness with which love runs to its object, as a schoolboy runs from his lessons. No other line in the play could have served. It is the tragedy's one great image drawn from schoolboy life, and Brown's film is the schoolboy realisation of it: a corps of boys released, by an emptied campus, from the academy's books into the play's. The simile's suppressed second half completes the thought the poster leaves hanging, for the line continues with its opposite, love parting from love as reluctantly as a boy trudges toward school. The film lives inside both halves. Its cadets run toward the play exactly as schoolboys run from their books, and the institution waits at the story's edge like the school in the simile, ready to reclaim them at the exercise's end. A tagline is a small thing, a distributor's ornament, but this one was chosen with a scholar's precision, and it tells a first-time spectator the film's whole method before a frame has passed: the play will not be modernised into the cadets' world; the cadets will be released into the play, at schoolboy speed, in the direction of love.


The frame also governs how the film's spaces work. Because the cadets never leave the academy, every location is double: the classroom is a classroom and then a stage; the basketball court is where Sam first meets Glenn in the film's re-staging of the lovers' meeting, two boys who know each other by sight seeing each other, for the first time in earnest, because the emptied campus has changed the context in which they meet; the dormitory, the locker room, the parking garage and the science laboratory each take a second, Shakespearean identity as the text spreads. Shakespeare's stage built Verona out of words spoken on a bare platform, and Brown's film, for all its location realism, works the same way: the academy is never disguised, only re-read. A staircase in darkness carries the Queen Mab speech; a laboratory bench carries the Friar's cell. The spectator's pleasure is the double exposure, and the film's method is to keep both images in focus at once rather than letting the modern one dissolve into costume drama.

## Why "Private"? The Title's Three Duties

The title does quiet triple duty, and a study of the film should account for it. Private is first a rank, the lowest rung of the military ladder, the standing of boys who command nothing and are commanded by everyone; a film about the least powerful people inside an armed institution names itself for their powerlessness, and its Romeo is a private in both the army's sense and the story's, a junior whose desires have no rank at all. Private is second a condition, the privacy the cadets are systematically denied: dormitory bunks, communal showers of attention, a corps in which every feeling is potentially public property and discovery is the only crime. The lovers' whole plot is a campaign for privacy, for a classroom text's speeches to mean something between two people that the institution cannot audit, and the title names the scarcity they are fighting over. Private is third an interior, the private self that the governing policy of the film's moment ordered its soldiers to keep unspoken; Don't Ask, Don't Tell was, in its formal essence, a law about the border between the private and the declared, and Brown's title plants the film exactly on that border. A Romeo who is a private, seeking the private, in defence of the private: the three senses converge on the film's single subject, and the economy of the naming matches the economy of the production, one word doing the work of three, as eight actors do the work of a city and one academy does the work of two households.

## From a Forbidden Book to an Assigned Text: The Calarco Inheritance

Private Romeo did not invent its device, and an honest account of the film has to begin one step earlier, with the stage play from which it descends. Joe Calarco's Shakespeare's R&J, first produced in New York in 1997 and a long-running off-Broadway success in the years that followed, sets four students at a strict boys' boarding school discovering a forbidden copy of Romeo and Juliet and acting it out among themselves, taking every part, until the enactment exposes the feelings the school forbids. Brown saw Calarco's play and built his film on its foundation; reference listings for Private Romeo credit the writing to Brown alongside Calarco and Shakespeare, and Aurélie Bourg's dissertation on queering canonical Shakespeare (Université de Nantes, 2018) treats the two works as a genealogy, the film taking its inspiration from the play. The descent matters because Brown's alterations to the inherited design are exactly where his film's argument lives, and they can be listed with precision.

### What separates Brown's academy from the boarding school of Shakespeare's R&J?

Three substitutions separate the film from its parent. Brown moves the school from Calarco's boarding house to a military academy, so discipline is drilled and ranked rather than ruled. He enlarges four students to eight cadets, enough to staff two factions. And he inverts the book's status: Calarco's students steal a forbidden text, while Brown's are assigned it in class.

Each substitution does work. The military setting converts school discipline into the play's true subject, institutional authority over young bodies, and it connects the film to the American military's own policy battle over gay service, which Brown named as his starting point. The enlarged cast lets the academy divide against itself, so a feud can form inside one corps rather than between two families. The inverted book is the subtlest change and the most telling. In Calarco, Shakespeare is contraband: the boys' desire and the text are both smuggled goods, and the play's romance with the forbidden writes itself. In Brown, the text is curriculum. The institution orders the cadets to read the most famous love story in the language, drills them in its speeches, and then polices the consequences when two of them take the speeches seriously. The hypocrisy is the film's engine, and it is a more political machine than Calarco's: the danger is no longer that the boys found the book, but that the school cannot control what its own set text says. A spectator who treats Private Romeo as a simple relocation of the play misses the genealogy; a spectator who treats it as a filming of Calarco misses the redesign. It is a second-generation adaptation, and its differences from its parent are its thesis.

The credit question deserves plain statement, because popular accounts of the film routinely assign the conception to Brown alone. The documented record is more layered. Brown is the film's writer and director; the device of schoolboys living the play is Calarco's invention, carried into the film with Calarco's name standing in the writing credits as the listings preserve them; the words are Shakespeare's. That three-deep authorship is not a scandal but the normal condition of adaptation, and Brown's contribution is not diminished by naming it: the military academy, the Don't Ask, Don't Tell frame, the doubling scheme worked out from the cadets' motives, the refusal of the tragic ending and the closing song are his. What the genealogy corrects is the lazy summary in which a lone auteur transplants a classic. The film is better understood as an argument conducted across three texts, and its quality lies in how deliberately Brown re-aims the device he inherited.
The inversion of the book also repositions the audience, in a way that separates the film's politics from its parent's. Calarco's spectators watch a discovery: the forbidden copy is found, the enactment is a secret shared with the house, and the audience's pleasure is the pleasure of complicity with contraband. Brown's spectators are seated, from the first scene, inside the institution's own classroom, watching set work. The complicity on offer is the opposite kind: the audience is enrolled on the academy's side of the desk, issued the same text, and then made to watch what the text does to the boys it was issued to. When the corps turns on Glenn, the spectator's position has already been compromised in a way Calarco never attempts, because the spectator has been sitting in the institution's chair since the opening scene, reading along. The film's indictment lands on its own audience first. That is a rarer and more uncomfortable achievement than the frisson of the forbidden book, and it is wholly Brown's, purchased by the single change of the book's status from stolen to assigned.


## Shakespeare's Words, Kept: Pronouns, Cuts and the Retained Text

The film's most quoted decision is its refusal to alter the play's language to fit its casting. Brown was, in his own word, emphatic about retaining the gender pronouns. His reasoning, given to Ue, runs on two rails. The first is musical: to change a line such as "Here comes the lady" into "Here comes the gentleman" would be, he said, messing with the music and beauty of Shakespeare's language; cutting lines is one thing, rewriting them another, and he was not about to start rewriting Shakespeare. The second is historical, and it is the stronger of the two: in Shakespeare's own theatre the women's parts were all played by men and boys, without changing the pronouns. The film's practice is therefore not a modern imposition on the text but a recovery of the text's first performance conditions, in which Juliet was a male performer and the verse's feminine address was already a performed fiction. The result, sustained over 98 minutes, is that the spectator stops hearing the pronouns as errors. The language re-settles. Sam calls Glenn his love in Juliet's words and is answered in Romeo's, and the exchange demonstrates the film's central proposition: the play's desire was never stored in the pronouns. It is stored in the speeches' situations, and those transfer intact.

### Why does Glenn keep Juliet's pronouns in the film?

Glenn keeps Juliet's pronouns because Brown treated the verse as unalterable music and all-male casting as the play's original condition, not a problem to be solved. Changing "lady" to "gentleman," Brown argued, would rewrite Shakespeare; Shakespeare's company changed nothing when boys played the women. The kept pronouns prove the love story needs no translation.

What the film cuts is as significant as what it keeps, and the cutting follows a legible principle: whatever belongs to Verona's civic machinery shrinks, and whatever belongs to the lovers, their friends and their coercion survives. Brown's original script carried a far larger population, adults among them, teachers and commanders for the academy's authority figures; the budget forbade it, and he reimagined the film around eight cadets, rewriting as production began. The finished text redistributes the survivors' lines among the eight and drops the rest of the dramatis personae entirely, Paris most consequentially among them, a case examined in the feud section below. Brown also shot a body of contemporary, non-Shakespearean scenes establishing the cadets' ordinary lives, and removed most of them in the edit to reach the play sooner. The non-Shakespearian dialogue that remains is, as Slant Magazine complained, kept to a minimum. The complaint is accurate as description and mistaken as criticism, for reasons the adjudication section takes up: the film's cadets are meant to be boys who have no adequate language of their own, which is why the borrowed one takes them over. Brown said the conception, embraced by the whole cast, was of teenage boys using Shakespeare's language to express what could not be expressed in their own everyday language, and that rehearsal and shooting never named the cadets by their Shakespearean names, keeping the actors' foundation in their cadet characters. The film's famous fidelity is thus a performance discipline before it is a textual one: eight young men so thoroughly prepared in the verse that the verse can plausibly be the only fluent speech they own.

The fidelity has a texture worth describing. These are not highlights. The film works through the play's sequence with its less excerpted passages intact, and the classroom opening establishes the principle of recitation before the drama re-stages the scenes in earnest: the balcony exchange restaged in the academy's residential quarter, the Queen Mab speech delivered by Hale Appleman's Josh in darkness on a staircase, the secret marriage arranged through Adam Barrie's Friar in a science laboratory, the coercion of Juliet restaged as cadet peer pressure. Quotations from the play in this article follow Levenson's Oxford text, and they show what the film leans on. When Glenn, as Juliet, reaches the name speech, the film's whole method is inside it: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44). The academy runs on names, ranks and labels; the play's most famous speech argues that the thing survives the label. A cadet culture that cannot say what Sam and Glenn are to each other is answered, in advance, by the set text on the syllabus. The film does not underline the moment. It simply lets the speech occur, in a male voice, in uniform, and trusts the audience to hear the argument.
## The Play Staged in Barracks Space: Court, Staircase and Laboratory

A scene-by-scene account of the film's staging shows how completely the academy's floor plan has been mapped onto the play's. The classroom comes first, desks and uniforms, the text at its most institutional. The lovers' meeting is then restaged as a series of meetings rather than a single ballroom encounter, since McKinley holds no balls; the cadets, who have known each other by sight all along, meet in earnest on the basketball court, seeing each other for the first time because the emptied campus has altered the context in which they meet. The transposition is exact in function and inverted in costume: Shakespeare's lovers meet at a feast, in masks and music, inside the enemy's house; Brown's meet in gym clothes, in an echoing court, inside their own. The Queen Mab speech migrates to a staircase in darkness, Mercutio's flight of fancy delivered where the academy's architecture turns vertical and unlit, a cadet's voice unspooling the play's most virtuoso set piece as confidences are actually exchanged in such buildings, half-whispered, on the stairs, after hours. The Friar's cell becomes a science laboratory, its benches and bottles lending the potion plot a plausibility no apothecary set could improve. The coercion of Juliet takes place among the dormitory's bunks. The duel, which Bourg's frame study places at roughly fifty-one minutes in, tips the image into the warm oversaturation her dissertation records, and the final scene, at roughly eighty-seven minutes, carries the same saturation to its height.

The pattern across these stagings is a discipline of substitution that never cheats. Nothing is imported that a military academy would not contain: no ballroom is faked, no street set built, no tomb dressed. The play must find its spaces inside the institution's inventory or go unstaged, and it finds them all, which is the film's quiet proof that the tragedy's geography was never Verona's. Shakespeare's scenes are functions, a meeting, a wooing at a height, a counsel in a cell, a threat in a bedroom, a fight in a public place, and any sufficiently ordered institution contains a room for each. The academy is unusually rich in such rooms precisely because it is an institution for the young: courts for its contests, staircases for its secrets, laboratories for its forbidden knowledge, dormitories where privacy is rationed and surveillance is architecture. Brown's location work simply reads the play's scene list against the academy's floor plan and lets the matches declare themselves.

The staging also sustains the film's central ambiguity, which its admirers and detractors divide over: whether the cadets are performing the play or being performed by it. The classroom opening establishes recitation, boys reading parts aloud under instruction. By the court meeting, the reading has become enactment, the words fitted to real feeling with no audience present and no assignment requiring them. By the hazing and the coercion, the play's machinery is operating on cadets who did not choose their parts, Glenn bound in plastic while the corps enacts its own older ritual in the play's shadow. Brown never supplies a marker, no change of light or music sting, at which recitation ends and possession begins; the colour design does the work instead, saturation deepening as the text's hold deepens, so the spectator gauges the takeover by the temperature of the image. The device keeps the film honest to its source's own ambiguity, for Shakespeare's play likewise never marks the moment at which its lovers stop performing the roles Verona assigned them, the dutiful son, the obedient daughter, and start inhabiting the parts the speeches give them instead. In both works the borrowed script is first a school exercise and then a life, and the horror and the glory of it is that nobody can say when the change occurred.


## The InsightCrunch Private Romeo Note

For the second edition of this study, the material was organised into a reference table, the InsightCrunch Private Romeo note, which sets the play's elements against the film's reframings and names the effect of each change. The note exists because the film's method is systematic: nearly every element of the tragedy is either kept intact, doubled onto a second cadet, translated into an academy equivalent, or removed on a stated principle, and a table is the honest way to display a system. Read down the rows and a pattern emerges that prose alone would blur. The lovers' language is the great constant; the social machinery around them is the great variable. Brown alters who enforces, who threatens and who survives, while altering almost nothing of what is said. The table also exposes the film's boldest absences, above all Paris, whose removal from the cast list quietly deletes the tragedy's arranged-marriage engine and forces the film to find its coercion elsewhere. Scholars and students citing the film in adaptation essays will find the note's sharpest use in its final column, where each reframing's dramatic consequence is stated as a claim that can be argued with, rather than as a summary that cannot.

The note's pattern repays slow reading. Nothing in the left-hand column is merely modernised, in the way a relocation dresses the play in new clothes; each row records a decision about where the tragedy's force actually resides. Brown's answers are consistent. Force resides in enforcement, so the enforcers are all rebuilt. It resides in language, so the language is left alone. It resides in the expectation of death, so the deaths are the one element openly refused, and the refusal is prepared from the first scene by the missing Prologue. An adaptation that changed the pronouns and kept the corpses would be the conventional product; Brown inverts the convention at both ends, and the table is where the inversion can be seen entire.
Two rows of the note reward particular pressure. The Prince row is the table's smallest and its most diagnostic, since a one-line change, the law spoken by a fellow cadet, converts the play's political frame into the film's: Verona's order descends from above the feud, while McKinley's circulates inside it, and the whole institutional reading of the adaptation can be unfolded from that single casting fact. The closing-music row is the table's only entry with no Shakespearean counterpart to invert, a song where the play has a tomb, and it functions as the note's control case: when Brown finally needs a text the play cannot supply, he reaches outside drama altogether, to a popular standard in the public domain, and the reach confirms the system the other rows describe. Everything the play could be made to provide, kept, doubled or translated, was used. What it could not provide, a surviving lovers' future in their own voices, had to be imported, and the import is marked in the table by the one row whose left-hand column names an element the film simply refuses.
The note's discipline is worth stating as a rule for adaptation study generally: no reframing is credited to the film unless the film can be shown performing it, in a casting decision, a cut, a reassignment of lines or a photographed register. Brown's work survives that discipline intact, which is more than most celebrated adaptations can claim, and the table's fourteen rows are the audit trail.



## The Institution Is the Feud: The Academy as Both Households

The brief for any military retelling names its own central problem: two households require a feud, and a single school has only itself. Brown's solution is the film's most original structural idea, and it begins with an admission. The rival-families feud, he told Ue, is downplayed in the film; the actors found it the hardest element to motivate, two groups of cadets inventing a hatred with no ground under it. In its place Brown set the conflict he considered the real one: individual desire set against authority and institutions. The academy therefore supplies both households from inside one corps. The cadets divide into the lovers' circle and its enforcers, and the division is not blood but function: rank, seniority, sporting rivalry, dormitory custom and the unwritten curriculum of hazing. Josh Neff, the film's Mercutio, is also its chief aggressor, leading the pranks, the challenges and the physical attacks that reviewer Rich Cline catalogued in Shadows on the Wall; the feud's violence, elsewhere in the play a matter of swords in the street, becomes the discipline cadets inflict on each other in the institution's own idiom.

### Why is the Prince of Verona a cadet in the film?

The Prince is a cadet because Brown had no adults to give the part to and made a virtue of the constraint: authority at McKinley is peer-administered, a senior boy imitating the institution that trained him. Ken Lee's Escalus issues the play's warnings in the academy's voice, which is the film's point about how such institutions govern, through boys formed.

Shakespeare's Prince opens the play's civic action with a threat aimed at both houses alike: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1.93-94). In Brown's film the same warning issues from Charlie Barnett's Ken Lee, one cadet among eight, and the difference is the analysis. Verona's prince is law from outside the feud; McKinley's is the feud's own product, a boy performing command. The film's academy does not need parents, elders or officers to crush its lovers, because it has already taught its children to crush each other. That is the sense in which the institutional setting is the feud, exactly as the adaptation's design requires: the two households have been merged into a single self-policing body, and the lovers' offence is not against a rival name but against the corps' code of what a cadet may feel.

The hazing sequence is where the translation becomes literal. Glenn is seized at night by his fellow cadets, bound to a chair with plastic wrap and silenced with tape across his mouth, in a scene Brown researched from accounts and footage of real academy hazing, reasoning that the victim's ordeal was procedural rather than personal: it was, in the phrase he used, Glenn's turn. The staging matters more than the violence. Mercutio's great line of communal blame, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99), has no purchase in a world with one house, and Brown's film does not pretend otherwise; what it offers instead is the single house turning its plague inward, on the member whose desire has been detected. The scene is the feud's street brawl rewritten as initiation ritual, and it carries an implication the play's Verona only implies: the institution harms its own not despite its code of loyalty but through it.

Paris completes the design, by absence. Brown considered embodying the Count and rejected it: Paris, he reasoned, was more concept than character in his thinking, the idea of heterosexuality itself, a standing threat rather than a person. The reasoning is exact about the play. Shakespeare's Paris is barely characterised because his function is pressure: he is the future the Capulets have chosen for Juliet, the acceptable desire against which hers is criminal. Remove the actor and the pressure remains, diffused into everything the academy expects of its cadets, their futures, their families' hopes, the code. The coercion scene of the play's third act, in which Juliet is ordered toward the match, survives in the film with Capulet's lines reassigned to Josh and the Lady's lines to Gus: the arranged future is urged on Glenn by his friends, in the dormitory, as concern. No father is required. The film thereby finds the one form of the feud that a family romance could never supply: oppression with no oppressor's face, only the institution speaking through the people the victim loves. A useful contrast stands inside the play's modern screen tradition: the action remix treated in the series' study of Romeo Must Die armoured its feud in the masculine theatre of crime syndicates and martial arts, giving the households money, weapons and territory, whereas Brown strips all three away and leaves the feud standing on nothing but a school rule and the boys who enforce it, which is why [the action remix and its syndicate feud](/2028/10/13/romeo-must-die-action-remix/) reads, beside this film, as the feud with everything added, and Private Romeo as the feud with everything removed except the enforcement.

## Sport, Rifles and the Duel: Violence in the Academy's Idiom

An academy trains its boys for violence and then must find peacetime channels for the training, and the film's feud runs through those channels: sport first of all. The basketball court that hosts the lovers' meeting is also the corps' licensed arena, the place where aggression between cadets is not merely permitted but organised, watched and scored, and the film's geography insists on the doubleness, desire and combat sharing the one sprung floor. Cline's catalogue of Josh's conduct, the pranks, the sporting challenges, the physical attacks, traces the feud's escalation through exactly these idioms, from the games cadets play to the attacks they stage, with no border marked between them. That continuum is the film's version of Verona's street culture, where honour's quarrels likewise begin in gesture and banter and end in steel. Shakespeare's opening brawl grows out of servants' thumb-biting; Brown's grows out of the locker room's economy of dares. In both worlds the young men's violence is a language they are fluent in before they are fluent in anything else, and in both worlds the love plot is conducted by boys who must learn a second language, poetry in the play, the play itself in the film, while the first language keeps interrupting.

The duel, when the play's machinery reaches it, is staged inside that continuum rather than imported from Verona's fencing culture, and Bourg's observation that the sequence tips the film's colour into warm oversaturation marks it as the point where the text's possession of the academy becomes total: the institution's own violence, finally spoken in the play's words, photographed in the play's colours. The sequence matters to the film's argument because it decides what the survival ending means. Brown does not spare his cadets the feud's violence; the corps fights, and the fighting is real. What he withholds is the violence's traditional yield, the bodies that would convert a schoolboy war into a tragedy and let the institution mourn itself clean. The duel without the deaths is the film's thesis in a single sequence: the machinery can be run, at full heat and full colour, and stopped before its customary payment, and the stopping is legible as mercy rather than failure because everything before it has been played at full seriousness. A version that softened the violence would earn no such legibility. Brown's earns it by refusing to soften anything except the outcome.

## Two Visual Registers: The Grey Academy and the Saturated Play

The film's cinematography states its thesis before its dialogue can. Brown and McKane designed two palettes and achieved them in camera, not in post-production. The academy's official life is desaturated to the edge of monotony: greys, khakis, the pale blues of institutional paint, cadets filed through spaces that photograph like administration. When the play takes hold, the colour deepens and warms, the frame saturates, and the same dormitories and courts appear transfigured. The spectator learns the film's geography of mind within minutes: flat colour is the institution, rich colour is the borrowed language, and the lovers live in the second register whenever they can reach it. Bourg's dissertation, the most detailed frame-level study the film has received, records the effect at its strongest in the duel and in the final scene, where the image tips into warm oversaturation, an excess of colour answering an excess of feeling that the academy's palette cannot contain. The design is the opposite of decorative. It tells the audience, in every shot, which of the film's two worlds a scene belongs to, and the tragedy of the cadets' situation can be measured by how rarely the saturated register is allowed to hold.

The camera's stillness serves the same argument. Brown stages the verse in long, close attention: faces at intimate distance, speeches delivered into darkness or across a court rather than out at an audience, the Queen Mab flight unspooling on a staircase with the speaker nearly invisible. Where the play's public scenes are required, the emptied campus supplies a ready-made theatrical vacancy, corridors and courts cleared of everyone but the eight, so that the academy's spaces keep their architectural truth while functioning as the bare stage the text was written for. The film was shot in the summer of 2010 on the SUNY Maritime campus in the Bronx, a working maritime college standing in for McKinley, and the location's authenticity, its drills fields, its river light, its barracks geometry, does quiet work throughout: nothing on screen looks built, so the play's invasion of the real reads as an event inside a documentary world rather than a costume laid over one. Close framing is also the film's answer to the charge, examined later in this article, that its lovers blur into sameness: at the distance Brown shoots them, Numrich's and Doyle's faces are as differentiated as faces can be, and the similarity the hostile reviews perceived belongs to the uniforms, which is to say to the institution, which is the subject.
The desaturation deserves one more sentence of analysis, because it is the closest the film comes to visualising censorship. The academy's grey is not realism; real campuses are colourful enough. It is the institution's preferred image of itself, drained of the hues in which feeling would show, and the play's saturated register is what the world looks like when the drainage stops. A spectator who learns the code early begins to watch for colour the way the cadets watch for privacy, as the scarce resource the story is about. That the saturation peaks, on Bourg's evidence, in the duel and at the close yokes the film's most violent and most tender passages in a single register, the register of the text fully in possession of the image, which is as concise a statement of the film's position as its director ever gave in words: inside the play, everything is more alive, including the danger.


## Doubling at McKinley: Eight Actors, Eleven Parts

The cast is the film's smallest number and its largest idea. Eight actors carry the play's world between them, eleven named parts on the reference count, and the doublings are not economy measures disguised as concept; Brown worked them out from the cadets' psychology, and said so. The clearest case is the film's most consequential reassignment. Capulet's coercion of Juliet, the threats and the deadline, is delivered by Josh Neff, the Mercutio figure, while Lady Capulet's share of the scene falls to Gus Sanchez, the Benvolio figure. Brown's reasoning, explained to Ue, was that if the scene's purpose is a friend pressuring Glenn about the acceptable future, then the lines belong to Glenn's actual friend, because the coercion that matters in a cadet's life arrives from inside his circle: the threat is Josh's because Josh is the person Glenn has to survive agreeing with. The doubling thus converts the play's domestic tyranny into peer enforcement, and it makes Mercutio's later fate, translated into the academy's violence, carry a double loss, the wit and the persecutor extinguished in the same body.

### Why does Josh Neff deliver Capulet's threats to Glenn?

Josh delivers Capulet's threats because Brown assigned every doubled line by motive rather than by chart: the pressure on Glenn to accept the future chosen for him had to come from the cadet whose opinion governs his standing in the corps. A friend's coercion is the academy's real instrument, so Mercutio's actor speaks the father's lines.

The remaining doublings follow the same logic of compression. Omar Madsen, Chris Bresky's cadet, takes the Nurse, and the play's bawdy confidante becomes a contemporary inside the lovers' secret, giggling where the original teases, complicit where the original conspires. Adam Hersh, played by Adam Barrie, takes Friar Laurence, and Brown's conception of the Friar as the school's drug dealer gives the part its academy logic: the cadet who can supply altered states in a science laboratory is the cadet who can plausibly supply a potion, a plan and a hiding place, counsel and chemistry from the same unsupervised peer. Brown shot and then cut a scene of the Friar and the Nurse smoking together, a loss to the film's comedy that its discipline accepted. Gus Sanchez doubles Benvolio with the Lady Capulet part already described; Carlos Moreno, Bobby Moreno's cadet, concentrates the feud's aggression in Tybalt; Ken Lee's Prince has been examined with the feud. The pattern to notice is that no cadet doubles across the film's moral divide at random: wit doubles with tyranny, friendship with parenthood, peacekeeping with its opposite, while the lovers themselves never double at all. Sam and Glenn play one part each, because the film's lovers are the only people in it who are not divided against themselves.

The casting that made the scheme work was theatrical to an unusual degree. Casting director Stephanie Holbrook saw more than a hundred actors, Brown insisting from the outset on performers with Shakespeare in their training, on the grounds that the audience for such a film would be Shakespeare-literate and would detect pretence instantly. Callbacks were run as workshops, the candidates worked on their feet, and Brown told Ue that what he was watching for, in Doyle's case explicitly, was a particular quality of spirit rather than a look. The two leads arrived with a shared fate attached: Numrich and Doyle met on this film, auditioned for War Horse at Lincoln Center while it was shooting, and went on to appear together in that production, Numrich originating Albert Narracott and Doyle playing Billy Narracott and understudying Albert, a coincidence of casting that followed the film's own themes of young men, uniformed service and devotion into the theatre's record books. Doyle sings, which became the film's ending, a piece of casting fortune examined in its own section below. The ensemble's joint Outfest acting prize, shared by all eight rather than awarded to a lead, ratified the design from outside: the film is an ensemble instrument, and its jury citation at Los Angeles praised the performances' coherence as a company's work.
The insistence on Shakespeare-trained actors repays attention, because it reverses the usual economy of screen adaptation. Commercial Shakespeare films cast for recognition and coach the verse; Brown cast for the verse and let recognition go. The consequence is audible. The film's speeches are not decorated dialogue but working language, handled with the ease of actors who have carried such lines on stages, and the ease is load-bearing: the premise asks the audience to believe that eight teenagers could plausibly live inside this text, and only performers for whom the text is a native tongue can sell that belief for ninety-eight minutes. The workshop callbacks belong to the same economy. Brown was not matching faces to a character chart; he was testing whether young actors could think in the verse together, in a room, as an ensemble, because the film he was making has no star performance to hide behind. Its lovers are first among equals in a company of eight, and the company's joint prize at Outfest recognised the fact with unusual accuracy. The award's distribution is the film's method stated as an honour: nobody in Private Romeo acts alone, and the play, which distributes its poetry across a whole city, is honoured by a production that distributes its acting the same way.


## No Adults at McKinley: A World Governed by Peers

The film's most eloquent absence is generational. Shakespeare's Verona is run by adults who fail: parents who coerce, a friar who schemes, a prince who threatens and arrives too late. Brown's early scripts contained their counterparts, the academy's teachers and commanders, and the production's budget removed them; he reimagined the film around the eight cadets, and the constraint became the film's sociology. McKinley has no adults in it. Every function the play assigns to age and office is impersonated by a teenager: the Friar's counsel and chemistry come from a cadet, the Prince's law from a cadet, the parents' coercion from cadets doubling the parts in the dormitory. The result is a world in which authority is entirely ventriloquised, boys performing the institution that formed them, and the performance is the critique. Verona's tragedy can be blamed on its elders, on Capulet's temper and the Friar's plotting, and the blame consoles: the young are innocent, the old are guilty, the system is its officers. McKinley offers no such consolation. The system at McKinley is its victims, reproduced in them, enforced by them, suffered by them, and the film's horror is the discovery that an institution thorough enough does not need to attend its own atrocities. It has already been memorised by the children.

The absence also rewrites the play's machinery of rescue. In Shakespeare, the lovers' hope runs through adult channels, a friar's plan, a letter, a potion timed to a family's grief, and the machinery fails by accident. In the film there are no channels, because there is nobody senior to appeal to, nobody outside the corps to whom the secret could be carried; Adam's laboratory counsel is the whole of the lovers' infrastructure, a peer's chemistry set against an institution. The tragedy's what-if, the letter that might have arrived, has no equivalent, and the film is honest enough not to invent one. What replaces the machinery is endurance. The lovers survive not because a plan succeeds but because the film declines to operate the machine that kills them, and their survival, unrescued and unabsolved, is the peer world's one mercy: where no adults govern, no adult sentence is finally carried out either. The corps wounds its own, in the hazing chair and the coercion scene, but it cannot quite bring itself, or is never quite ordered, to finish the play's work. Brown's ending grows directly out of his emptiest set: a school with no teachers in it cannot expel anybody properly, and so, at the last, it sings instead.

## The Ending the Play Forbids: Survival and a Sung Standard

Every adaptation of the play negotiates with its ending, and most negotiations are cosmetic: the deaths arrive on schedule, dressed in the period's style. Brown's film is the rare retelling that refuses the delivery. Sam and Glenn survive. Josh survives; Carlos survives; the academy closes over the episode and the lovers are left standing in it, alive, their future unwritten rather than sealed. Brown's reasons were stated without embarrassment and deserve to be taken as seriously as any director's note in the play's screen history. He refused, he said, to make a film in which the gay lovers are punished or killed off; the culture already had its monument to that pattern in Brokeback Mountain; and he wanted, in his words, gay love stories on film that end well. The refusal is usually reported as sentiment, a director sparing his audience. It is better understood as criticism. The tragic ending of Romeo and Juliet is not a law of the story; it is a settlement the story reaches with the world that forbids the lovers, a settlement in which the feud buries its children and is shamed by the burial. Brown's film asks what the story becomes when the settlement is declined, when the forbidding world is denied its corpses and its catharsis. The play's own Friar names the mechanism the film is refusing: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9). Brown keeps the delights, edits out the ends, and leaves the audience to decide whether the prophecy failed or was simply never spoken in this world, because the Prologue that would have spoken it was cut.
What survival costs the film should be counted as carefully as what it buys. The play's ending purchases a civic reconciliation with the lovers' bodies: the houses are shamed into peace, statues are promised, the Prince delivers the epitaph. Brown's survivors get none of that settlement. No authority at McKinley repents on screen; no golden statues are commissioned; the institution that produced the crisis is not arraigned by it, because there are no corpses to arraign it with. The lovers win their lives and nothing else, no absolution for the corps, no public vindication, only the private fact of each other and a song. The austerity is the argument's completion. A film that killed its lovers would let the academy, and the audience, discharge the debt in tears and call the discharge justice, which is the bargain the play itself strikes and the bargain Don't Ask, Don't Tell struck with its victims, whose service was honoured most safely when it was silent or finished. Brown denies every party that comfort. The cadets remain inside the institution, unabsolved and unburied, and the spectator is sent away with the unresolved thing itself, two living boys in a place built to deny them, which is a harder ending than the tomb and, on the film's terms, the only honest one.
The play's fifth act is, structurally, an investigation: a watch is summoned, letters are read, the Friar explains, the Prince apportions blame, and the audience's last experience of Verona is of a community assembling the truth too late. Brown's survival ending abolishes the investigation along with the deaths, and the abolition is easy to miss because the film's final movement, which Bourg's study locates in the warm oversaturation of its closing frames, retains the play's shape of gathering and reckoning. What the corps gathers to reckon with is not a mystery but a fact, each other, alive, in the aftermath of the hazing and the coercion and the duel, with no corpse to organise their remorse and no elder to organise their pardon. The play's community is educated by death into statues and speeches; the film's is left uneducated, holding a living problem it must go on housing. That is why the song is necessary and why it must come from Doyle alone: the investigation's verdict, which Shakespeare assigns to the Prince, is reassigned by Brown to the person the investigation would have been about, and he delivers it not as a judgement but as a standard, in his own voice, to nobody in particular and to the whole corps at once.



### Why does a 1913 popular song close a film built from Shakespeare's verse?

The standard closes the film because it is the one text in the picture belonging to neither the play nor the academy: a public-domain song, chosen after Brown heard Matt Doyle sing at Joe's Pub, sung in the actor's own voice. After ninety minutes of borrowed language, it is the film's first utterance no institution assigned.

The song's production history is a small parable of the film's economy. Doyle had been cast before Brown knew he could sing; a video of the actor performing Sam Cooke's "A Change Is Gonna Come" at Joe's Pub reached the director, and the ending was rebuilt around the discovery. The original plan had been a closing sequence in which all eight cadets performed a music video together, in the manner of the two lip-synched indie-rock videos the film incorporates elsewhere, the cadets' own media culture breaking through the verse. What replaced it is sparer and stronger: one cadet, alone, singing a standard written by James V. Monaco and Joseph McCarthy in 1913, a song old enough to be in the public domain, which mattered to a production with no licensing budget, and direct enough in its lyric to say, in plain commercial English, what the play's poetry had been saying in code for the whole film. The choice converts constraint into meaning twice over. A penniless production reaches for a free song; a film about boys who cannot speak their desire in their own words ends with a boy singing somebody else's words that are, at last, only about desire, with no character standing between the singer and the song. The cadet is not performing Glenn performing Juliet. He is performing, and the distinction is the film's final claim about what its lovers have won.

Music elsewhere in the film works by similar indirection. Nicholas Wright's credited score underlines rather than competes, and the two lip-synch videos, in which the cadets mouth along to indie rock for their own cameras, are the film's only other sustained musical statements. Critics who disliked the film singled those sequences out in opposite directions: Slant Magazine thought them the film's best moments, the points where Shakespeare haunts a recognisably contemporary boyhood instead of replacing it, while the New York Daily News found the video and social-media additions clunky. The disagreement is instructive because both notices agree on the fact: the videos are where the cadets' own culture surfaces. Brown's film is often described as austere, and in its verse passages it is, but its musical design tells a more complete story, a corps of boys whose native art forms are the group lip-synch and the borrowed standard, singing other people's songs at every level of the production, because singing their own was never on the syllabus. The Liebestod tradition, in which love and death consummate each other and which the series examines in [the Liebestod of love and death entwined](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/), is the operatic form of the bargain Brown refuses; his lovers get a song instead of a tomb, and the song is a beginning.

## A Film of the Policy's Last Year: Don't Ask, Don't Tell

Private Romeo cannot be separated from the American military's Don't Ask, Don't Tell policy, the law under which gay service members could serve only on condition of silence, in force from 21 December 1993 until its repeal took effect on 20 September 2011. Brown called the film his rumination on homophobia, and more specifically on that policy, and the production's dates fix the film inside the policy's history with unusual precision. The film was shot in the summer of 2010, while the policy stood unrepealed and its future was still contested; Brown noted that the law had not yet been overturned when the film was made. It premiered at Frameline on 20 June 2011, after the repeal had been signed in December 2010 but before the repeal took effect, so its first audiences watched it inside the policy's long twilight. Its theatrical opening in New York came on 10 February 2012, months after the policy ended, when the world the film depicts had already become, in law, a historical subject. Few films are so neatly bracketed by the statute they address, and the bracketing gives Private Romeo a documentary value beyond its artistry: it is a portrait of an institution's sexual regime painted from inside the regime's final year, by a production that did not know, while shooting, whether the regime would survive.

The policy's logic is the academy's logic, which is why the setting does argumentative work no boarding school could do. Don't Ask, Don't Tell did not forbid homosexuality as a fact; it forbade its utterance and its evidence, policing speech, association and disclosure. A military academy is the policy in miniature and in training: a total institution for the young, where desire is governed by rules about what may be said and shown, where surveillance is a form of education, and where the punished offence is never the feeling but its discovery. Brown's cadets live under exactly that dispensation. Nobody at McKinley investigates what Sam and Glenn feel; the machinery engages when the feeling becomes visible, speakable, undeniable. The film's deepest congruence with its moment is linguistic. A policy whose essence was the management of naming produced a film in which desire can be enacted, filmed, even celebrated, so long as it is spoken in a dead dramatist's words rather than in the cadets' own. The scholarly quarrel examined in the next sections turns on precisely this point, and it is not a coincidence: van den Oever's essay on the film reads it through Judith Butler's account of injurious and regulated speech, the framework Don't Ask, Don't Tell itself invites. The policy is not the film's background. It is the film's subject wearing the play as its uniform.
The repeal's chronology adds one more layer to the film's position. Because the repeal was signed in December 2010 yet took effect only in September 2011, the film's festival audiences in the summer of 2011 watched it in a country where the policy had been condemned by its own legislature and still governed every serving cadet's real counterpart. The academy on screen was therefore neither documentary present nor settled past but a portrait of a condemned institution in its final season, and the film's classroom scenes acquire a particular point in that light: the military's own educational machinery, drilling the young in silence about themselves, is shown handing those same young men the most eloquent love poetry in the language as set work. Brown could not have timed the irony deliberately, since the shoot preceded the signing, but the film's reception was shaped by it. Private Romeo arrived as an elegy for a regime its first audiences were in the act of burying, and its refusal to bury its lovers reads, against that calendar, as the film's contribution to the burial: the policy died in 2011, and the lovers, for once, did not.


## The Queer Genealogy: Mercutio, Boy Actors and the Play's Own Fabric

A same-sex Romeo and Juliet is sometimes reviewed as though the idea were a provocation invented for the occasion. The scholarly record runs the other way. Patricia's study, the fullest the film has received, begins from the premise that cross-sex love in Romeo and Juliet does not operate independently of same-sex love: the play's heterosexual romance is, in his formulation, imbricated in male homosexual love, and vice versa. The evidence is the play's own fabric. Mercutio's bawdy intimacy with Romeo, his jealous possession of Romeo's company and his contempt for the Rosaline affair, sustain the reading the series pursues in [the question of Mercutio's desire and the queer readings of the play](/2022/09/23/mercutio-queer-reading-romeo/), where the friend's claim on the lover precedes the heroine's. The Queen Mab speech, which the film gives to Josh on a dark staircase, is the play's great set piece of male imaginative excess, delivered by the character whose energy the heterosexual plot must kill before it can proceed. Patricia's argument is that Brown, by casting the desire the text already circulates, does not decorate the play with a modern agenda; he develops a potential the text holds in suspension, and the film's fidelity to the words is what lets him prove it, since nothing in the speeches had to change for the proof to work.

The stage's own history stands behind the film as precedent.
Patricia's premise can be tested against the film's own casting, where it passes a hard examination. If the play's heterosexual romance is genuinely imbricated in male attachment, then Mercutio, the romance's great saboteur inside the text, should be the figure through whom a male-cast production's energies concentrate, and in Brown's film that is exactly the distribution of force: Josh's Mercutio is the corps' sun, the wit everyone orbits, the aggressor everyone fears, the cadet whose staircase Queen Mab and whose coercion scene bracket the lovers' story as its two poles of enchantment and enforcement. The film did not have to be designed that way; the doubling was Brown's choice, argued from motive, and its effect is to build Patricia's thesis into the cast list. The desire that the play circulates between Romeo and Mercutio, and that criticism has traced in Mercutio's jealous mockery of the Rosaline affair and his proprietary hold on Romeo's company, becomes in the film a structural fact: the same boy who woos the audience with the play's most erotic imagination is the boy who polices the heroine's obedience. A spectator persuaded by the film has been persuaded by casting before argument, which is the strongest form adaptation criticism can take.
 Shakespeare's Juliet was created by a boy actor, in a company where every woman's part was a male performance, and the play's text carries the marks of that origin in its jokes and its meta-theatrical turns. The tradition of all-male performance never fully died, and the gender devices of the early modern stage, cross-dressing, doubling and the boy heroine, form a system that the series treats at length elsewhere; within that system, Private Romeo is best placed as the screen's most literal recovery of the original casting condition, with one decisive difference. On Shakespeare's stage the boy Juliet was a convention the audience was trained not to see. In Brown's film the male Juliet is the thing the audience is asked to see, and to keep seeing, until the pronouns stop sounding strange and the convention inverts: the uniformed male body becomes the transparent medium, and the verse's femininity becomes the costume. Brown understood the continuity and used it as his licence for the pronouns. The film is, in that precise sense, an original-practices production that happens to be set in a barracks.

The genealogy also runs through the film's nearer ancestors. Calarco's stage play established the schoolboy frame in 1997; Brown's film carried it into the military academy in 2011; and the pairing of Private Romeo with Tom Gustafson's Were the World Mine (2008), the queer school musical built on A Midsummer Night's Dream, has become a standard scholarly conjunction, the two films treated together in the literature on queering Shakespeare on screen as the twin exhibits of the school-Shakespeare subgenre. The conjunction flatters both. Gustafson's film dissolves the play into fantasy and song; Brown's refuses fantasy and keeps the text under discipline. Between them they mark the range of what a queer school adaptation can do, and Brown's is the harder, colder and more durable object, the one that trusts the play to generate the queerness itself rather than importing it. That trust is the film's critical signature, and it is why the film rewards the kind of close, text-first attention this series exists to give.
The original-practices parallel can be pressed one step further, because it separates Brown's film from every previous all-male staging in one respect. On Shakespeare's stage, the boy heroine was a convention the audience was trained to see through; the performer's sex was the theatre's open secret, politely unobserved. In Calarco's schoolroom, the all-male enactment was a discovery the characters made about themselves, the play as contraband revelation. Brown's film takes the third position: the male casting is neither transparent convention nor private discovery but public fact, visible to every character in the fiction and policed by the institution around it. Glenn's Juliet is not hidden from the corps; the corps watches it happen and responds with the hazing chair and the coercion scene. The film thereby becomes the first version of the device in which the all-male casting has consequences inside the story, which is why it, rather than its stage parent, is the version that belongs to the history of the policy it was made under. What the academy punishes is not a feeling it cannot see. It punishes a performance it has watched, in a text it assigned, spoken in pronouns it never thought to change.

## Sam and Glenn: Two Performances Inside One Uniform

The film's design throws unusual weight onto its two leads, since they are the only cadets who never double, and the performances repay the attention the structure forces on them. Numrich's Sam is built on hesitancy. His Romeo enters the text the way his cadet enters the feeling, by drift and accident, a boy who finds Romeo's speeches waiting for him at the exact points where his own vocabulary fails, and the performance's intelligence lies in letting the verse seem discovered rather than delivered. The casting carried a resonance nobody planned: Numrich went from this film into originating Albert Narracott in War Horse at Lincoln Center, a boy-and-horse story about devotion inside a military catastrophe, while Doyle, who had played Glenn opposite him, took the part of Billy Narracott and understudied Albert in the same production, the two cadets re-enlisted together in the theatre's other great fable of young men and war. The simultaneity was already a fact during the film's shoot, when both actors were auditioning for the play, and it gives Private Romeo a documentary undertow for any spectator who knows it: the fiction of cadets rehearsing a text between duties was being lived, in mirror image, by the actors rehearsing their next production between takes.

Doyle's Glenn is the harder assignment, and the film's admirers have usually known it. Juliet is the play's risk-taker, the character who proposes the marriage, drinks the potion and drives the plot's every irreversible step, and the film's structure honours the fact by giving its Juliet the story's initiative at every turn. Doyle plays the part without a single gesture of apology or camp, which is the production's central discipline in miniature: the performance trusts that a young man in uniform speaking Juliet's lines at full seriousness will read as truth rather than stunt, and the trust is vindicated in scene after scene, above all in the name speech, where the play's argument about labels is delivered by the person in the film with most reason to feel labelled. The singing that closes the film was a discovery made after casting, when Brown saw the video of Doyle performing Sam Cooke at Joe's Pub and rebuilt an ending around a voice he had not known he had hired. The accident completes the performance's arc: the cadet who spent the film speaking other people's words ends it with a voice unmistakably his own, and the audience, without being instructed, understands that the voice was the destination all along.

Appleman's Josh stands third in the film's economy and first in its dangers, because the doubling makes him both the play's wittiest voice and its persecutor. The Queen Mab speech on the dark staircase is the performance's summit, virtuosity placed deliberately in near-invisibility, a cadet showing off for other cadets in the one register the academy cannot grade. Around that summit Appleman builds the aggression Cline catalogued, the pranks, the sporting challenges, the physical attacks, so that Mercutio's charm and the corps' cruelty share a face and the audience's affection is made complicit in the enforcement it deplores. When the play's machinery reaches the duel, translated into the academy's own violence, the doubling collects its debt: the boy the audience has enjoyed most is the boy through whom the institution has been doing its work. The remaining five performances are sized to the design, Barnett's Prince a study in borrowed authority, Hudock's Gus divided between friendship and the Lady's pressure, Bresky's Omar the circle's licensed fool, Barrie's Adam its quiet chemist, Moreno's Carlos its concentrated threat, and the ensemble's joint acting prize at Outfest stands as the record's judgement that the film's acting, like its authorship, is a company's work.

## The Critics Divide: Perfection, Sterility and the Naming Question

The film's reception splits into two quarrels, one among the reviewers who met it in 2011 and 2012, and one among the scholars who have claimed it since, and the second quarrel is the first one grown rigorous. The daily reviewers divided on the film's heat. Jeannette Catsoulis, in the New York Times of 9 February 2012, made it a Critic's Pick and reached for the largest available compliment: Shakespeare himself, she wrote, would spring for a ticket to Brown's restaging of the play as a modern-day gay tragedy, performed as it is, in her phrase, to perfection. Her capsule summary caught the film's double allegiance with a reviewer's economy, calling it an earnest experiment in don't-ask-don't-tell drama as indebted to shirtlessness as to iambic pentameter. Rex Reed in the New York Observer praised the film as hugely adventurous and highly liberated, and added the sentence its distributor had reason to treasure: no contemporary film that promotes love instead of war should be overlooked. Variety credited the dynamite ensemble with investing the poetry in energetic immediacy, a trade-paper verdict that the verse, in these trained mouths, played as action rather than recitation. Against that front rank stood the dissenters. Slant Magazine judged that the film feels more like a side project from the producers of Glee than a novel queering of Shakespeare's text, and delivered the phrase that has attached itself to the film's negative reputation: for a story about passionate love in its most delusional proportion, the review concluded, Private Romeo is decidedly sterile. Kyle Smith in the New York Post awarded one star of four and mocked the production's means, suggesting the cost of tickets for a small party might exceed the budget. Joe Neumaier in the New York Daily News split his verdict, finding the modern additions clunky while granting that the leads were intense, engaged and appropriately tragic.
Rich Cline's review for Shadows on the Wall states the sceptical case at its most reasonable, and it deserves separation from the Post's jeering. Cline found the film bold in conception yet felt it never quite brings the story to life, and he objected on craft grounds to the double naming, the cadets carrying their own names laid over the play's parts, which he judged a distancing device that keeps the audience from surrendering to the fiction. The objection identifies a real cost and misprices it. The double naming is the film's alienation effect, the seam it deliberately refuses to hide: a spectator who always knows that Glenn is Glenn, even while Juliet's speeches are in his mouth, is being asked to watch two things at once, a cadet and a heroine, and to hold the institution responsible for the distance between them. A seamless film, in which the cadets simply became their parts, would be the escapist object Cline's standard implies; Brown's seams are where its politics show. The naming complaint thus joins the sterility complaint as a description of the design mistaken for a defect of it, and the pattern is worth naming because it recurs through the film's negative reception: the reviewers who disliked Private Romeo most intelligently disliked exactly what it was doing, on purpose, for reasons their notices rarely pause to ask.


The scholars inherit the quarrel at a higher resolution. Patricia's chapter on the film, in Queering the Shakespeare Film (2017), argues that Brown succeeds at fully queering the play, that the film stands as a sustained and successful challenge to the always assumed heteronormativity of the text and its screen tradition. His evidence is formal: the retained words, the male bodies, and above all the ending, in which the refusal to kill the lovers breaks the tragedy's deepest habit, the conversion of queer desire into beautiful death. Van den Oever's essay, "What's in a Name?" (2021), answers that the film, intended as an affirmative depiction of gay love, achieves for three reasons the opposite effect. No character in the film speaks the word homosexuality, although the play the cadets read, and the policy under which the film was made, both turn on the naming of desire. The film pairs homosexuality on its visual track, two male bodies kissing, with a declaration of heterosexuality on its audio track, the retained verse of Romeo and Juliet's romance, so that the kiss is superseded by the Shakespearean characters and their story. And reading the film as a representation of homosexuality at all requires the spectator to supply a homophobic context, the academy and the policy, without which the love story would not register as transgressive. Where Patricia sees the text liberated by the bodies, van den Oever sees the bodies ventriloquised by the text.

The adjudication this article reaches is that both scholars are right about different layers of the same object, and that the film is best judged by holding their claims apart. On everything the film does with performance, design and plot, Patricia's account stands. The retained verse in trained voices is the film's life, exactly as Catsoulis and Reed reported from the auditorium; the colour design argues the lovers' case in every frame; and the ending is a genuine formal event, the tragedy's machinery disengaged on principle, which no account of the film as a timid or sterile object can explain. Slant's sterility charge, examined closely, mistakes the film's subject for its failure: the sameness the review deplored, the lovers perceived as interchangeable figures in identical dress, is the academy's uniform doing its intended work, and the film's cold surfaces are the temperature of the institution it indicts. On the narrower question of speech, however, van den Oever wins his point, and it is not a small one. The film cannot name its subject in its own voice. Every declaration arrives in quotation, attributed to characters in a play, and the word at the centre of the film's politics is never spoken by anyone in it. Brown's defenders reply that the silence is documentary, that inside the policy the declaration could only be made in borrowed words, and the reply is strong; but it concedes the fact while contesting the blame. The film's own ending appears to accept van den Oever's diagnosis and answer it. The closing song is the one moment a cadet voices desire outright, in his own voice, in words owned by no dramatist and no institution, a popular standard sung because the singer, not the syllabus, chose it. Judged whole, then: Patricia is right about the achievement, van den Oever is right about the boundary, and the film knows where its boundary lies, which is why it ends with a song instead of a speech. The text the film trusts for its quotations is Levenson's Oxford edition, and the quarrel's terms are worth one final precision: nobody in the debate accuses Brown of mishandling the words. The fight is over what keeping them perfectly does.

## Festivals, Wolfe Releasing and the Film's Afterlife

The film's public life began in the festival circuit whose judgement it has kept. The premiere came at Frameline, the San Francisco lesbian and gay film festival, on 20 June 2011, in the policy's twilight described earlier. At Outfest in Los Angeles the following month, the Grand Jury Prize for Outstanding Actor in a Feature Film went not to either lead but to the company entire, all eight cadets sharing the award, a distribution of credit that matched the film's ensemble construction; the jury's citation praised the cast for lending the story a twenty-first-century immediacy and for the brilliant coherence of its performances. Wolfe Releasing, the film's distributor and a specialist in gay cinema, opened it theatrically at Cinema Village in New York on 10 February 2012, where business held strongly enough for the engagement to be extended into March, and the home release followed in May 2012, with a British release that April. The aggregator record preserved from the release period, 53 out of 100 across seven critics at Metacritic and 55 percent across eleven reviews at Rotten Tomatoes, understates the film's standing, because it averages the Critic's Pick enthusiasm of the Times and the Observer against the Post's one-star dismissal as though the disagreement were about quality rather than about what kind of object the film is. Disagreement of that shape is itself data: the film polarised because it committed, and polarising objects outlast agreeable ones.

A production of this scale lives or dies by its paper trail, and the film's trail is unusually well kept: the Frameline and Outfest programmes, the distributor's synopsis and jury citation, the metropolitan reviews, the Film International interview in which Brown explains the work at length, two dissertations' worth of scholarly attention and Patricia's chapter in a standard academic monograph. Researchers assembling such a record for an adaptation study, cross-checking a festival date against a review date against a director's later recollection, do their filing where it cannot silently change under them; a personal archive tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) suits exactly this work, holding the clippings, the citations and the drafts in one local store while the links around them rot. The discipline matters for this film in particular, because its reputation has been carried by small documents, a festival citation here, a capsule review there, and the record's survival is the reason a 98-minute independent film can sustain the weight of analysis this article has placed on it.

The afterlife has been kind in the ways that count for a small film. The leads' simultaneous appearance in War Horse at Lincoln Center carried the film's casting into theatre history; Doyle's later Tony Award for Company gave the Juliet of McKinley Academy a second, brighter line in the record books; and the film settled into the syllabuses and the scholarly literature, where its conjunction with Calarco's play and with Were the World Mine keeps it in active discussion rather than in archive. In the series' recurring inquiry into [which film version of the play stands as the best](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/), Private Romeo will never win a popularity count against the famous romances of 1968 or 1996, and it does not enter that contest; its claim is different in kind, the claim of the experiment that isolated the variable.
The record books have since added their own footnote to the casting. Doyle's Tony Award for Company placed the film's Juliet inside Broadway's highest honours, a distinction no other screen Juliet of the modern era carries in quite that form, the tragedy's heroine decorated by the musical theatre for playing, among other things, a commitment-shy New Yorker in a Sondheim revival. The irony is gentle and worth recording: the actor whose Juliet was accused by one magazine of sterility was later honoured for a performance built on withheld feeling, as though the quality the film's detractors deplored were simply the actor's register, misread in one house and prized in another. Numrich's stage career likewise carried the film's themes forward, the War Horse simultaneity hardening into a specialism in young men at war. Casting histories rarely comment on a source text, but this one does: the film hired theatre actors at the start of distinguished stage careers, and their subsequent work keeps re-staging the film's constellation, youth, uniform, devotion and song, as if the production had been an audition for the decade that followed it.
 By changing the bodies and the ending while changing nothing in the language, it measured what the language was carrying. Few adaptations of any play can say as much, and none of this play's other retellings can.

## The Verdict on Private Romeo

The film's achievement can be stated as three findings, each earned by the sections that precede this one. First, the play's text survives same-sex casting without a syllable's gender altered, and more than survives: the retained pronouns, licensed by the play's own first casting, turn the verse into the lovers' only available language, which is a discovery about the text and not merely about the production. Second, the feud survives translation into a single institution, because Brown correctly located the feud's essence in enforcement rather than in ancestry: ranks, rituals, peer coercion and a concept named Paris do the work of two households, and do it with a political precision the family version cannot reach inside a story about a policy of enforced silence. Third, the tragedy does not survive, and is not meant to: the withheld Prologue and the living lovers refuse the settlement by which the forbidding world purchases absolution with the lovers' bodies, and the refusal, sealed by a closing song sung in the actor's own voice, is the film's thesis about its source. Against these findings stand the limits, which this article has not hidden: the original dialogue is thin wherever the cadets speak for themselves, the picture needs its hostile frame to generate the stakes van den Oever identified, and its means are modest to the point that hostile reviewers could mistake economy for poverty.

Ranked among the modern retellings, Private Romeo is the most intellectually consequential of them, because it is the only one whose interventions are confined to selection, casting and frame while the words that remain are Shakespeare's, unaltered in gender and sense; it is also, not coincidentally, the retelling that scholars argue about rather than merely cite.
Set beside its companions in the modern wave, the film's singularity sharpens. The jukebox and action retellings keep the plot and replace the language; the celebrated romances keep the language and embalm it in period splendour; the classroom and stage versions that share Brown's device inherit Calarco's contraband rather than Brown's curriculum. Only Private Romeo keeps the words entire, changes the bodies entirely, and sends the bodies home alive, and the combination is not a compromise among the other strategies but a refusal of their common assumption, that something in the text must be surrendered to make the story playable in a new world. Brown surrendered nothing in the text that the text itself had not already surrendered, its Chorus, its Paris, its corpses being precisely the elements the play's own history had shown to be negotiable, quartos and promptbooks having cut and reassigned them for centuries. The film is therefore best filed not as a novelty within the play's screen history but as a demonstration inside it, the control experiment the other versions can be measured against: given the words, unchanged, what else must change for the story to mean something new? Brown's answer, an institution, a policy and an ending, is the most economical answer the screen has produced.
 The cliché of the play fixes its lovers as a man and a woman and its ending as a tomb. Brown's cadets, left alone with the set text in an emptied academy, restore the older and stranger truth the first audiences knew, that the lovers were two male performers and a book of speeches, and that the speeches were always the point. The film ends with the survivors singing. On the evidence gathered here, the singing is earned.

One consequence of the film's method deserves a final statement, because it reaches beyond this film to the series' whole subject. Adaptation is usually measured by what a version adds to its source: new settings, new subplots, new lines, new endings defended as interpretations. Private Romeo is the limiting case that exposes the measure's inadequacy, a version that adds almost nothing and alters the source's meaning almost completely, working only with selection, casting, doubling, colour and one withheld catastrophe. Its lesson for the study of the play on screen is that the meaning of Romeo and Juliet was never located solely in its text, nor solely in its staging, but in the pressure between the two, and that a director who understands the pressure can move the meaning by touching nothing but the frame. Brown's cadets are left alone with the set text for a single emptied term, and by the time the song ends, the text has acquitted itself of every charge the institution brought against it. The film is the proof, conducted under laboratory conditions of its own devising, and the laboratory is a school.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Private Romeo note, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch Private Romeo note is the reference table in this article that sets the play's elements against the film's reframings, row by row, with a third column stating what each change does dramatically. It places the Chorus, the lovers, Mercutio, the Nurse, the Friar, the Prince and Paris beside their academy counterparts, and it records the film's great absences, the cut Prologue and the unembodied Paris, alongside its presences. The pattern the note exposes is the film's method: the language is the constant, the enforcement is the variable, and the deaths are the one element openly refused.

### Q: Who wrote and directed Private Romeo, and when did it premiere?

Alan Brown wrote and directed Private Romeo, building the film on Joe Calarco's stage play Shakespeare's R&J, whose name stands with Brown's and Shakespeare's in the writing credits preserved by reference listings. Brown also produced, with Agathe David-Weill and Kevin Ginty. The film premiered at Frameline, the San Francisco lesbian and gay film festival, on 20 June 2011, in competition season that continued at Outfest in Los Angeles, where its cast won the ensemble acting prize. Its theatrical opening followed at Cinema Village in New York on 10 February 2012, through Wolfe Releasing, with a home release that May.

### Q: Where is Private Romeo set, and who are Sam Singleton and Glenn Mangan?

The film is set at the fictional McKinley Military Academy, emptied of all but eight cadets during a training exercise, and played out across its classrooms, dormitories, courts and laboratories on a deserted campus. Sam Singleton, played by Seth Numrich, is the cadet who drifts into Romeo's part as the class reading takes hold of the corps. Glenn Mangan, played by Matt Doyle, is the cadet who takes Juliet's part, keeping the play's pronouns and speeches unchanged. Their love story is conducted almost entirely in Shakespeare's words, inside the institution whose code forbids it.

### Q: Why are eight cadets left alone at the academy in the film?

The eight are left behind because the rest of the school has shipped out on a training exercise, leaving the campus to the cadets who remain; the scholarly account of the film adds that the eight did not qualify for the navigation exercises the others are performing. The vacancy is the film's enabling condition. With no officers, no classes in session except the literature reading, and no crowd to perform normality for, the academy's spaces empty into a stage, and the set text the cadets are studying becomes the only script available for what they feel and fear.

### Q: Does Private Romeo use Shakespeare's original dialogue?

Yes: the film's lovers' scenes, and most of its drama, are played in Shakespeare's text, largely intact and unaltered in gender, with Brown's own contemporary dialogue kept to a minimum. Brown shot additional modern scenes establishing the cadets' ordinary lives and removed most of them in the edit, reasoning that the play should be reached faster and that the cadets' poverty of language is the point: they express through the borrowed verse what their own speech cannot carry. The text is cited in this article from Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition, the version whose lines the film preserves.

### Q: Why does the film keep the play's female pronouns for its Juliet figure?

The film keeps the pronouns because Brown treated the verse as music that rewriting would damage, and because the play's first performances supply the precedent: Shakespeare's company staged Juliet with a boy actor and changed nothing. Brown argued that altering a line about the lady into a line about a gentleman would be rewriting Shakespeare, while cutting and redistributing lines was legitimate adaptation. The kept pronouns become the film's demonstration that its love story needs no translation, that the desire in the speeches was stored in their situations rather than in their grammar.

### Q: Which actor plays Mercutio, and what second role does he take?

Hale Appleman plays Josh Neff, the cadet who takes Mercutio's part, including the Queen Mab speech delivered on a darkened staircase. Josh's second function is the film's boldest doubling: he speaks Capulet's coercive lines in the scene where Glenn is pressed toward the future the academy has chosen for him. Brown assigned the father's threats to the friend because the pressure that governs a cadet's life arrives from inside his circle. The doubling makes the corps' wit and its chief enforcer the same person, so friendship itself becomes the instrument of the tragedy's coercion.

### Q: Who plays the Nurse in Private Romeo?

Chris Bresky plays Omar Madsen, the cadet who takes the Nurse's part in the film's distribution of roles. The play's bawdy, garrulous confidante becomes a contemporary of the lovers, a fellow cadet inside their secret rather than a servant beneath it, and the change follows the film's general law that every adult in Shakespeare becomes a peer at McKinley. Brown shot a scene of Omar's Nurse smoking with the Friar figure and cut it in the edit. The Nurse's complicity survives the translation intact: counsel, teasing and errands all pass through the corps' own membership.

### Q: What happened to Paris in the film's version of the story?

Paris was never embodied: Brown considered casting the Count and decided instead that Paris was more a concept than a character, the idea of heterosexuality itself and the threat it poses to the lovers. The suitor's pressure survives without the suitor, diffused into the academy's expectations, the families' hopes and the coercion scene in which Josh and Gus urge the acceptable future on Glenn. Removing the actor while keeping the function is the film's neatest analytical stroke, since Shakespeare's Paris is himself barely a personality, a deadline with a name, and the film simply keeps the deadline.

### Q: Why does nobody die at the end of Private Romeo?

Nobody dies because Brown refused, as a matter of stated principle, to make a film in which the gay lovers are punished or killed. He wanted gay love stories on film that end well, against the pattern the culture knew from Brokeback Mountain, and he prepared the refusal structurally by cutting the Prologue that announces the deaths in the play. Sam, Glenn, Josh and Carlos all survive the film. The tragedy's settlement, in which the forbidding world is shamed by the lovers' corpses, is declined, and the lovers are left alive inside the institution that forbade them.

### Q: What song does Matt Doyle sing at the end of the film, and why was it chosen?

Doyle closes the film singing "You Made Me Love You (I Didn't Want to Do It)", the 1913 standard by James V. Monaco and Joseph McCarthy, performed in his own voice. The song entered the production after Brown saw a video of Doyle singing Sam Cooke at Joe's Pub and rebuilt the ending around his voice, abandoning a planned sequence in which all eight cadets would perform a music video together. Its age placed it in the public domain, within reach of a production without a licensing budget. It is the film's one utterance chosen by the singer rather than assigned.

### Q: What award did the film's cast win at Outfest?

The entire eight-man cast shared the Grand Jury Prize for Outstanding Actor in a Feature Film at Outfest, the Los Angeles gay and lesbian film festival, in 2011. The award went to the company rather than to either lead, ratifying the film's ensemble construction, in which the lovers' scenes depend on the corps around them. The jury citation praised the performances for lending the story a twenty-first-century immediacy and for their brilliant coherence as a company's work. Seth Numrich and Matt Doyle, Hale Appleman, Charlie Barnett, Adam Barrie, Chris Bresky, Sean Hudock and Bobby Moreno were honoured together.

### Q: What did the New York Times say about Private Romeo?

The New York Times made the film a Critic's Pick on its theatrical opening, in Jeannette Catsoulis's review of 9 February 2012. Catsoulis wrote that Shakespeare himself would spring for a ticket to Brown's restaging of the play as a modern-day gay tragedy, and judged the performances to perfection. Her capsule summary described an earnest experiment in don't-ask-don't-tell drama as indebted to shirtlessness as to iambic pentameter, a sentence that caught both the film's politics and its physical candour. The review anchored the film's favourable metropolitan reception against the dismissals published elsewhere the same week.

### Q: Why did some critics call Private Romeo sterile while others praised it?

The split runs through the film's surfaces. Slant Magazine judged the picture decidedly sterile, a story of passionate love rendered without heat, and compared it unfavourably to a television musical's side project; the New York Post awarded one star and mocked its budget. The Times, the Observer and Variety praised the same object for its ensemble's energetic immediacy and its adventurousness. The disagreement is about what the film's coldness is: a failure of feeling, as the dissenters held, or the temperature of the institution the film indicts, its lovers' sameness the academy's uniform at work.

### Q: What does the scholar Anthony Guy Patricia argue the film achieves?

Patricia, in Queering the Shakespeare Film (2017), argues that Brown succeeds at fully queering the play, mounting a sustained and successful challenge to the always assumed heteronormativity of the text and its screen tradition. His premise is that heterosexual love in Romeo and Juliet is already imbricated in male homosexual love, and vice versa, so the film develops a potential the text holds rather than imposing an agenda on it. His strongest evidence is formal: the retained words, the male bodies, and the ending that refuses to convert queer desire into beautiful death.

### Q: What is Roel van den Oever's objection to the film's treatment of homosexuality?

Van den Oever, writing in 2021, argues that the film achieves the opposite of its affirmative intention, for three connected reasons. No character speaks the word homosexuality, although the play and the Don't Ask, Don't Tell context both turn on the naming of desire. The film pairs homosexuality on its visual track, two male bodies, with a declaration of heterosexuality on its audio track, the retained verse, so the kiss is superseded by the Shakespearean romance. And the film reads as a representation of homosexuality only if the spectator supplies the homophobic context that makes the love transgressive.

### Q: Where was Private Romeo filmed?

The film was shot in the summer of 2010 on the SUNY Maritime campus in the Bronx, New York, a working maritime college standing in for the fictional McKinley Military Academy after the production's location manager found it standing deserted for the season. Brown kept the campus's real geography in the frame, its drill fields, dormitories, courts and river light, while avoiding the views that would identify it. The authenticity does quiet work in the finished film: nothing looks built, so the play's invasion of the academy reads as an event inside a documentary world.

### Q: How does the film's colour design separate the academy from the play?

Brown and cinematographer Derek McKane built two palettes and achieved them in camera rather than in post-production. The academy's official life is desaturated toward grey, khaki and pale institutional blue, the colours of administration and uniform. When the play takes hold of a scene, the image deepens and warms into saturation, the same dormitories and courts transfigured. The spectator learns to read the film's two worlds from the colour alone. Scholarship on the film records the effect at its strongest in the duel and the final scene, where the frame tips into warm oversaturation.

### Q: How is Friar Laurence reimagined inside the academy?

Adam Barrie plays Adam Hersh, the cadet who takes Friar Laurence's part, and Brown conceived the figure as the school's drug dealer, the one cadet who can supply altered states. His cell is a science laboratory, which gives the translation its logic: the peer who controls the chemicals can plausibly furnish counsel, a plan and a potion. The reimagining follows the film's rule that Shakespeare's adults become cadets, and it sharpens the play's own portrait of the Friar as a well-meaning amateur whose chemistry outruns his judgement, without even the excuse of age or office.

### Q: How should the InsightCrunch Private Romeo note be used in an essay?

The note should be used as the evidence base for the argument that the film changes the frame and not the words. An essay can take its rows in sequence, showing that the lovers' speeches survive unaltered in gender while every enforcing element, the feud, the Prince, the coercion, Paris himself, is rebuilt or removed, and then put the note's final rows to work on the withheld Prologue and the refused deaths. The strongest essays will also test the note against the scholarly quarrel, asking whether van den Oever's objection about naming survives the table's evidence.
